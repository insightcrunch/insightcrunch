## Cold Open

![Nightjar: A Living Example in the Wharf Night Infirmary - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-47.webp)

Sabel Orry had learned the wharf at night by the order in which things had to be touched. The seal round began at the north shed where the canal water knocked soft against the pilings and the lamps burned low behind wet glass, and it went on by bonded doors and chained yards and narrow passages between warehouses where the stone held the damp of the day and gave it back slowly after dark. She carried the seal book against her chest under her coat when the rain came sideways, and she carried the keys in a pocket that had been mended twice, and she carried a pencil worn short from use because a long pencil snapped when she pressed too hard in the cold. She was two years older than she looked and she knew that men on the wharf mistook that for inexperience until they watched her work through a row of seals without pausing to ask which lock belonged to which door. She did not hurry. Hurrying on wet stone was how clerks fell and how seals were missed, and a missed seal became a question in the morning that nobody could answer cleanly.

The night had settled into the kind of quiet that was not empty. Water moved in the locks beyond the sheds. A cart stood where its driver had left it with one wheel turned. Somewhere beyond the bonded yards a chain lifted and settled and lifted again as a night crew shifted a load they could not leave half raised. Orry marked each seal as she passed it, the metal cold through her gloves, the stamp firm where firmness mattered and light where old iron had thinned. She wrote the hour in words as the round book required and she wrote the state of each door in the plain language the morning clerks preferred, no flourish, no guess. If a seal was sound she said so. If a seal was doubtful she said that too and she stood a little longer in the damp to be sure doubt was the right word.

She had done this round in the season when a room on the wharf had been found locked from inside and a dead man had been found within it, and the memory of that night lived not as fear but as a habit of attention. She checked corners other clerks passed. She looked at windows other clerks treated as walls. She noticed hands.

The wharf night infirmary stood where the bonded yards gave way to the working quay, set back from the water by a strip of stone and a low rail, its windows lit with the steady light that never quite went out. It served the men and women who cut themselves on wire and crushed fingers under crates and breathed foundry dust until their chests hurt, and it served them without asking first which shed had sent them or which master paid their wage. Orry passed it on every round. Usually the windows showed only the shape of beds and a nurse moving between them and the pale rectangle of a screen. That night one window showed a hand.

She stopped because the hand was raised. It rested against the glass from inside, palm open, fingers spread a little as if the man behind it had meant to steady himself and had forgotten to lower his arm. The light inside made the skin look bloodless except where the bands crossed it. Bands across the palm. Bands across the back where the hand bent at the window edge and the light caught the turn. The bands met at the edges of the hand in a way that made the whole pattern read as one injury rather than several. Orry stood on the wet stone and felt the seal book press hard against her ribs through her coat.

She knew the pattern. She knew it not from the wharf and not from any patient she had seen carried in, but from a photograph that had come to the wharf offices after the trouble in Valtessa, pinned for a time where clerks and seal keepers could see it when they collected their books, with an instruction spoken plainly and then written beneath it in a hand that allowed no argument. Report any sighting. The photograph had shown a palm with scarring in bands, and beside the photograph there had been talk that meant little to her at the time about a practice injury that matched none of the known kinds. Orry had looked because looking was her work. She had remembered because remembering was also her work, though nobody paid her extra for it.

The hand at the window did not move for a long count. Then the fingers flexed once, slowly, as if pain had travelled through them and the man had chosen not to make a sound about it. The back of the hand showed the same bands when it turned. Palm and back, meeting at the edges. The same pattern. Orry stepped closer to the rail that separated the path from the infirmary wall and she kept her distance from the glass itself, because a patient who had raised a hand to a window in the night had not invited a clerk to press her face near his. She could see a bed behind him and the edge of a locker and the shoulder of another patient asleep under a blanket, and she could see that the man with the raised hand was awake and looking out without seeming to see her. His face was partly in shadow. His other hand lay on the blanket and even at that distance she could see that the strength in it was the strength of lifting, broad through the knuckles, the skin thickened where loads had pressed.

She wrote nothing in the seal book. The seal book was for seals. She finished the next three doors with care that cost her effort, because attention that has been seized by one thing will wander from another if it is not held, and she would not have it said that Sabel Orry had marked a seal sound without touching it on the night she saw the hand. At the end of the infirmary wall she turned and looked back once. The hand had lowered. The window showed only light and the shape of a bed and a man sitting now with his head bowed, as if the effort of raising his arm had spent something he had been saving.

Orry walked the rest of the round in order. The gate keeper lifted his head when she passed his hut and she gave him the brief nod that meant the yards were as she had found them. The gate book lay open on his shelf with the night entries ruled in his careful hand. Beyond the gate the town side of Carrowmere slept in canal mist and shuttered shops, and behind her the wharf kept its own hours. She thought about the instruction beneath the photograph. She thought about who would want to be told and how quickly and whether a report made in the morning would be soon enough for a man who might be discharged before the offices opened. She thought about the clerk she had been when she found a locked room and how the Office had listened then, eventually, and how the listening had come with a cost to people who had only done their work in the right order.

In the clerks room at the end of the round she hung her coat where the damp from it would not spoil the books. She dried her gloves on the rail by the stove. She completed the seal book entry by entry and signed it in the place provided. Then she took a separate sheet, not wharf paper but plain paper from the drawer where plain paper was kept for messages that did not belong in any ledger, and she wrote her report in the clear hand that had carried her from junior clerk to the night round without a blot worth naming. She wrote where she had been and at what stage of the round. She wrote that the window had been lit. She wrote that a patient inside had raised a hand to the glass and that the scarring across palm and back, in bands meeting at the edges, matched the pattern in the photograph circulated after Valtessa. She wrote that she had not spoken to the man and had not entered the infirmary and had not learned his name. She wrote that the hand had been strong in the way a labouring hand is strong. She did not write what she thought it meant, because she did not know what it meant, and because a report that guesses invites the reader to argue with the guess instead of acting on the sighting.

She sealed the sheet in an envelope and wrote on the outside only that it concerned the sighting instruction and was to go to the Office without waiting for the morning sorting. She gave it to the night messenger with her own coin for the extra crossing, because the ordinary bag would sit until dawn and dawn felt too late for a hand that might be gone by then. The messenger asked no question. On the wharf at night a sealed envelope from Orry was understood to be exactly what it claimed to be and nothing more entertaining.

After he had gone she stood a moment in the clerks room doorway and listened to the wharf settle. The infirmary light still burned across the yard. No hand showed at the window now. She picked up her empty cup and rinsed it and set it down again in its place, and she thought, with the plain tiredness that comes after a round done properly, that she had reported a man for the shape of an injury he had not chosen to show her, and that if the Office came for his record or for him, her name would be somewhere in the paper that followed, however small the writing. She locked the clerks room. She walked home by the gate, where the keeper marked her out in his book without comment, and the chain at the gate sang once against its post as it settled behind her.

She thought also about the discipline of the round itself, which had taught her more than any instruction pinned beside a photograph. A seal was a small promise that a door had been left as its keeper intended, and her work was to test the promise without breaking it, to record the result without improving it, and to pass on before the testing itself became a kind of interference. The hand at the window asked the same discipline of her in a harder form. She had tested nothing. She had broken nothing. She had recorded only what sight had given her, and now she had to pass the record on and accept that other hands would decide what her seeing set in motion. It was not a comfortable place for a careful person to stand, between a patient who had not asked to be seen and an Office that had asked to be told, and she stood in it without pretending that comfort was the measure of whether she had done right.

## Tasking

The report reached Mira before the Office had decided whether it was a case at all, and that order of events decided everything that followed. It came by the night messenger to the lodgings where she had been trying and failing to sleep with a month of somebody else's chronic pain moving under her own skin, pain taken from an attacker's coat in Valtessa that woke and settled with her movements and that she could not always tell from her own when she turned in the dark. She read Orry's plain hand twice by lamplight. Palm and back. Bands meeting at the edges. The same pattern as the scarring across the palm and the back of one hand of Sabin Roche, taken in Valtessa, in bands that met at the edges of the hand, matching none of the three known practice injuries in the Office table, a pattern nobody in the Office could name. She sat with the sheet flat under her palm and felt the cold that had nothing to do with any read settle along her shoulders, because a living man in a wharf infirmary was carrying the injury that a dead inquiry had left unexplained, and he was lying eleven days into treatment within reach of the morning.

She did not wait for a tasking. That was the first fact and she knew it as she dressed. The officer who had carried the marked hands inquiry had standing to follow a sighting made under an instruction the Office itself had circulated, and if she sent the report upward and waited for a meeting and a minute and a decision about whether a labourer's scarred hand in a civilian infirmary belonged to the Ninth Office at all, the man could be discharged, or moved, or taken, or simply lost into the wharf's own population before any authorised officer set foot on the quay. She took the case herself. She wrote a short notice for the Office registry in the form that recorded an officer's assumption of an inquiry already begun, she named Orry's report as its ground and the infirmary as its place, and she sent it by the early bag with no request attached, because a request would have invited a refusal she had already decided not to wait for. The notice informed minimally and late by design, and she understood the design would be read exactly as she meant it by anyone who wished to read it unkindly.

She went to the wharf before the seal round changed hands. Orry met her in the clerks room with the seal book still open to the night entries, tired in the contained way of a person who finishes work properly even when the work has followed her home in her thoughts. From Orry, in order and without embroidery, Mira established the four things she needed before she set foot inside the infirmary. First, Orry's shift: the night seal round began at the north shed, passed the bonded doors in a fixed order, reached the infirmary wall a little after the middle of the round, and ended at the clerks room where the book was signed and the coat was hung; the round ran every night Orry was rostered, its hours known to every keeper and crew on the wharf, which meant Orry's movements were predictable to anyone who cared to learn them. Second, the ledger cabinet: Orry had carried messages into the infirmary often enough to describe the record room without having opened anything in it, a small room off the main ward, and in it a cabinet of dark metal with drawers in patient order, the current files in the upper drawers and the bound ledger kept beside it, the metal scuffed where years of hands had pulled the same handles. Third, the man's hands: seen at the window and described in the report, palm and back in bands, and strong with the strength of lifting even at rest. Fourth, the gate: the wharf gate at the end of the quay road, its keeper in his hut with the gate book open on a shelf at his elbow, every entry and exit marked by hand, the chain and the post and the narrow pedestrian way beside the cart way, the only ordinary way out that Orry would later walk with anything she carried from inside the wharf.

Mira asked no question twice. She thanked Orry as one worker thanks another and she did not promise that the report would stay separate from whatever followed, because she could not promise it honestly. Orry nodded once, as if that honesty were the reason she had sent the envelope by night messenger instead of leaving it for the morning sorting.

Mira walked the infirmary wall in daylight that was not yet full day. The window showed beds and a screen and no raised hand. Inside, the ward smelled of soap and wet wool and the faint iron smell of water boiled too often in the same kettle. She gave her name and her office to the desk and asked to see the record of the patient whose hands matched the circulated pattern, and she watched the request travel across the face of the woman at the desk and harden there, because medical paper was not Office paper and everyone on the wharf knew the difference even when an officer pretended the difference was procedural. She was made to wait. Waiting was information too. While she waited she learned the ward by looking: which bed stood nearest the window, which locker belonged to it, how the rail was fixed to the frame, where a man lying eleven days in pain would put his hands when the pain rose and where he would put them when he wished to appear at ease.

It was while she was already committed, with her name spoken at the desk and Orry's report copied into her own notebook and the infirmary's refusal still forming in the next room, that Arvo Leyden's message arrived. It came not as a summons, which might still have stopped her if she had chosen obedience over momentum, but as a written note carried by an Office runner who found her in the infirmary corridor and handed it over without staying for a reply. Leyden wrote in the spare hand he used when he wished every word to survive being read aloud in a room he was not in. He named the cost after she had taken the case, and the naming was new and worse than any warning before the fact could have been, because it could not stop her, only record her. She acted, he wrote, on a clerk's report, in the wharf she used to work, with a civilian asset, while carrying a month of pain that was not hers and a register she no longer held. Her private register of absences, a notebook she kept against instruction, given voluntarily to Harrand in the inquiry before last and now an Accord document in oversight's keeping with a numbered Accord label on its cover, had taught oversight how to read her, and oversight would read this the same way: as the register all over again, an officer building a private pattern out of other people's injuries and acting before the Office had agreed the pattern existed. He did not order her back. He recorded that she had been told the price while there was still time to pay a smaller one by stopping, and he left the stopping to her.

Mira folded the note into her notebook against Orry's report. She thought of the hand at the window and of eleven days in a strange bed and of a file in a metal cabinet that somebody else might already have read. She went back to the desk and asked again, in the same even voice, to see the record and to be taken to the bed by the window. The cost had been named. It had not been paid yet. She understood that the rest of the case would be the paying of it, in instalments she could not yet count, and she understood also that Leyden knew that and had chosen the timing of his note so that she would understand it with her feet already on the infirmary floor and her name already in the desk book by the door.

Before she left the clerks room she asked Orry one further question, and she asked it in the form that would make the answer useful rather than merely reassuring. If the man left the infirmary that day, where would he go and how quickly could a clerk who knew the wharf know it. Orry considered the question with the seriousness she gave to any question about movement through a place she policed by walking it. He would be marked out at the gate in the gate book if he left by the gate, she said. If he left by the water stairs with a crew, the crew boat book at the quay hut would carry the tally if the hut keeper was awake to his duty. If he simply walked into the bonded yards and found work and a corner to sleep in among men who asked no questions of a pair of hands that could lift, then no book on the wharf would carry him at all, because the yards absorbed labour the way the canal absorbed rain. Mira wrote that down too, because a case that begins with a man in a bed should know from its first hour how many doors the bed stands among.

She also asked what Orry would do if nobody came. The question surprised Orry, who had clearly expected the Office to arrive as inevitably as the morning sorting, and her answer stayed with Mira on the walk across the yard. She would do the next round, Orry said. She would pass the window. If the hand was there she would see it and if it was gone she would see that, and either way she would have a book to sign and a coat to hang and no instruction anywhere that told her what a clerk was supposed to do with a second sighting of a thing she had already reported once and heard nothing about. It was a plain account of how institutions lose the very witnesses they depend on, not by frightening them but by leaving them uninformed in the corridor outside the decision, and Mira resolved, with a resolve she noted as carefully as any evidence, that whatever else the case cost, it would not cost Orry the courtesy of being told what her report had started. That resolve, too, would have a price before the case was done, though neither woman could have named it in the clerks room with the stove ticking and the seal book drying its ink between them.

The walk to the infirmary in the half light gave Mira time to feel the state she was entering the case in, and she took the inventory honestly because an officer who lies about her own condition will lie about other evidence without noticing the habit form. Her endurance was reduced. The pain from Valtessa moved when she moved, a chronic accompaniment that sharpened when she reached for a latch with the wrong hand and dulled when she walked at an even pace on even ground, and the nights of broken sleep behind her had thinned the margin she usually carried between perception and conclusion. She was cold already, before any read, from the wind off the canal. She was an officer acting without a tasking in a place where she had once been junior enough to be known by sight to half the keepers, carrying a pattern in her head that she had matched from a photograph and a clerk's description, and every part of that sentence was a reason for a careful Office to slow her down. It was also true that the man might be gone by the time care had finished its deliberations. Both things were true at once, and the case would be shaped by her refusal to let either truth erase the other.

## Ground Truth

The bed by the window stood where Orry's report had led Mira to expect it, close enough to the glass that a man lying in it could raise one hand and touch the pane without sitting fully upright, and far enough from the ward door that the comings and goings of the desk did not pass directly beside his pillow. The frame was iron, painted and repainted until the paint had softened the edges of the joints. The rail along the side nearest the locker was fixed with bolts whose heads had been polished by hands and by cloth. The locker beside the bed was narrow and tall, with a cup and a folded cloth on its top and a pair of boots set squarely beneath it, the boots of a man who expected to put them on himself when the time came and who had placed them where his feet would find them without searching. Mira learned the bed before she spoke to the man in it, because the bed had held him for eleven days and because what a place holds can be asked in a way a person cannot.

Vaun Mell watched her learn it. He was awake, propped against pillows that the morning nurse had arranged with the economy of long practice, and his hands lay on the blanket where she could see them without asking him to show her anything. The scarring crossed one hand in bands, palm and back, meeting at the edges of the hand, the pattern stated once and plainly because it was the whole reason for her presence: the same bands that had crossed Roche's hand in Valtessa, the same meeting at the edges, the same refusal to match any known injury in the Office table. The other hand lay plainer in comparison and she noted the difference and kept it, because consistency in what was seen mattered more than symmetry in what was expected. Mell's hands were strong in the wrong places for fine work, though she did not yet allow herself that conclusion in its finished form. She allowed herself the observation. Broad knuckles. Thickened skin where weight had hung from the fingers and where rope and crate edges and the handles of heavy things had pressed over years. Strength built by loads carried at arm's length and on the shoulder, not by tools held fine between finger and thumb for hours at a bench.

He gave his name readily and his trade reluctantly, and the reluctance had a shape she had met before in men who had been paid to say less than they knew. He was a wharf labourer, he said. He had carried consignments. When she asked what consignments, he spoke of the alloy, and when he spoke of the alloy he began to describe the carrying work in terms that belonged elsewhere. He spoke of dressing a surface as a man speaks who has stood at a foundry floor and watched a maker judge heat by colour. He spoke of fine tolerances and of the moment when a piece must be turned or it would take a fault through its whole length. He spoke well. His speech was educated beyond what his stated trade would suggest, his sentences complete, his vocabulary chosen as if he had been taught by people who valued precision and had then gone out to earn his living with his shoulders. The error, when she listened for it as an error and not as a mystery, was consistent and specific. He described carrying as if carrying were a kind of making. He used the maker's words for the labourer's tasks, and he used them fluently, and he did not notice that the fluency itself was the seam in the account.

Mira asked about loads and routes, the plain questions any officer might ask a labourer about his work, and there his answers changed in texture. He knew loads. He knew which sheds took the heavy consignments and which quay crane complained under a particular weight and which foreman would accept a load after the light began to fail and which would not. He knew the route from the bonded sheds to the foundry stores by the canal path and the longer route by the cart road when the canal path flooded, and he knew how a consignment was signed over at each stage and whose initials meant the weight had been checked and whose meant it had only been witnessed. No maker would need half of that knowledge. A maker would need the other half of his account, the half he delivered like a lesson learned by heart. She kept both halves separate in her notebook and she wrote nothing in the margin yet.

She could not read his body. The scarring lay in the infirmary light, open to any eye, and weight told her nothing from it at all, because weight does not read a body and does not read the dead and does not gather itself in living skin for her to lift away like a cloth from a table. Whatever the bands meant, they would have to mean it through paper and through observation and through the testimony of objects that had held still around him while he suffered. That limit bit in this case with a particular cruelty, because the one surface that carried the answer visibly was the one surface her practice refused entirely. She looked at his hands as any investigator might look, with attention and without gift, and she found the looking slower and more honest for the absence of any shortcut.

The infirmary gave her the record only after argument, and the argument itself became part of the ground she had to map. The record of Vaun Mell covered eleven days of treatment. It lived as a patient file in the ledger cabinet in the record room and as entries in the bound ledger kept beside the cabinet, and the two forms were supposed to agree in every particular that mattered. The file held the admission note, the treatment notes for each day, the work notes written by whoever had dressed his hand or settled his bedding or watched him lift and carry within the ward as his strength returned, and the standing note that his pain was treated without a named cause because no cause the infirmary recognised could be written honestly in the place provided for causes. Two entries in the record had been written in a different hand from the rest. That fact was visible to a careful eye before it was admitted by any voice: the slope changed, the pressure changed, the way a particular letter closed changed, and then the familiar hand resumed as if nothing had interrupted it.

Mira read the record at a table in the record room with the cabinet at her back and the bound ledger open beside the file, and she copied the substance of it into her notebook in the form in which the infirmary itself kept it, because a table made by the Office later would be an Office artefact, while the infirmary's own arrangement, with its two intrusions standing in their places, was the findable thing itself.

She read the table twice and the entries around it three times. The second hand appeared where the record touched his recovery of strength and where it touched his handling of his own possessions, and she marked that placement without yet deciding whether placement was intention or accident. A hand that wished to alter a record might choose any line. A hand that wished to learn from a record might also leave its trace where it had lingered, if the trace was not writing at all in the author's intention but copying, or annotation, or an infirmary locum whose hand simply differed from the regular clerk's and whose presence had gone otherwise unrecorded. Fairness required her to hold that possibility open alongside the darker ones, and she held it open in the notebook with the same care she gave the suspicion, because a case built by discarding the innocent explanation early becomes a case that can only conclude what it assumed.

The three live possibilities stood in her notebook in the order in which the evidence had raised them, stated fairly, each given its full strength before any of them was tested. The first possibility was that the man was a maker, one of the people who applied the technique that left this injury and carried its mark as Roche had carried it, hiding in a labourer's bed under a labourer's name. His evasiveness supported it. His educated speech supported it. His wrongness about his own trade supported it most of all, if the wrongness were read as a cover story badly kept by a man whose real work was fine work and whose hands had forgotten how to lie about it. The second possibility was that the man was a labourer who had handled the alloy and been marked by handling it, that carrying itself, repeated over consignments and seasons, could write the same bands on a carrier that making wrote on a maker, and that his strange fluency with makers' language came from years spent listening at foundry doors while he waited for loads to be released into his keeping. The third possibility was that the record had been altered already, and what the file said about him had been written to be found, that the second hand was not an accident of staffing but an author, and that Mell himself might be exactly what he appeared to be while the paper around him had been arranged by somebody who understood what an Office officer would look for when she came.

Mira had stood once before in a room arranged to be read, in [the locked room where a clerk found a dead man and the room said nothing](/2026/11/01/nightjar-clean-room-001/), and the lesson of that room stayed with her in the record room: that an absence where weight should be, or a presence where paper should be plain, could be the signature of a hand that had prepared the scene for a reader. She did not let the memory decide the present case. She let it sharpen the questions she asked of the file.

She asked the infirmary for the bed before she asked it for anything else that required permission, because a bed could not refuse her and because its anchors were honest. The frame, the rail, the locker beside it. She set her hand to the rail where his hand had rested and she let the place give her what it had held, in the slow way that metal holds and stone holds and wood holds less faithfully, and the ward around her went on with its work because she had learned to read without ceremony and without drawing the eyes of patients who had their own suffering to attend to. Eleven days of pain came into her in a long, even pressure that had no spike of terror anywhere in it. Pain at the dressing change. Pain in the night when the draught thinned. Pain when the hand was asked to close and pain when it was asked to open and pain in the long afternoons when nothing was asked of it at all and it ached with the steady insistence of an injury that had settled into the architecture of the hand itself. She took on a portion of it as she always did, the affect arriving as cold along her forearms and a narrowing of her attention to the rail under her fingers, and underneath the pain, where fear should have lived in some measure for any man lying strange and hurt among strangers, there was nothing. No fear at all. A man in that much pain, for that long, in a strange bed, who was never afraid. She read again at the frame and again at the locker, because a finding that strange required her to doubt her own instrument before she doubted the man, and each anchor gave her the same account with the same hollow at its centre. Pain in abundance. Fear absent as if it had been taken out of the stay the way a stone is taken out of a shoe. She withdrew her hand and stood with the cold moving up toward her elbows and the borrowed ache settling into her own knuckles, and she wrote the finding in words she could defend: the bed read gave eleven days of pain and no fear, and the absence was the carried question of the case, the thing she would hold open through every later conclusion because a conclusion that explained the hands and the paper and still left the fear unexplained would be a conclusion with a hole in it shaped exactly like the truth.

The infirmary keeper kept the night ward as a keeper keeps a gate, not by standing in it the whole time but by knowing every sound it made when something entered that did not belong there. From the keeper's side of the record room door, the Office officer at the table was not a rescuer and not a colleague and not even, in the first assessment, a person acting in bad faith. She was a weather event arriving in a place built to shelter people from weather. The keeper had admitted labourers who arrived with no names they wished to give and clerks who arrived with names that belonged to other people and a foundry hand once who had given the name of a saint and answered to it for a fortnight without any of the staff supposing that heaven required the correction. The infirmary treated Accord and non Accord hands alike, because a crushed finger on the wharf did not ask about allegiance before it bled into a basin, and because the wharf would stop bringing its injured to a house that sorted them before it washed them. Its records were medical. They were not Office paper. They held what a body had needed and what had been done to meet the need, and they held it in confidence not as a courtesy but as the condition on which the next injured man would allow himself to be carried through the door at all. The keeper had watched officers before this one discover that argument and treat it as an obstacle, a local sentiment to be managed with forms. What the keeper wished this officer to understand, standing in the doorway with a night tray cooling in one hand and the ward breathing behind them both, was the price stated plainly. An Office that can walk a patient's file out of a night infirmary can never be trusted with the wharf's injured again. The next man with bands on his hand, or with worse, would hide the injury in his coat and work until the hand failed under a load and killed him or the man beside him, rather than lie eleven nights in a bed whose paper might be carried away by a woman with cold hands and a notebook. The keeper was right about one thing and the rightness stood even when Mira, on her side of the door, did not answer it. The record was medical. Taking it the way she already intended to take it would cost the wharf's trust, and the cost would be paid by people who had never heard her name. The keeper set the tray down inside the ward and closed the record room door gently, as a person closes a door in a house where somebody is sleeping in pain, and left the officer alone with the file and with the knowledge that no form the Office issued would refund what the taking spent.

Mira sat a while after the keeper had gone with the door's closing still present in the room. She did not argue the point in her notebook. She recorded it in the form in which it had been made, fairly, because ground truth that omits the strongest argument against the investigator's own course is not ground but advocacy wearing ground's coat. Then she turned to the ledger cabinet, because the cabinet was the second anchor the case had given her and because its metal would hold what paper held worst.

The cabinet stood against the record room wall in the order Orry had described: upper drawers for current files, bound ledger beside it, handles polished by years of pulling. She read it at its anchors, the cabinet body, the drawer that had held Mell's file, the place in the drawer where the file rested among its neighbours. Paper holds weight worst of all common materials, fading quickly, losing the shape of an event the way a footprint loses its edges in wet sand, but metal holds best, and the cabinet gave her what the file itself could not have kept. Somebody else had read the same file, and the reading had happened within the last week by the strength of what remained, and it had been no hurried riffling by a clerk searching for a misplaced page. It was sustained, unhurried attention. A reader who knew what they were looking for had stood where she was standing and had taken the file through its whole length with the patience of a person comparing it against knowledge they already carried. The read gave her the attention and its quality and its duration as a felt shape, the way a room gives back the fact of a long meeting in the warmth of its chairs. It did not give her who. Weight never gives identity. She could no more name the reader from the cabinet than she could name Mell's maker from his scarred skin, and the symmetry of those two refusals felt like the case arranging its own lesson for her: that everything decisive in it would have to be earned by eyes and paper and patience, the slow instruments, while her gift stood by offering only the shape of events and the ache they left behind.

She closed the drawer with care. In the ward beyond the record room, Mell was sitting up to take his draught, and through the half open door she watched him lift the cup. She thought of [the foundry list, where four places made the alloy and one sold it](/2026/11/15/nightjar-foundry-list-015/) and of how a list of places had taught her that making and moving were different trades with different hands at their centres, however often the same consignment passed through both. She thought also of the way the second reader's attention had rested in the cabinet metal, unhurried, informed, and she set that attention beside the second hand in the file and did not yet join them with a line. Two traces might belong to one visitor or to two. Ground truth was the discipline of letting traces lie separate until something other than neatness joined them.

By the time she left the record room the ward had settled into its afternoon quiet. Mell slept at last, or practised sleeping with the skill of a man who has had eleven days to learn the ward's rhythms and the value of being unapproachable within them. His boots still stood square beneath the locker. His cup stood empty on the locker's top beside the folded cloth. The window beside his bed showed the quay and the grey water beyond the rail, and Mira, passing, did not look for a hand against the glass. She had the ground now in the only sense ground is ever had at the start of a case: the place mapped, the paper copied, the possibilities stated at their strongest, the opposition heard and recorded without rebuttal, the bed's strange hollow finding written down where she could not later pretend she had not noticed it, and the cabinet's patient stranger held in mind as a presence without a face. What she did not have was the man himself, in any sense that would survive being written in a report. For that she would have to watch him work.

She spent the rest of that first examination on the small geography of the ward itself, because ground truth in an infirmary is partly a matter of who can reach what without being seen to reach for it. The record room door stood within sight of the desk but not within sight of the beds, which meant a visitor to the file had to pass the desk in order to reach it, and the desk was staffed through the night by people whose whole employment depended on noticing passage. A reader who had spent unhurried time with the file within the last week had therefore either been admitted by the desk as somebody with a right to be there, or had come at an hour when the desk's attention was claimed by an emergency in the ward, or had worn a shape the desk was trained to let pass without a question: a nurse's coat, a clerk's errand, a porter's burden carried on a shoulder that hid the face. She asked the desk, in the neutral tone of a person completing a form, about visitors to the record room in the past week, and she received the neutral answer of a desk that had already decided how much of the infirmary's life belonged to the Office. Deliveries had come. A locum had come whose papers the senior nurse had approved. The bound ledger had been taken once to the senior nurse's room and brought back by a porter. Nothing in the answer was a lie that she could detect and nothing in it was the whole account either, and she wrote it down as a partial wall rather than a doorway, a place where inquiry would have to find another way around if it needed what lay behind it.

The bound ledger beside the cabinet rewarded the slower attention she gave it after the desk had finished being helpful. Its entries for Mell agreed with the file in admission and in treatment and in the daily state of the hand, and they disagreed with it in one small particular that she almost passed over because agreement had trained her eye to skim. In the ledger, the entry for the eighth day recorded the handling of his kit without the remark about pain rising that the file carried, and the entry for the fourth day recorded the walk to the window without noting that his hand had been left undressed under watch. The differences were not contradictions. They were the ordinary differences between a summary written at a desk from notes and the notes themselves, and she recorded them as such, because a case in which a second hand is known to have touched a file is a case in which every difference tempts the investigator to call it evidence, and the discipline of calling ordinary differences ordinary is what keeps the extraordinary ones visible when they come. Neither differing entry was in the second hand. Both were in the infirmary hand. She let them stand as the record's natural grain.

She asked, finally, about the draught and about the treatment itself, and what she learned belonged in the ground because it bounded what the pain in the bed could be made to mean. The infirmary treated the pain without naming a cause because its whole practice with practice injuries, and the wharf produced a small, steady census of them, was to treat what presented and to write honestly where the cause was unknown rather than borrow a name from a table that did not fit. Mell's pain responded to the draught the way ordinary pain responds, lessening and returning on a rhythm the nurses could predict, and it responded to rest and to warmth and to the position of the hand, and in all of that it behaved as pain behaves in a living hand that has been injured by work. Nothing in the treatment notes suggested that the staff had seen the injury do anything they considered unnatural. Nothing suggested they had been instructed to see less than they saw. The senior nurse, when Mira put the question as plainly as courtesy allowed, said that the hand was a hand and the man was a patient and the infirmary had treated the one and sheltered the other, and that if the Office wanted a metaphysical report it had come to a house that dealt in dressings. Mira accepted the rebuke in the spirit in which rebukes from competent people are best accepted, as a boundary stone marking where her inquiry's rights ended and another institution's began, and she wrote the senior nurse's sentence down in full because it said, better than her own paraphrase could, what the infirmary believed itself to be.

There remained the matter of Mell's own account of his admission, and she took it from him in pieces across the afternoon rather than in one sitting, because a man in pain gives a straighter account in pieces, when each answer costs him only the effort of that answer. He had collapsed beside a consignment shed, he said, not dramatically but in the manner of a man whose hand simply declined a load it had accepted a thousand times, and the crew had carried him to the infirmary on a board because he had asked them not to carry him by the arms. He remembered the admission clearly. He remembered the second day less clearly, through the draught. He remembered asking, on the third day, whether the hand would take a load again, and being told that hands like his usually did, and he had held on to that sentence, he said, the way a man holds a rail. When she asked whether he had been afraid at any point in the stay, he looked at her with an expression she could not immediately read and said that he had been in pain the whole time and that pain left little room for anything else, which was an answer a frightened man might give to avoid the word and also the exact answer a man might give for whom fear had genuinely never arrived. She wrote his words down without choosing between those readings. The bed had already given her its testimony on the question, and the man's words and the bed's testimony sat beside each other in the notebook like two witnesses who agree on the facts and cannot agree on what the facts are called.

By the end of the examination she also knew the infirmary's hours as a working system rather than as a backdrop: when the desk changed hands, when the night keeper came on, when the record room stood empty during the evening dressing round, when the gate book was closed and reopened and when the seal round passed the window. She knew which of those hours the unhurried reader might have used and she knew she could not narrow it further without the desk's fuller cooperation, which the keeper's argument had already told her would not be given freely and should not be taken lightly. Ground, in the end, is as much a map of what will not be yielded as a map of what has been. She closed the ledger. She thanked the desk. She went out into the ward where the light had changed and the evening work was beginning, carrying a notebook fuller than it had been at noon and a question about fear that no page in it could answer.

Before she left the ward that evening she walked the distance between the bed and the record room and the desk once more, slowly, counting nothing and timing nothing, learning it the way Orry learned a round, by the order in which things had to be passed. Bed to door, door to corridor, corridor past the desk with its book and its bell and its keeper of the hour, then the record room with the cabinet against the wall and the ledger beside it and the small window that looked not onto the quay but onto a yard where carts waited under covers. The geography mattered because every claim the case would later make about access and attention and the walking out of a file would stand or fall on this geography, and because she had learned in earlier cases that a room misremembered is a conclusion misbuilt. She noted where a person carrying a file would be visible and where they would not. She noted where a person watching the infirmary from outside could stand, at the rail, at the corner of the shed across the yard, in the shadow of the cart covers, and see the lit window and the bed beside it without being remarked by anyone whose work faced inward. The wharf at night was a place built to be watched by its own people for its own purposes, seals and gates and loads, and a watcher who borrowed those purposes as a coat could stand in it for a long time. She carried that thought out of the ward with her, unformed, and it waited at the edge of the case like a figure at the edge of a yard, patient, content to be noticed later.

She also set down, before the day closed, the exact state of her own body as the record of the case required it to be set down, because the bed read had cost her and the cost would follow her into whatever came next. The cold from the read had settled into her hands and forearms and showed no sign of leaving before the night. The borrowed pain from Valtessa had woken under the new pain the bed had given her, the two of them braiding together until she could not always say which ache belonged to which history, and her sleep that night would be the thin, broken sleep of a woman whose body is keeping accounts for other people. None of that excused anything and none of it explained anything. It was simply the condition in which the rest of the case would have to be conducted, by her, on the authority she had taken, at the price Leyden had already named. She wrote the inventory in the notebook's private pages and turned back to the evidence with her hands cold around the pencil.

## The Collapse

The collapse did not arrive as a revelation. It arrived as a water jug, lifted at the wrong moment for the theory Mira had carried into the ward, and set down again without ceremony by a man who did not know he had just damaged the strongest account of himself that the evidence had yet produced.

She had arranged to be present when the afternoon work of the ward required Mell to move, because watching a man use his hands when he believes the use is ordinary tells more than any examination in which he arranges them for inspection. Orry was present too, on a clerking errand that had brought her legitimately through the infirmary with messages for the desk, and Orry's presence mattered because Orry knew wharf hands the way a seal clerk knows seals, by long exposure to the genuine article in all its varieties. The senior nurse set the jug on the locker as she set it every afternoon, full, with the cup beside it, and Mell, who had been sitting by the window with his file's eleventh day still undecided above him like weather, rose to pour for himself because he had made a point throughout his stay of doing for himself whatever the staff would allow.

He lifted the jug with both hands and the lift was wrong for a maker in every particular that could be observed. A man whose strength lives in fine work lifts a heavy jug by planning it: he sets his grip, he tests the balance, he protects the fingers that earn his living by letting the palms and the forearms take the strain in a distribution learned at a bench. Mell lifted the way a carrier lifts. The load went into the whole hand and up the arm in one unconsidered movement. His fingers closed not with precision but with the blunt confidence of a man for whom grip means the power to hold a weight against gravity for a distance, not the power to persuade a delicate thing into shape. The jug rose, tilted, poured clean, and settled back on the locker without a tremor that mattered, and the strength that performed all of that was strength in the wrong places for fine work: in the heel of the hand, in the broad muscles that close the fist around a handle meant to be carried, in the shoulders that had already begun, even in the act of pouring water in a quiet ward, to set themselves for a load that was not there.

Orry saw it at the same moment. Mira knew she saw it because Orry, who had been sorting her messages into the order of her round with the automatic neatness that made senior clerks trust her with sealed envelopes, stopped sorting. Two pairs of eyes met over a labourer's ordinary competence and understood each other without a word passing that the ward could overhear.

Mell gripped the rail next, at the turn by the window where the ward floor had been worn smooth, and he held his weight briefly on the injured hand as the record said he had done on the ninth day, and again the grip told its plain story. It was a carrying grip, heel down, fingers wrapped, the whole architecture of the hand devoted to not letting go of a fixed thing while the body moved around it. When the nurse afterwards gave him his own kit to handle, at Mira's quiet request framed as a discharge preparation, the story completed itself in the small things. He lifted his coat by its shoulders and shook it out with a carrier's economy. He folded his spare shirt with large, competent folds that owed nothing to fine work. He handled his belt, his small knife in its sheath, his wrapped bundle of personal paper, and each object was managed as freight in miniature, secured, balanced, stowed. Where a maker's hands would have lingered, testing edges, turning a thing to the light for the pleasure and habit of assessment, Mell's hands finished with each object and let it go. His hands were instruments for moving the world from one place to another. They had never been instruments for changing its shape.

Mira set the observation beside [the photograph of a palm pinned to a board beside three others that looked nothing like it](/2026/11/18/nightjar-marked-hands-018/) in her memory and she felt the maker reading, which had stood so plausibly at the head of her three possibilities, begin to lose the support that had made it reasonable. It had been a reasonable reading. She insisted on that in her notebook even as she dismantled it, because a false lead that was never reasonable teaches nothing and protects nobody from the next one. He was evasive, and makers with an injury the Office could not name would be evasive. He was educated in manner and speech beyond his stated trade, and a maker drawn from a foundry's skilled ranks might well be educated. He was wrong about his own trade, describing carrying in the language of making, and a maker hiding his making behind a labourer's story might be expected to slip exactly there, letting the true vocabulary bleed through the false one. Every plank of it had borne weight. What the jug and the rail and the kit showed was that the planks had been laid over a different structure entirely. His wrongness about his trade was not the wrongness of a maker hiding making. It was the wrongness of a man repeating words he had been given by the people who paid him.

That distinction, once seen, reorganised his whole account without requiring her to call him a liar in any simple sense. A carrier who waits at foundry doors hears makers talk. A carrier who is paid well for consignments that require discretion learns to answer questions about the work in the language of the people who hand the work over, because that language is the password that keeps the work coming. If the people who paid Mell had fed him a description of his own employment dressed in makers' terms, he would repeat it as he repeated it to her: fluently, consistently, specifically, and with the small deadness of recitation that she had mistaken, on first hearing, for the guardedness of concealment. He was not describing what he did. He was describing what he had been told the work was, by men who either did not know the difference between carrying and making or who found it convenient that their carrier should not know it either. His evasiveness then took its proper size. It was the evasiveness of a labourer who suspects his employers' business will not bear inspection and who has decided, sensibly, that an Office officer is not the person before whom to improvise a better story. His educated speech took its proper size too. Men are educated in more places than their trades, and a wharf that serves foundries and bonded warehouses and Accord offices collects educated men in labouring coats the way a harbour collects interesting wreckage, from every ship that ever broke on the coast of an economy.

The paper confirmed what the hands proposed, and the order of that confirmation mattered to Mira, because she had forbidden herself the one confirmation that would have been easiest to trust and easiest to misuse. Weight could not read his body. She wrote that sentence in the notebook in its own line, not as a complaint but as a boundary marker for whoever read the case after her. The collapse of the maker reading rested on observation and on paper, on eyes and on the infirmary's own work notes, and on nothing that required her gift to vouch for it. Any officer without her practice, given the same ward and the same file and the same afternoon, could have reached it. That made it stronger, not weaker, and she took a grim satisfaction in building the decisive turn of the case out of materials that oversight could inspect without having to believe in her at all.

The infirmary's work notes, which she now reread with the jug still standing in her mind's eye, had been saying the same thing in their plain clinical language since the middle of the stay. On the fifth day he had worked his fingers against a rolled cloth and the pain had eased when the load was spread across the palm: a carrier's relief, the relief of a hand returning to the distribution of weight it was built for. On the sixth day the note recorded the jug itself, lifted to the locker and set down without spilling, and the nurse who wrote it had underlined nothing, because to a nurse who watched wharf labourers recover every week there was nothing to underline in a carrier carrying. On the ninth day the rail grip was recorded as strong, with the observer's added remark that the hand was built for loads rather than for fine holding, a sentence Mira had read on her first pass as description and now read, on her second, as the infirmary quietly solving the case in its own vocabulary and filing the solution where anyone could find it. Even the entries in the second hand, when she returned to them with the maker reading dying, changed their aspect without changing their letters. They touched his recovery of strength and his handling of his possessions. A second reader interested in whether the patient was a maker would linger exactly there, at the places where the record described what his hands could do. So would a second writer wishing a later reader to notice those places. The traces in the cabinet and the traces in the file still did not join into a single visitor by any logic she would sign, but they now pointed in a common direction, toward an interest in the capacities of Mell's hands that somebody had brought to the infirmary before her and that she had now, independently, satisfied in the opposite sense from the one the maker reading required.

His account of loads and routes, taken down in her notebook during their first conversation, supplied the third leg. She went through it line by line at the record room table while the ward settled and Orry waited with the patience of a woman who knows that paper checked slowly is paper that will not need checking again at a worse hour. He had named sheds and cranes and foremen and signing practices with the unhesitating density of a man reciting his own working life. No maker would need to know which foreman accepted a late load, because a maker's work ended at the foundry door. No maker would need the flooded season alternative route by the cart road, because makers did not walk consignments through floods. No maker would carry in his head the initials of clerks at three separate signing stages along a route, because signing stages are the geography of custody, and custody was the whole of Mell's trade. He had carried the alloy, physically, as paid labour, consignment after consignment, and the work had marked his hands. He has carried, not made. She wrote the sentence in the notebook and then, after a pause in which she tested it against every observation she had honestly made, she underlined it once.

What remained standing after the collapse was more dangerous than what had fallen, and she did not allow the relief of a solved identification to disguise that from her. If a living person carried the injury and he was a carrier rather than a maker, then there was a maker. Somewhere behind the consignments Mell had walked from shed to store there stood a person, or a practice, that applied the technique at its source and sent the marked product out into the world in the hands of men who were paid to move it and to repeat a story about it that was not theirs. The carrier bore the injury the way a road bears the mark of a heavy cart: because weight passed over him repeatedly, in the same places, until the passage wrote itself into his surface. That raised questions the installment could hold without answering. How many carriers bore the same bands and had never been brought to an infirmary where an officer might see them. Whether the marking came from the alloy itself in transit or from the anchors and preparations that carrying the alloy required. Whether Mell knew he was marked in a pattern that matched a dead man's inquiry, and whether his employers knew, and whether the unhurried reader of his file had come to the infirmary precisely because that reader did know. Each question opened cleanly from the collapse and none of them could be closed with the materials in the record room, and she recorded them as open, in the discipline the case had taught her from its first hour, rather than letting the fall of the false lead rush her into a new construction built at the same speed as the old one.

The absence still waited at the centre of it all, untouched by the collapse, and she returned to it before she closed the notebook because a turn in a case that explains the hands and forgets the fear is a turn taken in the wrong direction. The bed had given her eleven days of pain and no fear. A maker hiding in a labourer's bed might be expected to fear discovery with a steadiness that would soak into a bed frame over eleven nights. A carrier who suspected his employers might fear their displeasure, or the loss of the work, or the officer at his bedside. Mell, by the testimony of iron and wood and locker, had feared none of it, at no point, in a stay long enough for fear to visit even the practised and the proud. Either the man possessed a composure that his evasiveness in conversation flatly contradicted, or fear had never been available to him in the register in which the bed could hold it, or something about the injury itself, or about what had been done to him in the making of the consignments he carried, had taken fear out of the account the way the second hand had written itself into the file: quietly, specifically, and in a place where only somebody looking for the shape of a whole stay would notice the gap. She did not choose among those. She carried the question forward exactly as she had found it, because it was the one question the installment had promised itself it would not pretend to settle cheaply.

Orry, when Mira told her the conclusion in the corridor in the low voice that ward corridors require, received it the way she received everything, by testing it against the wharf she knew. Carriers with educated speech, she said, were ten to a shed on the bonded side, because the bonded side paid for men who could read a consignment note and keep their mouths shut in two registers at once. She had seen Mell's sort lift all her working life. What she had not seen, she said, was one of them lying eleven days in the night infirmary with a photograph's injury on his hand and no fear in his bed, and she looked toward the ward door as she said it with the expression of a clerk who has found an entry that will not reconcile and knows the fault is not in her arithmetic.

Mira closed the file. The record itself was now the artifact in a sense it had not been that morning, because if Mell was a carrier and a maker stood behind him unnamed, then the paper that documented his marking, with its second hand and its unhurried second reader, was the one object in the case that connected the man to whoever had arranged his work, and it was also the one object that could be used to erase him: altered again, withdrawn, lost in a cabinet fire or a clerk's error, until the living example became a rumour Orry had once reported about a window. Whoever wrote those two entries could reach the man through the file. Whoever read it last week already had. The Office could not protect a man it could not follow and could not persuade to stay, but it could secure the record before the record was made to disappear, and securing it would require taking it out of a civilian infirmary in a way the keeper had already priced for her in the doorway of the record room. She stood with her notebook against her chest and the cold from the bed read still working slowly out of her forearms, and she began to consider, with the reluctance of an officer who has already been invoiced once that day for acting alone, exactly how the file was to walk out of the infirmary, and whose hands would have to carry it.

She tested the collapse once more before she allowed it to govern the case, and she tested it in the least theatrical way available to her, which was to sit with Mell while he ate his evening meal and watch what a man does with bread and a spoon and a cup when he believes the meal is the only business of the hour. Fine work shows itself at table in a hundred small economies. A maker will break bread with a precision that has nothing to do with manners, will steady a cup with two fingers placed exactly where the heat is least, will handle a spoon as a tool among tools, with the grip adjusted to the task in the moment the task presents itself. Mell ate as a carrier eats. He secured things. He set the cup where it could not be knocked from, he tore the bread in pieces sized for carrying to the mouth without loss, he rested his forearms on the table edge the way men rest them who are used to taking the weight of their own bodies on their arms while they wait for a load to be made ready. When the injured hand tired he did not favour it with a craftsman's protective cunning. He simply changed the distribution of the work to the other hand, wholesale, the way a crew changes the end of a rope. Nothing in the meal contradicted the jug. Nothing in the meal contradicted the rail or the kit or the record. A theory that survives being tested against a man's dinner has earned the right to be acted on, and she let the maker reading go with the respect due to an opponent that had fought fairly and lost on evidence rather than on her preference.

What his hands could not do was as instructive as what they could, and the nurse, unprompted, supplied the demonstration while changing the light dressing before the evening round. She asked him to hold a small folded pad in place against the back of his own hand with the fingers of that hand while she secured the bandage, a simple task the ward asked of any patient whose other hand was occupied, and his fingers, strong enough to close around a rail and hold his whole weight, fumbled the pad twice before the nurse took pity on the exercise and held it herself. The failure was not weakness. It was the absence of a whole register of control, the fine holding that makers practise until it becomes indistinguishable from rest. She watched his face while it happened and saw annoyance there, and embarrassment, but she did not see the particular shame of a skilled man betrayed by his own instrument, which she had seen once in a foundry in another case and had never forgotten. He was annoyed as a labourer is annoyed when a task from another trade is set in front of him and his body declines to pretend it knows the steps. The nurse recorded the dressing change in the file in the infirmary hand, in the ordinary words she used for every patient, and Mira, reading over the entry later, found herself grateful for a record that had never once tried to make the man more interesting than his treatment required.

There was also the testimony of his boots, which she had noticed on the first morning and which she now understood she had been reading, without crediting herself, for the whole of the case. They stood square beneath the locker, worn at the heel and at the outer edge in the pattern of a man who walks loaded, the leather darkened where canal water had soaked it season after season, the laces replaced with cord of two different kinds where the original laces had failed at different times and been mended from whatever the quay offered. A maker who worked at a bench wore out boots differently, at the toe, standing. A man who walked consignments wore them out exactly as Mell had worn his, in the long geography of the wharf written into leather. She did not put the boots in the notebook as evidence, because an officer's report that rests on boots invites the reader to laugh in the wrong place, but she put them in her private accounting of why she believed what she now believed, and the private accounting of an investigator is where conclusions are actually kept, whatever the report afterwards claims.

With the maker reading gone, the meaning of his educated speech settled into its final shape, and she went back to him one last time that day to hear it with new ears. She asked him who had taught him the foundry words he used for his work, and he answered without hesitation, as a man answers a question that has never before seemed to him to carry any weight. The consignments came with a talk, he said. The man who engaged the carriers at the shed door gave them the talk before the first load of any new consignment, what the stuff was, how it was to be handled, what it was for, in words Mell now recognised, hearing his own account returned to him, as words meant for other ears. They were told the work was fine work and they were to treat the loads as fine work required, gently, in balance, never dropped, never dragged. He had repeated the talk because the talk was part of the wage, the password at the shed door, and because some part of him had liked sounding like a man whose work had a finer name than carrying. She asked who gave the talk and he gave her a description rather than a name, a man known on the quay by a coat and a habit of engaging carriers away from the foreman's board, and the description matched no officer and no maker she could name and she wrote it down as a shape rather than a person, because shapes are what this case dealt in: the shape of a hand, the shape of an attention in a cabinet, the shape of a fear that was not there. He has carried, not made, and somebody had stood at a shed door and taught him to say otherwise, and that somebody had known the makers' language well enough to lend it. The collapse, completed, left her looking not at the man in the bed but past him, along the route his loads had walked, toward a door she could not yet see and a figure she must not yet name.

She told Orry the shape of what came next without yet naming the method, because the method would ask something of Orry that Mira had no right to request in a corridor with the ward settling around them. She said only that the record could not safely stay where a second hand and a second reader had already reached it, and that leaving it in place while the Office deliberated would be a decision too, with its own cost, paid by Mell if the paper vanished and by the infirmary if it did not. Orry listened with her messages held against her chest in the order of her round and said that the file was heavier than it looked, meaning the paper and meaning the other thing as well, and Mira, who had carried registers and reports and one private notebook she would never carry again, agreed that it was. The two of them stood a moment in the corridor while the evening round began around them, a nurse passing with a tray, a patient calling once and being answered, the desk bell sounding for a delivery at the outer door. The case had its ground and it had its collapse. What it needed now was a decision, and decisions, Mira had learned, are the only part of an inquiry that cannot be taken back and rewritten in a fairer hand.

## Pressure

Mira made the decision standing in the record room doorway where Orry could see her face and where the infirmary corridor behind them stayed empty long enough for the words to be heard once and not repeated. She did not requisition the file. A requisition would have gone on Office paper through the wharf office before the night was out, it would have carried her name and the patient name together in a clerk hand that half the wharf could read by morning, and it would have told whoever had read the file the week before, in that sustained and unhurried way the cabinet had held, that the Office had come for it at last and that they had been right to read it when they did. It would have told whoever had written two entries in a second hand that their writing had been noticed and weighed and found worth seizing, and a person who knows their work has been noticed does not wait beside it to be questioned, they finish it or they burn it or they move the man it describes while there is still dark to move him in. Mira had carried enough cases that announced themselves to know the shape of that loss, and she had carried the month of pain long enough to know she could not afford a chase through the wharf if the announcement sent Mell running before she understood what he was. So she asked Orry to walk the file out instead, in the ordinary course of the seal round, folded into the errands a bonded clerk already carried between the infirmary and the gate and the wharf office without anyone counting the papers in her hands, and she watched Orry understand what was being asked before she agreed to it, which mattered more to Mira than the agreement itself. Orry was not Office. She was a junior clerk of the bonded chain who did seal checks in the night and who had done the one brave and correct thing of reporting a hand at a window because a photograph had told her to, and nothing in her duties had ever required her to carry a medical file past people who might want it badly enough to watch an infirmary for it. Mira said that plainly. She said the safer course existed and that she was not taking it and that the reason was the safety of the record and of the man in the bed, not the safety of the clerk who would carry it, and she said Orry could refuse and that a refusal would be entered nowhere and held against her by no one Mira could reach. Orry listened with her seal bag already on her shoulder and her coat buttoned against the wharf damp, and she asked one question only, whether she was to run if someone stopped her, and Mira told her no, she was to hold the file and call out and let Mira come to her, because a clerk running through the wharf at night with a file under her coat would draw every eye the walk was meant to avoid and because Mira would be near enough that calling out would be enough. That was the promise the whole beat would test, near enough, made by a woman whose body had been lying to her about distance and speed for a month, whose left side woke the borrowed pain whenever she turned too fast and whose sleep had been broken in pieces since Valtessa until tiredness sat behind her eyes as a constant narrowing, and Mira knew the promise was thinner than she made it sound and she made it anyway because the alternative was the requisition and the announcement and the loss. Orry took the file from the drawer herself so that no other hands touched it in the taking, she slipped it into a plain folder among the seal returns she was already carrying to the gate office, she set the folder neither on top nor at the bottom of the bundle but in the middle where a carried thing settles when it is not being hidden, and she left the record room by the corridor door and not by the yard door, walking at the pace of a woman with two more seals to check before the gate and no reason in the world to hurry. Mira let a distance open that she had chosen in advance and hated from the first step, far enough that a watcher would see a clerk alone with her round, near enough that a shout would reach her and that she could close the ground in seconds if closing was needed, and she moved along the wharf in the shadow side of the warehouses where the lamps thinned and the canal smell came up strong off the water and the stone under her boots stayed slick from a damp that never left Carrowmere even in dry weather. The wharf at night had its own honest noises and she sorted them as she walked, the clink of a chain settling somewhere beyond the bonded sheds, the soft knock of a moored hull against its fenders, a night crew calling counts in a warehouse with its doors half open and lamplight spilling in a bar across the wet ground, and none of it covered the sound she was listening for because she did not know what sound it would be, only that the cabinet read had told her someone had already cared about this file with patience and patience rarely reads a thing once and then loses interest in where it goes. Orry checked a seal at the second warehouse without breaking stride beyond the pause the check required, she wrote the mark in her book by lamplight with the folder tucked under her arm in plain sight, and Mira felt the borrowed pain wake along her ribs at the small twist of her own body as she shifted to keep the angle, a pain that was not hers and that had learned her movements the way a tenant learns a house, settling when she settled and flaring when she reached or turned, so that she moved now the way she had watched Mell move in the infirmary, economising without thinking about it, and the likeness steadied her attention rather than shaming it because it told her exactly how much she had left and she spent it carefully. At the third warehouse the yard narrowed where crates were stacked for a morning loading, seal marked and tarpaulined, and the passage between the stack and the warehouse wall took Orry out of the direct lamplight for a stretch of perhaps twenty paces, and Mira closed her chosen distance by a third without deciding to, her boots quiet on the wet stone, her breathing kept even by effort, and she saw Orry come out the far side still alone and still unhurried and she let the distance open again and told herself the narrowing had been caution and not fear and was not entirely persuaded and walked on. The gate lay ahead past the open quay where the canal widened toward the lock, where a seal cart stood chained near the bollards and the gate chain hung in its heavy links between the posts and the keeper lodge showed one lit window with the book open on the desk inside as it always was, and the quay lamps made pools on the stone that did not join, so that a walker crossed light and dark by turns, and Orry crossed them steadily with the folder in her bundle and her seal bag knocking lightly at her hip, and Mira, two pools of light behind her and off to the water side where the moored hulls threw the deepest shadow, saw the man step out from the lee of the seal cart where he had been standing long enough that she should have marked him on her first scan of the quay and had not, and the lateness of seeing him was the first half second the night took from her. He did not run at Orry and he did not call out, he walked to meet her on an angle that would look to any distant eye like two night workers passing on the quay, and he spoke to her in a voice too low to carry to Mira, and Orry stopped, which she had been told to do, and she turned half toward him with the bundle held against her body in both arms as a clerk holds papers she does not want dropped in the wet, and the man put one hand on the bundle, not striking, not snatching yet, testing whether the papers would come away with a firm pull and whether the girl would let go out of surprise, and Orry said something Mira could not hear and did not let go, and the man pulled harder and Orry staggered one step toward the water side and kept her hold and opened her mouth to call out as she had been told, and Mira was already moving and the second half second went there, in the first stride, when the borrowed pain caught at her side and her left leg arrived under her a fraction later than her will had sent it, so that her closing run across the wet stone began from behind her own intention, depletion doing exactly what depletion does, narrowing the margin she had promised Orry she held in reserve. She did not waste the stride on anger at her body and she did not shorten her line to the nearer edge of the crates because the man was not nearer the crates, he was on the open quay with his back half to the water and his hand still on the bundle and his attention all on the clerk who would not let go, and Mira crossed the last pool of light at the speed she actually had rather than the speed she remembered having in Valtessa before the coat and the pain, and she reached them as the man shifted his grip to take the folder itself rather than the bundle, which would have torn the seal returns loose and scattered the night work across the quay, and she put her hand not on the man but on the seal cart at her side and shoved it hard on its iron wheels across the few feet of stone between the cart and the bollards so that it rolled clattering into the space behind his knees and stopped against the bollard with a ring of iron that made him flinch and step forward away from it, into her reach instead of away from it, and as he stepped she took his pulling arm at the wrist and the elbow and turned it down and across his own body using his forward weight and the slickness of the stone under his boots, and she bore him sideways into the stacked crates at the quay edge where the tarpaulin gave and the crate frames took his shoulder and hip with a hollow knock and held him pinned in the angle between the stack and a bollard while she kept the arm folded and her own weight set through her boots and her knee braced against the lowest crate, saying nothing at first because breath was a currency she was short of and then saying, low and clear, that he would let go of the papers now. He let go of the papers. Orry had not fallen and had not let go through any of it, her bundle was crushed against her coat and one seal return had bent double at the corner but the folder in the middle of it stayed shut and in her arms, and Mira saw the girl face clearly in the quay light and saw that the man saw it too, her face and her clerk coat and the seal bag that named her trade to anyone who knew the wharf, and that seeing went into Mira like a second and colder read because it could not be taken back by anything that happened next on that quay. The man said nothing that explained anything, he gave no name when Mira demanded the errand rather than the name, knowing even as she asked that a man sent to watch an infirmary and lift a file on a dark quay does not carry his sender in his mouth, and when Orry shifted her grip to free one hand for the gate chain that hung beside the bollard, meaning to drag it across as a barrier or a noise maker as the wharf clerks were taught to do when a load was threatened, the small movement opened the angle at which Mira held the stranger against the crates, and he used it with the quickness of a man who had kept that much in reserve, twisting down and out under her folded hold so that the tarpaulin tore loose above him and a crate lid clattered to the stone, and he was free and running before her second grip could set, running not toward the gate where the keeper was already coming out of the lodge with a lamp raised but along the quay toward the warehouse narrows where the lamps thinned, and Mira started after him and got three strides and read her own body honestly in those three strides, the pain standing up in her side like a hand pressed hard between the ribs, her breath coming short, the gap not closing, and she stopped, because the file was behind her in Orry arms and the file was the case and a chase she could not finish would leave the clerk alone on a quay where one watcher had already shown himself and a second might be standing where the first had stood, and she let him go into the narrows and heard his boots fade among the warehouse sounds and did not learn his face beyond a passing impression in lamplight that would fit a hundred wharf men and named no one. The keeper reached them with the lamp and questions that had no answers she could give him, and Mira gave him the shape that satisfied the gate book without entering anything false in it, a dispute over carried papers, no injury, no loss, the clerk unharmed and her bundle intact, and she watched him write it in his book in his slow hand while Orry stood beside her breathing hard and holding the folder as if her arms had forgotten how to do anything else, and when the book was shut Mira walked the last stretch to the gate at Orry pace and not her own, through the gate and out along the street beyond the wharf wall where the lamps were town lamps and the stone was drier, and only there, with the gate chain settled behind them and the keeper lamp gone back into the lodge, did she take the folder from Orry at last and feel its ordinary weight in her hands and understand that the walk was done and that it had cost exactly what she had known it might cost when she chose it in the record room doorway, and that knowing it beforehand did not make the cost anyone else to pay but hers.

## The Silence

Afterwards there was a stillness that Mira did not break at once, in a small room off the wharf office where the file lay on a table between her and the wall and Orry had gone to wash her hands and change out of the coat that had been seen, and in that stillness she laid out what the reader of the case already held, piece by piece, the way a weigher lays out weights she does not yet mean to use. She thought of the hands first, because the hands were where the case had turned. Mell hands were strong in the wrong places for fine work. The strength sat in the grip that lifts and carries, in the heel of the hand and the broad drive of the fingers closing on a rope or a crate edge or the rail of an infirmary bed, built by loads taken again and again until the body kept the shape of the labour, and the callus lay where weight passes through a carrying hand and not where a tool is held fine and turned and corrected a hundred times in an hour. A maker hands, the hands of the people who applied the technique and bore its injury as Roche had borne it, would have told a different story in their strength, fine strength, held strength, and these hands told the carrying story every time he lifted the water jug or gripped the rail or handled his own kit on the bed, without variation and without acting, because a man can rehearse words and cannot rehearse the way his hands close on a jug when he is tired and thirsty and not thinking of being watched. Then the ledger, because the ledger was the second place the case lived. The infirmary ledger and the file in its cabinet, the bound record of eleven days, admission and treatment and discharge still to come, written mostly in one steady infirmary hand that knew its abbreviations and its order, and written twice in another hand, a second hand, entries set down by someone whose letters formed differently and whose phrasing chose different words for the same treatments, someone who had access and a reason and the calm to write inside a medical record rather than steal it, which meant the file had been worth shaping and not merely worth reading. And over the ledger in her mind she set the scarring, restated as she had first received it from Valtessa and as she had now seen it again in the infirmary light. Sabin Roche, taken in Valtessa, had carried scarring across the palm and the back of one hand, in bands that met at the edges of the hand, a pattern that matched none of the three known practice injuries in the Office table and that nobody in the Office had been able to name, and Mell scarring was the same pattern on his hand, the same bands across palm and back meeting at the edges, seen plainly and telling her nothing by weight, because weight does not read a body and the scarred hand in the light was as silent to her practice as the hand at the window had been to Orry eyes, a fact and not a message. And last she laid out the strangeness that the bed had given her at its anchors, the frame and the rail and the locker, eleven days of pain held in metal and wood and repetition, pain that woke and settled and woke again as the infirmary treated what it could not name, and through all eleven days no fear at all, not the fear of a man hurt in a strange place, not the fear of a man who knows his injury means something, not the fear of a man waiting for whoever sent the watcher to the quay. A man in that much pain for that long who was never afraid. She sat with the four pieces and did not force them into a shape they had not earned, and the stillness held them plainly where she could see them whole.

## Close

The record was secured as paper is secured when an office means it to stay secured, and Mira insisted on the paper of it precisely because practice would have announced itself and paper, done properly, announced nothing beyond diligence. The file that Orry had carried through the gate was laid on the wharf office table in the presence of the gate keeper book entry that already described a dispute over carried papers and no loss, and a copy was made in full by a clerk who was not Orry and who copied what was put before her without being told whose record it was beyond the name on its cover, page by page, including the two entries in the second hand copied with their different letter forms noted in the margin by the copying clerk at Mira direction, because a copy that smooths away the second hand would have destroyed the very thing the original proved. The copy was checked against the original by Mira herself, slowly, in the small hours when the borrowed pain made sleep a rumour in any case, and the checking cost her the cold that follows sustained attention to a place where suffering had been recorded line by line, though the file itself, being paper, held weight worst of all the materials in the case and gave her almost nothing, for which she was grateful in a way she did not examine. The original and the copy were then separated as procedure required, the copy sealed in a cover with the seal pressed in the presence of a second officer of the wharf office whose name went on the seal line and not in Mira report, the original placed in a sealed packet of its own, and the two packets lodged by different routes before morning, one in the Office keeping in the town where oversight could reach it only by written application, and one in a keeping whose location Mira entered in her report by reference and not by description, so that neither the wharf nor any single reader of her report could quietly reach both and make the record of Vaun Mell disappear by making two packets disappear. She recalled, while the seals were pressing, the read she had already taken at the ledger cabinet in the record room, the metal cabinet and its drawer and the place of the file giving her somebody else reading the same file the week before, sustained and unhurried attention from a reader who knew what they were looking for, and she entered that recollection in her report as a read already done and dated to its doing, no second read taken and none needed, because the cabinet had held the visit better than paper ever holds anything and the fact of the visit was now itself part of the record the Office kept. Before either packet left the wharf office, Mira made the copying clerk read back the entries that mattered most in the order the infirmary had written them, admission on the first night with the hand already scarred and the pain already established, the treatments that followed in their plain sequence, the days when the record said only that the patient had rested or had walked the ward or had asked for water and been given it, and the two entries in the second hand set among the others where a reader who hurried would pass over them as part of the same keeping. Hearing them read aloud in another voice steadied the record in her mind as a document with a shape, a beginning in pain and a middle that someone had thought worth entering twice in a hand that was not the infirmary hand, and she marked the places where the second hand had chosen fuller words than the infirmary chose and where it had left a treatment vaguer than the infirmary ever left its own work, because vagueness in a medical record is itself a kind of entry and she wanted the copy to preserve it exactly rather than tidy it into sense. The packet seals were then entered in the wharf office book with their times and their witnesses, and the book entry was itself copied into her report, so that the securing existed in three places as paper and could be checked in any one of them by a reader who trusted none of the others, which was the closest that procedure comes to certainty and was, for a record that other hands had already reached for once, the least the Office owed it. She also entered what had not been taken. The bound ledger stayed in the infirmary. The gate book stayed with the keeper. Orry seal book stayed with Orry. Only the patient file had gone out, and the receipt described that file and no other paper, so that no later claim could widen the taking after the fact into a general seizure of infirmary records that had never happened and that the keeper, who had been right about the nature of the record, could fairly have resisted with the whole wharf behind her.

The infirmary was given a receipt for its file that described the taking as an Office lodging of a record under dispute, which was true as far as it went and went no further, and Mira knew as she signed the receipt copy that the keeper argument in the infirmary would stand against her in the wharf memory of the night, that a record taken, however receipted, was a record taken, and that the trust the keeper had spoken of would be spent in the spending and could not be unspent by correct procedure, and she let that stand in her report as well because a close that hides its own prices teaches the next officer to pay them unknowing. Within the hour of the file going out through the gate, Vaun Mell discharged himself from the wharf night infirmary. He did it in the infirmary own forms, declining the further treatment that had been entered for him for the following days, making his mark where the infirmary required a mark and refusing nothing that would have delayed him and accepting nothing that would have kept him, and he left by the yard door with his kit in a bundle his carrying hands handled without fumbling, a man going out into a wharf he knew the way a working man knows a place, by its shortcuts and its blind corners and the hours of its gates and the habits of its keepers. The infirmary staff who reported the discharge to the Office said he had been polite about it and unhurried, that he had asked for his own clothes and dressed without help though dressing meant buttons and laces worked by scarred hands, that he had drunk water standing by the bed and lifted his bundle onto his shoulder in the carrying way his body knew, and that nothing in his manner had suggested a man who expected to be followed or a man who feared what the night outside the yard door might hold, which accorded with everything the bed had already told her and told her nothing new except that the absence of fear was not a property of the bed or the pain alone but walked out of the infirmary with the man himself. The Office attempt to follow him was made properly and failed properly. The officer set to the following lost him first among the bonded sheds where the night crews were changing and a dozen men in wharf coats crossed and recrossed the lamplight, picked up a figure that might have been him at the canal steps and lost that figure at the lock where two alleys and a warehouse stair offered three departures at once, and reported within the hour that the man was not followed successfully and that no amount of additional following that night would mend it, because Mell had not run and had not hidden in any clever sense, he had simply gone home through geography that was his by labour, and a follower who knows a place by maps and instructions will lose a carrier who knows it by years of loads carried through it in all weathers. The Office kept the record. That was the shape of the success and Mira wrote it as a success without decoration, the artifact recovered and lodged and copied and sealed, the patient gone beyond following, the case closed with the object secured and the man at liberty in the town dark, and she did not call the liberty a failure because the brief of the case had been the record and the record was held. Then she named the cost, plainly, in the place in her report where costs are named and in the words she would have used to Leyden face had he been standing in the room. Orry is now known to someone. The man on the quay saw her face in the lamplight at close quarters and saw her clerk coat and her seal bag, and her name stands on the seal round for that night in the wharf office book where anyone with wharf business can learn which clerk walked that round and carried papers through that gate, and none of that can be withdrawn by procedure or by apology. Mira put her there. The decision to walk the file out rather than requisition it was Mira decision, taken in the record room doorway for reasons she still believed sound, the safety of the record and of the man in the bed, and the price of those reasons was charged to a junior clerk who had done nothing but report a hand at a window as she had been instructed to do, and Mira entered her own name against the cost in the report so that any later reading of the case would find the choosing and the chosen price on the same page. Leyden naming came after, as it had come after her taking of the case in the first place, a message and then a short exchange once she was already committed beyond recall, and she echoed it in the close of her report because it belonged to the record of the cost and not because it excused any part of it. He had named it without heat, which had made it worse. She had acted on a clerk report, in the wharf she used to work, with a civilian asset, while carrying a month of pain and a register she no longer held, the register from her private keeping of absences, the notebook she had kept against instruction and given voluntarily to Harrand and which now sat in oversight keeping as an Accord document with a numbered Accord label on its cover, and oversight would read the wharf night as the register all over again, an officer making her own cases out of her own history and her own people. He had not said she was wrong about Mell or about the file. He had said only that the cost would be read that way whether she was right or not, and that she had known it when she took the case herself and had taken it anyway, and in the close Mira let his naming stand beside her own naming of the Orry cost without merging them, two prices on one page, the clerk known to a stranger and the officer known to her own oversight, both of them entered, neither of them paid yet in full.

## The Notch

One notch only, and Mira set it down in the ledger in the form the ledger required, rung nineteen, no more of the spine than the case had earned and nothing borrowed from whatever rung might follow. A living person carries the injury. Not a coat left in a room in Valtessa, not a record in a cabinet, not a name in a report, a living man with the bands across his palm and the back of his hand meeting at the edges, who breathes and lifts water jugs and signs himself out of infirmaries and walks home through wharves he knows by labour. And he is a carrier, not a maker. His hands and his work notes and his own muddled account of loads and routes, words about making repeated by a man who never made, settle that as far as this installment settles anything: Mell carried the alloy for others, as paid labour, consignment by consignment, and the work marked him as it had marked Roche, and carrying is not making. Which means there is a maker. Somewhere behind the consignments and the paid carrying and the men marked by handling what they did not create, there stands a person or a practice that applies the technique itself, that makes what carriers then carry, and the Office does not have that maker name or face or place, and this installment does not pretend otherwise, it only fixes the shape of the absence where the maker stands, one rung and no further. She considered what the rung did not say, because a notch honestly cut is defined as much by what it leaves uncut. It did not say how the injury was made, or with what instrument or practice or intention, or whether the maker marked carriers deliberately as a signature or carelessly as a by product of work done too close to living hands. It did not say whether Roche and Mell had carried for the same maker or for different hands along the same chain, or whether the chain had one head or many, or whether the maker knew the names of the men who carried for them or knew only weights and routes and payments left at agreed places. It did not say why a carrier would spend eleven days in a wharf infirmary under his own name, in a bed the Office could find, with a record the Office could take, when the people who employed him plainly had readers and watchers enough to reach the file before the Office did. Those were not carried questions, they were the ordinary unknowns that any honest close leaves standing around its one fixed point, and Mira refused to promote any of them into the ledger beside the rung, because a ledger that carries everything carries nothing and the discipline of the notch was the discipline of choosing. What the rung fixed, it fixed firmly. The injury was not confined to the dead or to objects or to rooms that said nothing. It lived in a working man who could be spoken to, and who had chosen, for reasons the case had not earned, to discharge himself and walk out of reach rather than stay and be spoken to, and the man who carried was not the man who made, and that distinction, carrier and maker, would govern how the Office read every hand it was shown from now on, because a hand that carries tells you about a route and a hand that makes tells you about a source, and confusing the two had already cost this case its first and most reasonable reading.

The questions the case had opened in its course were closed in their course, the file secured, the man discharged and unfollowed, the watcher unnamed and gone into the warehouse narrows, the second hand recorded and copied and sealed into the keeping of the Office, all of it entered and all of it done, and one question only carried past the close of the case into whatever case came next, carried exactly as the bed at its anchors had given it to her and as the silence had laid it out last among its pieces. He was not afraid. Eleven days of pain and no fear. Mira wrote the carried question in the ledger in those terms and no others, because a carried question that grows ornaments in the writing will answer itself falsely later, and she left the rest of the page to the ordinary closing entries, the seals and the lodgings and the cost named on its own page, the night work of an office that had recovered a record and lost a patient and learned the shape of one rung of a thing it did not yet understand. The last of the night business was not Office business at all. In the street beyond the wharf gate, with the town lamps lit and the wharf sounds fallen behind the wall, Orry handed over the file in a paper bag from a bakery, the plain folder slipped inside it for the last stretch of the walk so that what she carried to the meeting place looked like nothing in the world but a clerk errand run past a bakery counter, and Mira took the bag and thanked her and did not make a speech of it, and Orry kept the bakery receipt, folding it once and putting it in her seal book, because she is the sort of person who does.

## Case File

### Q: How is the wharf night infirmary staffed through a full night shift?

The night infirmary is staffed on a standing rota held by the wharf itself, not by any Accord service. A senior keeper holds charge for the night, supported by attendants drawn from the wharf workforce and by a recorder who keeps the ledger as admissions occur. Relief is arranged within the rota, so that no hour is left without a keeper present and no attendant works beyond the span set for night labour. The Office holds no part in the rota, appoints no member of it, and may not direct its order. Names are entered in the infirmary book for the night in question, and that entry is the sole authority for who was on duty.

### Q: Which members of the wharf workforce may seek treatment at the night infirmary without prior referral?

Admission at night requires no referral and no prior paper. Any person employed on the wharf, whether as labourer, clerk, seal keeper, or member of a night crew, may present at the infirmary and be received on the statement of injury or illness alone. The keeper on duty decides treatment according to need, and the record is made after reception, not before it. Persons who are not of the wharf workforce are received only where delay would cause harm, and their reception is noted as exceptional in the ledger. No inquiry into Accord standing, Office interest, or bonded status is permitted to delay or refuse treatment within these terms. Such reception is recorded plainly.

### Q: How does the night infirmary keep its ledger and patient files apart from wharf administration?

The ledger and the patient files are kept in the record room of the infirmary, in a cabinet reserved for that purpose, under the charge of the keeper on duty. Wharf administration holds its own books in separate rooms and has no standing right of entry to the record room. Files are arranged by patient and by admission, not by employer, gang, or cargo, so that an administrative search cannot proceed by trade or by consignment. Removal of a file from the cabinet is entered at the time, with the reason stated in plain terms. A file returned is checked against its entry before it is replaced in its place.

### Q: What provision does the night infirmary make for patients whose treatment extends beyond a single night?

Where treatment must continue, the infirmary retains the patient under the same file opened at admission, adding each day of care as a fresh entry rather than opening a new record. Beds are held for continuing patients before new admissions are assigned where space is short, subject always to urgent need. Food, washing, and rest are provided as part of the treatment, not as separate charges upon the patient or upon a gang. Discharge is recorded by the keeper when treatment ends, whether at the patient's request or on the keeper's judgment, and the file is then closed and retained in the cabinet in its proper order. The arrangement is entered nightly.

### Q: On what showing may the Accord request sight of a civilian medical record held by a wharf infirmary?

A request must state the record sought, the purpose for which sight is required, and the authority under which the request is made, in writing, before any file is produced. General interest, convenience of inquiry, and proximity to a separate investigation are not sufficient showings. The keeper may require the request to be narrowed where it covers patients or entries beyond the stated purpose. Sight, where granted, is given in the record room under the keeper's presence, and the file does not leave the infirmary on the strength of a request alone. Every request, granted or refused, is entered in the infirmary book with its date in words and its outcome.

### Q: What limits apply when an Accord officer copies a medical record rather than merely reading it?

Copying is treated as a separate act from reading and requires its own justification on the request. A copy may include only those entries that fall within the stated purpose, and entries concerning other patients, other admissions, or treatment unrelated to that purpose must be excluded. The copy is certified as a copy, marked with the place from which it was taken, and lodged as Accord paper from that moment, with custody recorded in the ordinary way. The original remains with the infirmary at all times. Where a copy is later found to exceed its justification, the excess is struck from Accord use and the infirmary is informed in writing.

### Q: How is a patient's consent recorded when a medical file is disclosed to an Accord service?

Consent, where it is relied upon, is recorded in the patient's own file, in the patient's presence where the patient is able to attend, and in terms that name the service to which disclosure is made and the extent of what is to be shown. Consent given for one disclosure does not extend to a later disclosure, nor to a wider reading than the terms first stated. Where a patient cannot consent, the keeper records that fact and the ground for any disclosure must rest on authority other than consent. A patient may withdraw consent before disclosure occurs, and the withdrawal is entered with the same care as the consent itself.

### Q: What remedy remains to an infirmary when it believes an Accord request for records exceeds its scope?

The keeper may refuse the request in whole or in part, stating the refusal and its grounds in the infirmary book and on the request itself. Refusal does not expose the infirmary to penalty for the act of refusing, and treatment of any patient named in the request must continue without alteration. The service making the request may renew it in narrower terms or refer the question to the standing channel between the wharf and the Accord seat, where it is considered on the paper alone. Pending that consideration, no file is produced. A pattern of excessive requests may itself be reported, and such a report is retained with the infirmary records.

### Q: What physical effects are recorded in men who have carried alloy consignments over repeated journeys?

Repeated carrying is recorded as producing strength suited to lifting and holding, together with callus and thickening where loads rest against the hands. In a proportion of cases, scarring in bands across palm and back has been noted after sustained contact through handling, wrapping, or carriage where protection has failed or been omitted. Pain may persist after the work has ended, and may settle or wake with movement in a manner the infirmary records without assigning a cause beyond the work described. The Office table treats these effects as marks of handling, distinct in pattern and in history from injuries arising on the foundry floor itself. Such notes are retained for comparison.

### Q: How does the Office distinguish the marks of carrying from the marks of ordinary wharf labour?

Distinction rests on pattern, history, and correspondence with paper, taken together, and never on a single mark alone. Ordinary labour produces callus and strain in places explained by the tools and loads of the gang in question, and those marks accord with the work the man is known to have done. Carrying marks are examined for banding, for meeting at the edges of the hand, and for a history of consignments whose routes and weights are entered elsewhere. Where the history and the paper do not support the carrying account, the marks are recorded as unexplained. Weight is not used for this distinction, as weight does not read a body.

### Q: What duties are withheld from a carrier after scarring of the hands has been noted?

Once scarring of the described pattern has been noted and entered, the carrier is withheld from further consignments of the same material until the infirmary or the examining officer records that handling may resume. The withholding is a protective measure, not a finding about the man, and it is entered without comment on character or reliability. Other wharf duties that do not require contact with the material remain open, subject to the keeper's advice on pain and grip. A carrier who is withheld must be told the terms plainly and the entry must show that he was told. Resumption without a fresh entry is treated as a breach by the assigning authority.

### Q: How is a carrier's account of loads and routes tested against foundry and wharf paper?

The account is compared with foundry day books, wharf manifests, and gate entries for the consignments named, each source being read for weight, wrapping, handling, and timing. Agreement on broad outline is expected even from an honest account given long after the work, while exact recall of detail that paper itself does not contain is treated with caution. Error is weighed by kind. Error about sequence and duration is common and carries little weight. Error that borrows the language of another trade, repeated in the same form, suggests the account was supplied by others and is recorded as such, without treating the man himself as the author of the supply.

### Q: In what respects does strength develop differently in a maker's hands and in a carrier's hands?

A maker's strength is developed through sustained holding of tools in fine positions, through pressure applied in small areas, and through repetition of precise movements over long periods at a bench. A carrier's strength is developed through lifting, gripping loads that shift, and holding weight against the whole hand and forearm. Examination therefore attends to where strength lies, not merely to its amount. A hand strong in the forearm and in the broad grip, but unpractised in fine holding, accords with carrying. A hand strong in the fingers in the pattern of bench work accords with making. The finding is recorded as accordance, not as proof standing alone for the record.

### Q: What callus patterns are expected on a maker's hands that a carrier's hands do not show?

Bench work leaves callus and thickening at points of repeated contact with handles, edges, and instruments, often uneven between the hands according to the task each hand performs. Fine work may also leave smoothing, small cuts healed in lines, and tenderness at the fingertips from repeated testing of heat or surface. A carrier's hands show broader callus across the palm and at the base of the fingers, from rope, crate, and load, with less distinction between small points of contact. Absence of bench callus does not by itself exclude making, as gloves and differing tasks alter the pattern, but its absence must be entered and weighed with the history and the observed grip.

### Q: How does grip for fine work differ from grip for lifting in the Office examination of hands?

The examination asks the man to perform ordinary acts, such as lifting a vessel, holding a rail, and handling his own kit, and observes how the grip is formed for each act without instruction or correction. Grip for lifting closes the whole hand around the load and draws strength from the forearm, settling quickly and holding steady under weight. Grip for fine work isolates pressure, adjusts in small movements, and rests the object against particular fingers for control. An examiner records what is observed in plain terms and does not prompt the performance. The record is of behaviour under observation, and it is filed with the history rather than substituted for it.

### Q: Why does error about trade vocabulary assist in separating a maker from a carrier?

A man describing his own trade ordinarily uses its terms in their proper places, even where his account is brief or his education is limited, because the terms belong to movements his hands have performed. Error that places making terms upon carrying work, consistently and in the same form, suggests that the description was learned from another speaker rather than from the work itself. Such error does not establish what the man has done. It weakens the account as evidence of making and directs inquiry toward loads, routes, and paper. The distinction is recorded carefully, as a labourer repeating words supplied by an employer is in a different position from a maker concealing a practice.

### Q: What is recorded about Sabel Orry apart from statements she has given as a witness?

The file records her employment as a junior clerk of the bonded chain, her place on the seal round, her hours, and the order of her checks, as matters of ordinary administration. It also records conduct observed in the course of duty, including care with paper, punctuality on night rounds, and the keeping of small receipts and notes that others would discard. These entries are made because they bear on reliability in handling documents, not because they enlarge a witness statement. Personal detail beyond what duty requires is not entered. Where an entry concerns character, it must rest on an observed act, stated plainly, and not on general impression or repute among the wharf offices.

### Q: How are a junior clerk's ordinary duties and hours established when she is not giving evidence?

Ordinary duties are established from the wharf rota, the seal round order, and the gate book, each of which is kept in the normal course without reference to any investigation. The rota shows the nights assigned and the relief provided. The round order shows the sequence of seals to be checked and the places at which a clerk must attend. The gate book shows passage in and out where passage is required. Together these sources fix what a clerk was expected to do and where she was expected to be, independently of any later account. Discrepancy between the paper and an account is entered as discrepancy, without assuming which source is at fault.

### Q: What protections apply to a clerk who carries Office paper in the course of her own round?

A clerk asked to carry paper within her own round retains the standing of a civilian employee of the wharf, and the request does not convert her into an officer or an agent for other purposes. The paper must be described to her in terms sufficient for her to decide whether to carry it, and refusal carries no penalty and is recorded without adverse comment. Her name, where it appears on the round for that night, is treated as exposed once the carrying is done, and that exposure is named as a cost in the closing record. Protection after the event consists in plain recording, so that any approach to her can be recognised against the file.

### Q: How does the Office record a clerk's habits and reliability without reducing her to her testimony?

Habits are recorded only where they affect the handling of a case, such as keeping a receipt from an ordinary purchase, preserving the order of a round under interruption, or holding a document securely when challenged. Each habit is tied to a single observed occasion and entered in ordinary language, without turning the clerk into a type or a symbol for the file. Reliability is treated as a history of acts performed as promised, not as a quality asserted by a supervising officer. The record is kept separate from her statements as a witness, so that a later reader can distinguish what she did from what she said, and can weigh each on its own terms.
