A woman writes to her brother about a household dispute. A merchant instructs an agent about a shipment and complains about the price of flax. A father lists what his daughter is bringing to her marriage, item by item, down to the cooking pots. A schoolboy practises his letters on a scrap. None of these people expected to be read by anyone but their correspondent, and all of them are readable.

That is not how the medieval world usually reaches us. The claim this article defends is the storeroom lit city thesis: that Fatimid Cairo is among the best documented cities anywhere before the modern period, and that the reason is an accident of religious scruple rather than any decision to record it.

![Life in Fatimid Cairo, reconstructed from the discarded documents of a synagogue storeroom - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-62.webp)

The mechanism is worth stating at the outset because everything else depends on it. A synagogue in the older city maintained a storeroom into which written material was placed rather than destroyed, because writing might carry a sacred name and destroying it was forbidden. The rule was applied broadly, so the accumulation includes documents with no religious content at all.

The result is an archive nobody curated. A body of records assembled by an institution reflects that institution's priorities; a room that received everything reflects only what people happened to write, which is far closer to a random sample of a society's paperwork than historians usually obtain.

What that sample shows is a city of extraordinary variety and ordinary concerns. People worried about money, married, quarrelled with relatives, travelled, fell ill, sent gifts, appealed to community leaders, complained about officials and wrote to each other constantly.

This article owns daily life. The city's founding belongs to the article on [how the Fatimids founded Cairo](/2010/12/01/how-fatimids-founded-cairo/), the commerce that made it rich to the article on [Fatimid trade and the wealth of Egypt](/2011/04/01/fatimid-trade-and-wealth-of-egypt/), the older settlement beside it to the article on [the founding of Fustat](/2010/03/01/founding-of-fustat/), and the dynasty itself to the article on [Fatimid Egypt](/2010/11/01/fatimid-egypt-complete-guide/).

## The Storeroom

The archive that makes this article possible deserves a full description, since its character determines what can and cannot be said.

The practice behind it was a religious prohibition on destroying writing that might contain a sacred name. Rather than sort documents individually, the community placed discarded written material in a storeroom, and the sorting never happened.

### What is the Cairo Geniza?

A storeroom attached to a synagogue in the older city where discarded written material was deposited rather than destroyed, because writing might carry a sacred name. Applied broadly over centuries, the practice accumulated hundreds of thousands of fragments including letters, contracts, accounts, court records, marriage documents, school exercises and shopping lists.

The scale is enormous. Hundreds of thousands of fragments accumulated over roughly a thousand years, with the Fatimid centuries especially well represented because they were a period of intense commercial activity and heavy paper use.

The dispersal happened in the nineteenth century, when the contents were removed and distributed among collections in several countries, which is why the material is now held in many libraries and why reassembling documents from separated fragments has been a major scholarly task.

The uncurated character is the essential feature. An archive assembled by a court, a monastery or a government preserves what that body considered worth keeping; this one preserves what people threw away, which is a completely different sample.

The comparison that clarifies the value is with the papyri of the earlier period, treated in the article on the papyri of Roman Egypt in this series. Both bodies of evidence survive by accident, both document ordinary life, and both exist because somebody stopped throwing things away in a place where paper survives.

The medium matters as well. This period had paper rather than papyrus, and paper is cheaper, which means more people wrote more things, which means the archive documents a broader slice of society than an earlier one would.

## The Bathhouse and the Street

Two ordinary settings deserve a note because they appear constantly in the documents and shaped social life more than any institution.

The bathhouse was a normal part of urban routine rather than an occasional luxury. Cities of this world supported many, they were commercial enterprises, they served different communities and different sexes at different times, and they functioned as meeting places as much as washing places.

The documents record them as landmarks, as property, as business ventures and as the setting for incidents, which is a good indication of how central they were.

The street was the other great social setting, and its physical character determined how it worked. Narrow, shaded by the buildings on either side, frequently covered in the market sections, and crowded with people, animals and goods.

Commerce spilled into it constantly. Workshops opened onto it, sellers occupied it, and the regulation of encroachment onto public space is a recurring administrative concern.

Water sellers, food sellers and every kind of itinerant trade worked the streets, which means a household could meet many of its daily needs without going to a market at all.

Encounters in the street generated disputes and therefore documents, and the court records of assault, obstruction and public argument are among the more vivid material available.

Night changed the character entirely. Lanes were closed, movement was restricted, and the documents refer to the difference between day and night conditions in ways that assume the reader knows it.

The general observation is that the settings of ordinary sociability are usually invisible in historical sources, since nobody records the obvious, and that they become visible chiefly through the records of what went wrong in them.

## Language in the Streets

What people actually spoke and wrote in this city adds a dimension that the documents record almost incidentally.

Arabic was the spoken language of the great majority by this date, the article on [how Egypt became Arabic speaking](/2010/04/01/how-egypt-became-arabic-speaking/) having established that the shift was largely complete in the towns while lagging considerably in the countryside, so a city population was further along that curve than the country as a whole.

Coptic survived in liturgical use throughout, and in diminishing degree in some domestic and rural contexts, and the article on [the Coptic language](/2009/07/01/coptic-language-and-its-history/) sets out at length why the endpoint of its domestic transmission cannot be dated with any confidence from the surviving evidence.

The Jewish community wrote Arabic in Hebrew script, which is one of the archive's most distinctive features and a real technical obstacle for anyone approaching it, since the writer's spoken language and the writer's alphabet come from different traditions and reading the material requires competence in both at once.

Hebrew and Aramaic appear in religious and legal contexts within that community, so a single document may move between languages, registers and scripts within a few lines, which is normal for the material and demanding for anyone reading it.

Other languages appear in the commercial correspondence, since merchants dealt with partners across the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean and needed at least a working competence in the terms of trade, and the traces of that contact are visible in the vocabulary of goods, measures and shipping that fills the letters.

The court operated in Arabic with the elaborate chancery style described in the article on [the Fatimid caliphs](/2011/01/01/fatimid-caliphs-who-ruled-egypt/), written on long scrolls in a large widely spaced hand, which is a different register in every respect from the plain commercial Arabic of the merchants' letters.

The dialect features visible in the informal documents are of considerable interest to linguists, since people writing quickly to family members use the language they actually speak rather than the formal register they were taught, and the resulting evidence about medieval spoken Arabic is difficult to obtain anywhere else.

The general observation is that a commercial city is a linguistic environment rather than a single language, and that documents written for practical purposes preserve spoken usage in a way that formal literary texts systematically suppress. A grammarian records what ought to be said; a merchant writing hastily to his brother records what people actually said, and the second is the harder evidence to come by and the more valuable when it survives.

## The Two Settlements as One System

Understanding how the palace enclosure and the commercial city functioned together, rather than treating either alone, resolves several puzzles about daily life.

The functional division was clear. Government, ceremonial, the army and the court occupied the north; commerce, industry, the port and the great majority of the population occupied the south.

The traffic between them was constant. The court consumed enormous quantities of food, cloth, fuel and manufactured goods, all of which came from the commercial city, and the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo argues that this consumption is what eventually opened the closed enclosure.

The employment link ran the same way. Suppliers, craftsmen, servants and labourers working for the court lived in the south and travelled north, which means a portion of the commercial city's economy depended directly on the palace.

The road between them carried that traffic and became one of the most important arteries in the region.

The administrative link mattered for taxation, since the fiscal apparatus sat in the north and the taxpayers were in the south, and the article on taxation in this series describes the machinery involved.

The social separation was nonetheless real. A merchant in the older settlement had no ordinary occasion to enter the palace enclosure, and the ceremonial that displayed the caliph was in significant part staged behind a wall.

The eventual merging came with the northern quarter's opening and was completed by the burning of 1168, after which the two became a single metropolis with its centre in the north.

The general observation is that a purpose built government city and a working commercial city cannot remain separate, because the first consumes what the second produces, and the flow of goods and labour opens whatever barrier is put between them.

## What the Archive Does Not Contain

Listing the absences is as informative as listing the contents, and several are structural rather than accidental.

The countryside is absent. This is an urban commercial archive and the villages that fed the city appear only as places goods came from, so the agricultural population producing the surplus is invisible here.

The very poor are present only as objects. They appear on charity lists compiled by others and in appeals written for them, and almost never as authors of their own documents, since destitution and literacy rarely coincide.

The illiterate majority in general appears at one remove, mediated by scribes and by those who wrote about them.

The palace is absent, as the earlier section set out.

Politics is largely absent, which is the most striking omission. Caliphs, viziers, doctrine and the rivalry with Baghdad appear rarely, because a merchant writing about flax had no reason to mention them.

Interior life is absent in the modern sense. These documents record what people did and asked for rather than what they felt about existence, and inferring the second from the first is a step the evidence does not license.

The Muslim and Christian equivalents are absent because they were not preserved. No comparable storeroom survives for the other communities, so their internal documentation is known far less well, which distorts the picture of a mixed city.

The general observation is that every archive is defined by its silences as much as by its contents, and that a historian's first obligation is to establish what a source cannot show before deciding what it does.

## Merchants Away From Home

The commercial travel that generated the archive shaped family life so directly that it deserves separate treatment.

The absences were long. A voyage to the Indian Ocean and back could take up to two years, as the article on Fatimid trade explains, and Mediterranean voyages were seasonal, so a merchant might be away for the better part of a year routinely.

The household continued without him. Wives managed property, made decisions, dealt with creditors and raised children, and the letters instructing them to do so, and reporting that they had, are among the most informative documents in the archive.

The correspondence carried the relationship. Letters were the only contact, they took months to arrive, and a reply might cross the original in transit, which produces conversations conducted across enormous lags.

The anxieties are visible throughout. Whether a ship had arrived, whether goods had sold, whether the family was well, whether a partner was honest, and whether one would return at all fill the letters.

The community handled the consequences of failure. A merchant lost at sea left a household whose position depended on whether his affairs could be settled, and the resulting disputes and appeals are documented.

The abandoned wife was a recognized problem. A husband who did not return and could not be confirmed dead left a wife unable to remarry, and the community records show serious effort devoted to establishing what had happened to missing travellers.

The reunions appear too, in letters announcing arrivals, arranging meetings and reporting safe returns, which are the happiest documents in a collection that mostly records difficulty.

The general observation is that a long distance commercial economy imposes a specific family structure, with prolonged separation, female management of household affairs and a heavy reliance on written communication, and that this city documents the pattern better than anywhere else in the medieval world.

## Books, Learning and Leisure

What people read, studied and did for pleasure is recoverable in outline and adds a dimension the practical documents alone would miss.

Books circulated widely. The archive includes book lists, sale records and requests, which together show private libraries of substantial size in the households of the educated.

The trade in books was commercial and international, with volumes moving along the same routes as other goods, and the article on Fatimid trade describes the paper industry that made them affordable.

Study at an advanced level took place in the institutions the article on [al-Azhar](/2011/02/01/al-azhar-oldest-university/) treats, in community academies, and in the private circles that were the normal form of higher instruction in this world.

Poetry was the prestige literary form and it was social, composed for occasions, exchanged between correspondents and performed, and specimens appear in the archive.

Music and entertainment appear indirectly through complaints, regulations and occasional descriptions, and the city supported professional performers.

The bathhouse was a social institution as much as a sanitary one, appearing in the documents as a normal part of urban routine and as a meeting place.

Games and pastimes are documented lightly, with chess and backgammon appearing, and gambling on them generating the disapproval that constitutes most of the evidence.

Excursions outside the city for pleasure appear in letters, particularly to gardens and to the riverbanks, and the seasonal cycle governed when they were pleasant.

The general observation is that leisure is documented almost entirely by accident, through regulation, complaint and passing mention, since nobody records enjoyment for its own sake, and a society's pleasures are consequently the hardest part of its life to reconstruct.

## Continuity With What Came Before and After

Placing this city's daily life in the longer Egyptian sequence shows how much of it was neither Fatimid nor new.

The housing form of tall subdivided buildings around courtyards was established in the older settlement long before 969, as the article on the founding of Fustat describes, and it continued long afterward.

The occupational structure with textiles dominant had been in place for centuries and remained so, and the article on the papyri of Roman Egypt shows textile production as a major activity a millennium earlier.

The food was substantially the same. Bread, pulses, onions, fish and oil describe the Egyptian diet across an enormous span, and the continuity is one of the strongest in the country's history.

The flood governed the year for this city as it had for every Egyptian settlement, and the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt sets out why that layer never moved.

The mixed communities living institutionally apart and residentially together describes Egypt before the Fatimids and after them, and the article on Copts under early Islamic rule documents the same pattern two centuries earlier.

What was distinctively Fatimid was the political overlay: the palace city, the ceremonial, the dynastic festivals and the doctrine, all of which the article on Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate shows were removed in 1171 without difficulty.

The conclusion follows the argument this series has developed. The layers of life closest to the state changed with the state, and the layers furthest from it did not change at all, which is exactly what the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt sets out as the general pattern.

The general observation is that a period's daily life is usually far less distinctive than its politics, and that an article about ordinary living in any Egyptian century will describe much the same bread, the same river and much the same worries.

## The Palace City Compared

Since the archive documents the commercial settlement, saying what is known about life inside the walled enclosure balances the picture.

The evidence is entirely different in kind. Where the older settlement has an accidental documentary archive and productive excavation, the enclosure has narrative description, ceremonial accounts and almost no excavation, since a dense modern city sits on it.

The population was the court, the administration, the army and their dependants, which is a very large number of people and a completely different social composition from the commercial city.

The description that survives comes chiefly from the eleventh century traveller's account and from a later topographical compilation preserving lost material, both of which describe the palaces, the ceremonial and the layout rather than domestic life.

The residents' ordinary experience is essentially unrecorded. Nobody in the palace complex threw their letters into a storeroom that survived, so the daily life of the soldiers, servants and officials living there is invisible in a way the merchants' is not.

The asymmetry produces a distortion worth naming. Accounts of this period describe the court in ceremonial terms and the commercial city in intimate ones, and the difference is entirely an artefact of what survived.

The contrast in survival is the point the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo makes about the buildings. The palaces were dismantled after 1171 and the commercial district decayed slowly, and the second condition preserves far more than the first.

The general observation is that the visibility of a social group to historians depends on whether its paperwork happened to be preserved, and that the most powerful people in a society are frequently the least documented in their ordinary lives because their institutions were destroyed with their regime.

## The Neighbourhood

Below the level of the city and above the level of the household sat the neighbourhood, and it was the unit within which most people actually lived.

The physical form was a network of narrow lanes off larger streets, frequently terminating in dead ends, which produced enclosed clusters of buildings whose residents necessarily knew each other.

That form had social consequences. A lane with one entrance is effectively a shared space, and the households along it shared water sources, watched each other's affairs and dealt with each other constantly.

The mixing was at this scale. Households of different faiths appear in the same lanes and buildings, which is what makes the residential integration described earlier concrete rather than abstract.

Local disputes fill the court records, over shared walls, drainage, access, noise and the use of common space, and they document neighbourhood life more directly than any description could.

Mutual assistance operated at the same scale. The appeals for charity frequently invoke neighbours as witnesses to circumstances, and the informal support of people living close by is visible behind the formal charity of the community.

Local knowledge was the currency of reputation. A city of enclosed lanes is one where conduct is observed, and the letters defending reputation take for granted that neighbours know and will say.

The security dimension appears in the arrangements for closing lanes at night, which several sources describe, and which made each cluster a defensible unit.

The general observation is that pre modern urban life happened at a smaller scale than the city, and that the neighbourhood rather than the metropolis was the community most people belonged to in practice.

## The Older Settlement's Long Decline

Since the archive comes from a settlement that afterward declined, the trajectory deserves a section for the light it throws on the documents.

The peak was the eleventh and earlier twelfth centuries, exactly the period the archive documents most densely, when the settlement was the commercial and residential heart of the region.

The burning of 1168 was the turning point, deliberately set as the article on the founding of Fustat describes, and its consequences for the district were severe.

The population shift northward followed and continued, drawn by the opening of the formerly closed enclosure and by the concentration of government and later commerce there.

The decline was gradual rather than sudden. The settlement remained inhabited and functioning for a long time afterward, and the archive continues past the Fatimid period, so the burning accelerated a process rather than ending the place.

The eventual condition was that of a peripheral quarter with substantial vacant ground, which is what made the area available for the refuse dumping and later the excavation that has produced so much of the physical evidence.

The consequence for evidence is favourable and slightly perverse. A district that had declined was excavated far more easily than one continuously built over, so the settlement's misfortune is why its houses, water systems and pottery are known.

The synagogue survived, which is why the storeroom survived, and the article on the Mosque of Amr makes the parallel point about religious buildings persisting where secular ones do not.

The general observation is that the best documented places are frequently the ones that stopped growing. Continuous prosperity destroys its own past by building over it, and a district that declines preserves what a district that thrives removes.

## Health, Medicine and Dying

The medical dimension of ordinary life is documented from several directions here and deserves fuller treatment.

Physicians were numerous and their standing was high, with the profession drawing practitioners from all the city's communities and several distinguished figures serving at court while also treating ordinary patients.

Prescriptions survive in the archive, written for individual patients and discarded, which means the actual treatments given to named people can be examined rather than only the theory in medical books.

The medical literature of the period was substantial and circulated widely, and Egypt's position in the trade in drugs and aromatics, described in the article on Fatimid trade, supplied the materials the pharmacopoeia required.

The pharmacist was a distinct occupation and a commercial one, since preparing and selling compounded medicines is a trade, and disputes involving pharmacists appear in the record.

Hospitals existed, the article on the Tulunids describing an earlier foundation in the same region, and such institutions were a normal expression of rulers' charitable obligation.

Illness in the letters is unfiltered and immediate. People describe their symptoms, ask about others, report recoveries and deaths, and the emotional content of that correspondence is among the strongest in the archive.

Death and burial generated their own documents, with wills, inheritance disputes and community records of funeral arrangements, and each community maintained its own burial grounds.

The general observation is that a society's medical arrangements are best understood from what was actually done to actual patients rather than from what its medical literature recommends, and the archive supplies the first where most periods offer only the second.

## Reading a Single Document

Working through what a historian actually does with one of these fragments illustrates the method better than description.

The first questions are physical. What is the material, how large was the original, what is the hand, is it a draft or a final copy, and has anything been written on the reverse, since paper was expensive and reuse was normal.

The second questions are formal. What kind of document is this, does it follow a standard form, and where does it depart from the form, since departures are where the individual case shows through.

The third questions are contextual. Who are the parties, do they appear elsewhere in the archive, what places and dates are named, and can the document be fitted to a known sequence of events.

The fourth questions are evidential. What does this document establish directly, what does it imply, and what would be an over reading, since a marriage contract establishes the terms agreed and not whether the marriage was happy.

The fifth question is aggregative. What does this document look like alongside the others of its type, since a single contract shows one arrangement and a hundred show what was normal.

The reuse of paper is worth dwelling on because it produces a characteristic complication. A letter written on the back of an earlier document gives two texts of different dates on one fragment, which is a nuisance for cataloguing and a gift for dating.

The naming problem recurs constantly. Common names, patronymics and nicknames make identifying whether two documents concern the same person genuinely difficult, and much scholarly effort goes into it.

The general observation is that historical knowledge of this kind is built fragment by fragment through work that is technical, slow and cumulative, and that a confident general statement about daily life in this city rests on thousands of such individual determinations.

## How the Archive Was Found

The recovery of the storeroom's contents is a story worth telling briefly, because it explains the present state of the material.

The storeroom's existence was known locally and to occasional visitors long before it was systematically examined, and material had been removed piecemeal by various people over a long period.

The systematic removal occurred in the nineteenth century, when scholars recognized the significance of what the room contained and the bulk of it was transferred to institutional collections.

The dispersal is the consequence historians now live with. The contents went to several countries and multiple institutions, which means fragments of a single document can sit in different collections on different continents.

The reassembly has been decades of work. Identifying that two fragments belong together requires matching hands, formats, content and physical characteristics, and the task is far from complete.

Digitization has transformed it substantially, since fragments held apart can be compared without travel, and matches that were practically impossible have become routine.

The condition of the material is generally poor and highly variable. These were discarded documents stored in a room for centuries, so tearing, damage, insect activity and general deterioration are normal.

The reading is difficult independently of condition. Hands vary enormously, abbreviations are heavy, and much of the material is written in one language using another's script, which requires specialized competence.

The general observation is that the value of an archive is not realized on discovery. A body of material of this kind requires generations of work to become usable, and the account of Fatimid daily life in this article rests on more than a century of that work.

## What This City Can Teach

A closing analytical section extracting the transferable findings, since a well documented case supports conclusions that a poorly documented one cannot.

The first is that ordinary medieval life is knowable where the evidence permits, which means that its usual invisibility is a fact about preservation rather than about the period.

The second is that accidental archives outperform deliberate ones for social history. Nothing an institution chose to keep would document a shopping list, and a shopping list answers questions a charter cannot.

The third is that religious communities in a medieval city were institutionally separate and socially mixed, which is a pattern the documents establish concretely against a common assumption of segregation.

The fourth is that women in this society acted economically and legally in ways the normative sources understate, and that the corrective comes from documents recording what happened rather than texts describing what should.

The fifth is that political history and social history run on different tracks. The caliphs, the doctrine and the rivalry with Baghdad that fill the rest of this cluster barely appear in these documents, because they barely touched the lives the documents record.

The sixth is that the medieval urban standard of amenity has been systematically underestimated, and that excavation here has produced piped water and latrines where the expectation was neither.

The seventh is that recognition is a legitimate historical finding. When a thousand year old letter reads like a letter, the continuity in human concern is data about human beings and not merely a pleasing impression.

The general point is that a single exceptionally documented case is worth more than many poorly documented ones, because it establishes what was possible and therefore constrains what can be assumed about the cases where evidence is missing.

## The City Under Stress

The archive documents not only ordinary conditions but the periods when they broke down, and those documents are among the most valuable in it.

The great famine of the 1060s and early 1070s described in the article on Fatimid Egypt is the largest such episode, and the commercial correspondence from those years shows trade contracting sharply, prices moving violently and business relationships failing.

The comparison between the narrative accounts and the documents is instructive and appears in the article on Fatimid Egypt as a methodological point. Chronicles describe the famine in language that is partly rhetorical; the documents show commerce reduced but not stopped, and reading only one overstates or understates the collapse.

The army's factional fighting in the same period reached the streets, and the documents record disruption, displacement and losses in a way no chronicle does.

The burning of the older settlement in 1168 is the other great disruption, deliberately set to deny the city to an approaching army as the article on the founding of Fustat describes, and it accelerated the shift of population northward.

Ordinary crises appear throughout at household scale. Shipwreck, business failure, the death of a provider, illness and imprisonment recur in the appeals for assistance, and each represents a family whose position collapsed.

The charity records are the counterpart. Periods of higher need produce longer recipient lists, which means the archive contains a rough measure of social distress across time.

The resilience is as notable as the disruption. Communities reconstituted, trade resumed, and the documentary record continues through episodes that a narrative history presents as catastrophic.

The general observation is that documents produced during a crisis record what actually happened to people, while narratives written afterward record what the crisis meant, and a history using both is better than one using either.

## Community Government

Each of the city's communities governed its own internal affairs, and the mechanics are well documented for the one whose records survive.

The leadership was a combination of religious authority, judicial function and representation to the state, held by figures whose appointment and standing generated correspondence and occasional disputes.

The courts handled marriage, divorce, inheritance, property and commercial disputes within the community, applying its own law, and their records are among the richest documents in the archive.

The endowments funded communal functions. Property dedicated to support a synagogue, a school, a charitable distribution or an official produced income that had to be administered, and the administration produced paperwork.

Representation to the state was a central function and a dangerous one. Somebody had to deal with the authorities over taxes, over disputes involving members and over periodic difficulties, and the article on [Copts under early Islamic rule](/2010/05/01/copts-under-early-islamic-rule/) describes the parallel position of the Christian patriarch as a tax guarantor.

Internal politics were vigorous. Contested appointments, factional disputes and appeals over the heads of local leaders to more distant authorities appear repeatedly, and communities were not the harmonious bodies later idealization suggests.

The connection to communities elsewhere was strong and is documented in correspondence with authorities in other regions, since these were networks spanning the Mediterranean and beyond rather than isolated groups.

The state's relationship to all of this was largely hands off in the ordinary case. Communities managed themselves, delivered what the fiscal system required, and dealt with the authorities through recognized leaders.

The general observation is that a medieval city was a federation of self governing communities rather than a single administered population, and that the state's reach into daily life was correspondingly shallow, which the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt sets out as a general feature of the period.

## Slavery in the Household

Domestic slavery was a normal feature of prosperous households in this city and it belongs in any honest account of daily life.

Enslaved people appear in household documents, in marriage inventories, in wills and in sale contracts, which means the institution is documented in the same matter of fact way as furniture and property.

Origins were varied, with the article on Fatimid trade noting that the commerce in enslaved people ran along the same routes as other goods, from several directions.

Domestic function predominated in the households the archive documents, distinct from the military slavery that supplied the army and which belongs to the article on Fatimid Egypt.

Manumission is documented and was common enough to generate its own paperwork, with freed people appearing subsequently in the record.

The legal position was distinct and cut across the community structure, so an enslaved person in a household belonged to a legal category rather than to the social ladder described earlier.

The documents record it without commentary, which is itself informative. Nobody in the correspondence argues about the institution; it appears as a fact of life in the way that any unquestioned arrangement does.

The historian's obligation is to record it rather than to pass over it, since an account of daily life that omits a normal feature of prosperous households is not describing the society accurately.

The general observation is that the ordinariness with which a source treats something is evidence about the society and not a licence for the historian to treat it the same way.

## The Textile Economy Inside the Household

Because textiles dominated the economy, understanding how cloth production reached into ordinary households explains a great deal about how families lived.

Flax was grown in the countryside, brought to the city, and processed through a long sequence of operations each employing different people at different scales.

Spinning was the household stage. It required little equipment, could be done at home in intervals between other work, and supplied income to women in households at every social level, which the documents record as ordinary and unremarkable.

Weaving was more specialized, requiring a loom and space, and was organized in workshops of varying size as well as in homes.

Dyeing was a distinct trade with its own installations, materials and expertise, and dyers appear as a recognizable occupational group.

Finishing, bleaching and the various treatments that turned woven cloth into a marketable product each supported their own specialists.

Trade in the product operated at every level from the local seller to the international merchants whose correspondence about flax and finished cloth fills the archive.

The consequence for households is that the great industry of the economy was not confined to workshops. It entered ordinary homes through spinning, it employed household members at several stages, and its products were the most valuable movable property most families owned.

The general observation is that in a pre modern economy the dominant industry is usually distributed through households rather than concentrated in premises, which makes the boundary between home and workplace far less distinct than a modern reader assumes.

## What Ordinary People Worried About

Reading the archive for its emotional content rather than its economic content produces a list of concerns that is worth setting out.

Money dominates. Debts owed and owing, payments delayed, prices, business reverses and the difficulty of extracting what one is due fill the correspondence more than any other subject.

Family separation is the second theme. Letters complaining that a traveller has not written, asking when he will return, and reporting what he has missed recur constantly, which is the cost of a commercial economy paid in personal terms.

Illness appears throughout, in reports of one's own condition and anxious enquiries about others, and it is the subject on which the letters are most immediate.

Disputes with relatives occupy a substantial proportion, particularly over inheritance and marriage arrangements, which are the two occasions when family interests collide most sharply.

Reputation matters visibly. Letters defending conduct, complaining of slander and asking for support against accusations show a society where standing was a real asset and its loss a real danger.

Officials appear as a hazard. Complaints about demands, delays and treatment by authorities recur, which fits everything the article on taxation in this series establishes about the fiscal relationship.

Children and their futures appear in arrangements for marriage, apprenticeship and education, and in the frequent reports of their deaths.

The general observation is that the concerns filling these documents are recognizable, and that the recognition is the finding. A society a thousand years removed produced correspondence whose preoccupations a modern reader identifies immediately, which is not what a reader expecting alien medieval mentalities anticipates.

## Money and Prices

How ordinary transactions were conducted is documented in unusual detail, and the mechanics repay explanation.

The gold coin was the unit of account and the store of value, and the article on Fatimid trade explains why its consistent quality made it a preferred currency across the Mediterranean.

Silver and copper handled small transactions, and their relationship to the gold coin fluctuated, which meant everyday prices moved for reasons unconnected to the value of the goods.

Credit was pervasive rather than exceptional. Purchases on account, deferred payments, promissory arrangements and complicated webs of mutual obligation appear throughout the documents, which is what a commercial society with limited coin in circulation requires.

Offsetting was the standard method of settlement in trade. The article on Fatimid trade describes how debts between merchants were cancelled against each other rather than settled by moving bullion, and the same logic operated at smaller scales.

Prices appear constantly and their interpretation is difficult. Converting a medieval price into anything meaningful requires knowing wages, other prices and the coin's condition, and comparative statements are far safer than absolute ones.

What can be said is relative. The documents allow the cost of bread against the cost of cloth against the cost of a rent to be compared, and that structure of relative prices is genuinely informative about how households allocated resources.

Wages appear in some records, particularly for building and craft labour, and they permit rough statements about what a day's work bought.

The general observation is that a commercial economy running largely on credit and offsetting rather than on circulating coin is more sophisticated than a modern reader assumes, and the paperwork it generated is why this city is visible at all.

## The Working Day and the Working Year

The rhythm of ordinary time in this city can be reconstructed from accounts, contracts and the natural calendar the country has always run on.

The day began early and was structured by prayer times for Muslims, which divided it into intervals everyone recognized regardless of affiliation, since the call was audible to all.

Markets opened early and business was conducted through the morning, with the heat of the afternoon reducing activity in the summer months, which is the standard Mediterranean pattern.

The week had a different shape for each community, with Friday, Sunday and Saturday marking the communal day for Muslims, Christians and Jews respectively, so the city never fully stopped and each community's rest day fell on a working day for the others.

That staggering is worth noticing. A commercial city with three communities has activity on every day of the week, and the documents show business being transacted around the pattern.

The year ran on the flood, as it always had. The article on [taxation and land in early Islamic Egypt](/2010/06/01/tax-and-land-early-islamic-egypt/) sets out how the flood determined the agricultural cycle, and a city fed by that cycle experienced the year through the price and availability of grain.

The commercial year ran on the winds. The article on Fatimid trade explains how the monsoon and the Mediterranean sailing season governed when ships left and returned, which meant the arrival of a fleet was an event that reshaped the market.

The religious calendars added their own structure, with the fasting month reshaping the daily rhythm entirely for the Muslim majority, and festivals punctuating the year for everyone.

The general observation is that pre modern urban time was structured by things a modern city has removed: the sun, the flood, the wind and the prayer call, and none of them was optional.

## Children and Education

The documents allow something to be said about childhood and schooling that is unusual for a medieval city.

Schools existed at an elementary level for boys, teaching reading, writing and religious texts, and the practice sheets and exercises in the archive are direct evidence of them.

The teacher was often a paid functionary of a community or a private arrangement between families, and contracts and complaints about teachers appear in the record.

Literacy was consequently more widespread than the usual medieval picture allows, particularly among the commercial population, since a merchant's son needed to read, write and calculate.

The exercises are the most immediate evidence. Alphabets practised repeatedly, copied phrases and beginners' attempts survive because the paper was discarded, and they are the handwriting of children a thousand years ago.

Girls' education is less documented and did occur, since letters written by women exist and some at least were written rather than dictated, though the proportion cannot be established.

Apprenticeship handled vocational training, with contracts placing boys with craftsmen for a term, and such agreements survive.

Orphans appear frequently in community records, since their support and their property were community responsibilities and generated administration.

The general observation is that child mortality shaped everything. Letters mentioning children mention their deaths often, and family structures built around that expectation look different from modern ones in ways the documents make concrete rather than abstract.

## What the Documents Are

Before the findings, a short description of the kinds of document the archive holds, since the type determines what each can support.

Commercial letters are the largest and most studied category. Merchants writing to partners and agents about shipments, prices, market conditions, credit and disputes, in enormous numbers, and they are the foundation of everything known about the trading system.

Legal documents come next. Contracts of sale, lease, partnership and loan; marriage contracts and divorce settlements; court records and depositions; and powers of attorney, all executed before witnesses and drafted to standard forms.

Accounts and lists are the most immediately informative about daily life. Household accounts, charity distribution lists, inventories, and shopping lists, written for a practical purpose and discarded once used.

Private letters between family members are the smallest proportion and the most valuable for social history, since they record relationships rather than transactions.

Community records document the internal governance of the community: appointments, disputes, endowments, communal decisions and correspondence with authorities.

Literary and religious material forms a large part of the whole, since the original prohibition was aimed at exactly this, and it includes texts otherwise lost.

School exercises, scraps, drafts and practice writing round out the collection, and they are evidence about literacy and education that almost nothing else supplies.

The general point is that each type answers different questions. Contracts establish what arrangements were normal; letters establish what people worried about; lists establish what things cost; and using a document for the question it can answer is the whole discipline.

## The City People Actually Lived In

The most common misunderstanding about this period concerns which city is being described, and clearing it up is necessary before anything else.

The walled foundation of 969 was a palace enclosure, closed to the general population for roughly a century, containing the caliph, the court, the administration and the army. The article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo sets out how it worked and why.

The older settlement a short distance to the south was the actual city: the markets, the port, the industries, the housing and the great majority of the population. It had been Egypt's principal city for three centuries before the Fatimids arrived and it continued to be so for most of their rule.

### How did people live in Fatimid Cairo?

Mostly in the older commercial settlement to the south rather than in the walled palace enclosure, in multi storey buildings with apartments let by floor, courtyards and shared facilities. Excavation there has recovered piped water and latrines, and households of varying size occupied everything from single rooms to substantial properties with several storeys.

The storeroom archive comes from that older settlement, which is precisely why it documents ordinary life rather than court affairs, and any account of daily life in this period is really an account of life there.

The shift northward came later and was accelerated by the deliberate burning of the older settlement in 1168, described in the article on the founding of Fustat, after which population moved toward the newer enclosure that had by then opened to ordinary residents.

### How big was Fatimid Cairo?

Nobody knows and the figures in circulation are inferences. The two settlements together formed one of the largest urban areas in the world at the time, comparable with the great cities of the period, and estimates in the hundreds of thousands are common. No count was taken, and capacity calculations from building density are chains of assumption rather than measurements.

The population question deserves the same discipline the article on the Mosque of Amr applies. No census exists, building density estimates rest on assumptions about occupancy, and confident figures conceal several layers of guesswork inside a single number.

What can be said is comparative. Visitors from other regions described the city as exceptionally large and prosperous, the eleventh century Persian traveller's account is emphatic on the point, and Egypt's commercial position supported an urban population far beyond what the local agricultural surplus alone would sustain.

## Housing

The physical settings of ordinary life are unusually well recovered here, because excavation in the older settlement has been productive and because property documents describe buildings in detail.

The buildings were tall. Multi storey structures were normal, with several floors of apartments above ground level premises, which is a response to the property system described in the article on the founding of Fustat: a constrained site with fixed boundaries develops upward.

The apartments were let and sublet, sometimes floor by floor and sometimes room by room, and the property documents record shares, subdivisions and complicated arrangements of ownership and occupancy within a single building.

### What did houses look like in Fatimid Cairo?

Tall and subdivided. Multi storey buildings held apartments above ground level premises, often let floor by floor or room by room, arranged around courtyards providing light and air. Excavation has recovered piped water supply and latrines in some properties, and the documents describe shared entrances, staircases and facilities among unrelated households.

Courtyards provided light and air in dense construction, which is the standard solution in hot climates with narrow streets.

The amenities are the surprise. Excavation has recovered piped water supply and latrine arrangements in properties of the older settlement, which revises the usual assumption about medieval urban sanitation considerably.

Sharing was normal. Staircases, entrances, courtyards and water sources were used by unrelated households in the same building, which produces exactly the kind of dispute that ends up in a court record, and several such records survive.

The furnishing is documented indirectly through marriage inventories, which list what a bride brought and therefore describe what a household contained: bedding, textiles, cooking vessels, storage jars, lamps and personal items.

## The Communities

The mixed population of this city is well attested and the pattern of how the communities lived alongside each other is one of the more interesting findings.

Muslims were the majority, divided between the Sunni bulk of the population and the Ismaili adherents of the regime described in the article on [Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate](/2011/03/01/shia-islam-and-fatimid-caliphate/).

Coptic Christians were a very large minority, and the article on conquest or conversion sets out why the proportion at this date is genuinely uncertain and why any figure is an inference.

Jews were a substantial community with their own institutions, courts and leadership, and it is their storeroom that supplies the documentation.

### How diverse was Fatimid Cairo?

Very. Sunni and Ismaili Muslims, Coptic Christians and a substantial Jewish community lived in the same districts, often in the same buildings, and the documents show them as neighbours, business partners, landlords and tenants. Communities had their own courts and institutions, and their members dealt across the lines constantly in commercial and property matters.

### Did the communities live separately or together?

Together, in the practical sense that matters. The documents show members of different communities in the same buildings, sharing courtyards and entrances, transacting business, letting property to each other and appearing in each other's disputes. Institutions were separate, with each community having its own courts and leadership, and residence was not segregated by any rule.

The distinction between institutional separation and residential mixing is the key point. Each community ran its own courts, appointed its own leaders and handled its own internal affairs, and none of that prevented people living in the same street or the same building.

The commercial mixing is documented in detail because it generated paperwork. Partnerships across communities, agency arrangements, credit and joint ventures appear constantly, and the article on Fatimid trade and the wealth of Egypt treats the commercial system this reflects.

The legal crossing over is well attested as well. The article on Copts under early Islamic rule describes people choosing between available legal forums according to which suited them, and the same behaviour appears here, with community leaders occasionally complaining about it.

The friction was real and episodic rather than structural. Periods of tension appear, particularly during the reign treated in the article on [al-Hakim](/2011/06/01/al-hakim-controversial-caliph/), and the ordinary condition documented in the archive is unremarkable coexistence.

## Work

The occupational structure of the city can be reconstructed from names, contracts and partnership records with unusual precision for a medieval place.

Textiles dominated. Flax growing, spinning, weaving, dyeing, finishing and trading employed enormous numbers, and the article on Fatimid trade explains why textiles rather than spices were the largest industry of the period.

### What jobs did ordinary people do?

Textiles above all, in every stage from flax preparation through spinning, weaving, dyeing and finishing to trade. Beyond that, food trades including baking, milling and oil pressing, building trades, metalwork, leather, glass, pottery, paper, shipping and porterage, plus commerce at every scale and services including medicine, scribal work and teaching.

Food trades were the second great employer, with baking, milling, oil pressing, butchery and the supply chains behind them occupying a large workforce in a city that had to be fed daily.

Building and its trades employed many, in a city of tall structures requiring constant repair.

Craft production covered metalwork, glass, pottery, leather, woodwork and paper, and the article on Fatimid art in this cluster treats the luxury end of that production.

Commerce operated at every scale from the international merchants documented in the archive down to street sellers, and the range within a single occupational label is enormous.

Services included medicine, which is well documented since physicians wrote and their prescriptions survive, scribal work, teaching, and the religious functionaries of every community.

The women's work question is treated in its own section below, since the documents allow more to be said about it than for most medieval cities.

## Markets and Shopping

The commercial life of the city at street level is documented by accounts, lists and the topographical descriptions of later writers drawing on earlier sources.

### Were there markets in Fatimid Cairo?

Many, and organized by trade. Specialized markets grouped sellers of particular goods together, which made comparison easy for buyers and regulation easy for authorities, and the arrangement is recorded in later topographical description drawing on earlier sources. Permanent shops, workshops attached to dwellings and itinerant sellers all operated alongside them.

The specialization by trade is the organizing principle. Sellers of a given commodity clustered, producing named markets for particular goods, which is the standard arrangement in cities of this world and which serves buyers, sellers and the officials supervising them equally.

Regulation existed. An official was responsible for market supervision, weights, measures, quality and fair dealing, and complaints about market conduct appear in the record.

Shopping lists survive, and they are among the most immediate documents in the archive. Somebody wrote down what to buy, the paper was discarded, and it is now evidence about what a household consumed in a given week.

Prices appear constantly in commercial correspondence and accounts, which allows comparative statements about relative costs even where absolute figures resist conversion into anything meaningful.

Credit was normal and pervasive. Purchases on account, deferred payment and complicated webs of obligation appear throughout, which is what one would expect in a commercial society with a chronic shortage of small change.

The article on Fatimid trade treats the long distance commerce that supplied the luxury end of these markets, and what belongs here is the daily transaction: a household buying bread, oil, vegetables and cloth from people it knew.

## Food

What people ate is unusually recoverable here because shopping lists, accounts, marriage inventories and medical texts all bear on it.

Bread was the foundation, as in every Mediterranean society of the period, made from wheat and consumed daily, with the quality of the flour marking social level clearly.

### What did people eat in Fatimid Cairo?

Bread above all, made from wheat and eaten daily, with flour quality marking social level. Alongside it pulses, particularly beans and lentils, vegetables including onions, leeks and greens, fish from the river, dairy products, olive and sesame oil, fruit in season, and meat for those who could afford it. Sugar, spices and imported goods appear in wealthier households.

Pulses were the everyday protein, with beans and lentils appearing constantly, and the dishes built on them have a very long continuity in Egyptian food.

Vegetables from the intensively cultivated land around the city included onions, leeks, greens, gourds and cucumbers, and the river supplied fish, which is the cheap animal protein of a Nile city.

Oil came from olives and from sesame, dairy products appear regularly, and fruit was seasonal and abundant.

Meat was a marker of prosperity rather than a daily item for most households, and its appearance in an account tells you something about the household keeping it.

Sugar is a Fatimid period speciality worth noting, since Egypt produced it and the article on Fatimid trade sets out its importance as an export, and it appears in domestic use as well.

Spices and imported goods reached wealthier tables through exactly the trade routes that made the country rich, which is a nice demonstration of how the commercial economy touched daily life.

The medical texts add a dimension by discussing foods in terms of their properties, which records what was available and how it was thought about.

## Marriage and the Household

Marriage documents are among the most numerous and most informative items in the archive, and they open a view into domestic arrangements that few medieval societies allow.

### How were marriages arranged and recorded?

By contract, negotiated between families and recorded in detail before witnesses. The document specified the payment from the groom's side, listed the bride's trousseau item by item with valuations, and often included conditions the parties had agreed. Those inventories are among the richest sources for household possessions, since they describe in detail what a household actually contained.

The contract was a financial instrument as much as a religious act. It set out obligations, protected the wife's property, specified what she brought and what she would receive in given circumstances, and was enforceable.

The trousseau lists are the historian's gift. Bedding, clothing, textiles, cooking vessels, jewellery, storage containers and furniture appear item by item with valuations, which means the material contents of a household can be described precisely.

Conditions appear in some contracts and they are revealing about negotiation. Stipulations about residence, about the husband not taking another wife, about travel and about the wife's freedom of movement occur, which shows that terms were argued over rather than standard.

Divorce is documented and was not rare, with the financial consequences set out in the original contract, and the records of settlements are correspondingly detailed.

Remarriage appears frequently, particularly for widows, in a society where mortality made widowhood common at every age.

The household that resulted was often extended, with relatives, dependants and sometimes servants or slaves in the same establishment, and the letters show the tensions such arrangements produce as clearly as any modern account.

## Women

The documents allow more to be said about women's lives here than for most medieval cities, and what they show is more varied than expectation.

### What did women do in Fatimid Cairo?

A great deal that the documents record directly. They owned and inherited property, invested capital through agents, worked particularly in textile production, appeared in court over property and marriage, ran households, arranged marriages for their children, travelled, and wrote or dictated letters that survive. The archive includes correspondence in which women handle business and family affairs on their own account.

Property ownership is the best documented aspect. Women inherited, owned, bought, sold and let property, and their transactions generated exactly the paperwork that survives.

Investment appears through agency arrangements, since a woman with capital could place it in a commercial venture through an agent and share the returns, and such arrangements are recorded.

Work concentrated in textiles, particularly spinning, which could be done at home and which supplied income to households at every level, and the trade in the products is documented.

Letters written by or dictated for women survive in numbers, dealing with family matters, business, disputes, requests and complaints, and they are among the most immediate documents in the archive.

Legal action is well attested, with women appearing in court over marriage, property and inheritance, sometimes against relatives.

The constraints were real and this should not be read as a description of equality. Legal capacity differed, movement was governed by social expectation, and the same documents that show women acting also show the limits within which they acted.

The value of the evidence is that it is specific. Instead of generalizations about the position of women, the archive supplies individual cases with names, sums and outcomes, which is a different order of knowledge.

## Festivals and Public Life

The calendar of public occasions in this city was unusually crowded, because several communities' festivals coexisted alongside the dynasty's own and the country's oldest celebration.

### What festivals were celebrated in Fatimid Cairo?

Several calendars at once. The Islamic festivals, the Fatimid dynastic observances commemorating the Prophet's family described in the article on Shia Islam and the Fatimid caliphate, the Nile festival marking the flood, the Coptic seasonal festivals which Muslims also attended, and the Jewish festivals of the community whose records survive. The state distributed food and money on the major occasions.

The Nile festival was the greatest of them and the oldest by far. The ceremonial cutting of the canal when the flood reached the required level was a public celebration presided over by the ruler, attended by enormous crowds, and Egyptian governments had been associating themselves with the flood for three thousand years.

The dynastic observances were the regime's own contribution, with processions and distributions on the days commemorating the Prophet's family, and the article on Fatimid Egypt treats the ceremonial as an instrument of government.

The Coptic seasonal festivals were tolerated and attended across community lines, which is documented and which fits the general pattern of the period.

The distributions mattered materially. Food, money and clothing given out on festival days were a real transfer to the poor, and the connection between the ruler's visibility and material benefit was made deliberately.

Processions were the characteristic form. A city organized around a principal street, as the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo describes, is well suited to them, and the route was part of the occasion.

The everyday public life beyond festivals included the mosque, the church and the synagogue as gathering points, the market as a social space, and the bathhouse, which appears in the documents as a normal part of urban life.

## Water, Waste and Health

The practical infrastructure of the city is better recovered than for most medieval places, and the findings revise standard assumptions.

### How did people get water and dispose of waste?

Better than the usual picture of medieval cities suggests. Excavation in the older settlement has recovered piped water supply within properties and latrine arrangements with drainage, alongside the expected wells and water carriers. Waste disposal used the drains and, for solid refuse, dumps outside the built area whose accumulated layers are now a major archaeological resource.

The piped supply is the most surprising element and it was not universal. Some properties had it and many did not, with water carriers supplying households from the river or from wells, which was an occupation employing many.

The latrines and drainage recovered by excavation are a significant finding, and the article on the founding of Fustat notes that this evidence revises assumptions about medieval urban amenity substantially.

The refuse dumps became a resource in two ways. They were mined for fertilizer in the modern period, which destroyed a great deal, and what remains has supplied enormous quantities of pottery, glass and organic material to archaeology.

Health is documented through medical texts, prescriptions and letters mentioning illness, which together give a picture of the diseases that mattered and how they were treated.

Physicians appear in the archive as practitioners and as writers, and the community included distinguished medical figures whose works circulated widely.

Epidemic disease appears in the record and the article on Fatimid Egypt describes the great famine of the 1060s, which was accompanied by the mortality that famine always brings.

Charity for the sick and the destitute is documented in community records, which brings us to the next section.

## The Poor and the Machinery of Charity

One of the most valuable things the archive preserves is documentation of poverty, which is usually invisible in medieval sources.

### How were the poor supported?

Through organized community charity documented in unusual detail. The archive preserves lists of recipients receiving bread and clothing, lists of contributors, and appeals written by or for people in need. Support came from communal funds, endowed property, festival distributions by the state, and direct appeals to individuals of means.

The recipient lists are extraordinary documents. Names, sometimes with a note of circumstance, recorded because somebody had to administer a distribution, and preserved because the paper was discarded rather than destroyed.

The contributor lists sit alongside them, recording who gave and how much, which allows the charitable economy of a community to be reconstructed from both ends.

The appeals are the most affecting material in the archive. Letters from people in difficulty, written or dictated, describing circumstances and asking for help, and they document illness, widowhood, debt, captivity, business failure and old age.

The categories of need recur across the letters and they are recognizable: the sick, the widowed, the orphaned, the indebted, travellers stranded far from home, and those ransoming captives.

The organization was communal rather than state. Each community ran its own arrangements funded by contributions and endowments, and the state's role was the festival distributions and occasional intervention in crisis.

The scale of need was substantial. The proportion of a medieval urban population living close to destitution was always high, and the lists suggest it here as elsewhere.

The general observation is that poverty is documented only where somebody had an administrative reason to write it down. This archive preserves the paperwork of charity, which is why the poor of this city are visible when the poor of almost every contemporary city are not.

## Clothing and Appearance

Textiles were the great industry of this economy and the largest item in most household inventories, so clothing deserves its own treatment.

### What did people wear?

Layered garments of linen and cotton with wool for warmth and silk for those who could afford it, in a range of colours and qualities that marked status precisely. Marriage inventories list garments individually with valuations, which shows how substantial a proportion of household wealth clothing represented and how carefully quality was distinguished.

The value of clothing is the first surprise. Garments appear in inventories with valuations comparable to furniture and sometimes to property, because cloth was labour intensive and therefore expensive, and a wardrobe was a form of stored wealth.

The materials were principally linen, which Egypt produced in enormous quantity, with cotton increasingly present, wool for warmth and silk at the luxury end, and the article on Fatimid trade sets out the industry behind them.

Colour and quality signalled status with precision, and the vocabulary in the documents distinguishes grades and dyes in ways that indicate a highly developed system of visual social marking.

Distinguishing dress for non Muslims existed as a legal norm, and the article on Copts under early Islamic rule establishes that such rules were enforced episodically rather than continuously, with reissued decrees proving lapses between them.

Jewellery appears constantly in the marriage inventories, and it served the same dual function as clothing: display and stored value that a woman retained.

Second hand clothing was a significant trade, which follows from the value of garments, and the market for it is documented.

The general observation is that in a society where cloth is the largest industry and one of the most valuable manufactured goods, clothing carries an economic weight that a modern reader systematically underestimates.

## Social Structure

The layered society of this city can be described with more confidence than most, because documents survive from every level of it.

### What was the social class system in Fatimid Cairo?

A layered structure without formal legal orders. At the top the court, the commanders and the great merchants; below them substantial merchants, officials, physicians and scholars; then the artisan and shopkeeper majority; then labourers and servants; and the destitute documented by charity lists.

The absence of formal orders is worth noting. This was not a society of legally defined estates, and mobility between levels was possible through commerce, learning or state service, which the documents attest.

The court and military elite sat apart and largely lived apart, in the walled enclosure to the north, which is one of the more striking spatial expressions of social division in any medieval city.

The merchant elite is the best documented group because it generated the most paper, and the article on Fatimid trade describes the long distance commerce that produced its wealth.

The artisan and shopkeeper majority appears through occupational names, partnership records, leases and disputes, and the range within it was wide.

The labouring population is harder to see, since people who neither owned nor transacted generate little documentation, and they appear chiefly in the accounts of those who employed them.

Slavery existed and cut across the structure. Domestic slaves appear in household documents, and the military slaves who formed the army's core belong to the article on Fatimid Egypt, and the article on Fatimid trade notes the commerce in enslaved people as part of the same networks that carried other goods.

The religious communities cut across the class structure rather than aligning with it, so a wealthy Jewish merchant and a poor Jewish labourer belonged to the same community and to different social worlds.

## What Life Was Actually Like

Assembling the preceding sections into a single answer is worth doing, since the question is the one most readers arrive with.

### What was life like in Fatimid Cairo?

Crowded, commercial, mixed and documented. Most people lived in the older settlement south of the palace enclosure, in tall subdivided buildings with shared courtyards, worked in textiles or food or building, shopped in specialized markets, ate bread and pulses, married by detailed contract, and lived alongside neighbours of other faiths.

The texture that the archive adds is the individual specificity. This is not a generalized portrait but a body of named people with recorded circumstances, which is what makes the city unusual as a historical subject.

The prosperity was real and unevenly distributed, as the traveller's account described in the article on Fatimid Egypt makes clear, and the charity lists in the same archive establish the other end of the distribution.

The mobility was considerable. People travelled for trade, for family, for pilgrimage and for opportunity, and the letters that fill the archive exist because the writers and recipients were in different places.

The precariousness should not be understated. Illness, business failure, shipwreck, the death of a provider, a bad flood or a political crisis could destroy a household, and the appeals for charity document exactly those events.

The ordinariness is the finding that surprises most. People worried about money, quarrelled with relatives, worried about children, complained about officials and wrote to each other constantly, which is what the uncurated character of the archive was always going to reveal.

The comparison with what is knowable about other medieval cities makes the point sharply. For most, historians have chronicles, charters and archaeology; for this one they also have the paper people threw away.

## Letters and Family Life

The correspondence in the archive is its most distinctive content, and what it shows about family relationships deserves a section.

### What can letters tell us about family life?

A great deal that no other medieval source supplies. The archive holds letters between spouses, parents and children, siblings and more distant relatives, written or dictated by people at many social levels. They discuss money, illness, marriages, disputes, travel, business and complaint, and their combination of practical content and emotional register is unmatched for the period.

The reason so many exist is mobility. Long distance trade meant merchants were away for months or years, families were separated by travel, and letters were the only means of contact, so an active commercial society generates an enormous correspondence.

The registers vary widely. Some letters are formal and dictated to a professional scribe; others are hurried and personal; and the difference is itself informative about literacy and about how people used writing.

The subjects are relentlessly practical and intermittently intimate. A letter about a shipment ends with news of a child's health; a business instruction includes an instruction to greet relatives; and the mixing of registers is characteristic.

Conflict appears constantly. Disputes over inheritance, marriage arrangements, business shares and household matters occupy a large proportion of the correspondence, which is unsurprising since satisfied people write shorter letters.

Absence is the emotional keynote of much of it. Letters complaining that no reply has come, asking when a traveller will return, and reporting events the recipient has missed recur throughout, and they document the cost of a commercial economy in personal terms.

The general observation is that private correspondence is the rarest and most valuable kind of historical evidence, because it was written without any thought of a later reader and therefore records what people actually said to each other rather than what they wished to record.

## Travel and Movement

Movement within and beyond the city shaped ordinary life more than a modern reader expects, and the documents record it well.

### How did people travel within and beyond the city?

Within the settlements on foot or by donkey through narrow streets, with goods moved by porters and pack animals since wheeled transport suited neither. Beyond, by boat on the river for anything heavy, by camel caravan to the Red Sea ports, and by ship for the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean voyages.

Within the city, foot traffic dominated and donkeys carried people and goods, since the street pattern described in the article on how the Fatimids founded Cairo was not built for wheels.

Porters were a substantial occupation. In a city where goods move on backs and animals, the labour of carrying is a major employer and appears constantly in accounts.

The river was the highway for everything heavy, connecting the city to the Delta, to Alexandria and to Upper Egypt, and the article on the founding of Fustat explains why the site's river position determined its existence.

The desert roads to the Red Sea are treated in the article on Fatimid trade, and what matters here is that people as well as goods used them, including pilgrims travelling to the holy cities.

Pilgrimage was the great regular journey, undertaken by those who could afford it, and the arrangements, costs and hazards appear in the documents.

Commercial travel took merchants away for extended periods, as the correspondence attests, and the article on Fatimid trade explains why a full transit to the Indian Ocean and back could take up to two years.

The general observation is that this was a mobile society whose members thought nothing of journeys that a modern reader would regard as expeditions, and the archive exists because so many of them were somewhere else.

## The Limits of the Evidence

The archive's value depends on understanding what it cannot show, and the limits are specific and important.

### What are the limits of the Geniza evidence?

It is one community's paperwork. The archive documents Jewish life directly and the wider society only where the two touched. It over represents the literate and the commercial, comes from the older settlement rather than the palace city, and is fragmentary and dispersed, so generalizing requires care.

The community bias is the first and largest limit. This is a Jewish community's discarded paper, and while it documents dealings with Muslims and Christians constantly, it records those dealings from one side.

The literacy bias is the second. People who wrote appear; people who did not appear only when somebody wrote about them, which systematically over represents merchants, officials and the educated.

The commercial bias is the third and follows from the second. Transactions generate documents and agricultural labour does not, so the archive tilts toward the trading population.

The geographical limit is the fourth. This is the older settlement, not the palace enclosure and not the countryside, so it documents an urban commercial world rather than Egypt.

The fragmentation is a practical limit. Documents are torn, incomplete and scattered across collections, and reassembling them has been decades of work with more remaining.

The chronological unevenness matters. The archive spans roughly a thousand years and the coverage varies by period, with the Fatimid centuries well represented and others less so.

The general principle is the one this series applies throughout. An exceptional source produces exceptional knowledge about exactly what it documents, and the temptation is always to extend it further than it reaches. Naming the boundary is what makes the material inside it usable.

## Comparing With Earlier Egyptian Evidence

Setting this archive beside the other great documentary find of Egyptian history clarifies both.

The papyri of the Roman period, treated in the article on the papyri of Roman Egypt in this series, survive because dry conditions in provincial towns preserved discarded paper, and they document village and small town life in Middle Egypt.

The storeroom archive survives because a religious prohibition prevented destruction, and it documents urban commercial life in the capital region.

The differences are instructive. The papyri come from many places and are chiefly official and legal; this archive comes from one place and is chiefly private and commercial. The papyri document the countryside; this documents the city.

The similarity is more important. Both are accidental, both are uncurated, and both record what people wrote for immediate purposes rather than for any reader afterward, which is exactly why they are worth more than any deliberate record.

The combination gives Egypt an unusual position in medieval and ancient history. Very few regions have two such bodies of evidence a thousand years apart, and the continuity of certain features across them, the fiscal apparatus, the household arrangements, the agricultural year, is one of the more striking findings this series has traced.

The contrast in what each cannot show is equally useful. The papyri are poor on the capital; this archive is poor on the countryside; and neither covers the elite well, since the elite kept archives that were curated and then destroyed with their regimes.

The general observation is that the history of ordinary life depends almost entirely on accidents of preservation, and that a society's visibility to historians has very little to do with its importance.

## Studying Fatimid Cairo

For a student, this topic organizes around one central idea and a set of concrete findings, and it is an excellent case for understanding how social history is actually done.

Be able to distinguish the palace enclosure from the older commercial settlement and to state that daily life means the second. Be able to explain the storeroom mechanism and why an uncurated archive is worth more than a curated one. Be able to describe the mixed communities as institutionally separate and residentially mixed. Be able to name the main occupations with textiles first. Be able to give the housing pattern as tall, subdivided and surprisingly well serviced. Be able to state the food staples. And be able to list the archive's limits.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Six claims about this subject circulate and each misleads in a specific way.

The first is that daily life in the medieval world is unknowable. For this city it is knowable in remarkable detail, down to named individuals and their household possessions.

The second is that Fatimid Cairo means the walled enclosure. For most of the period the walled enclosure was a closed palace complex and the city was the older settlement to the south.

The third is that medieval cities were uniformly filthy. Excavation here has recovered piped water and latrines, which does not make the city sanitary by modern standards and does complicate the assumption considerably.

The fourth is that the communities lived in segregated quarters. Institutions were separate and residence was not, and the documents show people of different faiths in the same buildings.

The fifth is that the storeroom archive documents the whole city. It documents one community's paperwork and everything else at one remove.

The sixth is that a population figure for the city can be given. No count exists, the estimates rest on assumptions about building occupancy, and the honest statement is that it was among the largest cities in the world without a number attached.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended is that Fatimid Cairo is exceptionally knowable, and that the reason is an accident with a specific mechanism.

The mechanism was a prohibition on destroying writing, applied broadly enough to catch material with no religious content, in a place where paper survives, during centuries of intense commercial activity when a great deal was written.

The result is a body of evidence with the one property historians most want and almost never get: it was not selected. Nobody decided what was worth keeping, which means the sample reflects what people wrote rather than what an institution valued.

What it shows is a city of tall subdivided buildings, mixed neighbourhoods, dominant textile industry, specialized markets, contractual marriages with itemized inventories, organized charity with named recipients, constant travel and constant correspondence.

What it also shows is that the concerns filling ordinary lives had little to do with the political history this cluster otherwise traces. The caliphs, the doctrine, the missionary hierarchy and the rivalry with Baghdad appear in these documents hardly at all, because a merchant writing about flax prices had no reason to mention them.

The qualifications have been stated throughout and they are substantial. One community's archive, biased toward the literate and the commercial, from one settlement, fragmentary and dispersed, covering an uneven span.

What remains is the observation this article opened with. People wrote to each other about money, family, illness and travel; they threw the letters away; a religious rule meant the letters were not destroyed; and a thousand years later a city that would otherwise be a list of monuments and rulers is a place where individuals can be named.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was life like in Fatimid Cairo?

Crowded, commercial, mixed and unusually well documented. Most people lived not in the walled palace enclosure of 969 but in the older settlement to the south, in tall subdivided buildings arranged around courtyards, with apartments let floor by floor and facilities shared among unrelated households. They worked overwhelmingly in textiles, food trades and building, shopped in markets organized by commodity, ate bread and pulses with fish and seasonal vegetables, married by detailed written contract, and lived alongside neighbours of other faiths whose institutions were separate and whose streets and buildings were not.

### Q: How did people live in Fatimid Cairo?

In multi storey buildings that went upward because the site could not go outward, with apartments and single rooms let and sublet, courtyards providing light and air, and staircases, entrances and water sources shared among households that were not related. Excavation in the older settlement has recovered piped water supply within some properties and latrine arrangements with drainage, which revises the usual assumption about medieval urban amenity. Marriage inventories describe what a household contained, item by item: bedding, textiles, cooking vessels, storage jars, lamps and jewellery.

### Q: What did people eat in Fatimid Cairo?

Bread above all, made from wheat and eaten daily, with the quality of the flour marking social level clearly. Alongside it came pulses, especially beans and lentils, which supplied the everyday protein; vegetables from the intensively cultivated land around the city including onions, leeks, greens and gourds; fish from the river as the cheap animal protein; dairy products; olive and sesame oil; and seasonal fruit. Meat marked prosperity rather than appearing daily. Sugar, which Egypt produced and exported, appears in domestic use, and imported spices reached wealthier tables.

### Q: What festivals were celebrated in Fatimid Cairo?

Several calendars ran at once. The Islamic festivals; the Fatimid dynastic observances commemorating the Prophet's family, marked with processions and distributions; the Nile festival celebrating the flood, which was the greatest and oldest of them and which Egyptian rulers had presided over for three thousand years; the Coptic seasonal festivals, which people from other communities also attended; and the Jewish festivals of the community whose records survive. The state distributed food, money and clothing on major occasions, so the connection between the ruler's visibility and material benefit was made deliberately.

### Q: Were there markets in Fatimid Cairo?

Many, organized by trade so that sellers of a given commodity clustered together, producing named markets for particular goods. The arrangement served buyers comparing prices, sellers concentrating custom, and the official responsible for supervising weights, measures and quality. Permanent shops, workshops attached to dwellings and itinerant sellers all operated alongside them. Shopping lists survive in the archive, written to be used and then discarded, and they record what an individual household bought in a given week down to the specific items.

### Q: How diverse was Fatimid Cairo?

Very. Sunni and Ismaili Muslims, Coptic Christians and a substantial Jewish community occupied the same districts and frequently the same buildings. The documents show them as neighbours, landlords, tenants, business partners and litigants, with commercial partnerships across community lines appearing constantly and property let freely between them. Each community maintained its own courts, its own leadership and its own internal governance, and that institutional separation coexisted with thorough residential and commercial mixing at every level of the city. Friction appears episodically in the record, particularly during one severe reign in the early eleventh century, and the ordinary condition the archive documents across the centuries is an unremarkable coexistence that nobody found worth commenting on.

### Q: What was the social class system in Fatimid Cairo?

A layered structure without formal legal orders or fixed estates. At the top sat the court, the military commanders and the greatest merchants; below them substantial merchants, senior officials, physicians and scholars; then the artisan and shopkeeper majority; then labourers, porters and servants; and at the bottom the destitute who appear by name on charity distribution lists. Slavery existed as a legally distinct status cutting across the whole structure. Mobility was possible through commerce, learning or state service, and the religious communities cut across the levels rather than aligning with them.

### Q: How big was Fatimid Cairo?

Nobody knows, and every figure in circulation is an inference rather than a count. The two settlements together formed one of the largest urban areas in the world at the time, comparable with the great cities of the period, and estimates in the hundreds of thousands are common. Those estimates rest on assumptions about building density and occupancy that cannot be checked. What can be said with confidence is comparative: visitors from other regions described the city as exceptionally large and prosperous, and Egypt's commercial position supported an urban population far beyond local agricultural capacity.

### Q: What is the Cairo Geniza?

A storeroom attached to a synagogue in the older settlement, into which discarded written material was placed rather than destroyed because writing might carry a sacred name. The rule was applied broadly, so the accumulation includes documents with no religious content at all: letters, contracts, accounts, court records, marriage documents, school exercises and shopping lists. Hundreds of thousands of fragments accumulated across roughly a thousand years. Removed in the nineteenth century and dispersed among collections in several countries, it is the richest source for ordinary medieval life anywhere.

### Q: What did houses look like in Fatimid Cairo?

Tall and heavily subdivided. Multi storey buildings held apartments above ground level premises, with the height a response to a constrained site that could not expand outward. Apartments were let and sublet floor by floor or room by room, and property documents record shares, subdivisions and complicated overlapping arrangements within a single structure. Courtyards supplied light and air in dense construction with narrow streets. Excavation has recovered piped water and latrines in some properties, and unrelated households shared staircases, entrances and water sources, which generated disputes recorded in court documents.

### Q: How were marriages arranged and recorded?

By written contract, negotiated between families and executed before witnesses, functioning as a financial instrument as much as a religious act. The document specified the payment from the groom's side, protected the wife's property, and listed her trousseau item by item with valuations, which is why these inventories are among the richest sources for household possessions anywhere in the medieval world. Some contracts include negotiated conditions about residence, travel or the husband not taking another wife, which shows terms were argued rather than standard. Divorce was not rare and its consequences were set out in advance.

### Q: What did women do in Fatimid Cairo?

Far more than expectation suggests, and the documents record it directly. They owned, inherited, bought, sold and let property; invested capital in commercial ventures through agents; worked particularly in textile production, especially spinning, which supplied household income at every social level; appeared in court over marriage, property and inheritance, sometimes against relatives; arranged their children's marriages; travelled; and wrote or dictated letters that survive in numbers. The constraints were real and this is not a portrait of equality, and the same documents that show women acting also record the limits within which they acted.

### Q: How did people get water and dispose of waste?

Better than the standard picture of medieval cities suggests. Excavation in the older settlement has recovered piped water supply within properties and latrine arrangements with drainage, though these were not universal, and many households relied on wells and on water carriers who fetched supply from the river as a regular occupation. Waste went into drains, and solid refuse went to dumps outside the built area whose accumulated layers were later mined for fertilizer, destroying a great deal, and whose remains have supplied enormous quantities of pottery, glass and organic material to archaeology.

### Q: What jobs did ordinary people do?

Textiles above all, at every stage from flax preparation through spinning, weaving, dyeing and finishing to trade, employing enormous numbers. Beyond that: food trades including baking, milling, oil pressing and butchery, which a city needing daily feeding requires in quantity; building trades in a city of tall structures needing constant repair; craft production in metal, glass, pottery, leather, wood and paper; shipping and porterage, since goods moved on backs and animals; commerce at every scale from international merchants down to street sellers; and services including medicine, scribal work and teaching.

### Q: How were the poor supported?

Through organized communal charity documented in unusual detail. The archive preserves lists of recipients receiving bread and clothing with names and sometimes circumstances, lists of contributors recording who gave and how much, and appeals written or dictated by people in need. The recurring categories are recognizable: the sick, the widowed, the orphaned, the indebted, travellers stranded far from home, and those raising ransoms for captives. Funding came from communal collections and endowed property, supplemented by the state's festival distributions. Each community ran its own arrangements rather than relying on government provision.

### Q: What can letters tell us about family life?

More than any other medieval source. The archive holds correspondence between spouses, parents and children, siblings and distant relatives, written or dictated by people at many social levels, and it exists in such quantity because a commercial society kept its members constantly apart. The letters mix registers characteristically: a business instruction ends with news of a child's illness, a shipment query includes greetings to relatives. Conflict occupies a large proportion, since satisfied people write shorter letters, and absence is the emotional keynote, with complaints about unanswered letters recurring throughout.

### Q: Did the communities live separately or together?

Together in every practical sense. The documents show members of different communities occupying the same buildings, sharing courtyards and entrances, letting property to one another, forming commercial partnerships, and appearing in each other's disputes. What was separate was institutional: each community maintained its own courts, leadership and internal governance, and community leaders occasionally complained about members taking cases to another community's forum when the outcome suited them better. There was no rule segregating residence, and the pattern of mixed neighbourhoods is well attested throughout the archive.

### Q: What did people wear?

Layered garments of linen, which Egypt produced in enormous quantity, with cotton increasingly present, wool for warmth and silk at the luxury end. The economic weight of clothing is the fact a modern reader most underestimates: garments appear in marriage inventories with valuations comparable to furniture, because cloth was labour intensive and a wardrobe was stored wealth, which is also why a substantial second hand trade existed. Colour and quality signalled status with precision, and the documents distinguish grades and dyes in detail. Jewellery served the same dual purpose of display and retained value.

### Q: How did people travel within and beyond the city?

Within the settlements on foot or by donkey through narrow streets built for neither wheels nor wide loads, with porters and pack animals carrying goods, which made portering a substantial occupation. Beyond, the river was the highway for anything heavy, connecting the city to the Delta, Alexandria and Upper Egypt. Camel caravans crossed the deserts to the Red Sea ports, carrying pilgrims as well as goods, and ships made the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean voyages the merchants' letters document. A full commercial transit east and back could take up to two years.

### Q: What are the limits of the Geniza evidence?

It is one community's discarded paperwork. The archive documents Jewish life directly and the wider society only where the two touched, which is frequently but not always, and always from one side. It over represents the literate, since people who did not write appear only when others wrote about them, and the commercial, since transactions generate documents while agricultural labour does not. It comes from the older settlement rather than the palace enclosure or the countryside. It is fragmentary, dispersed across collections, and covers roughly a thousand years unevenly.
