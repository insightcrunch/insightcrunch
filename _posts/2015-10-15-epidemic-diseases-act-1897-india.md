In the first week of February 1897, with bubonic plague spreading through Bombay and the colonial government under pressure to show the world that Indian ports were safe for trade, the Governor General's Council passed the Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 in a matter of days. The statute it produced, Act 3 of 1897, ran to four operative sections. It defined almost nothing, created no institution, set no standard for the measures it authorised, and prescribed no limit on how long those measures could last. One hundred and twenty three years later, that same four-section statute became the legal instrument through which Indian states issued the epidemic regulations of the COVID-19 pandemic, while the national lockdown orders of March 2020 were made under a different statute altogether. The history of the Act in use is therefore a double record. It is the record of a text so open that governments of every era could pour their own emergency into it, and it is also the record of everything the text refused to supply: no criteria for action, no reasons to be recorded, no hearing, no compensation, no sunset and no review. The powers were there and the limits were not, and the pandemic years tested both halves of that sentence harder than any previous outbreak had done.

![Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 and its use in epidemic administration - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-97.webp)

This article studies the Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 as an implementation record rather than as a text alone. The text matters, and the four sections are read closely below, because everything that happened in 2020 and 2021 was built on their exact words. But the framework of the study is what governments did with those words across a century and a quarter: the plague searches of Poona in 1897, the plague at Surat in 1994, the influenza orders at Pune in 2009, the scattered dengue and cholera invocations by state and district authorities, the careful dual-track legality of March 2020, the prosecutions that followed under a borrowed penal provision, the litigation in which constitutional courts supplied from Article 21 the protections the statute omitted, and the single amendment of 2020, which protected healthcare workers and left the structure of the Act untouched. Through that record runs a genuine argument that deserves a fair hearing. One side holds that breadth is the correct design for an emergency statute, because no legislature can foresee the pathogen, the route of transmission or the measure that will answer it, and a short enabling Act that trusts the executive is a feature rather than a defect. The other side answers that breadth without criteria, reasons, time limits, review or entitlement is not flexibility but unconstrained discretion, and that the pandemic record shows the cost of that discretion in prosecutions, in uncompensated burdens and in rights protected only because courts intervened from outside the statute. The federal difficulty is also real, and it explains much of the failure to replace the Act: public health belongs to the states, and a national replacement must either win state consent under Article 252 of the Constitution or rest on the narrower inter-state disease entry in the Concurrent List. The article takes the record in order and returns to the argument at the end.

## The Epidemic Diseases Act 1897, Section by Section

The statute that governs epidemic power in India is short enough to be read in minutes, and its brevity is the first fact of its implementation history. The Act as enacted contained a short title provision and four operative sections. Section 1 gives the short title and the extent. Section 2 confers the state power. Section 2A, inserted by Act 38 of 1920, confers the parallel central power over vessels and, after 2020, over other modes of transport. Section 3 supplies the penalty by incorporation. Section 4 protects officials acting in good faith. Everything else that a reader might expect an epidemic statute to contain, from a definition of epidemic to a standard for quarantine, is absent, and the absences are not oversights scattered through a long text. They are the whole design.

Section 2 is the engine. Its first sub-section provides that when the state government is satisfied that the state or any part of it is visited by, or threatened with, an outbreak of any dangerous epidemic disease, and is of the opinion that the ordinary provisions of the law for the time being in force are insufficient for the purpose, the government may take, or require or empower any person to take, such measures as it deems necessary, and may by public notice prescribe such temporary regulations to be observed by the public or by any person or class of persons as it shall deem necessary to prevent the outbreak or spread of the disease. Three features of that sentence decided most of what followed. The trigger is satisfaction: the government must be satisfied of an outbreak or a threat of one, but the statute names no evidence, no threshold of incidence, no expert body whose advice must precede the satisfaction and no statement of reasons that must accompany it. The insufficiency condition is likewise self-judging, since the government that wishes to act is the government that decides whether ordinary law is insufficient. And the operative verbs are as wide as language allows: the government may take such measures as it deems necessary, a formulation that hands the choice of measure, its intensity, its territorial reach and its duration to the same authority that declared the occasion for it. The second sub-section illustrates without limiting. It provides, in particular and without prejudice to the generality of the power, for the inspection of persons travelling by railway or otherwise, and the segregation, in hospital, temporary accommodation or otherwise, of persons suspected by the inspecting officer of being infected. The same sub-section deals with the expenses of measures taken, providing that the state government may determine in what manner and by whom any expenses incurred, including compensation to be paid if any, shall be defrayed. That parenthesis, compensation if any, is the Act's entire engagement with the losses its measures impose: compensation exists in the text only as a possible head of expense that the government may choose to defray, never as a right of the person segregated, the trader closed or the traveller detained.

### What does Section 2 of the Epidemic Diseases Act authorise?

Section 2 authorises a state government that is satisfied of an outbreak, or a threat of an outbreak, of a dangerous epidemic disease to take any measures it deems necessary, to issue temporary regulations binding the public, and to inspect travellers and segregate suspected persons, without statutory criteria, stated reasons or any time limit.

The words reward close attention because implementation ran along their grain. Dangerous epidemic disease is nowhere defined, so the identification of the disease is itself an executive act: a government notifies that a disease is dangerous and epidemic, and the notification is the law's trigger. Temporary regulation is likewise undefined, and in practice the word temporary carried no legal weight, since nothing in the section fixes a period after which a regulation lapses or must be renewed. The regulations bind the public at large or any class of persons, so a regulation could be addressed to all residents of a district, to all arrivals at a railway station, to all traders in a market or to all persons above an age, on the strength of the same single clause. The inspecting officer's suspicion is the threshold for segregation, and the section does not say that the suspicion must rest on symptoms recorded, on a test, on contact with a confirmed case or on any other named ground. A person segregated has, under the Act itself, no right to be told the basis of the suspicion, no right to a hearing, no maximum period of segregation and no right to compensation for the days lost. Each of those propositions describes not a gap that administrators discovered in 2020 but the ordinary meaning of a text that has read the same way since 1897, through the plague era, through independence and through every invocation in between.

One procedural word in Section 2 deserves its own attention, because it is the Act's only procedural discipline: regulations are prescribed by public notice. The notice requirement does real work in the scheme, since the borrowed penalty in Section 3, through Section 188 of the Penal Code, presupposes an order duly promulgated, and a regulation never published cannot easily found a prosecution. But public notice is where the procedure begins and ends. The Act does not require the notice to state the satisfaction on which it rests, the material supporting it, the period for which the regulation will run, or the officer to whom a burdened person may apply. In the practice of 2020 the notices were gazette notifications and government orders of very uneven completeness, some reciting the satisfaction in a formulaic line and others reciting nothing at all, and the variation mattered later in court, where the promulgation and content of the underlying order became the ground on which prosecutions stood or fell. A single phrase, by public notice, thus carried the entire procedural weight of the most consequential subordinate legislation in independent India's history, and it carried that weight exactly as well as the administrators of each state chose to make it carry.

Section 2A gives the central government the corresponding power at the border. As inserted in 1920, it empowered the Centre, when satisfied of an outbreak or threat, to take measures with respect to any ship or vessel leaving or arriving at any port, including inspection of the vessel and detention of any person intending to sail or arriving. The genealogy of the provision is the genealogy of quarantine itself: the imperial government wanted central control over the maritime gateways through which plague and cholera travelled, while the interior belonged to the provinces. For a century the provision slept, because international health practice moved to the World Health Organization's International Health Regulations and to port health organisations working under other rules. The insertion of 1920 belongs to its own moment of alarm: the influenza pandemic of 1918 had demonstrated, at a cost in Indian lives estimated in the millions, that disease arrived by ship and train faster than provincial law could convene itself, and the imperial legislature's answer was to arm the centre at the point of arrival while leaving the interior to the provinces, a division of labour the Constitution would later constitutionalise in Entry 81's inter-state quarantine and Entry 29's inter-state disease bridge. The provision's century of sleep is therefore not evidence of its uselessness but of the system's habits: between emergencies, the border power was exercised, insofar as it was exercised at all, through the administrative machinery of port health and quarantine rules, and the statutory power waited, as the whole Act waited, for an emergency large enough to make officials reach for the text again. In 2020 the provision was rewritten, and its expansion belongs to the amendment discussed later in this article; the point here is structural. The Act divides epidemic power the way the Constitution would later divide it, between a state power over the population within the territory and a central power over movement across the boundary, and it leaves the space between those two powers, the movement of people and disease across state lines within the country, to be governed by neither text directly.

Section 3 is the whole of the Act's penal law, and it is borrowed. It provides that any person disobeying any regulation or order made under the Act shall be deemed to have committed an offence punishable under Section 188 of the Indian Penal Code. The Act therefore creates no offence of its own, fixes no penalty of its own and describes no prohibited conduct of its own. The conduct is whatever the regulation of the moment forbids, and the punishment is whatever the incorporated provision carries. Section 188 of the Penal Code punishes disobedience to an order duly promulgated by a public servant lawfully empowered to promulgate it, and it grades the punishment by consequence: disobedience simpliciter at the lower grade, and disobedience that causes or tends to cause danger to human life, health or safety, or a riot or affray, at the higher grade. Two doctrines grew around that incorporation and mattered in every prosecution era. First, because the offence lives in Section 188, its ingredients must be proved as Section 188 requires: a lawful order, duly promulgated, by a public servant empowered to make it, knowledge of the order in the accused, and disobedience with the tendency the section describes. The Madras High Court's reading of the provision in C. Jagannatha Chariar, reported in AIR 1959 Mad 89, insisted on those ingredients, and courts in the pandemic years quashed prosecution after prosecution in which the complaint recited the regulation but established no promulgation or no knowledge. Second, prosecution under Section 188 could be set in motion only on the complaint of the public servant whose order was disobeyed, or of a superior, under the procedural bar in Section 195 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, so police first information reports that bypassed that complaint requirement were vulnerable from the start. The 1897 Act's penalty was thus never a simple sword. It was a procedural obstacle course that rewarded careful paperwork and undid careless enforcement, which is one reason the enforcement record of 2020 reads so unevenly.

Section 4 completes the architecture with a shield. It provides that no suit or other legal proceeding shall lie against any person for anything done or in good faith intended to be done under the Act. The protection is conditional in form, since it covers good faith action, but it is cast against a background in which the Act requires no reasons and records no criteria, so the record from which bad faith might be proved is a record the statute never required anyone to create. Read together, the four operative sections form a closed system. The executive decides that an epidemic threatens, decides what measures the threat requires, prosecutes disobedience through a borrowed offence, and answers for its choices behind a good faith shield, while the person inspected, segregated, closed down or prosecuted holds no statutory right at any point in the sequence. The wider map of Indian health legislation, within which this small statute sits alongside hospital, drug, professional and environmental law, is drawn in the series guide to [health laws in India](/2015/06/01/health-laws-india-complete-guide/), and the contrast is instructive: the neighbouring statutes create councils, licences, standards and appeals, while the epidemic statute creates only power. Everything in the implementation record that follows flows from that asymmetry.

Two doctrines from the general law deserve recording here, because they mark the outer boundary of what courts could have done with the Act even in a challenge squarely aimed at it. The first concerns satisfaction. Indian administrative law has never treated a statutory formula of satisfaction as wholly beyond review: since the Supreme Court's early company law jurisprudence, of which Barium Chemicals v. Company Law Board is the standard citation, a satisfaction founded on no material, on irrelevant material or in mala fides is reviewable, because the satisfaction must be the satisfaction of a reasonable authority addressing itself to the statutory question. Section 2's satisfaction is therefore not, in strict doctrine, a lawless zone; it is a zone in which review is possible in principle and difficult in practice, because the Act requires no record from which the material could be tested and because courts in an emergency extend to epidemic judgment a deference they would not extend to a licensing decision. The second doctrine concerns the good faith shield. Protection clauses of the Section 4 type are read by the courts as they are written, as covering acts done in good faith under colour of the statute and nothing beyond it, so that an act wholly outside the Act, or done with demonstrated malice, stands unprotected. The doctrines are real, and both were available throughout the pandemic. That neither was ever brought to bear on the Act itself, in a reported judgment reorganising its meaning, tells the student of the Act something important about the difference between the law in the reports and the law in the district: the doctrines existed, the litigation that would have activated them was directed elsewhere, and the text passed through its greatest test unexamined by the only institutions that could have examined it.

## Born in Plague: The First Implementation, 1896 to 1897

The Act was not drafted in calm. Plague reached Bombay in 1896, almost certainly through the maritime trade the port lived by, and by the beginning of 1897 the city was losing residents to flight as fast as to disease. The colonial state faced two pressures at once. European trading nations threatened quarantine against Indian shipping and goods unless the government of India could show coercive sanitary powers, and the administration in Bombay was already taking improvised measures, searches, disinfection, segregation and the destruction of property, whose legal footing was doubtful. The Epidemic Diseases Bill answered both pressures. Introduced and passed within days in February 1897, receiving assent on 4 February 1897, it regularised retrospectively the kind of action officials were already taking and armed them prospectively with the widest formulation the draftsman could devise. The speed was itself a policy choice. A longer bill, with definitions, standards and procedural safeguards, would have taken a season the government believed it did not have, and the four-section enabling Act that resulted bears the mark of that haste in every line: it is a statute written to be used at once, by officials who were trusted entirely, against a disease that was understood imperfectly, in cities whose residents had no vote and no court to which the Act gave them anything to appeal to.

The first implementation showed what such a text does in frightened hands. In Poona, where plague was severe, the administration under the plague committee chaired by W. C. Rand conducted house to house searches for the sick, removed patients to segregation camps and hospitals, disinfected and in some cases destroyed belongings, and enforced measures through soldiers in ways that residents experienced as humiliation and violation, including the handling of women and the entry into homes and religious spaces. The historian David Arnold's work on the plague years documents how the campaign in Poona became the most notorious collision of the epidemic state with its subjects: the measures had a sanitary logic, and the discretion that authorised them had no legal brakes, no obligation to explain, no compensation for what was destroyed and no forum for complaint that the statute itself provided. In June 1897 Rand and a companion officer were assassinated by the Chapekar brothers, an act of political violence that contemporaries and historians alike connected to the resentments the plague administration had accumulated. The point for the implementation record is not the assassination itself but the mechanism it exposed. A statute that gives everything to satisfaction and nothing to standards will be administered, in a panic, at the maximum of its breadth, and the maximum of its breadth is very wide indeed. The generation that drafted the Act had weighed that risk and accepted it, because the constituency that bore the risk had no institutional voice. The text survived the empire that wrote it because independent India, facing its own epidemics, found the same breadth convenient, and convenience is the most durable argument a statute can have.

The Act's first decade also set the template for its geography. Plague was not a Bombay disease; it travelled the railway network into the Deccan, Gujarat, Karachi, Calcutta and, most destructively in the new century, the Punjab, where plague operations under the Act ran for years through thousands of villages. In the Punjab countryside the repertoire repeated at village scale: evacuation of infected villages into camps, inspection and disinfection of houses, inoculation drives that villagers variously welcomed, evaded or resisted, and the constant negotiation between district officers armed with the Act's satisfaction and communities who experienced the measures as a second calamity laid over the first. All of this unfolded, in 1896 and 1897 and again in the famine years that bracketed the century, alongside scarcity, so that epidemic administration and famine administration, the two coercive welfares of the colonial state, shared officers, camps and legal habits. The historiography of the period, Arnold's work among it, draws the through line plainly: the plague state taught Indian administration that epidemic power was ordinary power, to be exercised on satisfaction and defended on good faith, and the lesson was so well learned that no subsequent generation thought to ask the statute for more. When the Act was invoked in 2020 by officers who had never heard of the Poona plague committee, they worked, unknowingly, in a groove cut in 1897, which is what it means for a statute to have an implementation tradition rather than merely a text.

Between the plague years and independence the Act receded into the background of the statute book, used as occasion demanded by provincial governments during the influenza pandemic of 1918 and in cholera and smallpox years, amended once in 1920 to add the central power over shipping in Section 2A, and otherwise left alone. Its twentieth century pattern was set by then: long dormancy, sudden centrality, and a return to dormancy when the emergency passed, with no legislative reconsideration in between. Dormancy is a kind of implementation too. A statute that is never reviewed is never repaired, and the India that inherited the Act in 1947 inherited it unrepaired, its colonial assumptions about executive satisfaction and official good faith intact beneath a democratic Constitution that guaranteed life, liberty and equality in language the Act's drafters would not have recognised.

The plague years also supply the Act's first lesson in the difference between legal power and epidemic control. Alongside the searches and camps, the Bombay response ran the scientific work of Waldemar Haffkine, whose anti plague inoculation was developed in Bombay and administered from 1897 onward to volunteers in their tens of thousands, an intervention with a measurable effect on mortality that no section of the Act authorised, required or even contemplated. The statute armed the coercive half of the response and was silent about the protective half, and the pattern is worth naming because it recurs in every later era: the law organised compulsion, while protection, inoculation in 1897, hospital capacity in 1994, oxygen in 2021, was organised, when it was organised, by administration and science outside the statute. The deadliest event in the Act's history made the same point at continental scale. In the influenza pandemic of 1918, which killed in India on a scale estimated in the millions, provincial governments worked through the Act and the ordinary machinery of district administration, and the record of that catastrophe, of overwhelmed cities, absent staff and a virus that moved faster than any regulation, stands as the limiting case of the enabling design: when the emergency is total, the breadth of the power ceases to be the binding constraint, and capacity, which the Act does not create, becomes the whole question. No amendment followed 1918 either. The Act emerged from the worst mortality event in Indian history exactly as it had entered it, four sections and a maritime clause, its reputation as a sufficient instrument somehow enhanced by the scale of the events it had not visibly failed to govern.

## A Century of Quiet Invocations

### How often has the Epidemic Diseases Act actually been used?

The Act has been used in every generation since 1897: in the plague campaigns of its first years, by provincial governments during the influenza pandemic of 1918, at Surat in 1994, at Pune in 2009, and by states and districts against dengue, malaria and cholera before the COVID-19 pandemic made it famous.

The best documented of the modern invocations before 2020 is Surat. In September 1994, patients with pneumonic plague were hospitalised in Surat, the textile city in Gujarat, with cases concentrated in the Ved Road and Katargam localities after flooding of the Tapti river; the first patients were hospitalised on 19 September 1994. The Gujarat administration's first instinct was denial that the disease was plague at all, a delay the technical record treats as part of the disaster, and when the outbreak could no longer be denied the state reached for the statute it had: the government invoked the Epidemic Diseases Act, and the powers taken under it were used to legitimise the restriction of rail and air traffic out of the affected city, the quarantine of plague suspects in hospital wards and house to house checks for the sick. A contemporary magazine account of those days records special riot police deployed around hospitals and pharmacies as crowds demanded tetracycline, and records the government's invocation of the 1897 Act after the initial denials. The scholarly account of the outbreak in the literature of global and local health confirms the same legal spine: it was the 1897 Act whose use legitimised the traffic restrictions, the quarantining and the searches. The Centre's Technical Advisory Committee, appointed in October 1994, confirmed the causative agent as Yersinia pestis, the plague bacillus. A study of the outbreak records 1,088 suspected cases and 52 deaths in Surat city, and records that about two lakh people, roughly a third of the city's population, fled by 29 September 1994 despite the legal powers arrayed against movement. The aftermath ran on the same rails as the outbreak itself. The city's textile and diamond trades, the economic base of Surat, stopped under the closure and flight regime, and the municipal administration whose sanitation failures were widely blamed for the conditions in which plague took hold was shaken up by the state government in the outbreak's wake, with the municipal commissioner removed as accountability was assigned. The episode entered the administrative memory of India as a parable with two morals that pull against each other, and both morals are about the Act. The first moral is the administrator's: when the emergency came, the legal power was instantly available, unquestioned and sufficient, and no one in the Gujarat government of 1994 had to wait for a legislature. The second moral is the sanitarian's: the outbreak was a failure of the unglamorous law that prevents epidemics, of water, drainage, waste and municipal capacity, fields in which the Epidemic Diseases Act has never had a word to say, because it governs the response to disease and not the conditions that breed it. Surat was controlled in the end by the classic methods, antibiotics, sanitation drives, surveillance and the restoration of municipal function, under the warrant of the Act but by means the Act does not describe. A statute can be the warrant for a recovery it did nothing to equip, and Surat is the case study. Surat therefore stands in the record for two propositions at once. It shows the Act still functioning, ninety seven years after enactment, as the legal warrant for the full repertoire of movement restriction, quarantine and search. And it shows the limits of legal warrant as an instrument of public confidence: the powers did not prevent the flight, did not substitute for timely candour about the diagnosis, and did not equip the city with the laboratory and hospital capacity whose absence the outbreak exposed. Each of those figures belongs to its source and its period; the case counts of Surat are the counts of 1994, recorded in the study of that outbreak, and they are not added here to the counts of any later epidemic, because epidemics are not a single arithmetic.

The influenza pandemic of 2009 gave the Act its rehearsal for 2020, on a smaller stage. India's first death from the H1N1 influenza was recorded at Pune on 3 August 2009, when a fourteen year old schoolgirl died in a private hospital in the city after delayed diagnosis and treatment. The Maharashtra government responded by invoking the Epidemic Diseases Act in Pune and Satara districts, a use of the Act that contemporary reporting recorded at the time: Section 2 powers were used to establish screening centres, swine flu was declared a notifiable disease within the invoked area, and the district administration acquired the legal authority to direct suspected cases into designated facilities. A peer reviewed account of the Pune response, published in the International Journal of Health Policy and Management, confirms that the city's pandemic response plan relied on powers under the Epidemic Act of 1897, and records the administrators' own verdict on their instrument: they found the Act old and lacking guidelines, a Victorian frame stretched over a modern city's hospitals, schools and private practitioners. The Pune response also ran the full municipal repertoire that 2020 would nationalise: schools and colleges in the city were closed for a period in August 2009 as anxiety peaked, public gatherings were discouraged and then regulated, private hospitals were pressed into a reporting and treatment discipline they had not contracted for, and the district administration found itself managing, alongside the medicine, a crisis of parental fear and media tempo that no section of the Act addressed. The Pune experience is valuable precisely because it was small. It produced, a decade before the national emergency, the same findings the national emergency would produce: the power to act was ample, the guidance for acting was absent, the interface with private hospitals had to be improvised, and the compensation and communication questions were answered administratively or not at all. Nothing was amended in consequence. The decade between Pune and the pandemic was the decade in which the replacement bills also failed, and the two facts belong in the same paragraph of the record.

Below the headline outbreaks, the Act lived a quieter district life. Legal commentary on the Act's use records that in 2015 the administration at Chandigarh implemented the Act for malaria and dengue control, issuing notices and imposing challans of five hundred rupees on premises where mosquito breeding was found, a use that converted Section 2 into a municipal enforcement tool against householders. The same commentary records that in 2018 the district collector of Vadodara issued a notification under the Act declaring the village of Khedkarmsiya in Waghodia taluka cholera affected, after thirty one persons reported symptoms, bringing the village within the Act's regime of measures. These invocations are minor in scale and major in significance for the study of the text. They show the satisfaction trigger operating at district level on diseases that are endemic rather than novel, and they show temporary regulations functioning as a general public health ordinance power, available for mosquito breeding and village water contamination alike. A statute drawn for plague had become, by accumulation of practice, the all purpose epidemic clause of Indian administration, invoked by whoever held local power when a disease made local news. That breadth of practice is the background against which the events of March 2020 must be read: when the pandemic arrived, no administrator in India had to be told that the 1897 Act existed, because many of them had already used it.

## March 2020: Two Legal Tracks

The tracks were laid on ground prepared by six weeks of prelude. India's first confirmed COVID-19 case was recorded in Kerala on 30 January 2020, the day the World Health Organization declared the outbreak a public health emergency of international concern, and through January and February the Union's response ran through the port and airport health machinery, screening passengers arriving from affected countries under the standing public health rules for aircraft and ports rather than through any invocation of the Epidemic Diseases Act. Kerala, which had met the first cases and had managed a Nipah virus outbreak in 2018, traced, isolated and quarantined under state authority and administrative direction while the statute book still slept. The prelude matters because it shows the default settings of the system: screening at the boundary by executive machinery, case management by the state health administration, and the 1897 Act untouched until the scale of the threat changed the calculation in the second week of March.

The legal architecture of India's pandemic response was built in thirteen days, and its most important feature is that it was built twice. On 11 March 2020, a meeting chaired by the Cabinet Secretary decided, as the Press Trust of India reported the following day, that all states and union territories should be advised by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare to invoke Section 2 of the Epidemic Diseases Act, so that the advisories issued by the Union and state governments would be enforceable against the public. That decision set the first track: epidemic regulations made by state governments under the 1897 Act, carrying the Section 3 penalty, covering the ground of disease control as such: notification, inspection, segregation, isolation, the closure of gatherings and premises, the duties of travellers and the management of the sick. State after state laid that track in the second and third weeks of March. Maharashtra's Public Health Department issued its notification of 13 March 2020 invoking Section 2 and declaring a public health emergency, and the Maharashtra COVID-19 Regulations followed on 14 March 2020. Delhi issued the Delhi Epidemic Diseases, COVID-19 Regulations, 2020, Karnataka issued its own COVID-19 regulations under the Act in the same period, and parallel instruments multiplied across the states, each with the same skeleton of definitions, duties and prohibitions and each deriving its force from the four sections of 1897.

The second track was laid on 24 March 2020, and it was laid under a different statute. The national lockdown, announced that evening to run for twenty one days, rested on orders under the Disaster Management Act, 2005: the National Disaster Management Authority's order of 24 March 2020 issued under Section 6(2)(i) of that Act, directing ministries, states and union territories to take effective measures to prevent the spread of COVID-19, and the Ministry of Home Affairs' lockdown order of the same date issued under Section 10(2)(l), the executive committee provision, which carried the detailed guidelines closing offices, transport, commerce and gatherings across the country. The distinction between the tracks is not pedantry, and this article maintains it throughout, because the two instruments did different work and answered to different logics. The Epidemic Diseases Act regulations were disease instruments: they presupposed infection, suspicion and contact, and they spoke to the sick, the exposed and those who managed them. The Disaster Management Act orders were population instruments: they presupposed a disaster to be managed by administration, and they spoke to everyone, sick and well alike, restricting movement, work and assembly as such. The lockdown's prohibitions, the exemptions for essential services, the passes, the sealing of districts and the criminal enforcement that followed ran predominantly on the disaster track, while testing, isolation, quarantine and hospital duties ran predominantly on the epidemic track. A citizen stopped on a road in April 2020 was almost certainly being held to a Disaster Management Act order; a citizen taken to an isolation facility was almost certainly being held under an epidemic regulation. Confusing the two produces bad history and worse law, because the safeguards, such as they are, differ between the statutes, and the reform each requires is different.

The advisory of 11 March deserves a moment's legal attention of its own, because it displays the soft machinery on which the hard law rested. An advisory from a Union ministry to state governments is not law; it binds nobody, and its purpose in the design of March 2020 was to convert Union guidance into state legality, since the enforceable instrument, the Section 2 regulation, could be made only by the states under the 1897 Act and only within their territories. The Centre's advisories on travel, gatherings, distancing and closure acquired teeth exclusively through state regulations and disaster orders, which is why the same advisory read as stringent law in one state and as exhortation in another during the pandemic's first weeks, and why the litigation of the period so often turned on identifying the actual instrument, advisory, regulation, disaster guideline or Section 144 order, under which a particular act had been forbidden or a particular premises closed. The architecture was federalism working as designed and as improvised at once: designed, because the Constitution assigns public health to the states and inter-state coordination to the Union; improvised, because no statute choreographed the conversion of advice into obligation, set a template for the regulations that resulted, or provided for the uniformity on which travellers, traders and transporters moving between states depended. Into that choreography gap stepped the disaster framework, whose chain of authorities from the national executive committee to the district magistrate supplied the coordination the epidemic statute lacked, at the price of governing a health emergency through a law written for floods and earthquakes. The two tracks were never merged, never reconciled by an amending statute, and never given a common statement of the citizen's rights against either, an omission whose consequences the courts spent two years supplying case by case.

### Which law imposed the national lockdown of 2020?

The national lockdown was imposed under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, through the National Disaster Management Authority's order of 24 March 2020 under Section 6(2)(i) and the Home Ministry's order under Section 10(2)(l), while state epidemic regulations under the Epidemic Diseases Act governed disease-specific duties such as isolation, quarantine and notification.

The choice of the disaster statute for the national instrument was itself a federal judgment. Public health and sanitation is Entry 6 of the State List in the Seventh Schedule, the states' own field, and the Epidemic Diseases Act is built on that allocation, arming states within their territories and the Centre at the ports. A national lockdown ordered by the Union needed a different footing, and the Disaster Management Act supplied it: a post 2005 framework statute with a national authority, state authorities and district authorities in one chain, an executive committee able to issue binding directions, and a definition of disaster wide enough to carry a biological emergency. Alongside it stood the Constitution's own narrow bridge for epidemic disease, Entry 29 of the Concurrent List, which empowers Parliament and the state legislatures to legislate for the prevention of the extension from one state to another of infectious or contagious diseases or pests affecting men, animals or plants. Entry 29 is a power over inter-state spread, not over public health at large, and no national epidemic code was enacted on its strength in 2020. The Union also held Entry 81 of the Union List, inter-state migration and inter-state quarantine, the constitutional seat of the Centre's Section 2A concern with movement across boundaries. The legal map of March 2020 is therefore three layered: state epidemic regulations under an 1897 statute resting on the state field of public health, national disaster orders under a 2005 statute resting on the Union's disaster framework, and a constitutional bridge for inter-state disease that no new legislation used. When the Home Ministry eventually discontinued the Disaster Management Act orders after 31 March 2022, the disaster track closed by executive decision, and the epidemic track, the older and thinner one, remained on the books exactly as it had been left in 1897, save for the single amendment that the emergency had produced almost as an aside.

State practice filled the space between the tracks with improvisation of uneven quality. Kerala, which had met the first Indian cases in January and February 2020 and had a recent education in outbreak management from the Nipah virus, moved in April 2020 to consolidate its position through an ordinance directed at epidemic disease control in the state, an early sign that at least one state government found the 1897 skeleton too bare for the work demanded of it. Karnataka, as noted below in the questions, went on to enact a state epidemic diseases statute of its own during 2020. Other states amended their COVID-19 regulations repeatedly as the epidemic changed, adding mask mandates, gathering limits, containment zone rules and duties for workplaces and markets, each amendment an exercise of the same Section 2 discretion and each exposing the same absence: nothing in the parent Act told a state how long a regulation might run, what evidence should support it, or what a person burdened by it was owed. The regulations were, in substance, the public health code of the pandemic, and they were written entirely in subordinate legislation under a parent Act that imposed no discipline on their content. That fact, more than any single order, is the implementation story of the Epidemic Diseases Act in its greatest test.

## The Regulations in Operation

What did the epidemic track actually govern, once the notifications were issued? The state COVID-19 regulations of 2020, taken together, formed a shadow public health code, written in a fortnight, and their contents repay enumeration because they show how much law Section 2 was made to carry. The regulations declared COVID-19 a dangerous epidemic disease or a notifiable disease within the state, the declaration being the trigger the Act leaves to the government. They imposed duties of notification on hospitals, clinics, laboratories and medical practitioners, public and private, requiring every suspected and confirmed case to be reported to the health authority, and they armed the surveillance machinery that followed: contact tracing, the listing of travellers from affected places, and directions to submit to screening and testing. They authorised the designation of hospitals and facilities for treatment and isolation, the requisition in practice of beds and premises through the combined pressure of the epidemic and disaster frameworks, and the sealing of premises and areas where cases clustered, the administrative ancestor of the containment zone. They prohibited or conditioned gatherings, religious, social, political and commercial, closed or restricted educational institutions, markets, malls, cinemas and public transport within the state sphere, and regulated the conduct of funerals and the disposal of the dead. They mandated masks and distancing in public places as the emergency matured, prescribed the duties of persons in home isolation and institutional quarantine, and provided for the inspection of persons arriving from outside the state, with segregation of the suspected, in the words the Act itself had supplied in 1897. Every one of those rules was law for the citizen who lived under it, enforceable by prosecution under Section 3, and every one of them was made by an executive authority under a parent section that imposed no requirement of evidence, consultation, proportionality or duration on any of them.

Some of the operational detail of that code deserves preserving, because it shows the regulation power working at the level of the individual body and the individual door. Travellers arriving in Maharashtra in March 2020 who were directed to home quarantine had their hands stamped with indelible ink bearing the quarantine direction and its dates, a mark meant to let neighbours, officials and the travellers themselves read the obligation at sight, and an emblem of the whole system in miniature: an administrative improvisation of real ingenuity, performed under the authority of a statute that neither prescribed it nor limited it, and answerable, when challenged, to no standard the statute supplied. Door to door surveillance by health workers and accredited community workers converted the inspecting officer of Section 2(2)(b) into a visiting presence in millions of homes, recording symptoms, marking households and directing the suspected into isolation, with the workers' own protection resting on administrative arrangement until the amendment of 2020 gave some of them the criminal law's attention. The containment zone, the era's characteristic legal geography, was drawn street by street on case counts by district authorities working across the epidemic and disaster frameworks, its perimeter a legal fact with consequences for everyone inside it, its criteria a matter of guideline rather than of law. None of these instruments was illegitimate; each answered a genuine need of the response. The implementation point is narrower and harder: a republic's most intimate exercises of power over bodies, doors and streets ran for two years on instruments whose entire legal pedigree was four sections written for another century's plague, and the pedigree was never the problem in any particular case, which is precisely why the structure was never repaired.

The railways, the conveyance the Act's draftsman named first, supplied the era's most instructive jurisdictional tangle. Section 2(2)(b) contemplates the inspection of persons travelling by railway and the segregation of the suspected among them, and station screening and on arrival testing were practised under the regulations through the pandemic. But the trains themselves were a Union subject worked by a Union ministry, the passengers' destinations were states with their own regulations, and when the Shramik Special trains began moving stranded workers home in May 2020, the arrangements ran through negotiation between the railways and the sending and receiving states, including a period in which the consent of the receiving state was treated as a condition of a train's running. A worker's journey home therefore crossed three legal territories, the disaster framework that had stranded and then released him, the railway administration that carried him, and the epidemic regulations of two states that screened him at each end, and no statute assigned the whole journey to anyone's charge. The Supreme Court's migrant directions of that month, requiring transport to be provided without fare to the worker and food to be served on the way, were addressed to the system at large precisely because the system, in law, had no single address. The railway paragraphs of the 1897 Act had imagined an inspecting officer on a platform detaining a suspect from a carriage; they had not imagined a million passengers whose claim was not to travel despite the state but to be carried home by it, and the distance between those two imaginings is the distance between the epidemic law of a colony and the epidemic law a republic requires.

The operational texture varied by state in ways the uniform parent Act did nothing to discipline. Maharashtra, facing the heaviest caseload, amended and reissued its regulations and orders in rapid succession and combined them with municipal action in Mumbai's containment zones, where buildings and lanes were sealed on the strength of case counts and policed by a machinery the regulations sketched and the municipal corporation built. Delhi's regulations worked alongside the disaster framework in a capital administered jointly by an elected government and the Union, so that the epidemic track and the disaster track crossed in the same streets under different signatures. Kerala's early ordinances and its later consolidation reflected a state that had begun its response in January, before the Act was invoked anywhere, and that treated the regulation power as one instrument among several rather than as the whole of its authority. In the districts, the working instrument of daily control was often neither statute but the prohibitory order under Section 144 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, issued by district magistrates to forbid assembly, layered over the epidemic regulations and the disaster orders until the citizen faced three texts for a single walk to a shop. The layering was not lawlessness; each layer had its warrant. But it reproduced, at the level of the street, the structure of the whole system: abundant authority, thinly specified, coordinated by administration rather than by law.

The relaxation of the emergency displayed the same structure in reverse. The phased reopening from June 2020 ran through the disaster track, in the Home Ministry's unlock guidelines issued under the Disaster Management Act, which returned activities to the permitted list in stages while expressly leaving states free to impose stricter measures within their own frameworks. The epidemic regulations were amended in step, gathering limits raised, categories of premises reopened, mask and distancing duties retained after most others fell away. Nothing in either statute required the relaxation to follow the epidemiology, set criteria for the stages, or gave a trader closed in March and reopened in October any claim for the interval; the phases were administrative judgment, published as guidelines, exactly as the closures had been administrative judgment published as orders. When later waves arrived, the machinery reversed direction without new law: night curfews and weekend restrictions in the second wave of 2021 were imposed through the same combination of state regulation, disaster direction and Section 144 order, and the Omicron wave of the following winter was met with a lighter version of the same toolkit. By then the implementation pattern of the Act had completed a full cycle within two years, enactment by notification, operation by regulation, relaxation by guideline, reimposition by order, and it had done so without the parent statute being opened by a legislature at any point in the cycle except once, for the protection of healthcare workers. The regulations in operation are therefore the strongest evidence on both sides of this article's argument. They show a four section Act carrying, without visible strain, the entire disease law of a pandemic in a republic of states, which is the defence. And they show that law being made, amended, relaxed and reimposed wholly within the executive, under a parent that asked nothing of it, which is the charge.

## Enforcement and the Borrowed Penalty

A regulation is only ever a request until a penalty stands behind it, and the penalty behind every epidemic regulation in India is the one Section 3 borrows from the Penal Code. The enforcement record of the pandemic years is therefore best read as a record about Section 188 of the Indian Penal Code: the offence of disobeying an order duly promulgated by a public servant. Police forces across the states registered cases under Section 188, frequently together with Sections 269 and 270 of the Penal Code, which punish negligent and malignant acts likely to spread infection dangerous to life, and together with Section 51 of the Disaster Management Act for disobedience of disaster orders, against persons found travelling without cause, opening closed premises, gathering in prohibited numbers, or breaching quarantine and isolation directions. The prosecutions proceeded at proposition level along three distinguishable paths. The first path was volume: in the lockdown months the borrowed offence became one of the most used provisions in the criminal process, applied to conduct as various as a shopkeeper opening a shutter, a group returning from worship, a traveller walking a highway and a family gathering for a funeral, because the regulation of the week forbade all of it and Section 3 made all of it, in form, the same offence. The second path was doctrinal attrition: in the high courts, prosecutions repeatedly failed on the ingredients of the incorporated offence, because the complaint did not establish a duly promulgated order, or the accused's knowledge of it, or because the proceeding had been set in motion by a police report rather than by the complaint of the public servant concerned as Section 195 of the Code of Criminal Procedure requires for Section 188. Quashing petitions became a standard epilogue to lockdown enforcement, and the pattern of those orders teaches the same lesson as the Poona searches from the opposite direction: a statute that leaves everything to the regulation leaves the prosecution to the paperwork, and paperwork done in haste, under a provision designed in 1860 for disobedience of individual orders, fits mass regulation badly. The third path was disparity: because the regulations were state and district instruments, identically described conduct drew prosecution in one jurisdiction and a warning in another, and the borrowed penalty's modest scale, imprisonment measured in months and fines in hundreds of rupees at the lower grade, sat oddly beside the severity with which some states announced their enforcement drives. None of these paths required any court to examine the Epidemic Diseases Act itself. The Act's design ensured that enforcement litigation would always be about the regulation and the Penal Code, never about the four sections that made both possible, and the Act accordingly survived its greatest enforcement era without a single authoritative reading of its own limits.

One prosecution episode drew the enforcement pattern into a single frame. In the first weeks of the lockdown, a religious congregation held in Delhi in March 2020 was identified as a cluster of infection, and prosecutions followed against attendees, including foreign nationals, under the borrowed penal provisions and the foreigners law, for attending gatherings, breaching visa conditions and violating epidemic orders. In August 2020 the Bombay High Court, dealing with the prosecutions of foreign members of the congregation, quashed the cases before it, holding that the material did not establish the violations alleged and observing, in terms that travelled widely, that the petitioners had been made scapegoats by a machinery that needed a narrative for the spread of the disease. The episode is recorded here at the level the judgment supports: prosecutions were launched in the atmosphere of a frightening cluster, the evidentiary discipline that Section 188 imports was not met, and a constitutional court ended the proceedings. It is the Pune lesson of 1897 restated in the language of prosecution rather than search: power exercised at the breadth of its text, in a panic, against a marked group, corrected afterwards by institutions outside the statute and never by the statute itself.

The penalty's afterlife deserves a sentence of its own, because the incorporation is dynamic in effect even where the reference is old. Section 3 of the 1897 Act still speaks of the Indian Penal Code, and the Penal Code has since been replaced as the general penal law by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, in which the disobedience offence is carried into Section 223 with somewhat enhanced fines: disobedience simpliciter carries up to six months of imprisonment or a fine of up to two thousand five hundred rupees, and disobedience causing danger to human life, health or safety up to one year or five thousand rupees. The numbering change, and what it does to a penalty borrowed by reference in 1897, is examined in the series comparison of [the Penal Code and its successor](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/), and it is noted here for a structural reason: a penalty incorporated by reference is a penalty the epidemic legislature never chose, at a scale it never debated, for conduct it never described. When the fines rose in the new code, the epidemic penalty rose with them, automatically and without a word of parliamentary attention to epidemic policy. Borrowed teeth bite at the strength of another statute's jaw.

## The Courts as the Missing Chapter

Because the Act confers no rights, the rights that Indians actually held during the pandemic were conferred by the Constitution and enforced by courts working outside the statute, and the litigation record is the clearest X ray of the Act's omissions. When the lockdown stranded migrant workers in cities without work, food or transport, the Supreme Court took suo motu cognisance in May 2020 in the proceedings titled In Re: Problems and Miseries of Migrant Labourers, Suo Motu Writ Petition (Civil) No. 6 of 2020, and by its order of 28 May 2020 and the directions that followed it required the Centre and the states to provide transport, food and shelter to the stranded workers free of cost. Nothing in the Epidemic Diseases Act or the Disaster Management Act entitled a stranded worker to a train seat or a meal; the entitlement was constructed by the Court out of Article 21, the right to life, in the face of the emergency the statutes had authorised. In 2021, during the second wave, the Court again proceeded suo motu, in In Re: Distribution of Essential Supplies and Services During Pandemic, turning its attention to the supply of oxygen and essential drugs and to the scrutiny of vaccination policy, while high courts across the country heard the granular litigation of the emergency: the condition of quarantine centres, the pricing and practices of hospitals, the availability of beds and oxygen, and the treatment of the dead. In the essential supplies proceedings the Supreme Court went beyond exhortation and constituted a National Task Force in May 2021 to address the methodology of oxygen allocation and audit, an administrative instrument designed by a court because the statutory frameworks in play, the epidemic Act and the disaster Act alike, contained no machinery for the equitable distribution of a scarce medical necessity between states in a national emergency. The migrant labour proceedings closed in the same season with the Court's final directions of June 2021, requiring the completion of the One Nation One Ration Card scheme within a stated period and the provision of dry rations and community kitchens for migrant workers, directions framed entirely in the language of Article 21 and the food security framework, not in the language of any epidemic statute. Read together, the two proceedings complete the map of the missing chapter: distribution of oxygen, distribution of food, transport of the stranded, each function essential to the management of the emergency, each performed under judicial direction sourced in the Constitution, and none of them contemplated by the statute under whose authority the emergency had been declared. The pattern beneath the docket is constant. Wherever a person subjected to epidemic power needed something the law would enforce, a court had to manufacture it from constitutional text, because the governing statute had been written for a state that owed its subjects nothing it did not choose to give.

### What rights did a person under an epidemic order actually hold?

A person subjected to an epidemic order held no rights under the Epidemic Diseases Act itself: the statute grants no hearing, no statement of reasons, no maximum period of isolation and no compensation. Such protection as the person had came from the Constitution, chiefly Article 21, applied by courts to the measures taken under the Act.

The judgment that states the constitutional settlement most completely is Jacob Puliyel v. Union of India, decided in 2022, in which the Supreme Court considered the vaccination programme and the mandates attached to it. The Court held that bodily integrity is protected under Article 21 and that no individual can be forced to be vaccinated; that personal autonomy includes the right to refuse medical treatment; that the government may nonetheless regulate for communitarian health by imposing limitations on individual rights, provided the limitation satisfies the threefold requirement drawn from K. S. Puttaswamy: legality, a legitimate state aim, and proportionality; and that, on the material before it, restrictions on unvaccinated individuals imposed through state mandates could not be said to be proportionate. The significance for the epidemic statute is exact. The proportionality discipline that Jacob Puliyel applied to pandemic measures is the discipline the Epidemic Diseases Act omits: the judgment asks whether a measure answers a legitimate aim and whether its burden is proportionate to that aim, while the Act asks only whether the government is satisfied and deems the measure necessary. Between those two questions lies the whole difference between the law of 1897 and the constitutional law of the republic, and the pandemic was governed, in the last resort, by whichever of them a litigant could persuade a court to apply. It is also worth recording what the litigation did not do. No court struck down the Epidemic Diseases Act, and no authoritative challenge to its validity reorganised its text; the challenges proceeded measure by measure and prosecution by prosecution, leaving the four sections intact for the next emergency. Judicial review in the pandemic was real but retail: it corrected orders, quashed complaints and articulated principles, while the wholesale question, whether a statute of this design should govern a republic's epidemics at all, was left where the Act's survival had always left it, with the legislature.

The high courts' vaccine mandate cases of 2021 fill in the retail picture at the level of the street and the shop. As states and local bodies attached vaccination to the reopening of livelihoods, orders requiring vaccination as a condition of opening shops, plying vehicles or entering workplaces were challenged across the country, and the Meghalaya High Court's intervention of that year, against compulsory vaccination imposed as a condition of resuming trade, stated the emerging judicial position in its most citizen facing form: vaccination could be encouraged, incentivised and facilitated, but the state's power over the body stopped short of compulsion leveraged through livelihood. The doctrinal destination of those cases was the proportionality settlement the Supreme Court would announce in Jacob Puliyel, and their collective significance for the epidemic statute is that, once again, the operative limits on epidemic power in India were written by constitutional courts in fundamental rights language, in proceedings the epidemic statute neither invited nor informed. A trader in Shillong, a worker in Guwahati and a traveller at a state border each learned what the law allowed from a court order, because the statute under which they were governed had nothing to tell them.

## The One Amendment: Ordinance to Act in 2020

In a hundred and twenty three years the Epidemic Diseases Act had been substantively amended once, in 1920. The pandemic amended it a second time, and the circumstances explain the content. Through March and April 2020, as hospitals became the front line of the emergency, reports accumulated of violence against doctors, nurses and community health workers: assaults on teams conducting surveillance, attacks in hospitals, obstruction of cremations and burials, and the social boycott of health workers by landlords and neighbours. An attack on a medical team in Moradabad in April 2020, pelted with stones while conducting screening in a neighbourhood under watch, was among the incidents that concentrated official attention in the days before the ordinance. The government answered not with a public health code but with a criminal amendment aimed at that violence, promulgated first as an ordinance. The Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Ordinance, 2020, Ordinance 5 of 2020, was promulgated on 22 April 2020, and Parliament converted it, in the monsoon session, into the Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act, 2020, Act 34 of 2020, which received the President's assent on 28 September 2020 and was deemed to have come into force on 22 April 2020, the ordinance's date. The sequence, emergency ordinance first and parliamentary enactment after, followed the familiar constitutional choreography of Article 123 that the series examines in its study of [ordinance making power in India](/2022/03/01/ordinance-making-power-india/), and the amendment's text was published in the Gazette of India of 29 September 2020, the document of record from which the account that follows is taken; readers who work through official records of this kind in presentation form can open them with the [PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) maintained for that purpose.

The Act largely carried forward the ordinance it replaced, and its retrospective commencement, deemed from 22 April 2020, performed the standard constitutional function of the sequence: acts done and offences committed under the ordinance continued under the Act without a gap in legality, so that the violence prosecutions launched in the spring were prosecuted in the autumn under a parliamentary statute. The five months between ordinance and Act are themselves a datum of implementation. Parliament was not in session when the emergency demanded the law, the ordinance route carried the demand, and the legislature's contribution, when it came, was deliberation after the fact on a text already in force and already being enforced, a pattern the epidemic statute shares with much of Indian emergency legislation. Nothing in the converting debates altered the scheme's essentials, and the scheme's essentials, as the next paragraphs show, were a complete criminal code for one class of epidemic wrongdoing inserted into a statute that still lacked a single standard for the exercise of its central power.

The amendment did five things. First, it inserted Section 1A, a definitions clause the Act had lacked since 1897, defining an act of violence to include harassment impacting the living or working conditions of healthcare service personnel, defining healthcare service personnel to include public and clinical healthcare providers such as doctors, nurses, paramedical workers and community health workers, any other person empowered under the Act to take measures, and any other person declared by state notification, and defining property to include clinical establishments within the Clinical Establishments Act, 2010, quarantine and isolation facilities and mobile medical units. Second, it prohibited the protected conduct in a new Section 2B: no person shall indulge in any act of violence against healthcare service personnel or cause damage or loss to any property during an epidemic. Third, it rewrote the Centre's transport power, substituting Section 2A so that the power of inspection and detention extends to any bus or train or goods vehicle or ship or vessel or aircraft leaving or arriving at any land port or port or aerodrome, converting a maritime quarantine clause of 1920 into an all modes border power and quietly ratifying, in the epidemic statute, the transport reach the response had already assumed. Fourth, it built a complete procedural code for the new offences in Sections 3(2) and 3(3) and Sections 3A to 3E: committing or abetting violence against healthcare service personnel, or damage to property, draws imprisonment of three months to five years and a fine of fifty thousand to two lakh rupees; causing grievous hurt, as defined in Section 320 of the Penal Code, in such violence draws six months to seven years and a fine of one lakh to five lakh rupees; the offences are cognizable and non bailable; investigation must be conducted by an officer not below the rank of Inspector and completed within thirty days of the registration of the first information report; trials are to proceed day to day with an endeavour to conclude within one year; a Section 3(2) offence may be compounded by the victim with the court's permission; the court shall presume the grievous hurt offence unless the contrary is proved, and shall presume culpable mental state; and compensation, payable in addition to punishment and fixed for property at twice the amount of fair market value of the damage, is recoverable as an arrear of land revenue under the Revenue Recovery Act, 1890. Fifth, by omitting the old Part B States exception from Section 1, it tidied the Act's extent into uniformity.

Two features of the new scheme repay the implementer's attention, because both will decide how the amendment works in the next epidemic rather than the last. The definition of healthcare service personnel is open at both ends: it names doctors, nurses, paramedical workers and community health workers, adds any other person empowered under the Act to take measures, and adds any other person the state government declares by notification. The notified tail means the protected class can expand by executive act to drivers, sanitation staff, survey workers and volunteers, a breadth appropriate to the improvised workforce of an epidemic and characteristic of the parent Act's habits, in which the consequential choices are left to notification. The compensation machinery is equally characteristic in the opposite direction: compensation awarded under the new sections is recoverable as an arrear of land revenue under the Revenue Recovery Act, 1890, a nineteenth century fiscal recovery code pressed into service because the amendment built no recovery machinery of its own. The epidemic statute book is a sediment of such borrowings, a penalty borrowed from the Penal Code in 1897, a recovery borrowed from the land revenue law in 2020, and the pattern of borrowing is itself a finding: Parliament, legislating for epidemics at speed in two different centuries, has each time reached for the nearest existing machinery rather than designing the machinery the subject requires. The trial discipline of the amendment, day to day proceedings and an endeavour to conclude within a year, belongs to the same family of provisions that Indian criminal statutes recite and Indian court dockets routinely defeat, and its presence in the text says more about legislative intention than about the experience an accused, a victim or a hospital is likely to have.

Read against the emergency that produced it, the amendment is a study in selective repair. It answered a real problem: the attacks on health workers were documented, shameful and corrosive of the response itself, and a legislature that had not protected the persons staffing its epidemic had reason to act with speed and severity. It also demonstrated, in its procedure sections, that Parliament could write standards into this statute book when it wished: thirty day investigations, inspector rank officers, day to day trials, presumptions, compounding and quantified compensation are all disciplines of exactly the kind the parent Act had never imposed on epidemic power itself. But the discipline runs in one direction only. The amendment regulated the citizen's violence against the response; it did not regulate the response. It added no definition of epidemic to govern the trigger, no criteria for the measures Section 2 authorises, no time limit for regulations, no hearing for the person segregated, no compensation for the person whose livelihood a regulation extinguished, and no protection for the patient or the family inside the system it armed. The one compensation it quantifies, twice the fair market value of damaged property, flows to the owners of clinical establishments and facilities, not to the citizen burdened by epidemic measures. The 2020 amendment is therefore the purest evidence for this article's central claim: confronted with a pandemic, Parliament legislated energetically about the epidemic and barely at all about the epidemic power. The powers were there, enlarged at the border and sharpened against violence, and the limits were not, before the amendment, during it or after it.

The amendment's background sharpens the point. Violence against medical personnel was not a discovery of 2020: over the preceding decade a majority of states had enacted protection laws for medicare service persons and institutions, statutes criminalising violence in hospitals and damage to hospital property, and those laws were widely criticised, including by the medical profession's own bodies, as weakly enforced and rarely invoked. The Indian Medical Association had for years demanded a central law on violence against healthcare professionals, and the demand had been resisted in part on the federal ground that law and order and health were state fields. The pandemic dissolved that resistance in a single month, not by resolving the federal question but by routing around it: the Union legislated inside an existing Union epidemic statute, during a proclaimed epidemic, for the duration logic the statute supplies. The parliamentary debate on the converting bill recorded the tension faithfully. Members across parties supported the protection of health workers and several pressed the structural criticism that the amendment criminalised the symptom while leaving the conditions of the response, the staffing, security and grievance machinery of hospitals, and the burdens borne by patients and families, outside the statute, criticism the government answered with the urgency of the hour. The enforcement record of the new offences in the years since has been thin in the reported material, a fraction of the volume of the Section 188 prosecutions of the same period, which suggests the amendment functioned in part as the criminal law often functions in Indian emergencies: as a statement of official seriousness, made at speed, whose deterrent life depends on a prosecution machinery that the emergency itself had stretched. None of this diminishes the amendment's necessity as the profession experienced it. It locates the amendment precisely: the one legislative act of the pandemic on the epidemic statute book was a criminal shield for the responders, enacted through the ordinance route, in a field where states had legislated before, while the civil architecture of epidemic power, the triggers, standards, rights and compensations, remained exactly where 1897 had left it.

## The Replacement That Never Arrived

The failure to replace the Act is not a story of nobody trying. It is a story of drafts, and the drafts are good, which is what makes the failure worth studying. The lineage begins with model legislation: in 1955 the Union health ministry circulated a model public health act to the states, and a revised model followed in 1987, each an attempt to persuade states to enact modern public health codes within their own constitutional field. Most states did not take the models up, and the state statute book remained a patchwork in which a few states, Tamil Nadu with its Public Health Act of 1939 among them, had comprehensive codes of their own while most relied on municipal law, the Epidemic Diseases Act and improvisation. The Union's first modern attempt was the National Health Bill of 2009, a working draft released for consultation in January 2009 by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, prepared with the advice of a task force, which addressed rights, duties, institutions and the management of public health emergencies in one framework. It was never introduced in Parliament. When the question was pressed, the government told the Lok Sabha in 2013 that it had no plans to introduce the National Health Bill, and the draft joined the models in the archive of unenacted Indian public health law.

The second attempt was closer to the target and its fate is the more instructive. In February 2017 the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare released for public comment a draft Public Health (Prevention, Control and Management of Epidemics, Bio-terrorism and Disasters) Bill, prepared with the National Centre for Disease Control, which proposed four tier national, state, district and block public health authorities, a dedicated public health cadre, defined powers and procedures for epidemic management, and the repeal of the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897. Every element the 1897 Act lacks was present in embryo: institutions, definitions, graded measures and a modern statement of the relationship between the levels of government. The draft received comments. It was never introduced. When the pandemic arrived three years later, the legal system therefore faced it with the 1897 Act and the disaster statute, and the bill that would have repealed the 1897 Act sat in the same archive as the 2009 draft. The pandemic produced the political sequel: the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs, in its report on the management of the COVID-19 pandemic submitted in 2020, called for a comprehensive public health Act at the national level and described the Epidemic Diseases Act as outdated and framed in the colonial era, recommending its amendment. A committee's call is not a bill, and no bill followed it into either House. The replacement of the epidemic statute has thus been recommended by draftsmen in 1955, 1987, 2009 and 2017 and by a parliamentary committee in 2020, and has been enacted by nobody.

The 2017 draft deserves a closer reading before the failure is assigned, because its reception contains the whole politics of the question. Public health commentators welcomed the draft's institutions and its repeal of the 1897 Act while criticising, from opposite directions, its silences and its reach. From the side of the states, a four tier authority structure crowned by a national authority, with a national public health cadre, looked like centralisation in a state subject dressed as reform, and the draft's provisions on epidemics, bioterrorism and disasters in a single frame invited the objection that health emergencies were being governed through a security lens. From the side of civil liberties, the draft was faulted for carrying forward, in more elaborate language, the coercive repertoire of the old law, powers of entry, isolation and compulsion, without building around them the rights architecture, the hearings, compensations and sunsets, whose absence was the old law's chief defect. A draft attacked for centralising too much and for liberalising too little had no natural constituency in a Parliament that had not asked for it, and the ministry that released it for comment did not spend the authority required to carry it. The episode refines the story of the failure: replacement is not blocked by a single veto but by a geometry of objections, federal, libertarian and fiscal, each sufficient to stall a bill whose sponsoring government has other priorities, and the 1897 Act, which asks nothing of anybody until an epidemic arrives, sits at the centre of that geometry undisturbed.

The reason is only partly inertia. Public health and sanitation is a state subject, Entry 6 of the State List, and a Parliament that wishes to enact a comprehensive national public health code must find its competence somewhere. The honest routes are two. The first is Article 252, under which Parliament may legislate on a state subject for consenting states whose legislatures resolve to invite it, with the resulting Act open to adoption by others; that consent architecture has carried Indian legislation before, and the series traces its classic deployment in the history of [the urban land ceiling legislation](/2013/12/01/urban-land-ceiling-act-repeal/), which was built state by state on Article 252 resolutions. The same mechanism carried another health statute: the Transplantation of Human Organs Act was enacted by Parliament on the strength of state resolutions under Article 252 and adopted progressively across the country, a precedent this series examines in its study of [the Transplantation of Human Organs Act 1994](/2015/09/01/transplantation-human-organs-act-1994/). Article 252 works, but it works at the speed of state consent, and it produces a code whose map is the map of consenting states. The second route is Entry 29 of the Concurrent List, the prevention of the extension of infectious disease from one state to another, which supports a national law about inter-state spread but fits awkwardly around the interior substance of public health: hospitals, sanitation, local measures and the duties of local bodies sit on the state side of the line. A draftsman of a national epidemic code must therefore either accept the Entry 29 frame and write a law about transmission between states that reaches, by careful drafting, much of the substance, or pursue Article 252 and accept delay and patchwork, or propose a constitutional amendment and accept the largest delay of all. Each route is genuinely available and genuinely costly, and governments facing an emergency next quarter have always chosen the instrument already on the shelf. The 1897 Act survives, in part, because it is the only epidemic law India has that never has to be enacted again.

One development of 2020 points a way out of the geometry, and it came from the states rather than from the Union. Karnataka, whose COVID-19 regulations had run under the 1897 Act like everyone else's, enacted an epidemic diseases statute of its own during 2020, converting the emergency's lessons into a state code with its own statement of powers and offences. Kerala, which had met the pandemic first and had consolidated its epidemic powers by ordinance in the pandemic's first months, moved in the same direction of state level codification. The pattern is constitutionally orthodox: public health is the states' field, and a state legislature replacing the 1897 shell within its own territory faces no Article 252 arithmetic and no Entry 29 contortion. A federation can modernise epidemic law state by state, as it built much of its public health law historically, accepting non uniformity as the price of movement. The objection is the oldest one in this article: epidemics do not respect the borders that make state by state reform easy, and a patchwork of modern state codes with an unreformed centre at the ports and an unused Entry 29 between them would reproduce, in better drafted form, the coordination problem the pandemic exposed. The state route is the most plausible future for reform and, on its own, an incomplete one, which is a fair summary of every route this subject offers.

## Four Sections Against a Pandemic: The Artifact

The comparison the brief for this article demands is best made in one place. The table below sets what a modern public health emergency statute is generally expected to provide against what the 1897 statute provides under each head, and adds, from the implementation record traced above, where each function was actually performed during the pandemic years. The table is the article's argument in miniature, and the sections around it are its evidence.

| What a modern statute is expected to provide | What the Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 provides | Where the function was performed in the pandemic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definitions and a declared trigger for emergency powers | No definition of epidemic or of dangerous epidemic disease; the trigger is the government's satisfaction that an outbreak exists or threatens | State notifications under Section 2 identified COVID-19 and opened the power; the national emergency was framed separately under the Disaster Management Act |
| Graduated powers scaled to the severity of the threat | A single undifferentiated power to take any measures deemed necessary, with inspection and segregation named by way of illustration | Gradation was improvised in regulations and disaster orders, through containment zones, phased lockdowns and unlock guidelines written outside the Act |
| Standards for isolation and quarantine | Segregation of suspected persons in hospital, temporary accommodation or otherwise, on an inspecting officer's suspicion, with no stated standard | Protocols were issued administratively by health authorities and revised as knowledge changed; the statute supplied the detention power and none of the protocol |
| Rights of the person subjected to measures | No hearing, no reasons, no maximum period, no appeal; a good faith shield for officials in Section 4 | Rights were supplied by the Constitution and the courts, through Article 21 litigation, the migrant labour proceedings and Jacob Puliyel |
| Provision for supplies, medicines and vaccines | Nothing; the Act contains no supply, procurement or vaccination provision of any kind | Supply and vaccination ran on executive programmes, disaster funds and the drugs and emergency use framework outside the Act |
| Compensation for burdens imposed by measures | Expenses, including compensation if any, may be defrayed as the state government determines; no entitlement | Relief was ex gratia and scheme based; the only quantified compensation in the Act, from 2020, covers damage to healthcare property at twice fair market value |
| Coordination between levels of government | A state power for the interior and a central power at ports and, after 2020, at land ports and aerodromes; no coordination machinery | Coordination ran through the disaster management authorities, the Home Ministry's orders and intergovernmental consultation, none of it under this Act |
| Sunset clauses and review of emergency measures | No time limit on regulations, no renewal requirement, no legislative review | Disaster orders were withdrawn by executive decision after 31 March 2022; epidemic regulations lapsed by withdrawal, not by any statutory clock |

Read the middle column slowly and the table's point makes itself: under six of the eight heads the 1897 Act provides nothing at all, and under the remaining two, the trigger and the powers, it provides the occasion and the authority while leaving every question of standard, scale and duration to the authority it creates. Read the third column and the pandemic's institutional history appears: function after function migrated out of the epidemic statute into the disaster framework, into administrative protocol, into welfare schemes and into constitutional litigation. The Act was indispensable and insufficient at the same time, the warrant for everything and the guide to nothing. That is not a paradox. It is what an enabling Act of four sections, drafted in a week of plague, predictably becomes when a republic asks it to carry a pandemic.

## The Neighbourhood: What India's Other Emergency Law Looks Like

The charge that the 1897 Act lacks institutions, standards and entitlements gains force from a comparison within India's own statute book, because the Indian legislature has demonstrated, in the neighbouring field of disaster law, that it knows how to build such things when it decides to. The Disaster Management Act, 2005, the statute that carried the national response in 2020, contains everything the epidemic Act omits: a National Disaster Management Authority chaired by the Prime Minister, state and district authorities in a defined chain, executive committees with power to issue binding directions, national and state disaster response and mitigation funds, a dedicated institute, a response force, duties laid on ministries and local authorities, offences with graded penalties for obstruction and false claims, and an overriding effect clause in Section 72. Whatever criticisms the working of that framework invites, and the pandemic supplied several, its legislature did not confuse brevity with design. It wrote a framework statute for the emergencies it could foresee in kind though not in detail, built the institutions in advance of the emergency, and funded them. Public health, the field in which emergencies are more frequent than earthquakes and more lethal than floods, never received its 2005 moment. The comparison is the sharpest single exhibit in the case about the 1897 Act, because it removes the excuse of legislative culture: the same Parliament, in the same decade in which it let the National Health Bill lapse, maintained a fully institutional emergency code for disasters, and the difference between the two fields is not technique but attention.

The international neighbourhood tells the same story from the other side. India's obligations for epidemic control at its borders run through the International Health Regulations of 2005, the World Health Organization framework that requires states to maintain core capacities for surveillance and response at ports, airports and ground crossings, to notify events that may constitute a public health emergency of international concern, and to justify health measures that interfere with international traffic. The designated machinery for those obligations in India, the national focal point functions associated with the National Centre for Disease Control and the port and airport health organisations, operates, like so much else in this article, beside the epidemic statute rather than under it: the Act of 1897, even after the 2020 expansion of Section 2A to every mode of transport, contains no reference to the international framework, no notification duty, no standard for the measures applied to arriving travellers and no protection for the traveller detained. The traveller held at an aerodrome in 2020 was held under a Victorian port power, in a century in which his government had undertaken, internationally, to apply health measures with respect for dignity, human rights and scientific evidence. The statute and the undertaking coexisted without ever being introduced to each other, which is the international form of this article's domestic finding: the legal instruments that govern Indian epidemics are old, sparse and sufficient for power, while the standards that discipline power live in other documents, in other frameworks, in the Constitution, everywhere except in the four sections that authorise the measures themselves.

The money tells the same story in figures rather than words. The Disaster Management Act carries statutory funds at national and state level, charged and governed by the Act itself, because its legislature understood that emergency power without emergency finance is a direction to spend money that has not been appropriated for the purpose. The epidemic statute carries no fund, no appropriation mechanism and no fiscal duty of any kind; the expenses clause of Section 2 leaves the defraying of costs, including compensation if any, to the determination of the spending government. In the pandemic, the consequence was that the largest single pool of epidemic money in the country, the PM CARES Fund, was constituted outside the statute book altogether, as a charitable trust announced in March 2020, receiving public donations and corporate contributions for pandemic relief under trust law rather than under any epidemic or disaster appropriation. Hospitals were equipped, ventilators procured and relief financed through a mixture of budgetary heads, disaster funds and the trust, each defensible in itself, and none of it organised by the statute under which the emergency had been declared. A public health emergency law that says nothing about money leaves the financing of the emergency to improvisation, and improvisation in public finance, however energetic, is not a system: it cannot be audited against statutory purposes that were never enacted, claimed against by citizens by way of entitlement, or reviewed by a legislature against heads it never approved. The silence of the four sections on finance is the quietest of their silences and, in a long emergency, among the most consequential.

## The Argument, Staged Fairly

The defence of the 1897 design begins from a truth about pathogens: they do not read statutes. A legislature writing an epidemic law in advance cannot know whether the next emergency will travel by air, water, vector or touch, whether it will kill the old or the young, whether the answering measure will be a border closure, a drug, a mask or a school holiday. On this view, the modern statute imagined in the table above is a comforting fiction: its definitions would be outrun by the novel organism, its graduated powers would grade a threat whose scale is unknown at the moment grading matters, and its procedures would consume the days in which containment is still possible. The short enabling Act, defended in this spirit during the pandemic years by the administrators who worked with it, trusts the only actors who can know the emergency, the executive on the ground advised by its scientists, and the pandemic record offers the defence some honest evidence. India did act fast in March 2020. The dual track was assembled in thirteen days without a legislative session, the state regulations were issued by governments close to their outbreaks, the Centre's border power was extended to every mode of transport by an amendment Parliament passed within months, and the disaster framework supplied the coordination the old Act lacked. Flexibility, the defence concludes, is not the absence of law but a deliberate allocation of judgment to the moment of crisis, and the alternative actually on offer in 2020 was not a perfect modern code but no code at all, since the modern drafts had been sitting unenacted for a decade.

The answer begins by conceding the premise and disputing the conclusion. Breadth may suit an emergency statute; the question is what accompanies the breadth. The critics of the Act, a company that runs from the administrators at Pune who found it old and lacking guidelines in 2009 to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs that called it outdated in 2020, do not demand that a statute predict the pathogen. They demand the disciplines that make discretion governable: a trigger that requires a stated satisfaction on recorded grounds, measures that must be proportionate and time bound, reasons that must be published so that courts and legislatures can review them, a hearing or its emergency equivalent for the person detained, and entitlements, to food, shelter, wages or compensation, for the person whose life the measure suspends. None of those disciplines would have slowed the response of March 2020 by a day, because all of them operate after the first order, disciplining its continuation rather than preventing its issue. What the record shows, the critics continue, is the price of their absence: prosecutions in volume under a borrowed offence, quashed in volume for failures of elementary process; segregation and quarantine whose terms rested on suspicion without stated standards; a migrant catastrophe answered by a constitutional court because no statute entitled the stranded to anything; violence against health workers answered, rightly, with a criminal amendment, while the conditions of the workers and the burdens of the patients went unlegislated; and a compensation principle that appears in the Act only as a parenthesis, if any, in 1897 and, in 2020, as a quantified entitlement for damaged property rather than displaced people. Breadth without criteria, reasons, time limit, review or entitlement is not the same thing as breadth, and the conflation of the two is the defence's central sleight.

There is a third voice in the argument, quieter than either, and a fair staging must give it a paragraph: the district administrator's. The officers who worked the Act in 2020 and 2021 did not experience its silence as freedom. They experienced it as exposure. Every containment order, every sealing of a building, every direction to a private hospital was issued in the knowledge that the parent statute gave them authority without instruction, and that the instruction would be supplied afterwards, by a court's questions, a commission's findings or a newspaper's inquiry, against standards nobody had stated in advance. The good faith shield of Section 4 protected their persons; it did not protect their decisions from retrospective judgment by principles articulated in the middle of the emergency, nor did it tell them, at the moment of decision, what a lawful measure looked like beyond its warrant in their own satisfaction. From the district, the case for a modern statute is not libertarian or centralising but administrative: standards are a protection for the official as well as for the citizen, criteria are guidance as well as constraint, and a legislature that writes neither leaves its own officers to improvise the rule of law at the checkpoint and the hospital gate. The Pune administrators who found the Act old and lacking guidelines in 2009 were making exactly this complaint, and the fact that it was made by the wielders of the power, a decade before the pandemic, may be the single most authoritative criticism the Act has received.

The federal difficulty, which both sides must honour, cuts across the argument. Even a legislature persuaded by the critics must build its code on Entry 6's state field, on the Article 252 consent of states, or on the narrow bridge of Entry 29, and each construction has a cost in speed, uniformity or reach. It is entirely possible that the replacement bills stalled not because nobody believed in them but because every government that priced the constitutional route preferred the shelf instrument, and a fair account records that the 2017 draft, which proposed to solve the problem with four tier authorities and a national cadre, asked states to accept a centralised architecture in their own field at the instance of a health ministry whose fiscal commitments to state health systems did not match its institutional ambitions. The states' reluctance was not obscurantism. A national epidemic code written in Delhi, staffed by a national cadre and overriding state satisfaction in a state subject, raises democratic questions of its own, and the design problem for a successor statute is to write Union coordination for inter-state spread and national emergencies while keeping the primary power, and the primary accountability, where the Constitution put them.

## Verdict: What the Implementation Record Proves

The verdict this article was commissioned to test was that the powers were there and the limits were not. The implementation record proves the verdict, with one refinement. The powers were there: no Indian government between 1897 and 2022, colonial, provincial, state or Union, ever failed to find in the four sections the authority it wanted, for plague searches, plague flight control, influenza screening, dengue notices, cholera declarations or pandemic regulations, and the one time more power was wanted, at the transport border, Parliament supplied it in a single amending Act within a season. The limits were not: across the same century and a quarter, no person inspected, segregated, quarantined, closed down or prosecuted under the Act ever held, from the Act, a right to reasons, a standard for the suspicion that seized him, a limit on the duration of his segregation, a hearing, or a rupee of compensation by entitlement. The refinement is about where the limits eventually came from. They came from outside the statute: from the Disaster Management Act's framework for the national measures, from administrative protocol for the clinical ones, from welfare schemes and ex gratia payments for the economic ones, and from the Constitution, through courts applying Article 21 and the proportionality test, for the legal ones. A legal system can run an epidemic that way, and India did. But a system in which every limit on epidemic power must be imported from elsewhere is a system whose epidemic statute is doing only half the work of law, the half that empowers, while the half that restrains is left to improvisation and litigation.

One further finding belongs to the verdict, because it disciplines the hopes placed in replacement. The pandemic record shows two systems running in parallel wherever the emergency bit hardest: the legal system of notifications, regulations and orders, and the delivery system of hospitals, laboratories, oxygen, trains and rations, and the failures that cost the most were, in the main, failures of the second system rather than of the first. Surat in 1994, Pune in 2009 and the oxygen crisis of 2021 were not, at their core, crises of legal authority; they were crises of capacity, candour and logistics, in which the legal power was plentiful and the beds, tests and cylinders were not. A modern statute cannot manufacture capacity, and this article does not pretend that the disciplines it recommends, criteria, reasons, sunsets and entitlements, would by themselves have produced a single additional hospital bed. What those disciplines govern is the distribution of the emergency's burdens and the answerability of the state that imposes them: who is closed, who is detained, who is compensated, who is prosecuted and who must explain. The 1897 Act's failure is located exactly there, in the juridical layer of the emergency, and it is a real failure even though it is not the whole of the pandemic's story. The state that cannot yet guarantee the oxygen can at least guarantee the hearing, the reasoned order, the dated regulation and the compensated burden, and a statute that guarantees none of them has failed at the part of the work that was always within its gift.

The legacy follows from the verdict. The Epidemic Diseases Act remains in force, amended once for violence against health workers, unamended in its theory of power, awaiting the next satisfaction, the next notification and the next borrowed prosecution. Whether it is replaced will depend on a legislature willing to pay the federal price recorded above and to write, into the replacement, the disciplines this record shows to be necessary: defined triggers with recorded reasons, graduated and proportionate measures, time limits with legislative renewal, standards for isolation and quarantine, rights for the person subjected to measures, compensation as entitlement rather than parenthesis, coordination machinery that respects the state field while governing inter-state spread, and a sunset on the emergency itself. The drafts exist. The committee reports exist. The experience, purchased at the price the pandemic charged, exists. What has never existed is the session of Parliament that treats the limit half of epidemic law as urgent in the absence of an epidemic, and until that session sits, the implementation history of the Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 will remain what this article has recorded: the longest running demonstration in Indian law that a state can be fully armed against a disease and barely governed in the arming.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What powers does the Epidemic Diseases Act 1897 give?

Section 2 empowers a state government that is satisfied of an outbreak, or a threat of an outbreak, of a dangerous epidemic disease to take any measures it deems necessary and to prescribe temporary regulations binding the public, including the inspection of travellers and the segregation of suspected persons in hospital, temporary accommodation or otherwise. Section 2A gives the central government the corresponding power over transport arriving or departing, extended in 2020 to buses, trains, goods vehicles, ships, vessels and aircraft at land ports, ports and aerodromes. Section 3 punishes disobedience through Section 188 of the Penal Code, and Section 4 shields action taken in good faith. The powers are deliberately open textured: the Act names no measure it forbids and sets no criteria, duration or standard for the measures it allows.

### Q: Why was an 1897 law used during the pandemic?

Because it was there, because it was sufficient in form, and because nothing had replaced it. The Act had never been repealed, state governments had invoked it within living administrative memory, at Pune in 2009 and in district level dengue and cholera actions, and its Section 2 could carry COVID-19 regulations without amendment. The modern alternatives had all failed at the same gate: the National Health Bill of 2009 was never introduced, with the government telling the Lok Sabha in 2013 that it had no plans to introduce it, and the draft Public Health Bill of 2017, which would have repealed the 1897 Act, was released for comment and never introduced either. A government facing an epidemic in March 2020 reached for the instrument on the shelf, and the instrument on the shelf was dated 4 February 1897.

### Q: What does the Epidemic Diseases Act not contain?

The Act contains no definition of epidemic or of dangerous epidemic disease, no criteria for the measures a government may take, no requirement to record or publish reasons, no standards for isolation or quarantine, no maximum period for any measure, no hearing or appeal for a person inspected, segregated or burdened, no entitlement to compensation, no institution, no expert body, no coordination machinery between the Union and the states, no provision for supplies, medicines or vaccines, and no sunset clause or legislative review. Its one gesture toward loss, the expenses clause of Section 2, speaks only of compensation to be paid if any, as the government determines. What the statute contains is power: a trigger, an authorisation, a borrowed penalty and a good faith shield. Every protective element of the pandemic legal order had to be supplied from outside its text.

### Q: What did the 2020 epidemic amendment add?

The Epidemic Diseases (Amendment) Act, 2020, Act 34 of 2020, assented to on 28 September 2020 and deemed in force from 22 April 2020, the date of the ordinance it replaced, addressed violence against healthcare service personnel during an epidemic. It inserted definitions in Section 1A, including healthcare service personnel and property, prohibited violence and property damage in Section 2B, and created graded offences: three months to five years with a fine of fifty thousand to two lakh rupees, rising to six months to seven years with a fine of one lakh to five lakh rupees where grievous hurt is caused. It made the offences cognizable and non bailable, required investigation by an officer of Inspector rank within thirty days, added presumptions and compensation at twice the fair market value of damaged property, and expanded Section 2A to all modes of transport. It changed nothing about the powers over the public.

### Q: How did the Disaster Management Act work alongside it?

The two statutes divided the pandemic between them. The Epidemic Diseases Act carried the disease specific track: on 11 March 2020 states were advised to invoke Section 2, and state governments issued epidemic regulations governing notification, testing, isolation, quarantine, gatherings and the duties of institutions and travellers. The Disaster Management Act, 2005 carried the population wide track: the national lockdown rested on the National Disaster Management Authority's order of 24 March 2020 under Section 6(2)(i) and the Home Ministry's order of the same date under Section 10(2)(l), and the disaster framework supplied the national, state and district authorities through which movement restrictions, exemptions and relief were administered. In practice a person was held to the disaster orders for being on the road and to the epidemic regulations for being ill, exposed or in contact. The Home Ministry discontinued the disaster orders after 31 March 2022.

### Q: What penalties apply for breaching an epidemic order?

Disobedience of a regulation or order under the Act is deemed by Section 3 to be an offence under Section 188 of the Indian Penal Code, which punishes disobedience of a duly promulgated order of a public servant: at the lower grade with imprisonment of up to one month, or a fine, or both, and at the higher grade, where disobedience causes or tends to cause danger to human life, health or safety, or a riot, with imprisonment of up to six months, or a fine, or both, at the Penal Code's historic scales. Prosecution also requires the complaint of the public servant concerned under Section 195 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and pandemic era prosecutions were frequently quashed for want of a duly promulgated order or of that complaint. Violence against healthcare personnel attracts the heavier penalties inserted in 2020, described in the answer on the amendment above.

### Q: Why was the public health bill never introduced?

Two bills share the description, and each failed for connected reasons. The National Health Bill of 2009, a working draft released in January 2009, was never introduced at all; the government told the Lok Sabha in 2013 that it had no plans to introduce it. The draft Public Health (Prevention, Control and Management of Epidemics, Bio-terrorism and Disasters) Bill of 2017, released for public comment in February 2017 with four tier public health authorities and a public health cadre, was likewise never introduced. Behind both failures sits the same structure: public health is a state subject under Entry 6 of the State List, so a national code must either win state consent under Article 252 or rest on the narrow inter-state disease entry, Entry 29 of the Concurrent List, and governments have repeatedly preferred the administrative convenience of the existing Act to the constitutional price of replacing it.

### Q: Should India replace the Epidemic Diseases Act?

The case for replacement rests on the record. The Act supplies power without criteria, reasons, time limits, standards, review or entitlement, and the pandemic showed each absence operating at national scale: mass prosecutions under a borrowed offence, segregation without statutory standards, and relief for the stranded constructed by a constitutional court because no statute provided it. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Home Affairs recommended a comprehensive public health Act in 2020 and described the 1897 Act as outdated. The honest difficulties are federal rather than technical: a replacement must respect the state field of public health while governing inter-state spread, and the drafts of 2009 and 2017 show the design work is feasible. Replacement that merely renames the discretion would change little; replacement that adds triggers, proportionality, sunsets, standards, rights and compensation would change the character of epidemic power in India.

### Q: What is Section 188 of the Penal Code and why does the Epidemic Diseases Act rely on it?

Section 188 of the Indian Penal Code punishes disobedience to an order duly promulgated by a public servant who is lawfully empowered to promulgate it, grading the punishment by the harm the disobedience causes or tends to cause. The Epidemic Diseases Act relies on it because the Act creates no offence of its own: Section 3 simply deems disobedience of any regulation or order under the Act to be an offence punishable under Section 188. The borrowing has consequences that shaped enforcement in every epidemic. The prosecution must prove Section 188's ingredients, including a lawful, promulgated order and the accused's knowledge of it; the proceeding can begin only on the complaint of the public servant concerned under Section 195 of the Code of Criminal Procedure; and the punishment scale is one the epidemic legislature never debated. The offence has since been carried into Section 223 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

### Q: Which states used the Epidemic Diseases Act before 2020?

The pre pandemic record is a pattern of state and district invocation rather than a single national use. Maharashtra invoked the Act at Pune and Satara during the influenza pandemic of 2009, using Section 2 to establish screening centres and to declare swine flu notifiable in the invoked districts. Gujarat's administration invoked it at Surat during the plague outbreak of 1994, when its powers supported restrictions on rail and air movement, quarantine of suspects and house to house checks. Legal commentary on the Act's working records Chandigarh implementing it in 2015 for malaria and dengue control, with notices and five hundred rupee challans for mosquito breeding premises, and a Vadodara district notification in 2018 declaring a village cholera affected after thirty one persons reported symptoms. Provincial governments had reached for the same Act during the influenza pandemic of 1918.

### Q: What happened in Pune when the Act was invoked in 2009?

India's first death in the H1N1 influenza pandemic was recorded at Pune on 3 August 2009, when a fourteen year old schoolgirl died after delayed diagnosis and treatment in a private hospital. The Maharashtra government invoked the Epidemic Diseases Act in Pune and Satara districts, and Section 2 powers were used to set up screening centres across the city, to declare swine flu a notifiable disease within the invoked area, and to direct suspected cases into designated facilities. A peer reviewed account of the Pune response in the International Journal of Health Policy and Management confirms that the city's pandemic response plan relied on powers under the Act of 1897, and records the administrators' judgment on their own instrument: they found the Act old and lacking guidelines for the work it was asked to organise. The invocation worked as authority and failed as guidance, a decade before the same finding was made at national scale.

### Q: Why did state governments issue their own epidemic regulations in 2020?

Because the Act is built that way. The Epidemic Diseases Act is a shell: it confers power on state governments but contains no operative rules of its own, so the actual law of the pandemic, the duties, prohibitions and procedures citizens lived under, had to be written by each state as regulations under Section 2. Acting on the advice issued after the Cabinet Secretary's meeting of 11 March 2020, states laid that track through March: Maharashtra notified its invocation on 13 March 2020 and issued the Maharashtra COVID-19 Regulations on 14 March, Delhi issued the Delhi Epidemic Diseases, COVID-19 Regulations, 2020, Karnataka issued its own regulations in the same period, and parallel instruments followed across the country. Each regulation defined the disease, named the authorities, imposed duties of notification and isolation and prohibited specified conduct, with Section 3 supplying the penalty for breach.

### Q: What protection does Section 4 of the Act give to officials?

Section 4 provides that no suit or other legal proceeding shall lie against any person for anything done, or in good faith intended to be done, under the Act. It is a good faith shield of the classic colonial pattern: the official who inspects, segregates, closes, seizes or prosecutes under colour of the Act is protected from civil and criminal proceedings for the act itself, provided the act was done in good faith. The condition matters in form, since action shown to be mala fide falls outside the protection. In practice the shield is stronger than its condition suggests, because the Act requires no reasons to be recorded and no criteria to be observed, so the official record from which bad faith might be proved is a record the statute never required anyone to create. Challenges in the pandemic therefore ran against orders, regulations and prosecutions rather than against the officials who made them.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court say about forced vaccination during the pandemic?

In Jacob Puliyel v. Union of India, decided in 2022, the Supreme Court held that bodily integrity is protected under Article 21 of the Constitution and that no individual can be forced to be vaccinated. Personal autonomy, the Court held, includes the right to refuse medical treatment, while the government remains entitled to regulate for communitarian health by imposing limitations that satisfy the threefold test drawn from K. S. Puttaswamy: legality, a legitimate state aim and proportionality. On the material before it, the Court found the Union's vaccination policy itself informed by relevant considerations and not manifestly arbitrary, but held that restrictions on unvaccinated individuals imposed through state mandates could not be said to be proportionate, given the evidence on transmission placed before the Court. The judgment supplied, from constitutional law, the proportionality discipline that the epidemic statute itself nowhere contains.

### Q: How does the Epidemic Diseases Act fit with state public health laws?

Public health and sanitation is Entry 6 of the State List, and the statute book reflects that allocation. A minority of states built comprehensive public health codes of their own, Tamil Nadu's Public Health Act of 1939 being the durable example, and those codes govern sanitation, notification and local health administration within their states. The Epidemic Diseases Act operates across the whole field as a Union enacted emergency shell: it does not displace state codes in ordinary times, because it does nothing until a government is satisfied of an epidemic, but once invoked its regulations supply the emergency layer above municipal and public health law. The patchwork showed its seams in 2020, when states with thin public health statutes leaned entirely on the 1897 Act, Kerala moved to consolidate its position by ordinance, and Karnataka went further and enacted an epidemic diseases statute of its own during that year.

### Q: What compensation can a person claim under the Epidemic Diseases Act?

By entitlement, none. The Act's only engagement with loss is the expenses clause in Section 2, under which the state government may determine in what manner and by whom any expenses incurred, including compensation to be paid if any, shall be defrayed. Compensation is therefore a discretionary head of public expense, not an entitlement of the person segregated, quarantined, closed down or otherwise burdened by epidemic measures, and no provision fixes its amount, its conditions or a forum for claiming it. Relief during the pandemic was accordingly ex gratia and scheme based, announced administratively rather than claimed legally. The single quantified compensation in the Act arrived with the 2020 amendment and runs in a different direction: compensation, in addition to punishment, for violence against healthcare personnel and for damage to healthcare property, fixed for property at twice its fair market value.

### Q: Why do attempts to replace the 1897 Act keep failing?

The drafts keep succeeding and the enactment keeps failing, and the difference is constitutional arithmetic. Public health belongs to the states under Entry 6 of the State List, so a national replacement must be built either on state consent under Article 252, which works but proceeds at the speed of consenting legislatures and produces a patchwork map, or on Entry 29 of the Concurrent List, which covers the prevention of inter-state spread of disease but fits awkwardly around hospitals, sanitation and local measures, or on a constitutional amendment, the slowest route of all. The model acts of 1955 and 1987 depended on voluntary state adoption and were mostly not adopted; the National Health Bill of 2009 and the draft Public Health Bill of 2017 each required a government to spend that constitutional capital, and each government declined. The existing Act, needing no enactment and offending no state's field, wins by default in every session that has other business.

### Q: How long do regulations under the Epidemic Diseases Act remain in force?

The Act fixes no period at all. Section 2 speaks of temporary regulations, but nothing in the statute defines temporary, sets a maximum duration, requires renewal, provides for legislative laying or review, or causes a regulation to lapse when the outbreak subsides. A regulation therefore remains in force for exactly as long as the government that made it leaves it in force, and ends by withdrawal or replacement, as the state COVID-19 regulations were progressively amended, relaxed and withdrawn by executive action. The position contrasts with the discipline Parliament wrote into the 2020 amendment for criminal trials under the same Act, and with the Disaster Management Act orders of the pandemic, which were likewise closed by executive decision when the Home Ministry discontinued them after 31 March 2022. Duration under the epidemic statute is a matter of administrative memory, not of law, which is why sunset clauses head the list of reforms proposed for any successor.

### Q: Has any court struck down the Epidemic Diseases Act?

No. Across a century and a quarter, and through the intensive litigation of the pandemic years, no court has struck down the Act or read down its central grant of power. The challenges that succeeded proceeded retail rather than wholesale: prosecutions under Section 188 were quashed for failure of that offence's ingredients or of the complaint requirement, individual orders and conditions were corrected in proceedings before the high courts, and the Supreme Court articulated the constitutional limits on epidemic measures in cases such as the migrant labour proceedings of 2020 and Jacob Puliyel in 2022, without invalidating the statute under which the measures were taken. The Act's constitutional exposure is real but untested at the level of the text: a modern challenge would have to argue that a power without criteria, standards or duration fails the proportionality and non arbitrariness disciplines of Articles 14 and 21, and that argument awaits its case.

### Q: What should a modern Indian public health emergency law contain?

The pandemic record writes the specification. A modern law should define its trigger and require the satisfaction behind it to be recorded and published; graduate its powers to the severity of the threat instead of granting all measures at once; state standards for isolation, quarantine and detention, with maximum periods and a route to review; guarantee the person subjected to measures the essentials of life during them, and compensation as entitlement rather than grace; provide for supplies, medicines and vaccination within the statute rather than beside it; build coordination between the Union, the states and local bodies that respects the state field of public health while governing inter-state spread; protect healthcare workers, as the 2020 amendment began to do; and carry a sunset, so that emergency power expires unless a legislature renews it. The drafts of 2009 and 2017 contain much of this. What they lacked was never drafting.
