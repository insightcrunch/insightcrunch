The plan for how to stop Bukayo Saka can be stated in one sentence, and the sentence is deliberately strange: do not defend him. Defend the pair. What accepts giving up is the harmless outcome, meaning the crossed ball delivered from a position and an angle you have chosen in advance, thrown into a box you have organized to win. What it refuses to give up is the moment two attackers occupy one defender and the defender improvises, because every goal this profile produces begins in that moment and almost none of them begin in a one-versus-one.

![How to stop Bukayo Saka, defending the wide pair with a fixed hierarchy of ball and space - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-68.webp)

Almost every opposition report written about a wide attacker of this type is built the wrong way round. It scouts the player: his preferred direction, his shooting angles, his first touch, his cut inside, his crossing foot. All of that information is accurate and almost none of it is decisive, because the situations in which the information would help are the situations that do not occur. He rarely takes on an isolated defender in a genuine one-versus-one with no support, and when he does, the outcome is close to a coin flip that a competent full-back wins often enough to live with. The situations that hurt are the ones where a second attacker has already changed the arithmetic before the first touch, and no amount of scouting the individual will prepare a full-back for a situation in which he was always going to be wrong.

## The pair, not the player

That is the framework this article builds and it is worth naming precisely, because everything downstream is an application of it.

A wide attacker who combines is defended by a pair of defenders operating under a fixed hierarchy, decided at the whiteboard and rehearsed on the training pitch, which specifies who owns the ball and who owns the space. The hierarchy has to be fixed in advance for a reason that is not obvious until you have watched it fail: in-match improvisation always resolves toward the ball. Two defenders left to work it out will both drift toward the carrier, because the ball is the visible threat and human attention follows the visible threat. The space behind, which is where the goal comes from, is defended by nobody, and both defenders will finish the sequence believing the other one had it.

That is the entire mechanism of the goals this profile creates. Not a beaten defender. Two defenders who each did something reasonable.

The hierarchy fixes it by making the assignments unequal and explicit. One defender owns the ball, unconditionally, and does not leave the carrier under any circumstance short of the carrier releasing it. The other owns the space behind and inside, unconditionally, and does not step to the ball under any circumstance short of the first defender being beaten. Neither decision is made during the sequence, which is the point, because the sequence is over in two seconds and no decision made inside two seconds under pressure is reliable.

### Which defender owns the ball and which owns the space?

The ball is owned by whichever defender is closest to the carrier at the moment the ball enters the wide zone, normally the full-back. The space behind and inside is owned by the covering defender, normally the near-side centre-back for an underlap and the near-side midfielder for an overlap. Assign both before kick off; neither is decided during the sequence.

## The two-versus-one problem, stated properly

Before the plan, the arithmetic. Most coaches can describe the overlap problem in general terms and very few can state exactly why every individual choice available to the defender is wrong, which is why so many plans quietly ask the full-back to solve something unsolvable and then blame him afterward.

A wide attacker holds width, receiving with his back partially to the touchline and his body angled infield, roughly level with or slightly inside the edge of the penalty area. A full-back is in front of him. Behind the attacker, a second attacker, usually his own full-back, is arriving at pace into the space outside.

The defending full-back has four options and every one of them loses.

He can hold his position and defend the carrier squarely. The overlapping runner goes past him on the outside into the space behind, receives, and reaches the byline. The full-back has defended the duel perfectly and conceded a cutback from the goal line.

He can turn and follow the overlapping runner. The carrier now has the inside lane completely open, cuts in, and either shoots or plays the ball across the face of the defence. The full-back has defended the run perfectly and conceded a shot from the corner of the box.

He can jockey backward, trying to stay between both. This is the improvised compromise most defenders choose and it is the worst of the three, because it concedes the reception, concedes the momentum, concedes the ground, and still forces the same choice five yards deeper with the goal that much closer.

He can step aggressively to the carrier and try to win the ball before the runner arrives. Against a competent carrier this is beaten with one touch, and once beaten, both attackers are behind the defensive line simultaneously.

Four options, four losses. The conclusion is not that the full-back should choose better. It is that the situation cannot be solved by the full-back and that any plan asking him to solve it has already failed at the whiteboard. The resolution is a second defender with a rehearsed job, and the entire remainder of this article is a specification of who that defender is, when he moves, and what the team accepts conceding while he is doing it.

## The shape and the line height

The plan works from a mid-block with a back four and a genuine wide midfielder ahead of the full-back on the relevant side. That is the default, and the reasons for it are structural rather than preferential.

A high press is a legitimate alternative and a different article, because a high press does not defend this problem, it prevents the situation from arising. If your pressing coordination is genuinely good, prevention is better than containment and you should press. If it is not, a partial press produces the exact condition that hurts most, which is a wide pair attacking a displaced defensive shape in transition, and a partial press is the worst available plan.

A deep block is also legitimate and carries a specific cost that coaches consistently underestimate. Dropping deep compresses the space behind the full-back, which sounds like it solves the overlap, and it does reduce the value of the run in behind. What it does instead is deliver both attackers into the penalty area with the defence already on its own line, which converts every sequence into a box-defending problem and multiplies the number of cutbacks you have to survive. A deep block against a combination pair does not remove the problem; it moves it fifteen yards closer to your goal and takes away your ability to attack the delivery. Choose it only if your box defending is genuinely strong and your midfield cannot hold a mid-block line.

The mid-block is the default because it preserves the one thing the plan needs most, which is the distance between the defensive line and the goal that allows a covering defender to step forward into the inside lane without immediately exposing the six-yard box. Take that distance away and the covering job becomes impossible, because stepping forward from your own penalty spot means abandoning the goal.

Line height should be set so that the defensive line sits roughly on the edge of the defensive third when the ball is in the middle third, and drops no more than five to eight yards when the ball enters the wide attacking zone. The instinct is to drop further, and the instinct is wrong: dropping is what invites the carrier forward, and every yard the carrier advances unopposed is a yard the covering defender has to give up as well.

## The individual assignment and the defender profile

The full-back facing this profile owns the ball, which means his job is narrower than it looks and considerably harder to execute than a general instruction suggests.

Owning the ball means the carrier does not get past him on the inside, does not get a clean strike from the corner of the box, and does not get to the byline. It does not mean winning the ball, and the distinction matters more than any other coaching point in this article. A full-back attempting to win the ball against a combination pair is a full-back committing, and a committed defender in a two-versus-one is a defender who has removed himself from the sequence. The instruction is to delay, contain, and stay attached, and to make the tackle only in the two situations where it is safe: when the carrier's touch is heavy enough to be genuinely loose, and when the covering defender is already set behind him.

### The profile that copes

The most useful defender against this profile is not the fastest and not the most physical. He is the one with the shortest reaction time from a stationary set position and the discipline to hold a stance he has been told to hold when the stance feels wrong.

Recovery pace matters, because everything in a wide duel eventually reduces to whether a beaten defender can get back into the sequence, but it matters less here than against a pure runner, because this profile beats defenders with a change of direction and a combination rather than with a straight-line burst. What matters more is lateral quickness across the first two steps, since the sequence that hurts is the shift from defending an outside run to defending an inside cut, and that shift lives entirely in the first two steps.

Height and physicality matter least of all, and coaches who select a big, strong full-back for this fixture are usually selecting for the aerial duel that the plan is designed to make harmless.

The most underrated quality is compliance, and it is worth being blunt about it. The stance this plan requires will feel wrong to a good defender, because it involves letting a runner go past on the outside while staying attached to the carrier, which every defensive instinct screams against. A full-back who cannot suppress that instinct will chase the runner in the twentieth minute, and the plan will fail with the whiteboard version intact. Select for the defender who does what he is told when it feels wrong, and if you do not have one, change the plan rather than hoping.

### The stance

The stance is a half-open body position, hips angled so that the inside lane is the more difficult route, with the defender's weight on the balls of the feet and the near foot slightly withdrawn. The distance is roughly two to three yards at the moment of reception, closing to two as the carrier settles.

The reason for angling to protect the inside is that the inside is the higher-value route and the outside is the route the plan has decided to concede. It is deliberately conceded, which is different from being beaten there, and the difference is that a conceded route is a route into a covered space. The whole plan is a machine for converting the dangerous route into the covered one, and the stance is where that conversion starts.

The most common error here is the square stance. A full-back standing square is a full-back who has declined to make a choice, and declining to make a choice hands the choice to the attacker, which is the one outcome the plan exists to prevent.

The detailed technique of the individual duel, the footwork, the touch-tight moments, and the timing of the engagement, belongs to the dedicated treatment of [the one-on-one duel against an inverted wide attacker](/2021/08/15/defending-inverted-wingers-1v1/), and this article deliberately does not re-teach it, because the point being made here is that the duel is not what decides the sequence.

## The overlap: who steps and when

The overlap is the pattern most sides prepare for and the one they defend least badly, so it comes first.

The runner comes from behind and outside, aiming for the space between the full-back and the touchline, arriving level with or beyond the carrier. The defensive answer is that the wide midfielder ahead of the full-back owns that runner, and he owns him from the moment the run starts rather than from the moment the ball is played.

That timing detail is the whole instruction. A wide midfielder who tracks the overlap when the pass is played is always half a second late, and half a second in that channel is five yards, which is the difference between a contested cross and a free one. He has to leave with the run, not with the ball.

The trigger is visual and specific: the moment the overlapping full-back's second stride commits him past his own attacker's shoulder. Before that, the run can still be a decoy and the wide midfielder holds. After it, the run is genuine and the wide midfielder goes, and he goes on a recovery line that takes him goal-side of the runner rather than ball-side, arriving to defend the delivery rather than to make a tackle.

The failure signal for this assignment is unmistakable once you know it: the wide midfielder arrives ball-side, on the inside shoulder of the runner, from where he can neither block the cross nor prevent the byline. Ball-side recovery in a wide channel is the single most common error in amateur football and one of the more common ones at higher levels, because ball-side feels like defending and goal-side feels like retreating.

While the wide midfielder is going, the full-back does not move. That is the second half of the instruction and the half that gets abandoned first. His job has not changed because a runner went past him. He still owns the ball, still stays attached to the carrier, still protects the inside lane. If he turns his head to check the runner, the carrier cuts inside on the head turn, and a good one will do it deliberately.

## The underlap: the assignment nobody rehearses
### Why is the underlap more dangerous than the overlap?

Because it arrives in a shooting position rather than a crossing one, it attacks the seam between two defenders instead of one defender's channel, and it is owned by the centre-back rather than the full-back. Most sides brief the full-back and the wide midfielder thoroughly and never tell the centre-back anything.


The underlap is the pattern that beats most defensive plans, and it beats them because it is defended by a different player and almost nobody tells that player.

The underlapping run comes from behind and inside, between the defending full-back and the near-side centre-back, aiming for the space at the corner of the six-yard box or the penalty spot rather than the byline. It is more dangerous than the overlap for three reasons: it arrives in a shooting position rather than a crossing position, it attacks a seam between two defenders rather than a single defender's channel, and it drags the covering defender into a decision he has usually not been briefed on.

The underlap is owned by the near-side centre-back, and the instruction is that he steps into the inside lane to meet the runner rather than holding the defensive line.

Every word of that sentence costs something and the costs must be accepted explicitly. Stepping means the back four is no longer aligned, which means the offside line is compromised and the far-side centre-back is defending a wider zone alone. It means a striker occupying the centre now has a free run at the space the stepping centre-back vacated. It means the goalkeeper's starting position has to account for a hole in front of him.

Those costs are real, and the alternative costs more. A centre-back who holds the line through an underlap concedes a runner arriving unopposed at the corner of the six-yard box with the ball being delivered flat across him, which is the highest-value chance the pair can manufacture. Holding the line is the instinct, holding the line is what coaching a back four teaches, and holding the line is wrong here.

The trigger is the underlapping runner crossing the imaginary line extending from the near post out through the corner of the penalty area. Before that line he is not yet a threat and the centre-back holds. After it he is, and the centre-back steps.

The holding midfielder has a job attached to this, and it is the job that makes the whole thing survivable: he drops into the space the stepping centre-back vacated, screening the penalty spot. He is not marking anyone. He is occupying a hole, which is a job most midfielders find deeply unsatisfying and which prevents more goals than any tackle he will make all season.

## The collective triggers and the cover behaviour

Three collective behaviours make the individual assignments survivable, and none of them is optional.

The first is the entry-pass shade. The near-side central midfielder does not press the passer who is about to feed the wide attacker. He shades the lane, meaning he positions his body so that the direct pass into the wide zone requires more air or more angle than the passer would like. The purpose is not to intercept, which will happen rarely. The purpose is to delay the entry by a fraction and to force the pass to arrive slightly behind or slightly under-hit, and either of those buys the full-back the half-yard he needs to set his stance before contact. A midfielder who presses the ball instead achieves nothing and removes himself from the covering structure.

The second is the far-side compression. When the ball enters the wide zone on one flank, the opposite full-back tucks inside toward the far post rather than holding width. This is standard defensive organization and it is worth restating because it is the behaviour that makes conceding the cross safe. A cross into a box where the far post is unoccupied is a chance. A cross into a box where the far post has a defender in it is a clearance most of the time. The whole plan is built on choosing which delivery to concede, and the choice is worthless if the box is not organized to receive it.

The third is the rest-defense discipline of the near-side full-back's own attacking position. If your full-back on that side is advanced when possession turns over, none of this applies, because the pair will attack a defensive shape with a hole in it and the covering hierarchy will be one player short. That is a possession-phase decision rather than a defending decision, and it is the reason opposition plans against combination pairs so often fail in matches where the team was otherwise organized: the shape was correct in settled defending and absent in transition.

## The plan in sequence, phase by phase

What follows is the plan as it actually unfolds, narrated in order, so a coach can deliver it as a sequence rather than as a set of rules.

The sequence begins in the middle third with his side in possession and the ball on the opposite flank. At this point the plan is entirely about the entry. The near-side midfielder is shading, the full-back has set his depth relative to the defensive line, and the wide midfielder is holding a position from which he can see both the carrier and the overlapping full-back. Nobody is chasing anything. The single objective of this phase is that the pass into the wide zone arrives at a moment of your choosing rather than his.

The sequence's second phase begins at the reception. The full-back closes to two to three yards, sets the half-open stance protecting the inside, and stops. He does not tackle. The wide midfielder now has one job, which is to watch the overlapping full-back's hips rather than the ball, because the run announces itself in the hips a full stride before it announces itself anywhere else.

The third phase is the fork, and it resolves in one of three ways.

If the overlap comes, the wide midfielder leaves on the second stride, recovers goal-side, and the full-back does not move. The likely outcome is a delivery from wide, which is what the plan wanted.

If the underlap comes, the near-side centre-back steps at the trigger line and the holding midfielder drops into the vacated zone. The likely outcome is a contested reception in a crowded area or a pass that has to be played backward, which is also what the plan wanted.

If neither comes and the carrier attacks the full-back alone, the plan has already won, because a genuine one-versus-one is the outcome with the lowest expected value for the attacking side. The full-back's instruction in this branch is to hold the stance, force the extra touch, and let the near-side central midfielder arrive on the blind side to double once the carrier has committed his second touch. Doubling before the second touch is a foul or a beaten pair; doubling after it is a recovery.

The fourth phase is the delivery. Whichever branch fired, the sequence ends with a ball into the box from a position you chose. Far-side full-back is at the far post. Both centre-backs are attacking the flight if it is aerial and holding their ground if it is flat. The holding midfielder is on the penalty spot. The goalkeeper is set for the delivery type described in the table rather than the delivery type he expects out of habit.

The fifth phase is the second ball, and it is the phase every plan forgets. A cleared delivery lands at the edge of the box, where the attacking side will have stationed a midfielder specifically to shoot or to recycle. Your holding midfielder cannot be there, because he is screening the penalty spot. The near-side central midfielder has to be, which means his recovery run during the delivery phase should end at the top of the box rather than inside it. Sides that concede repeatedly from second balls are almost always sides whose midfielders all ended up inside the penalty area.

## Why the individual scouting report is almost useless here

This is the counter-reading the article has to address, because the individual scouting report is what most sides produce and it is what most sides will produce again next week.

The standard opposition report on a wide attacker contains his preferred direction, his shooting zones, his crossing profile, his first-touch tendencies, and a note on which foot he favours. Every line of it is accurate and the report will not change a single outcome, for a reason that is structural rather than a matter of quality.

The report describes what he does with the ball. The goals come from what happens before he has it, or from what a second attacker does while he has it. A defender armed with perfect knowledge of the carrier's preferences is still, in the moment that matters, a defender facing two attackers with one body, and preferences do not help with arithmetic.

There is a sharper version of the point. Detailed individual scouting can actively harm the plan, because it tempts the full-back into anticipation. A defender who has been told the attacker prefers to cut inside will lean inside, and leaning is a form of committing. The pair's whole function is to punish a committed defender, and the report has just produced one.

The report that helps looks different and takes longer to build. It describes relationships rather than actions. Which run does the full-back behind him make most often, the overlap or the underlap, and does the choice depend on where the ball came from. How early does the run start relative to the reception. Does the carrier hold the ball waiting for the run or release it early and re-run. Which of the two attackers is the decision maker, meaning which one's movement the other reacts to. What happens on the third-man rotation, and how often do they use it.

Those five questions are worth more than twenty on the individual, and they are answered by watching two matches with the eyes off the ball, tracking the two players as a unit. That is a different analytical discipline from player scouting and it is the discipline this profile demands.

For coaches who want to keep those relationship notes in a form that survives the week and can be pulled up on a Friday night, [building the opposition plan free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/football-match-planner.html) keeps the assignments, the triggers, and the concessions in one place rather than scattered across a notebook and a group chat, which is where most relationship-level scouting goes to die.

## The set-piece addendum

Two set-piece dimensions attach to this plan and both are frequently ignored.

The first is that the plan generates corners. A plan that deliberately concedes deliveries from wide positions will produce blocked crosses, and blocked crosses produce corners. A side that contains a wide pair excellently for eighty minutes and defends corners poorly has not contained anything. The corner-defending scheme is therefore part of this plan rather than separate from it, and the specific consideration is that your holding midfielder, who has been screening the penalty spot all match, is now needed in the box, which changes your short-corner coverage. Decide in advance who takes the short-corner responsibility on the near side, because it will not be the player who normally does it.

The second is the attacking set piece and the transition it creates. When your side wins a corner, the wide pair you have spent the match containing is the outlet, and the attacker holding width is the first pass. The rest-defense instruction on your own attacking corners should therefore station a defender specifically on his shoulder, not to mark him in the box, but to be the first contact if the ball is cleared to him. Sides who leave two players back on corners against a side with a fast wide pair are leaving one too few, because the pair does not need a numerical advantage to hurt you, it needs a head start.

Free kicks in the wide areas of your defensive third have a related consideration. A delivery from that zone is the pattern this plan is least equipped to handle, because the pair structure does not exist at a dead ball and the box coverage is a different scheme. Nothing in this plan transfers, and the specific instruction is to avoid conceding fouls in that zone, which means the full-back's non-tackling instruction matters as much for the fouls it prevents as for the duels it wins.

## The transition addendum

The plan assumes a settled defensive shape and the pair is at its most dangerous when the shape is not settled, so the transition case needs its own treatment.

When your side loses the ball in the attacking half with your full-back advanced on that flank, the hierarchy is broken before the sequence starts, because the defender who owns the ball is fifty yards away. The replacement hierarchy must be declared in advance, and the standard version is that the near-side centre-back becomes the ball owner and the holding midfielder becomes the space owner, with the far-side centre-back sliding across to preserve a pair centrally.

That arrangement concedes the width entirely, which is correct: in transition the priority is the central lane and the delivery is survivable. What it must not do is produce the improvised version in which the centre-back steps out and nobody covers behind, which is how a transition against a fast wide pair becomes a shot rather than a cross.

The second transition case is the one that follows your own attacking corner and it is covered above. The third is the case of a turnover in the middle third with the shape roughly intact, where the standard hierarchy applies but the trigger timings compress, and the practical instruction is that the wide midfielder leaves earlier than he would in a settled sequence, because there is no time for the second-stride confirmation.

## The fallbacks: what to do when the first plan fails

Every counter-plan needs a documented failure route, and this one has three, ordered by cost.

The first fallback is a stance change without a personnel change. If the pair is beating the plan by consistently reaching the byline, the full-back's stance moves from protecting the inside to protecting the outside, and the covering job flips: the near-side centre-back now covers the cut inside rather than the underlap, and the wide midfielder tucks in rather than recovering wide. This concedes the inside cut and the shot from the corner of the box, deliberately, in exchange for removing the byline. It is a bad trade in general and an acceptable one against a pair whose cutback is producing chances and whose shooting is not.

The second fallback is the third defender, meaning the near-side central midfielder is reassigned permanently to the wide zone. Your midfield is now a two in central areas and you have effectively conceded control of the middle third. This is a large price and it is the correct price when the wide pair is the opposition's only route to goal, which is more often true than coaches admit. Against a side with one dominant flank and no central threat, moving a midfielder permanently to that flank is the highest-value adjustment available and it should be made at half time rather than in the seventieth minute.

The third fallback is the shape change to a back five, with the wide midfielder becoming a wing-back and the near-side centre-back becoming a wide centre-back. This resolves the geometry cleanly, for the reason described in the wider cluster material: a wide centre-back can step into the inside lane without breaking a line, because two centre-backs remain behind him. It costs the middle third almost entirely and it costs your attacking outlet on that flank. Make it when you are protecting a result rather than chasing one, and make it as a planned substitution rather than a reshuffle, because a back five improvised by pushing a midfielder into defence is a back five with no rehearsed handovers.

## What to concede deliberately

Naming the concessions is the part of a counter-plan that coaches skip and players need most, because a concession that has not been named will be treated as a mistake by whoever concedes it, and a player who thinks he has made a mistake stops executing the plan.

Concede the crossed ball from the wide area. This is the plan's central trade. You have organized a box with a far-side full-back at the back post, two centre-backs attacking the flight, a holding midfielder on the penalty spot, and a goalkeeper set for the delivery type. A delivery into that box is a low-probability chance and you should be content to face twenty of them. Say the number out loud in the meeting, because a team that has been told to expect twenty crosses will not panic at the eighth.

Concede the corner. Blocked crosses become corners and the plan produces blocked crosses by design. A side that treats each conceded corner as a failure will start stepping out to intercept crosses, and stepping out is how the pair gets in behind.

Concede the recycled possession. When the underlap is covered and the overlap is tracked, the pair's remaining option is a pass backward and a reset. That is a win, and it will look like sustained pressure on the touchline because sustained pressure and sustained containment produce identical highlight footage. Judge the plan on entries into the penalty area and shots from central zones rather than on territory.

Concede the throw-in in your defensive third. The full-back's non-tackling instruction means the ball will frequently go out for a throw rather than being won cleanly. That is the correct outcome and it should be praised rather than tolerated.

Do not concede the underlap, the flat cutback to the penalty spot, or the free strike from the corner of the box. Those three are the plan's actual failure conditions and everything else is noise.

## How the plan degrades with a weaker squad

Every counter-plan in this series has to survive contact with a side that does not have the personnel to run it, because most sides do not, and a plan that only works with elite players is a description rather than a plan.

The first thing to give up is the entry-pass shade. It requires a central midfielder with the discipline to position rather than press, and that discipline is rare below a certain level. Without it the reception happens cleanly and the full-back has to set his stance under more pressure, which is survivable.

The second thing to give up is the stepping centre-back on the underlap, and this is the painful one, because the underlap is the dangerous pattern. The reason to give it up is that a stepping centre-back who does not know when to step is worse than a centre-back who holds the line, since a mistimed step produces a runner in behind with the line broken and nobody covering. If your centre-back cannot execute the trigger reliably, instruct him to hold the line and instruct the holding midfielder to take the underlapping runner instead. This is materially worse, because a midfielder recovering from in front is late by definition, and it is better than a broken line.

The third adjustment is the one that saves an amateur side more goals than any other: reduce the number of triggers to one. A team that cannot hold three assignments should hold one, and the one to hold is the wide midfielder tracking the overlap goal-side. That single behaviour, coached until it is automatic, removes the most common goal in wide-pair football at amateur level, which is a full-back turned by a runner going outside and a cutback arriving with the defence facing its own goal.

The fourth is the amateur-level shape recommendation, and it is a back five rather than a back four. At elite level a back five costs too much elsewhere. At amateur level the middle third is contested less systematically, the cost of conceding it is lower, and the geometric resolution the back five provides is worth more, because it removes the decision that amateur defenders make wrongly. Teach the wing-back to own the ball, the wide centre-back to own the space, and accept that your attacking play down that side is finished for the afternoon.

The fifth applies to a side with a genuinely weak full-back on that flank, which is the most common situation of all. Do not try to protect him with a plan; move him. Put your best defensive player on that side regardless of his usual position and accept the disruption elsewhere. A plan that depends on a defender who cannot execute the stance is a plan that will produce three goals, and no amount of covering structure compensates for a defender who is beaten before the covering structure activates.

### How should an amateur coach install this in one session?

Coach the goal-side recovery run first and coach nothing else until it is automatic. Set a wide two-versus-two with a target zone at the byline, and score the defending pair on whether the covering defender arrives goal-side rather than on whether the ball is won. Add the underlap trigger only once the recovery habit holds.

## Reading the debate honestly

Five positions recur in discussions of this profile and each contains something worth engaging.

The position that he is dependent on the full-back behind him gets the mechanism right and the judgment wrong. The relationship is genuine and it is the reason this article is structured around a pair rather than a player. Calling it dependency implies a deficiency, and it is better described as a designed relationship, since the alternative version of the same attacker, one who takes on defenders in isolation without support, produces lower-value chances at a lower rate. A wide attacker who combines is not a wide attacker who cannot dribble; he is one whose team has organized the higher-percentage route and rehearsed it.

The position that he is easier to defend when forced outside gets the direction right and the confidence wrong. Forcing outside does reduce the highest-value action, which is the arrival in a shooting position, and it substitutes the delivery, which is lower value. What the position misses is that forcing outside is exactly what activates the overlap, because the space outside is where the second attacker is going. Forcing outside without a covering defender assigned to the overlap is not a plan, it is the plan's failure mode described as a plan.

The position that his end product is inconsistent is the one this article declines to engage on its own terms, because output variance in a wide attacker across small samples is not a property of the player in any way that a defending coach can use. What is usable is the structural version: a wide pair's output depends heavily on whether the opposition covers the underlap, and against sides that cover it well the numbers fall. That is a statement about opponents rather than about the attacker, and it is the only version of the observation worth putting on a whiteboard.

The position that doubling him up removes the threat gets the arithmetic exactly backward. Doubling a combining attacker is what he wants, because the double is what creates the space his partner is already running into. The plan in this article is not a doubling plan; it is an assignment plan, and the distinction is that a double concentrates two defenders on the ball while an assignment separates them onto the ball and the space. Sides who double this profile concede more, not less, and they concede in exactly the zone the second defender left.

The position that he is a system product is a version of an argument this series meets constantly, and the honest response is that every wide attacker's output is a function of the structure around him and that the test is relative rather than absolute. What is specific and true here is that this profile's structure requires a genuinely attacking full-back on his side and a central occupation that keeps the covering centre-back honest, and that both of those are jobs somebody else performs. Whether that counts as being a system product is a question about words rather than football.

## The centre-back's underlap job, in full

The single most commonly missed assignment in wide defending deserves its own treatment, because a coach who gets only this one thing right from this article will concede fewer goals immediately.

The centre-back's default programming is horizontal. He holds a line with his partner, he steps and drops as a unit, and every hour of defensive coaching he has received since childhood has reinforced that the line is the thing you do not break. The underlap asks him to break it, and it asks him to break it forward and diagonally, which is the direction that feels most dangerous.

The mechanics of the step are specific. He does not sprint at the runner. He opens his hips toward the touchline, takes two or three controlled strides into the inside lane, and arrives with his body between the runner and the goal rather than between the runner and the ball. The objective is not to intercept the pass and it is not to tackle. The objective is to make the pass into the runner require more accuracy than the carrier can guarantee under pressure, and to be positioned so that if the pass arrives anyway, the runner's first touch has to go away from goal.

The timing is governed by the trigger line already stated, the line running from the near post out through the corner of the penalty area, and it needs one refinement. If the carrier's head is up and his body is open, the centre-back steps at the trigger. If the carrier's head is down and he is taking a touch to steady himself, the centre-back holds an extra half-second, because the pass cannot come yet and stepping early gives the carrier a target to play behind.

The recovery is the part nobody coaches. When the step is made and the pass does not come, the centre-back has to get back into the line, and he has to do it without turning his back on the ball. The correct recovery is a backward diagonal with the body still half-open, which is slower than a sprint and which keeps him available if the ball is played into the space he is returning to. Centre-backs who turn and sprint back into line concede the ball over the top, because for the two strides of the turn they cannot see it.

The relationship with the goalkeeper matters here. A goalkeeper who knows the underlap step is part of the plan should be positioned higher than his default whenever the ball is in that wide zone, because the space in front of him is the space the stepping centre-back has left, and a ball played into it is a ball he should be attacking rather than reacting to. That is a conversation to have before the match, in specific terms, because a goalkeeper surprised by a hole in front of him will stay on his line and the hole becomes a chance.

## The goalkeeper's job

Goalkeepers are usually briefed on a wide attacker's shooting preferences and almost never on the delivery types a defensive plan is designed to produce, which is backwards, because the plan determines what he will actually face.

The first instruction is the near-post reference. When the plan forces the carrier outside and he strikes from a narrow angle, the goalkeeper's job is to hold the near post and force the far-post finish, which from that angle is a very difficult strike. The failure here is the goalkeeper who drifts toward the middle in anticipation of a cross, leaving the near post available, and it is a failure produced by not knowing what the plan is conceding.

The second is the flat cutback. This delivery does not pull the goalkeeper toward his line the way a curled cross does, and his correct action is to hold position and stay available for the strike that follows, rather than committing forward. Goalkeepers who attack flat cutbacks are attacking a ball that is travelling away from them and across a crowded area, and they arrive late in a place where their absence is fatal.

The third is the covering position when the centre-back steps. Already covered, and worth restating because it is the instruction goalkeepers most often have not received.

The fourth is the communication, and it is the highest-value goalkeeping contribution in this entire plan. The goalkeeper is the only player who can see the underlapping runner, the stepping centre-back, and the carrier simultaneously. His call is what tells the centre-back the runner has crossed the trigger line, and a goalkeeper who makes that call reliably is worth more to this plan than any single defender's technique. The call has to be short, agreed in advance, and specific to the runner rather than general, because "man on" tells a defender nothing about which man.

## What each player needs to hear in the meeting

Opposition meetings fail when everyone hears everything, because a player who has been given eleven instructions retains none. The delivery below is what each position actually needs.

The full-back on that side hears three sentences. You own the ball. You do not turn your head. You will be beaten on the outside deliberately and somebody is behind you.

The wide midfielder ahead of him hears two. You leave on the second stride of the overlap, not on the pass. You recover goal-side, never ball-side.

The near-side centre-back hears three. You step at the trigger line. You arrive between the runner and the goal, not between the runner and the ball. You recover backward and half-open, and you do not turn.

The far-side centre-back hears one. You are defending a wider zone than usual because your partner is stepping, and the offside line is not your priority while that is happening.

The holding midfielder hears two. When the centre-back steps, you occupy the space he left. You are not marking anyone and you will feel useless, which is correct.

The near-side central midfielder hears two. Shade the entry pass rather than pressing the passer. Your recovery run in the delivery phase ends at the top of the box, not inside it.

The far-side full-back hears one. Tuck to the far post every time the ball enters the opposite wide zone.

The goalkeeper hears three. Hold the near post on the narrow strike. Do not attack the flat cutback. Call the underlapping runner across the trigger line, by name.

The centre-forward hears one that has nothing to do with defending and matters as much as any of the above. When we clear, the outlet is the opposite flank, because their attacking full-back on this side is the player furthest from his own goal.

That last instruction is the one that turns a containment plan into a match plan. Containing a wide pair means the opposition full-back is high for ninety minutes, and a high full-back is an exploitable absence. The counter-attacking route against a side built around a wide pair is almost always the flank the pair occupies, which is counterintuitive and correct. The full development of that attacking argument belongs to the article on [attacking the modern full-back](/2024/03/01/attacking-the-modern-full-back/), which owns the offensive side of this relationship and should be read alongside this one by any coach who wants the plan to produce goals rather than only prevent them.

## Reading whether the plan is working, in-match
### What does successful containment look like from the touchline?

Like being under siege. They keep the ball in your defensive third, complete passes along the touchline, and cross repeatedly, and your full-back is beaten on the outside deliberately. Judge it on penalty-area entries and central shots rather than on territory, or somebody will abandon a working plan.


Coaches usually judge a containment plan by whether the attacker has scored, which is a lagging indicator with enormous variance and which will mislead you in both directions. Four leading indicators tell you within twenty minutes.

The first is the count of underlapping runs that arrived unopposed. This should be zero. One is a warning, two means the trigger is not being executed and you should switch to the holding-midfielder version described in the degradation section.

The second is the location of deliveries. If they are arriving from the byline and flat, the plan is failing in a specific way, because the byline is the position you decided to concede only after the wide midfielder had recovered goal-side. Deliveries from deeper and at a crossing angle mean the plan is working.

The third is the direction of the full-back's head. This is visible from the touchline and it is the single most diagnostic thing you can watch. A full-back checking over his shoulder for the overlapping runner is a full-back who has stopped trusting the wide midfielder, and the moment he starts checking, the carrier will cut inside on the head turn. Fix it by telling the wide midfielder to call, loudly and every time, that he has the runner.

The fourth is where your holding midfielder is standing when a cross comes in. If he is inside the six-yard box, the second-ball zone is unoccupied and you will concede from the edge. If he is on the penalty spot, the plan is being run.

None of these four requires data, video, or an assistant with a tablet. All four are visible from the technical area within the first quarter of an hour, which is when adjustments are cheap.

## The near-post shot and the cutback, defended as separate problems

Two final actions attach to this profile and both get defended as if they were one, which is why sides that contain the combination still concede.

The near-post strike arises when the plan works. The carrier has been forced outside, has not been able to reach the byline because the wide midfielder recovered, and takes the shot from a narrowing angle because the alternative is running the ball out of play. This is the plan's intended outcome and it is only dangerous if two things go wrong: the goalkeeper drifts off the near post in anticipation of a cross, or the full-back dives into a block.

The block is the more common failure and it needs an explicit instruction. A defender sliding into a shot from a narrow angle achieves one of three things, and two are bad. He may block it cleanly, which is good. He may deflect it, which is worse than not blocking, because a deflected shot from that angle wrong-foots a goalkeeper who had correctly set for the original line. Or he may miss and take himself out of the play, leaving the second ball uncontested. The instruction is to close the angle standing, with feet, keeping the body upright and the legs closed, and to let the goalkeeper handle a strike he can see. Standing up in that duel is unglamorous and it is right.

The cutback is a different problem entirely and it arrives from a different position, infield of the goal line rather than on it, travelling flat and fast across the top of the six-yard box toward the penalty spot. The reason it is dangerous is that every defender in the box is facing the wrong way and moving toward his own goal, while every attacker arriving is facing goal and moving forward. Reversing a defender's momentum takes longer than starting an attacker's, which is the whole physics of the pattern.

Defending it requires one player who never turns toward his own goal, and that player is the holding midfielder on the penalty spot. His body is open to the field, he is watching the delivery and the arriving runner simultaneously, and he is the only player in the box in a position to attack a flat ball moving across him. Everyone else is a recovering defender.

The second requirement is that the near-side centre-back does not follow the ball toward the near post. His instinct will be to attack the delivery at its entry point. The delivery is not aimed there, it is aimed behind him, and a centre-back who follows the ball leaves the exact zone the pattern targets.

The third is that the full-back who deliberately conceded the byline has a specific recovery destination, which is the near post rather than the ball. He arrives to cover the goalkeeper's near-post zone so the goalkeeper can move across for the flat delivery. Full-backs who chase the ball into the corner after conceding the byline are chasing something that has already gone.

## Scouting the relationship: what to actually watch

Analysis method belongs elsewhere in this series, and the narrow version relevant to this plan is worth stating because the plan cannot be built without it.

Watch two matches with the ball ignored. Track the wide attacker and the full-back behind him as a single object. Count four things.

Count the ratio of overlaps to underlaps, and note whether the choice correlates with where the ball came from. Many pairs use the overlap when the ball arrives from a switch and the underlap when it arrives from a central midfielder, because the underlap needs the defensive line to be already engaged.

Count how many strides before the reception the run begins. A run that starts before the pass is a rehearsed pattern and will happen every time. A run that starts after the reception is reactive and can be discouraged by pressure on the carrier.

Note which of the two makes the decision. In some pairs the wide attacker holds the ball and the full-back runs off him; in others the full-back's run comes first and the attacker's touch reacts to it. This determines whether pressure on the carrier disrupts the pattern, and it is the single most useful thing the report can contain.

Note the third-man rotation frequency. A pair that releases inside and re-runs outside is a pair that will beat a hierarchy built for two patterns, and if it appears more than a handful of times you need the verbal re-declaration written into the plan rather than assumed.

The general question of whether to attack a full-back at all, and how a coach decides which flank to target, belongs to the decision article on [attacking the full-back or avoiding him](/2024/07/15/attack-the-full-back-or-avoid-him/), and this article deliberately does not answer it, because it is a different decision with a different owner.

## The second fixture

A plan executed once is information the opponent now holds, and the pair will have rehearsed a response.

The likely response to a goal-side tracking wide midfielder is the underlap, because the overlap has been closed and the underlap attacks the seam the tracking creates. The likely response to a stepping centre-back is the third-man rotation, because a stepping defender can be baited and released. The likely response to the entry-pass shade is a longer switch played over the shading midfielder rather than through him.

The instruction for the second fixture is the same as it is across this series: change the condition and keep the concession. If you tracked goal-side in the first fixture, consider committing the near-side central midfielder permanently to the wide zone in the second, which is a different arrangement producing a similar concession. The rehearsed response will be aimed at a plan you are no longer running, and the fifteen minutes it takes them to recognize that is worth more than any individual duel.

The corollary is that you need a record of what you actually did, in the detail of triggers and concessions rather than in the general form of "we doubled up on him." Plans that are not written down are re-invented every time, and re-invented plans are the ones that fail in the twentieth minute.

## The verdict

The plan for stopping this profile is a hierarchy rather than a marking scheme, and the hierarchy is worth more than any individual defender's quality.

Run a mid-block with a back four and a wide midfielder ahead of the full-back on his side. The full-back owns the ball, holds a half-open stance protecting the inside, does not tackle, and does not turn his head. The wide midfielder owns the overlap, leaves on the runner's second stride rather than on the pass, and recovers goal-side without exception. The near-side centre-back owns the underlap, steps into the inside lane at the trigger line, and arrives between the runner and the goal. The holding midfielder occupies the vacated central space and screens the penalty spot. The near-side central midfielder shades the entry pass rather than pressing it and finishes his recovery run at the top of the box. The far-side full-back tucks to the far post every time. The goalkeeper holds the near post on the narrow strike, does not attack the flat cutback, and calls the underlapping runner by name.

Concede the delivery from wide, the corner, the throw-in, and the recycled possession. Refuse the underlap, the flat cutback to the penalty spot, and the free strike from the corner of the box.

If it fails, flip the stance and cover before you change personnel, then commit a midfielder permanently to the flank, then change shape to a back five as a planned substitution rather than a reshuffle.

If your squad cannot run all of it, run one behaviour: the wide midfielder recovering goal-side. That single habit removes the most common goal in wide-pair football at every level below the elite, and it is coachable in a session.

The reason the plan is built this way, rather than around a defender's duel technique, is the claim this article has argued throughout. This is not a player you defend. It is a pair, with a fixed hierarchy of ball and space, decided before kick off, because two defenders left to improvise will both go to the ball and the goal will come from the space they left. The general version of that argument, applied across the whole family of elite wide attackers rather than to this one, belongs to the cluster's [complete guide to stopping elite wingers](/2021/04/01/stopping-elite-wingers-complete-guide/), which is the right place to start if you are building a defensive philosophy rather than a single match plan.

## When the pair separates: the reception in the inside channel

The plan so far assumes the wide attacker starts wide, which is his default and not his only starting point. The pattern that breaks a wide-pair plan is the one where he abandons the width entirely and appears in the inside channel between the opposition midfield and defensive lines, receiving on the half-turn with the full-back holding the width behind him.

That reversal is dangerous precisely because it inverts the hierarchy. The defender who owns the ball is now not the full-back, because the ball is not in the full-back's zone. The player nearest the reception is a central midfielder who has been told to shade rather than press, and who is therefore in exactly the wrong body position to contest a reception in front of him.

The answer is a declared handover rather than an improvised one, and it needs three components.

The first is a named owner for the inside channel. In a mid-block with a back four, that owner is the near-side central midfielder, and his instruction changes when the attacker leaves the touchline: he stops shading the lane and starts marking the receiver, tightly enough to contest the reception, from the moment the attacker's starting position moves inside the width of the penalty area. The shading job is now performed by the holding midfielder instead, who slides across.

The second is the full-back's instruction, which is not to follow. This is the discipline that fails most often. A full-back who follows an attacker inside vacates the widest channel on the pitch, and the attacking full-back holding width behind will receive the switch with the entire flank empty. The full-back's job when his direct opponent goes inside is to hold the width and pick up whoever arrives there, which will normally be the overlapping full-back, and the pair has now been separated onto two defenders in two zones, which is the outcome you want.

The third is the centre-back's adjustment. With the receiver in the inside channel, the underlap threat has become a runner from behind him rather than beside him, and the stepping trigger changes: the centre-back now steps to the receiver's blind side at the moment of the reception rather than at a spatial trigger line. The effect is that the receiver's half-turn is contested from a direction he cannot see, which is the highest-value pressure available against any half-turn reception.

The general principle is worth stating because it applies well beyond this profile. A pair is dangerous when it occupies two defenders in one zone. Separating the pair into two zones is the objective of every wide-defending plan, and an attacker moving inside has done half of that job for you, provided your full-back holds the width he vacated. Sides that follow the attacker inside have converted a favourable separation back into an overload, and they have done it out of an instinct that feels like conscientious defending.

## The plan across ninety minutes

A containment plan has a fatigue profile and a match-state profile, and both should be planned rather than discovered.

The fatigue cost of this plan is concentrated on two players. The wide midfielder tracking overlaps goal-side is performing repeated high-intensity recovery runs in one direction, which is among the most demanding patterns in football because the runs are decelerating and lengthening rather than steady. The near-side full-back is performing repeated set-and-hold defensive actions with almost no attacking outlet, which is less demanding physically and considerably more demanding in concentration, and concentration is what degrades first.

The practical consequences are two. Plan a substitution for the wide midfielder around the hour mark and prepare the replacement specifically, meaning he has rehearsed the goal-side recovery and has been told the trigger, because a fresh player who does not know the assignment is worse than a tired one who does. And accept that the full-back's concentration will lapse somewhere between the sixtieth and seventy-fifth minute, which is when the head-turn failure appears, and instruct the goalkeeper and the wide midfielder to increase their calling in that window rather than hoping.

The match-state profile is the more interesting planning problem.

Leading by one goal, the plan should tighten rather than deepen. The temptation is to drop the line, and dropping the line delivers the pair into the box, which is the trade already argued against. The correct tightening is to commit the near-side central midfielder permanently to the wide zone, accepting the loss of the middle third because you no longer need it.

Level, the plan runs as written.

Trailing, the plan has to be partially abandoned, and it should be abandoned deliberately rather than eroded. A side chasing a goal cannot keep a wide midfielder performing pure defensive tracking, because that is the player it needs high. The honest adjustment is to accept a one-versus-two on that flank, move the covering responsibility entirely to the near-side centre-back for both patterns, and instruct the full-back to delay rather than contain, buying time for a central recovery rather than attempting to hold the zone. That concedes a great deal and it is the correct concession when the alternative is losing anyway.

The substitution to prepare for on the opposition side is the change of the full-back behind him, because a different partner changes the pattern mix. A more attacking replacement usually raises the overlap frequency; a more conservative one usually raises the frequency of the attacker receiving in the inside channel. Watch the first three sequences after any change on that flank rather than assuming continuity.

## Six ways this plan fails

Every failure mode below has been described somewhere above, and collecting them is worth a section because a coach reviewing a conceded goal needs a checklist rather than a narrative.

The first is the ball-side recovery. The wide midfielder arrives on the inside shoulder of the overlapping runner rather than goal-side, from where he can block nothing and tackle nothing. Cause: instinct, and the feeling that goal-side recovery is giving up. Fix: coach it as the only acceptable line and score it in training independently of the outcome.

The second is the held line through an underlap. The centre-back stays in the back four and the runner arrives unopposed at the corner of the six-yard box. Cause: the line is the deepest habit in defending and the trigger has usually not been rehearsed under fatigue. Fix: the trigger line, rehearsed, or the fallback in which the holding midfielder takes the runner instead.

The third is the head turn. The full-back checks his shoulder for the overlap and the carrier cuts inside on the turn. Cause: loss of trust in the covering player, usually after the covering player has been late once. Fix: constant verbal confirmation from the wide midfielder, and a substitution if trust does not return.

The fourth is the converging double. Both defenders go to the ball, the space behind is unoccupied, and both are certain the other had it. Cause: no declared hierarchy, or a hierarchy declared but not rehearsed under pressure. Fix: the hierarchy, stated in the meeting in the exact words each player will use.

The fifth is the holding midfielder in the six-yard box. The cross is cleared to the edge of the area and the recycled shot arrives from an unoccupied zone. Cause: the instinct to defend the ball rather than the space. Fix: give him the penalty spot as a physical location rather than a job description.

The sixth is the broken transition hierarchy. Possession turns over with the near-side full-back advanced and the replacement hierarchy has not been declared, so the centre-back steps out and nobody covers behind. Cause: rest-defense planning treated as a possession-phase problem and therefore never covered in the defensive meeting. Fix: state the transition hierarchy in the same breath as the settled one, every time.

The pattern across all six is the same, which is why the framework matters more than any individual instruction. Five of the six are failures of assignment rather than failures of ability. The defender in each case did something defensible, and the goal came from the gap between two defensible actions. That gap is what a hierarchy closes, and closing it is available to any side at any level, because it costs nothing but agreement.

## The plan against different attacking structures

The hierarchy is stable and the personnel change depending on what the opposition puts around the pair, and a coach who does not adjust will find the assignments pointing at nobody.

Against a side using an attacking full-back and a central midfielder who also rotates into the wide zone, the pair becomes a trio and the arithmetic changes again. The correct response is not to add a third defender, which is how a shape unravels, but to hand the third attacker to the holding midfielder as a marking assignment and accept that the penalty-spot screen is now unmanned. That is a genuine concession and it is smaller than the alternative, because a trio in the wide zone is only a trio for as long as the ball stays there, and the moment the delivery comes, the third attacker is a long way from goal.

Against a side using an inverted full-back who tucks into midfield rather than overlapping, the pair does not exist in the wide zone at all, and this plan is largely unnecessary. What replaces it is a straightforward one-versus-one with a covering centre-back, because the wide attacker is genuinely isolated. Sides that keep running a wide-pair plan against an inverted full-back are defending a pattern that is not occurring, and they are doing it with a wide midfielder who should be pressing the inverted full-back in central areas instead.

Against a side with a centre-forward who consistently drags the near-side centre-back across, the underlap step becomes much harder, because the centre-back has an occupied assignment before the runner arrives. The adjustment is to hand the underlap to the holding midfielder permanently rather than as a fallback, and to accept that his coverage will be late, compensating by having the far-side centre-back slide earlier than he otherwise would. This is the arrangement most elite sides actually use against elite combination pairs, and it is worse than the stepping version in isolation and better than it in context.

Against a back three that produces the wide pair as a wing-back and a wide forward, the combination arrives with more depth and less width. The wing-back starts deeper, which means the overlap has more distance to cover and the trigger fires later, and it means the wide midfielder can hold longer. The underlap is correspondingly more dangerous, because the wide forward in a back three tends to start narrower and the seam between full-back and centre-back is where he already lives. Weight the plan toward the underlap and relax the overlap tracking, which is the opposite of the weighting against a back four.

Against a side that switches the pair to the opposite flank, either through personnel or through the attacker drifting across, the hierarchy has to be mirrored and the mirror has to be rehearsed. Most sides rehearse one side and defend the other by improvisation, and improvisation on the unrehearsed side is where the goal comes from. If your opponent has a genuine combination pair on both flanks, you cannot run the full plan on both, and the correct decision is to run it on the stronger side and run a simple deep-cover arrangement on the weaker one, because a plan half-executed on both flanks is worse than a plan fully executed on one.

## What successful containment actually looks like from the touchline

Coaches watching this plan work for the first time frequently believe it is failing, and the reason is that successful containment against a combination pair is visually indistinguishable from sustained pressure.

The opposition will have the ball in your defensive third repeatedly. They will complete passes along the touchline. They will cross. Your full-back will be beaten on the outside multiple times, deliberately. The crowd, the bench, and the players themselves will experience this as being under siege, and if nobody has been told what success looks like in advance, the plan will be abandoned by somebody, usually the full-back, who will start stepping out to intercept because standing still while an opponent runs past feels like failure.

What success looks like, stated in advance and in specific terms, is this. The pair reaches the byline sometimes and the delivery arrives into a box with a far-post defender and a penalty-spot screen. The underlap does not arrive unopposed. Shots come from wide angles and from the top of the box rather than from the corner of the six-yard box. Possession is recycled backward from the wide zone more often than it enters the penalty area. The scoreline stays where it is.

The point of naming those markers before kick off is not motivational. It is that a plan built on deliberate concessions requires everyone to recognize the concession when it happens, and a team that has not been briefed will interpret its own plan as a collapse. This is the reason opposition meetings should end with the concession list rather than the assignment list, and it is the single most common omission in the meetings this series has seen described.

## Rehearsing it

Session design belongs elsewhere in this series and the outline below is the minimum needed to make the plan real rather than theoretical.

The core practice is a wide two-versus-two into a target zone, with the defending pair scored on assignment execution rather than on the outcome. That scoring rule is the whole thing. A defending pair scored on whether the ball was won will converge on the ball, which is the behaviour the plan exists to prevent. A pair scored on whether the covering defender arrived goal-side and whether the ball owner stayed attached will produce the behaviour even in the repetitions they lose.

Add the underlap only once the overlap habit is automatic, and add it with the runner starting from a fixed position so the trigger line is unambiguous before it is made variable. Add the third-man rotation last, and add it specifically to break the hierarchy, so that the verbal re-declaration becomes a rehearsed habit rather than a paragraph in a document.

Run it under fatigue at least once, because every failure mode in this plan appears under fatigue and none of them appear in a fresh eight-minute block at the start of a session. A pair that executes perfectly in the first practice and converges on the ball in the last is a pair that will convert perfectly for an hour on Saturday and concede in the seventy-second minute.

## Teaching wide two-versus-two to young players

The hierarchy is one of the few elite defensive concepts that transfers downward almost unchanged, and it transfers because it is a decision rather than a skill. A twelve-year-old cannot execute an elite full-back's jockeying technique and can execute an agreement about who owns the ball perfectly well, which is why this is among the highest-return things a youth coach can teach.

The teaching order matters and the usual order is wrong. Most youth coaching starts with the individual duel, teaching a defender to stay on his feet, show one way, and delay. That is a good thing to teach and it is the wrong thing to teach first, because the situation a young defender actually faces most often on a wide area is not a duel. It is two attackers and one defender, and a child who has been taught duel technique and nothing else will apply duel technique to a situation duel technique cannot solve, and will conclude he is bad at defending.

Start instead with the pair. Two defenders, two attackers, a wide channel, and one instruction: one of you takes the ball, one of you takes the space, decide before it starts and say it out loud. Children will get this immediately and will violate it immediately, because the ball is magnetic, and the coaching is entirely in the repetitions where both go to the ball. Stop the practice, ask who had the space, and let them answer. They always know once they are asked.

The second concept is goal-side recovery, and it should be taught as a physical rule rather than a tactical one, because the tactical explanation is too abstract at a young age. The rule is: when someone runs past you, run to the goal, not to the ball. Say it in those words. It is not a complete description of a recovery line and it produces the correct behaviour in about ninety percent of situations, which is a good trade for an instruction a child can remember while sprinting.

The third concept is the one most youth coaches never introduce and the one that produces the biggest immediate improvement: the centre-back stepping across into the inside lane. Young centre-backs are taught to hold a line before they are taught anything about zones, and a line held through an underlap concedes the easiest goal in youth football. Teach the step as a specific, named behaviour with a physical trigger, and teach it in a practice where the runner comes from behind the full-back rather than in front, so the pattern is recognized rather than reasoned about.

There are two things not to teach at that age. Do not teach doubling up, because it is the wrong solution and children who learn it will apply it for years. And do not teach the offside line as a priority during a wide sequence, because the two instructions conflict and the child will resolve the conflict by holding the line, which is exactly the failure the step exists to prevent.

The wider benefit is that a group taught the pair concept early will apply it in every zone of the pitch without being told, because the underlying idea generalizes. Two defenders and two attackers in a central area, in a corner, at a throw-in, and in a counter-attack are all the same problem, and the answer in every case is a hierarchy declared before the sequence rather than negotiated during it. Coaches who teach it once at eleven years old have players at sixteen who organize themselves, which is worth more than any individual technique on the syllabus.

The one adaptation for small-sided formats is that the covering defender is often the goalkeeper, since there may not be a spare outfield player. That works, and it should be taught explicitly rather than allowed to happen by accident, because a goalkeeper who understands that he owns the space behind a beaten defender will start positioning for it, and a goalkeeper who has been taught to stay on his line will not.

## What the plan costs you going forward

Every defensive plan is also an attacking decision, and this one is expensive in a way that coaches routinely fail to price.

The wide midfielder performing goal-side recovery runs for ninety minutes is not an attacking outlet. He starts deeper than he would otherwise, he spends his high-intensity running budget on retreats rather than advances, and by the time your side wins possession he is thirty yards from where an attacking wide midfielder wants to be. Whatever your attacking plan is, it cannot route through that player.

The full-back on that side is similarly removed. He is holding a stance, he is not tackling, and he is under instruction to stay attached to a carrier who is between him and the rest of the pitch. He will not overlap, he will not receive high, and he will contribute almost nothing to the attacking phase. That is two of your eleven effectively withdrawn from the attack for the duration.

The near-side centre-back is stepping and recovering repeatedly, which affects his ability to step out with the ball into midfield, a contribution many modern sides depend on. The holding midfielder is occupying a screening position rather than a receiving one, which reduces the number of clean build-up options in front of your defence.

Add those together and the honest accounting is that this plan removes roughly a third of your side from meaningful attacking contribution on one flank. A coach who runs it without adjusting the attacking plan will contain the pair beautifully and create nothing, and will lose to a set piece or a mistake.

The adjustment is straightforward once the cost is stated. Route your attacking plan entirely through the opposite flank, and do it deliberately rather than by default. That means your opposite full-back is high, your opposite wide player holds width, and your centre-forward's first movement on any turnover is toward that side of the pitch. It also means your goalkeeper and centre-backs should be distributing to that side rather than playing the ball short into the congested flank you are defending.

There is a second and better opportunity available, and it is the counterintuitive one already mentioned. The flank you are containing is the flank where the opposition full-back spends the entire match in an advanced position, because that is what the combination requires. He is therefore the player on the pitch furthest from his own goal at every turnover. The space behind him is the largest available space on the field, and it is available every single time you win the ball.

Exploiting it requires one player willing to stay high on that side while you defend, which conflicts directly with the containment plan and has to be resolved deliberately. The resolution most sides should choose is the centre-forward: he does not track back, he holds a position on the shoulder of the near-side centre-back angled toward that flank, and he becomes the target for the first clearance. That gives up a body in your own defensive third, which the plan can afford, because the plan's numbers in the wide zone are already adequate and the extra body was going to be the least useful defender in the box anyway.

The instruction to the goalkeeper and the centre-backs completes it. Clearances go long and to that channel rather than out for a throw or straight down the middle. A cleared ball into the space behind an advanced full-back, with your centre-forward already running, converts a defensive sequence into an attacking one in a single action, and it does it in the moment the opposition is least organized, since their two most attacking players are both in your defensive third.

That is the reason to spend a whole article on containment rather than treating it as damage limitation. A plan that contains a wide pair correctly does not only prevent goals. It manufactures the single most reliable attacking opportunity available against a side built that way, and a coach who prepares only the defensive half of it has done the hard work and declined the reward.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you stop Bukayo Saka?

You stop the pair rather than the player. Run a mid-block with a back four and a wide midfielder ahead of the full-back on his side. The full-back owns the ball, sets a half-open stance protecting the inside, does not tackle and does not turn his head. The wide midfielder owns the overlapping runner and leaves on the runner's second stride, recovering goal-side rather than ball-side. The near-side centre-back owns the underlap and steps into the inside lane at the trigger. The holding midfielder occupies the space the centre-back vacates and screens the penalty spot. Concede the delivery from wide into a box you have organized, and refuse the underlap, the flat cutback, and the free strike from the corner of the box.

### Q: What is Bukayo Saka's biggest weakness?

The most exploitable feature of the profile is not an individual limitation at all, which is why individual scouting reports on him change so few outcomes. It is the structural dependency of the combination: the pattern needs a second attacker occupying the space outside or inside him, and the pattern is neutralized when that second attacker is assigned to a specific defender rather than left to be picked up by whoever is nearest. Separate the pair into two zones with two owners and the remaining situation is a genuine one-versus-one, which is the outcome with the lowest expected value for any wide attacker. In isolation, without a partner running beyond him and against a covering defender in the inside lane, the threat is ordinary rather than exceptional.

### Q: How do you defend Saka and an overlapping full-back together?

With a declared hierarchy fixed before kick off. The full-back owns the ball and does not leave the carrier under any circumstance. The wide midfielder ahead of him owns the overlapping runner and departs on the runner's second stride, which is roughly a full stride before the pass is played, because a midfielder who leaves with the ball is always half a second late and half a second in that channel is five yards. His recovery line must be goal-side of the runner rather than ball-side, arriving to defend the delivery rather than to attempt a tackle. The failure signal is unmistakable: a wide midfielder on the runner's inside shoulder can block nothing and prevent nothing.

### Q: Should the full-back follow Saka inside?

No, and this is the discipline that fails most often. A full-back who follows his direct opponent into the inside channel vacates the widest zone on the pitch, and the attacking full-back holding width behind will receive the switch with the whole flank empty. The correct instruction is to hold the width and pick up whoever arrives in it, which is normally the overlapping full-back. The pair has now been separated onto two defenders in two zones, which is exactly the outcome the plan wants. The receiver in the inside channel becomes the near-side central midfielder's assignment, who stops shading the entry lane and starts contesting the reception, with the holding midfielder sliding across to shade in his place.

### Q: How do you stop the underlap around Saka?

The near-side centre-back owns it and steps into the inside lane rather than holding the defensive line. The trigger is the runner crossing an imaginary line extending from the near post out through the corner of the penalty area. He does not sprint at the runner; he opens his hips toward the touchline, takes two or three controlled strides, and arrives with his body between the runner and the goal rather than between the runner and the ball. The holding midfielder simultaneously drops into the space he vacated and screens the penalty spot. Every part of that costs something: the line is broken, the far-side centre-back defends a wider zone alone, and the offside line is compromised. Holding the line instead concedes the highest-value chance the pair can manufacture.

### Q: Which defender profile copes with Saka?

Not the fastest and not the biggest. The most useful profile is a full-back with quick lateral movement across the first two strides, a short reaction time from a stationary set position, and the discipline to hold a stance that feels wrong. Recovery pace matters but matters less than it does against a pure straight-line runner, because this profile beats defenders with a change of direction and a combination rather than with a burst. Height and physicality matter least, since the plan is designed to make the aerial duel harmless. The most underrated quality is compliance: the stance requires letting a runner go past on the outside while staying attached to the carrier, and a defender who cannot suppress the instinct to chase will abandon the plan by the twentieth minute.

### Q: How do you defend Saka's cutback from the byline?

With one player who never turns toward his own goal, and that player is the holding midfielder standing on the penalty spot with his body open to the field. The delivery travels flat and fast across the top of the six-yard box, and every recovering defender is facing the wrong way with his momentum going toward his own goal, which takes longer to reverse than it takes an arriving attacker to start. Two further instructions matter. The near-side centre-back must not follow the ball toward the near post, since the delivery is aimed behind him. The full-back who conceded the byline recovers to the near post rather than chasing the ball into the corner, which frees the goalkeeper to move across for the flat delivery.

### Q: Does doubling up on Saka open space elsewhere?

It does, and worse than that, it opens the specific space the pattern is designed to attack. Doubling a combining attacker gives him what he wants, because the second defender arriving on the ball is a defender leaving the space his partner is already running into. The plan in this article is not a doubling plan; it is an assignment plan, and the distinction is exact: a double concentrates two defenders on the ball while an assignment separates them onto the ball and the space. Sides that double this profile concede more rather than fewer chances, and they concede them in the zone the second defender left. The only doubling that works is late, from the blind side, after the carrier has committed a second touch with no runner beyond him.

### Q: How do you stop Saka receiving between the lines?

Hand the inside channel to a named owner and make the handover explicit. When his starting position moves inside the width of the penalty area, the near-side central midfielder stops shading the entry lane and starts marking him tightly enough to contest the reception, while the holding midfielder slides across to take over the shading job. The centre-back's trigger changes too: instead of a spatial trigger line, he steps to the receiver's blind side at the moment of reception, which contests the half-turn from a direction the receiver cannot see. What must not happen is the full-back following him inside, because that converts a favourable separation back into an overload on the flank he just left.

### Q: What does the wide midfielder in front of the full-back do against Saka?

He owns the overlapping runner and nothing else. He watches the attacking full-back's hips rather than the ball, because the run announces itself in the hips a full stride before it announces itself anywhere else. He leaves on the second stride that commits the runner past his own attacker's shoulder, not on the pass. He recovers goal-side, arriving to defend the delivery rather than to make a tackle. And he calls, loudly and every time, that he has the runner, because the moment the full-back stops trusting him and checks over his shoulder, the carrier will cut inside on the head turn. His recovery pattern is the most physically demanding job in the plan, so prepare a substitution around the hour and brief the replacement on the trigger before he goes on.

### Q: How do goalkeepers deal with Saka shooting at the near post?

By holding the near-post reference rather than drifting toward the middle in anticipation of a cross. When the plan works, the carrier is forced outside, cannot reach the byline because the wide midfielder recovered, and strikes from a narrowing angle because the alternative is running the ball out of play. That is the intended outcome and it is only dangerous if the goalkeeper has vacated the near post or if a defender dives into a block and deflects it. The defender's instruction in that duel is to close the angle standing, with feet, body upright and legs closed, letting the goalkeeper handle a strike he can see. A deflection from a narrow angle is worse than no block at all, because it wrong-foots a goalkeeper who had set correctly.

### Q: What do you accept conceding in order to contain Saka?

Four things, named out loud in the meeting so nobody treats them as mistakes. Concede the delivery from wide into a box organized with a far-post defender, two centre-backs on the flight, a penalty-spot screen and a goalkeeper set for the delivery type; say the number, twenty crosses, so the eighth does not cause panic. Concede the corner, since blocked crosses produce corners by design. Concede the throw-in, because the full-back's non-tackling instruction sends the ball out rather than winning it cleanly. Concede the recycled possession, since containment and sustained pressure look identical from the touchline. Refuse only three things: the underlap, the flat cutback to the penalty spot, and the free strike from the corner of the box.

### Q: What is the fallback if the plan against Saka fails?

Three fallbacks in order of cost. First, change the stance and the cover without changing personnel: the full-back protects the outside instead of the inside, the near-side centre-back covers the cut rather than the underlap, and the wide midfielder tucks in. This deliberately concedes the shot from the corner of the box in exchange for removing the byline, and it is the right trade only when the cutbacks are producing chances and the shooting is not. Second, commit the near-side central midfielder permanently to the wide zone, conceding control of the middle third, which is correct when the flank is the opponent's only route to goal. Third, change to a back five as a planned substitution rather than a reshuffle, since an improvised back five has no rehearsed handovers.

### Q: How should an amateur side handle a wide pairing like Saka's?

Reduce the plan to one behaviour and coach that behaviour until it is automatic: the covering player recovers goal-side of the overlapping runner rather than ball-side. That single habit removes the most common goal in wide-pair football at amateur level, which is a full-back turned by an outside run and a cutback arriving with every defender facing his own goal. After that, prefer a back five to a back four, because the geometric resolution it provides removes the decision amateur defenders most often make wrongly, and the middle third you surrender is contested less systematically at that level. If your centre-back cannot execute the underlap step reliably, do not ask him to; instruct him to hold the line and give the underlapping runner to the holding midfielder instead.

### Q: What do analysts watch when scouting Saka?

The relationship rather than the player, tracked across two matches with the eyes off the ball. Four counts carry almost all of the value. The ratio of overlaps to underlaps, and whether the choice correlates with where the ball arrived from, since many pairs overlap off a switch and underlap off a central pass. How many strides before the reception the run begins, which distinguishes a rehearsed pattern from a reactive one. Which of the two players makes the decision, meaning whose movement the other reacts to, since that determines whether pressure on the carrier disrupts the pattern at all. And the frequency of the third-man rotation, because a pair that releases inside and re-runs outside will beat a hierarchy built for only two patterns.

### Q: Which sides have coped with Saka and what did they do?

The sides that cope share a structural signature rather than a personnel one, which is the useful finding. They assign the second attacker to a specific defender before kick off instead of leaving him to whoever is nearest. They recover goal-side in the wide channel without exception. They step a defender into the inside lane on the underlap and accept a broken line while it happens. They keep one midfielder on the penalty spot through every delivery rather than pulling everyone into the six-yard box. And they hold the width when he drifts inside instead of following him. None of that requires elite defenders, which is why sides of very different quality have contained the pattern and sides with better individual defenders have not.
