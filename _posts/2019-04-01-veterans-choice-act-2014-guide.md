## The Question Behind the Statute

What does a government owe patients when its own hospitals cannot see them in time, and what should Congress build in answer when delay itself becomes a form of denial? The Veterans Choice Act, enacted as the central part of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, Public Law 113-146, was the legislative reply to that question. The statute did not begin as an abstract debate about public medicine or private medicine. The statute began as an emergency response to a failure of access inside the Department of Veterans Affairs, a department charged with providing health care to men and women whose entitlement to that care rested on military service. The operative promise was simple to state and difficult to honor: a veteran enrolled in the system would receive timely care, in a department facility when the department could provide it, and through paid community care when the department could not.

![Veterans Choice Act community care eligibility and the forty mile rule explained - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-80.webp)

That promise carried a moral weight distinct from ordinary health policy. Veterans health care rests on a compact articulated across generations of law, from Civil War pensions through the creation of a cabinet level department. The compact holds that service connected injury, illness, and need create a direct obligation, and that obligation does not dissolve when an appointment desk falls behind. By the spring of 2014, reporting from Phoenix suggested that the obligation had been obscured by paperwork, that veterans waited on unofficial lists while official records showed prompt service. The allegation struck at competence and at honesty. A long wait can be explained by demand, funding, or staffing. A hidden wait suggests that the institution charged with measuring the problem had instead concealed the problem from oversight.

Congress answered with speed that reflected the political and institutional pressure of the moment. Between the first national attention to Phoenix in the spring of 2014 and enactment on August 7, 2014, roughly three months passed. Within that interval came an inspector general interim report, a nationwide access audit, the resignation of a cabinet secretary, competing House and Senate bills, a conference negotiation, and final passage by overwhelming majorities. The resulting law combined money, a temporary private care option called the Choice Program, management reforms, and new authority to remove senior executives. The opening provisions matter because the statute attempted two tasks at once: relief for patients caught in queues, and repair of the system that had produced the queues. Relief without repair would leave the underlying capacity problem untouched. Repair without relief would ask waiting veterans to endure delay while the department rebuilt itself.

The tensions inside that dual purpose shaped every later argument about the law. One line of reasoning, advanced by proponents of expanded community care in Congress and in veterans organizations that supported the bill, treated distance and delay as disqualifying conditions: when a department facility stood more than 40 miles away or an appointment could not be scheduled within 30 days, the veteran gained a route to private care at department expense. Another line of reasoning, advanced by defenders of the integrated department system, warned that sending patients outward in large numbers could fragment care, drain resources from department hospitals, and weaken a system built around service connected conditions and coordinated treatment. The statute did not resolve that tension. The statute institutionalized that tension, funding both the department and an exit from the department, and left later Congresses to decide where the balance belonged.

A statute written in a crisis also inherits the limits of crisis knowledge. In the summer of 2014, Congress legislated before the final inspector general report on Phoenix had issued, before the full extent of scheduling practices across the Veterans Health Administration had been mapped facility by facility, and before any reliable estimate existed for how many veterans would use a private care option or what that use would cost over time. Legislators acted on interim findings, audit totals, and testimony that conveyed urgency more clearly than precision. That sequence matters for understanding the law on its own terms. The Veterans Choice Act was not the product of a completed inquiry followed by calm design. The Veterans Choice Act was a remedy built while the diagnosis remained incomplete, and the incompleteness explains both the breadth of the appropriations and the temporary character of the Choice Program, authorized for three years or until the dedicated fund was exhausted, whichever came earlier.

The question that opened the debate therefore remained open after enactment. A government that cannot see patients in time owes those patients something more than an apology and a plan. Whether the owed remedy is a rebuilt public system, a paid alternative outside that system, or a durable combination of the two divided opinion in 2014 and divided opinion through the succession debates that followed. The Phoenix record supplied the factual ground on which that division formed, and the record requires careful statement: substantiated findings at the confidence the investigations support, attributed figures from named official sources, and a clear separation between scheduling manipulation that investigators confirmed and causal claims about deaths that investigators declined to confirm conclusively.

## Phoenix and the Collapse of Confidence

Reporting in the spring of 2014 centered on the Phoenix VA Health Care System in Arizona. Accounts from whistleblowers, families, and department employees, carried first in Arizona reporting and then in national coverage, alleged that Phoenix staff maintained unofficial waiting lists separate from the official electronic waiting list. According to those accounts, veterans seeking appointments were placed on the unofficial lists, and entries reached the official list only when an appointment slot appeared within the window that department policy treated as timely. The effect, if the accounts proved accurate, was to make measured performance look acceptable while actual waits stretched far beyond the reported figures.

The benchmark against which performance was measured gave the allegations their institutional significance. The Department of Veterans Affairs operated with an internal scheduling target of 14 days: veterans were to be seen, or at least scheduled, within 14 days of a requested or clinically indicated date. A 14 day target functioned as a management standard and, in the reporting, as an incentive. Facilities reported compliance with the target to superiors, and compliance figures fed assessments of facility performance. When a veteran could not be accommodated within 14 days, the reporting alleged, the unofficial list absorbed the discrepancy. The veteran waited in fact. The record showed something else.

Such allegations demanded verification beyond journalism, and verification arrived in stages. On May 28, 2014, the Office of Inspector General for the Department of Veterans Affairs issued an interim report on the Phoenix system. The interim report substantiated the core scheduling allegation. Investigators found that 1,700 veterans in Phoenix were waiting for care and had been left off the official waiting list, placed instead on unofficial lists or omitted through scheduling practices that concealed the true duration of delay. Investigators further found delays of up to 115 days for veterans seeking appointments. The interim report characterized the practices as systemic within Phoenix scheduling operations rather than the product of isolated error, and the report warned that the same review methods would be extended to other facilities. The interim findings did not complete the inquiry. The interim findings confirmed enough to transform a regional scandal into a national question about the reliability of department access data as a whole.

The political consequence followed within two days. Secretary Eric Shinseki resigned on May 30, 2014, after a meeting with President Barack Obama. Sloan Gibson, the deputy secretary, became acting secretary and assumed leadership of a department under simultaneous investigation by the inspector general, review by the White House, and scrutiny from the House and Senate committees with jurisdiction over veterans affairs. The change in leadership did not slow the factual disclosures. Attention shifted from one facility in Arizona to the entire Veterans Health Administration, the largest integrated health care system in the United States, operating hospitals and clinics across every region of the country and serving millions of enrolled veterans.

On June 9, 2014, the department published the results of a nationwide access audit, an internal review ordered in response to the Phoenix disclosures and conducted across department facilities. The access audit figures, attributed to the department audit of June 2014, reported that more than 57,000 veterans were waiting for appointments beyond the periods the department treated as acceptable, and that more than 64,000 veterans had enrolled in department health care over the preceding decade and had never been seen for an appointment. The audit also reported widespread confusion among scheduling staff about proper procedures and instances at multiple facilities where schedulers had been directed, or had felt directed, to record desired appointment dates in ways that understated actual waits. The audit totals changed the scale of the legislative problem. Phoenix could no longer be treated as an anomaly contained within one medical center in the Southwest. The audit described access failure and unreliable scheduling data as conditions present, in varying degree, across the system.

Congress received the audit in an atmosphere shaped by constituency pressure that crossed party lines. Veterans service organizations demanded corrective legislation before the summer recess. Families described parents, spouses, and adult children who had waited months for specialty consultations or diagnostic procedures. Committee hearings in the House and Senate collected testimony on appointment backlogs, on the gap between reported and experienced wait times, and on the difficulty of recruiting physicians and nurses into department service in competitive labor markets. The legislative vehicles moved quickly: the House passed a community care bill on June 10, 2014, the Senate passed a broader bill on June 11, 2014 by a vote of 93 to 3, and conferees under Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Jeff Miller negotiated the compromise that became Public Law 113-146. The speed reflected consensus that delay had become indefensible, even as disagreement persisted about remedy, cost, and the long term structure of veterans health care.

An honest account of the scandal requires a counter reading alongside the narrative of concealment, because access strain in large health systems has causes that extend beyond misconduct at a scheduling desk. The Department of Veterans Affairs entered 2014 carrying demand generated by more than a decade of war in Iraq and Afghanistan. A new generation of veterans sought care for physical wounds, traumatic brain injury, post traumatic stress, and the ordinary illnesses of aging, while Vietnam era veterans, the largest cohort in the system, required increasing volumes of complex chronic care. Enrollment and appointment demand had grown substantially over the preceding decade. Treatment advances meant that conditions once fatal on the battlefield produced survivors who needed lifelong department care. Demand growth alone, however, does not explain unofficial lists. Demand growth explains pressure. Unofficial lists explain how pressure was hidden rather than reported upward as a resource failure requiring relief.

Capacity constraints compounded the demand problem. The department faced persistent difficulty hiring physicians, mental health professionals, and nurses at salaries and in locations that competed with private sector employers. Rural facilities struggled to recruit specialists. Urban facilities struggled with space, with examination rooms and clinic sessions that could not expand as fast as enrollment. Capital construction moved slowly, subject to appropriations cycles and project delays measured in years. Schedulers, the lowest paid participants in the chain, worked inside a measurement system that rewarded reported timeliness without supplying the appointment supply that genuine timeliness required. That context does not exonerate the practices investigators substantiated. That context locates the practices inside a system where the distance between the announced standard and the achievable standard had widened until some facilities bridged the distance with falsified records instead of escalation.

Comparison with health systems outside the department also counsels precision. Long waits for specialty care occur in private American medicine, in the British National Health Service, in Canadian provincial systems, and in other large integrated networks. A wait, standing alone, does not prove manipulation, and a backlog, standing alone, does not prove bad faith. The Phoenix findings mattered because investigators substantiated manipulation of the records that were supposed to reveal waits, not merely the existence of waits. The distinction governed the credibility of everything the department reported about access. Once scheduling data at one major facility had been shown to conceal 1,700 waiting veterans, every reassuring access figure from the system became contestable, and only an audit independent of local reporting chains could restore a factual baseline. The June 2014 access audit, conducted under acting leadership with new instructions, served that baseline function, even though the audit rested on department personnel and department records.

The most sensitive question in the Phoenix record concerned deaths. Families and some reporting attributed deaths of Phoenix veterans to delays in care, and the allegation that veterans had died while waiting on unofficial lists supplied much of the moral force behind the demand for immediate legislation. The investigations treated that allegation with a caution that public debate often set aside. Scheduling manipulation could be documented from lists, records, and staff interviews. Causation of an individual death requires medical review of a specific clinical course, judgment about whether earlier intervention would have altered the outcome, and evidence tying the delay to the death rather than to the underlying disease. The inspector general applied that stricter standard.

The final inspector general report on Phoenix issued on August 26, 2014, a dated event that followed enactment by less than three weeks, stated the conclusions with that distinction intact. The final report confirmed systemic scheduling manipulation in Phoenix and identified scheduling problems across the Veterans Health Administration. The final report also found instances of poor quality care and delays affecting seriously ill veterans. On causation, the final report declined to conclude conclusively that deaths of Phoenix veterans were caused by delays in care. Reviewers stated that the medical records examined did not permit a conclusive determination that specific deaths resulted from appointment delays, even where delay had occurred and even where care fell short of department standards. The finding neither absolved the department of the scheduling record nor converted every death of a waiting veteran into a death caused by waiting. Responsible statement of the scandal preserves that boundary: manipulation substantiated, delays substantiated, deaths during periods of delay documented in individual cases, causation of specific deaths by delay not conclusively established by the inspector general.

That boundary mattered for the legislation even though the legislation preceded the final report. Congress wrote the Veterans Choice Act against interim findings, and the interim findings sufficed to establish the access and data integrity failures that the statute addressed. A law expanding paid community care does not depend on proof that delays caused particular deaths; entitlement to timely care rests on the promise of timely care, and breach of the promise, demonstrated at scale, justifies a remedy. At the same time, the absence of a conclusive causation finding cautions against describing the statute as a response to a proven toll of delay caused deaths. The statute responded to proven concealment, proven delay, and a proven collapse of confidence in department reporting. Those proven elements were sufficient. Exaggeration beyond those elements weakens, rather than strengthens, the historical record on which [the longer sequence of veterans legislation](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/) should be judged.

The scandal also exposed a structural feature of veterans health care that predated Phoenix and survived the 2014 law. The department operates simultaneously as insurer and provider: the department pays for care and delivers care in department facilities staffed by department employees. When delivery capacity fails, the insurer function offers an escape valve, payment for care elsewhere, but before 2014 that valve opened narrowly, through individual authorizations for fee basis care that varied across facilities. Veterans in Phoenix and elsewhere described inconsistent access to outside care even when department waits were long. The Choice Program converted that discretionary valve into a defined entitlement triggered by distance or delay, with a dedicated fund of 10 billion dollars under section 101 of the act. Whether conversion of discretion into entitlement improved timeliness in practice became the central administrative question of the years that followed, taken up in later sections on implementation.

By the time President Obama signed the act at Fort Belvoir, Virginia, on August 7, 2014, the department had new leadership taking shape, with Robert A. McDonald confirmed by the Senate on July 29, 2014 as secretary by a vote of 97 to 0, succeeding the acting leadership of Sloan Gibson. The sequence from resignation to confirmation illustrated the institutional reset Congress expected to accompany the money. Funds for hiring and facility improvement, 5 billion dollars under Title VIII of the act, addressed the capacity side of the diagnosis. The Choice Fund addressed the immediate queue. Accountability provisions addressed the integrity side, on the premise that a measurement system that had rewarded concealment required consequences for senior officials responsible for facility performance. Each element traced back to Phoenix. Each element also reached beyond Phoenix, because the June audit had shown that the conditions producing the scandal, if not the identical practices, existed in facilities across the country.

Phoenix therefore stands in the statute not as background color but as the evidentiary foundation. The unofficial lists explained why Congress distrusted department assurances and wrote eligibility triggers into law rather than leaving access to administrative discretion. The 14 day target explained how a management metric, detached from appointment supply, could corrupt the data on which oversight depended. The interim report figures, 1,700 veterans off the official list and delays reaching 115 days, gave the failure a human scale that abstract backlog totals could not. The nationwide audit translated a facility scandal into a system mandate. The resignation of Secretary Shinseki marked the point at which administrative correction alone no longer sufficed politically, and legislation became the chosen instrument. Understanding the Veterans Choice Act begins there, with a department that had promised care within 14 days, reported compliance that did not occur, and left Congress to decide what a government owes patients when internal promises fail. The statute that followed is best read as the answer Congress constructed under pressure, in haste, and on an incomplete record, to a failure that investigators had substantiated in its essentials before the final word on Phoenix had been written.

## Passage Through Congress

The Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 reached the statute book by a route that compressed introduction, amendment, conference, and enactment into a single summer, and the compression shaped the text that emerged. Reporting on unofficial waiting lists at the Phoenix VA Health Care System broke into national headlines in April 2014. An interim report by the Office of Inspector General followed on May 28, 2014, substantiating that 1,700 veterans in the Phoenix system had been placed on unofficial waiting lists or left off the official list, with delays of up to 115 days against an internal target of 14 days. Secretary Eric Shinseki resigned on May 30, 2014. A nationwide access audit on June 9, 2014, recorded more than 57,000 veterans waiting for appointments and more than 64,000 enrolled veterans who had never been seen. Roughly ninety days elapsed between the first headlines and signature on August 7, 2014, leaving little room for the successive drafts and technical corrections that ordinarily refine a statute of comparable reach.

Two vehicles carried the response through the two chambers in June 2014. In the Senate, S. 2450, the Veterans Access to Care through Choice, Accountability, and Transparency Act of 2014, was introduced on June 9, 2014, under the sponsorship associated with Senator Bernie Sanders and Senator John McCain. The Senate passed S. 2450 on June 11, 2014, by a vote of 93 to 3. Rather than sending S. 2450 forward as a freestanding bill, the Senate substituted the text of S. 2450 into H.R. 3230, a measure that had previously been received from the House, so that H.R. 3230 became the legislative vehicle for the Senate position. In the House, H.R. 4810, the Veteran Access to Care Act of 2014, was introduced on June 9, 2014, and passed on June 10, 2014, one day before Senate passage of S. 2450. The House then amended the Senate substitute for H.R. 3230 by substituting the text of H.R. 4810 together with the text of H.R. 4031, the Department of Veterans Affairs Management Accountability Act of 2014, which the House had previously passed on May 21, 2014. That exchange placed the two chambers in conference on a single numbered bill, H.R. 3230, although the positions under negotiation had originated in three distinct measures.

The sequence offers a compact illustration of [how a bill becomes law in practice](/2022/02/01/how-a-bill-becomes-law-in-practice/) when political pressure rewards speed over sequential deliberation, because the decisive bargaining occurred not in open amendment on either floor but in conference between two committee leaders with defined institutional mandates. The conference committee was co chaired by Senator Bernie Sanders, chairman of the Senate Committee on Veterans Affairs, and Representative Jeff Miller, chairman of the House Committee on Veterans Affairs. Sanders defended expanded access outside the Department alongside substantial new investment inside the Department. Miller pressed for broader non Department authority and stronger power to remove senior executives. The conference paired a temporary program for non Department care with a large appropriation for internal capacity, expedited removal authority, an independent assessment, and a commission charged with examining organization over a twenty year horizon. Agreement was announced on July 28, 2014, and the conference report, House Report 113 564, was filed on that same date.

Floor action on the conference report took two days and produced margins that recorded near unanimity without recording agreement on every underlying question. The House agreed to the conference report on July 30, 2014, by a vote of 420 to 5. The Senate agreed on July 31, 2014, by a vote of 91 to 3. President Barack Obama signed the measure on August 7, 2014. The margins concealed a deliberate decision to postpone hard questions. Cost had been contained not by narrowing eligibility to a precisely measured population but by capping the money available for the new program and by limiting the duration of the authority, devices that allowed agreement without resolving how many veterans would seek non Department care or how an authorization system would operate at national scale.

The drafting consequences of that timetable appear throughout the enrolled text. First, the central innovation was framed as a temporary program rather than as a permanent amendment to the ordinary rules governing hospital care and medical services, with an end date tied to exhaustion of a fixed fund or to a date three years after enactment, whichever occurred earlier. A permanent entitlement would have required greater precision on eligibility and reimbursement, and would have confronted cost estimates that the Congressional Budget Office described in June 2014 for Title III of S. 2450 as introduced as highly uncertain. A capped, temporary program assigned the cost of uncertainty to the fund itself, so that exhaustion rather than design would mark the boundary of the commitment. Second, the statute supplied deadlines in place of finished systems. Section 101 directed interim final regulations within 90 days after enactment, by November 5, 2014, and required claims processing, provider agreements, credential verification, and care coordination to be built while eligible veterans were already seeking authorizations. Third, key definitions were left to implementation in a manner that altered the practical meaning of eligibility after enactment. Distance was measured initially as straight line distance, and on April 24, 2015, the Department announced and implemented driving distance instead, stating that the change would roughly double the number of eligible veterans. A definition that could double the eligible population upon reinterpretation had not been a minor technical detail. The definition had been a central policy choice, deferred under time pressure to a rulemaking stage that lacked the visibility of the July 2014 votes.

## The Architecture of the Statute

The formal identity of the measure fixes its place in the session laws and in the codified law, and careful attention to that identity prevents confusion between the temporary program and the permanent provisions enacted alongside the program. The enacted measure is the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, Public Law 113 146 of the 113th Congress, printed at 128 Statutes at Large 1754, with H.R. 3230 as the bill number. Section 1 supplies the short title, and section 2 supplies definitions by reference to definitions in title 38 of the United States Code for terms including facilities of the Department, hospital care, and medical services. Section 101, which created the expanded authority for care through non Department entities, was codified as a note at 38 United States Code section 1703, reflecting the temporary and conditional character of the authority in relation to the underlying provision governing care through community or contract providers. Section 707, which created expedited removal and demotion authority for senior executives, amended title 38 directly and was codified at 38 United States Code section 713. Parts of the act operated as freestanding session law, parts as notes attached to existing codified authority, and parts as direct rewrites of codified personnel law.

Title I, Improvement of Access to Care from Non Department of Veterans Affairs Providers, contains sections 101 through 106 and places the Veterans Choice Program at the center of the act. Section 101 required the Department to authorize care through non Department entities, to enter into provider agreements, to coordinate care, to issue a Veterans Choice Card to each enrolled veteran within 90 days after enactment, and to maintain an electronic waiting list. Eligible providers included Medicare providers, federally qualified health centers, Department of Defense facilities, Indian Health Service facilities, and other providers meeting criteria established by the Secretary. Reimbursement was in general capped at the Medicare rate, with exceptions for highly rural areas, Alaska, and states under an all payer model agreement. The Department was in general secondary payer for non service connected conditions when a veteran held private insurance, and primary payer for veterans enrolled only in Medicare, Medicaid, or TRICARE. Department copayments applied on the same basis, and authorizations were required to cover a complete episode of care.

Eligibility under section 101 was gated at two levels, and the gates define both the population covered by the statute and the population left outside the program. The threshold gate was enrollment. Eligibility was limited, in the formulation scored by the Congressional Budget Office for the conference agreement, to individuals enrolled as of August 1, 2014, and to combat veterans who had separated within five years of enrollment. A veteran outside both categories stood outside the expanded authority even if distance or delay would otherwise have applied. The second gate was a qualifying trigger, of which section 101 supplied four. A veteran qualified through inability to schedule an appointment within the wait time goals of the Veterans Health Administration, defined in the act as a date not more than 30 days from the date on which the veteran requested an appointment, subject to a procedure by which the Secretary could establish different goals after notice to Congress. A veteran qualified through residence more than 40 miles from the nearest Department medical facility, with a refined measurement for primary care that looked to a facility able to provide the care sought through a full time primary care physician. A veteran qualified through residence in a state without a Department facility providing hospital care, emergency services, and surgical care at the applicable complexity standard, combined with residence more than 20 miles from such a facility. A veteran qualified through an unusual or excessive travel burden, including a requirement to travel by air, boat, or ferry, or geographical challenges, environmental factors, or a medical condition affecting the ability to travel, as determined by the Department. Satisfaction of a trigger did not by itself authorize treatment. Department authorization was required before private care, and the statement printed on the Veterans Choice Card directed the holder to call the Department telephone number specified on the card to ensure that treatment had been authorized. The card evidenced potential eligibility. The card alone conferred no entitlement to obtain care at Department expense.

The exclusions were structural. Veterans who lived within 40 miles of a Department facility and who could obtain an appointment within the wait time goals received no authority under section 101, however strong a preference for a private physician. Care obtained without prior approval fell outside the payment structure that sections 101 and 802 created, and providers outside the listed categories could not participate merely by treating an eligible veteran. The statute preserved Department control over coordination and records, requiring comparable credentials, annual verification, and copies of medical records for the departmental record system. Choice, as the term operated in the statute, meant authorized choice among participating providers for an approved episode of care, within a capped fund and for a limited duration. Sections 102 through 106 added collaboration with the Indian Health Service and with the Native Hawaiian health care system, a two year reauthorization of Project ARCH in section 104, prompt payment direction in section 105, and transfer of payment authority to the Chief Business Office effective October 1, 2014, in section 106.

Title II, Health Care Administrative Matters, contains sections 201 through 209 and addresses diagnosis and oversight rather than direct delivery of care. Section 201 required the Secretary, not later than 90 days after enactment, to enter into contracts with a private sector entity or entities for an independent assessment of hospital care and medical services furnished in Department medical facilities. The assessment was required to address patient demographics and needs, departmental capabilities, an appropriate access standard, information technology, and construction and leasing processes, with submission to Congress and public availability. Section 202 established a Commission on Care of 15 voting members, appointed by the Speaker of the House, the House minority leader, the Senate majority leader, the Senate minority leader, and the President, with qualifications spanning veterans service organizations, private integrated health care management, government health care systems, and facility construction and leasing. The Commission was charged with a comprehensive evaluation of access and with strategic examination of organization and delivery over the twenty year period beginning on enactment. Sections 203 through 209 added a technology task force report on the scheduling system due within 45 days, requirements for mobile Vet Centers and mobile medical centers, a prohibition on the use of wait time metrics in performance awards for specified leaders, publication of wait times and quality data, physician credential information, budget reporting on contract care, and penalties for knowing falsification of wait time and quality data. Title II thus converted the factual dispute that had produced the statute into reporting and assessment obligations with dated deliverables.

Title III, Health Care Staffing, Recruitment, and Training Matters, contains sections 301 through 303 and addresses the personnel shortages that conferees treated as a cause of delay independent of authorization policy. Section 301 required the Inspector General to determine, and the Secretary to publish, the five occupations among health professions with the largest staffing shortages, with the first determination due not later than 180 days after enactment and annual determinations thereafter by September 30, and authorized direct appointment of qualified personnel in identified occupations. Section 301 also required up to 1,500 added residency positions during the five year period beginning one year after enactment, with priority for primary care, mental health, and facilities without an existing residency program. Section 302 extended the scholarship authorization through December 31, 2019, and raised the Education Debt Reduction Program maximum over five years from 60,000 dollars to 120,000 dollars. Section 303 required a two year clinic management training program covering schedules, appointment supply, and use of the scheduling system. Personnel was Title III business, separated from the administrative studies of Title II and from the facility authorizations later in the act.

Title IV, Health Care Related to Sexual Trauma, contains sections 401 through 403 and liberalizes eligibility for counseling, care, and services connected to military sexual trauma. Section 401 extended authority to veterans and to certain servicemembers without veteran status who experienced sexual trauma during active duty for training or inactive duty training. Section 402 amended section 1720D of title 38 to permit counseling and treatment for members of the Armed Forces, including members of the National Guard and Reserve components on active duty, in consultation with the Secretary of Defense and without an initial consultation and referral from the Department of Defense. Section 403 required reports to Congress on military sexual trauma. Title V, Other Health Care Matters, contains a single operative provision, section 501, which extended the pilot program on assisted living services for veterans with traumatic brain injury through October 6, 2017. The pilot had been established under section 1705 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2008, and Title V preserved that program without restructuring the program.

Title VI, Major Medical Facility Leases, contains sections 601 and 602 and authorizes the physical expansion that Title III staffing and Title VIII appropriations were intended to support. Section 601 authorized leases at 27 specified locations, with a stated amount for each, ranging from near two million dollars to above twenty million dollars at Hines, Illinois, and Phoenix, Arizona. The Congressional Budget Office estimated about 1.3 billion dollars in direct spending from the leases over the period from 2017 through 2024. Section 602 required the Secretary to record the full cost of the obligation at execution and to submit a prospectus analysis, notice, and scoring analysis to Congress not less than 30 days before entry into a lease. The leases were Title VI business, and section 601 supplied the authorization.

Title VII, Other Veterans Matters, contains sections 701 through 707. Section 701 expanded the Marine Gunnery Sergeant John David Fry Scholarship to spouses of individuals who died in the line of duty on or after September 11, 2001, with an irrevocable election required between scholarship benefits and Survivors and Dependents Educational Assistance. Section 702 conditioned approval of courses at public institutions on charging qualifying veterans and dependents no more than the in state tuition rate, effective for terms beginning after July 1, 2015. Sections 703 through 706 extended a pension reduction for veterans and survivors in Medicaid approved nursing homes, extended home loan guarantee fees, limited aggregate bonuses, and extended income verification authority. Section 707 supplied the accountability provision that gave the act the third word of the short title in operative form. Section 707, codified at 38 United States Code section 713, authorized the Secretary to remove an individual from the Senior Executive Service, or to remove the individual from the civil service or transfer the individual to a General Schedule position at an appropriate grade, when the Secretary determined that performance or misconduct warranted the action. The Secretary was required to give written notice to the veterans affairs committees within 30 days, with reasons. An affected individual had seven days to appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, for referral to an administrative judge required to decide within 21 days, with the decision designated as final and with the removal becoming final if no decision issued in that period. Compensation was withheld during the appeal. The design traded deliberative depth for speed in a second domain, mirroring the trade made in Title I, and the finality features of section 707 later drew the Appointments Clause litigation recorded in Helman v. Department of Veterans Affairs, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit on May 9, 2017.

Title VIII, Other Matters, contains sections 801 through 803 and supplies the money and the budgetary designation on which the earlier titles depended. Section 801 authorized and appropriated 5 billion dollars for use by the Secretary to improve access to care and physical infrastructure, described by the Congressional Research Service as funding to hire primary care and specialty care physicians and other medical staff, including nurses, social workers, and mental health professionals, and as funding available for maintenance and operation of facilities of the Veterans Health Administration, including leases and minor construction. The Secretary was required to submit a funding plan to Congress and to report within one year after enactment on obligation of the funds. Section 802 established the Veterans Choice Fund in the Treasury and authorized and appropriated 10 billion dollars to the fund, available until expended for implementation of section 101 only, with no more than 300 million dollars of the amount available for administrative expenses of implementing section 101. The separation between section 801 and section 802 encoded the central compromise of the conference in appropriations form. Section 801 money strengthened the Department from inside, through hiring and infrastructure under ordinary departmental administration. Section 802 money financed care outside the Department, through a segregated fund tied exclusively to the temporary authority of section 101, so that the outside program could expire by exhaustion without disturbing the internal investment. Section 803 designated amounts in the act as emergency requirements and exempted the act from budget enforcement rules. Total package figures of about 16.3 billion dollars combined the Choice Fund, the section 801 appropriation, and the lease effect, while a deficit increase estimate of 12.8 billion dollars over five years, as summarized by the Senate Republican Policy Committee, measured the package over a defined budget window. Read together, the eight titles authorized exit from the system for a gated population, funded additional personnel and facilities inside the system, commissioned external diagnosis of the system, and accelerated removal of executives, with each remedy drafted under a timetable that privileged enactment in the summer of 2014 over resolution of the administrative questions that enactment created.

## A Card in Every Mailbox and a Gate at Every Door

The Veterans Choice Program began operating in November 2014, on a timetable that left almost no room for gradual preparation. Section 101 of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 directed the Secretary of Veterans Affairs to furnish hospital care and medical services through eligible non-Department providers for eligible veterans, and the Department of Veterans Affairs published the interim final rule implementing the program on November 5, 2014. The statute required a Veterans Choice Card to reach enrolled veterans within ninety days of enactment, and the Department began mailing cards on November 5, 2014, the same date the implementing rule appeared. By the end of the mailing, about 8.5 million cards had been issued, a figure attributed to the Department. The scale of that mailing shaped everything that followed, because the card arrived in the hands of millions of veterans whose connection to the program ranged from immediate eligibility to no eligibility at all, and the difference between possession of a card and permission to use the program became the central misunderstanding of the program design.

The card itself announced the limit in print. The statutory design required the card to carry a statement directing the holder to call the Department to ensure that treatment had been authorized, and Secretary Robert A. McDonald repeated the point in the letter that accompanied the first mailings, as reported by Military.com in November 2014, explaining that the card provided neither guaranteed coverage nor an unlimited medical benefit and that eligibility had to be verified and advance authorization received before the card could be used for a given benefit. A plastic card in a wallet therefore functioned as an invitation to ask a question rather than as an answer to that question. The answer depended on an enrollment gate, on at least one of several eligibility triggers written into section 101, and on a separate authorization decision for each episode of care, and each of those three layers filtered the population further than the layer before.

The enrollment gate came first, and the gate had a date built into the frame. Under section 101 as enacted in August 2014, a veteran had to be enrolled in the Department health care system as of August 1, 2014, or, for a veteran who enrolled after that date, had to have served on active duty in a theater of combat operations and to have been discharged or released from active duty during the five year period preceding enrollment, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service in its report on the act, R43704. Enrollment alone satisfied nothing beyond the gate itself, since an enrolled veteran also had to meet one of the statutory triggers, and a veteran outside the gate could meet every trigger on paper and remain outside the program. Congress revisited that restriction in the Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act of 2015, Public Law 114-41, enacted in July 2015, after which, as the Congressional Research Service recorded, the August 1, 2014 enrollment date criterion and the combat theater criterion no longer applied. The gate thus belongs to the history of the program launch rather than to the whole life of the program.

Beyond the gate, the statute offered four distinct routes into eligibility, and the routes had different logics. One route measured time, one route measured distance, one route measured the completeness of the Department facility network within a state, and one route measured the character of the journey between a residence and a facility. A veteran needed only one route, but each route carried its own definitions, its own reference points, and its own evidence, and the administrative work of the program consisted in large part of deciding which reference point governed a given case. The two numbers that dominated public discussion, thirty days and forty miles, described only the first two routes, and a veteran could fail both numbers and qualify under a route that contained no number at all.

The time route turned on a comparison between a need for care and a calendar. A veteran qualified when the Department could not schedule an appointment within thirty days of the clinically indicated date, meaning the date a clinician determined that the veteran needed to be seen, or, when no clinically indicated date had been given, within thirty days of the date the veteran specified as the preferred date for the appointment. The choice of the clinically indicated date as the starting point mattered, because the clock described in the statute measured from clinical need rather than from the moment a scheduling clerk opened a calendar, and a delay that looked modest when counted from a telephone call could look very different when counted from the date a physician had set. The thirty day figure also connected the program to the access failure that had produced the statute, since the wait time route allowed a veteran trapped in a queue to leave the queue for a defined episode of care.

The distance route turned on residence. A veteran qualified by residing more than forty miles from the nearest Department medical facility, and that single sentence in section 101 generated more interpretive work than any other eligibility language in the statute, because the sentence named a distance without naming a method for measuring distance. The residence end of the measurement was comparatively clear, since the veteran lived where the veteran lived, and the facility end raised subsidiary questions about which facilities counted as Department medical facilities for the purpose of the test. The method question, straight line or road network, sat underneath both ends of the measurement, invisible in the statutory text and decisive in application, and the discussion below takes up that question in full.

The third route addressed the structure of the Department network within a state. A veteran qualified by residing in a state without a Department medical facility that provided hospital care, emergency medical services, and surgical care, the combination the Department described as a full service facility, and by residing more than twenty miles from such a facility. Department guidance identified Alaska, Hawaii, and New Hampshire as the states within that category, and Department materials also applied the category to United States territories without a full service facility, while excluding Puerto Rico, where a full service facility operated. The New Hampshire application carried a refinement that illustrated the precision of the category, since a New Hampshire veteran who lived within twenty miles of the White River Junction Department medical center in Vermont fell outside the category despite residing in a listed state, as the Department explained in its program guidance. A full service facility, for the purpose of the test, meant a facility offering all three of hospital care, emergency services, and surgical care, so a state could contain clinics, outpatient sites, and even a medical center of substantial size and remain within the category if no facility inside the state offered the complete combination.

The fourth route addressed the burden of the journey rather than the length of the journey on a map. One branch covered a veteran who resided within forty miles of a Department medical facility and yet had to travel by air, by boat, or by ferry to reach that facility, a circumstance that arose where water separated a residence from a facility that sat nearby in geographic terms and far away in practical terms. The other branch covered a veteran who faced an unusual or excessive burden in accessing the facility, and the Congressional Research Service summary of the act described the burden in terms of geographical challenges, inaccessible roads or hazardous weather, a medical condition that would prevent the veteran from traveling, or any other factor as determined by the Veterans Health Administration. Department guidance issued on December 1, 2015 elaborated the same idea through geographic challenges, environmental factors, and a medical condition, with local facility staff working with the veteran to determine eligibility on that ground. The burden route therefore required judgment in a way the distance route did not, since miles could be computed by a formula and burdens had to be assessed case by case against the circumstances of a particular veteran, a particular route, and a particular course of treatment.

### How did a veteran actually use the Veterans Choice Program?

A veteran first confirmed eligibility with the Department of Veterans Affairs, then obtained advance authorization for a specific episode of care, then received that care from an eligible non-Department provider, and the Department paid the provider for the authorized services under the program rules and payment limits in effect.

The sequence sounded simple in summary and proved demanding in operation, because each step depended on a determination made by the Department rather than on a decision made by the veteran alone. Confirmation came through the Department, by telephone or through the local facility, and the confirmation established that the veteran stood inside the enrollment gate and inside at least one eligibility trigger. Authorization followed confirmation, and authorization was specific rather than general, covering a defined course of hospital care or medical services rather than opening an account against which any provider could draw. The card facilitated the transaction at the point of care, identifying the veteran and directing the provider to the Department for confirmation that the services in question had been authorized, and Department communications to providers described how, when, and what care a veteran had been authorized to receive. A veteran who presented the card to a provider without a prior authorization had skipped the step on which the entire payment structure rested, and care received outside an authorization stood outside the program even when the veteran would have qualified for an authorization on request.

The providers on the other side of the transaction were defined by the statute rather than chosen freely from the whole medical economy. Section 101 directed care through agreements with specified categories of non-Department entities, including health care providers participating in the Medicare program, federally qualified health centers, the Department of Defense, and the Indian Health Service, as summarized by the Congressional Research Service. Providers furnishing care under the program had to maintain at least the same or similar credentials and licenses required of Department health care providers and to submit verification of those credentials and licenses. The eligible provider pool therefore reached deep into community medicine, since Medicare participation covered a large share of American physicians and hospitals, and the pool remained a defined network rather than an open market, bounded by participation status, credentialing, and the terms of each authorization.

Payment followed the authorization rather than the card, and the Department paid the non-Department provider for authorized care, with the money drawn from the Veterans Choice Fund that section 101 had capitalized with ten billion dollars. The payment rules contained a coordination structure that many summaries omitted. For care connected to a service-connected disability, the Department stood in its accustomed position as payer, while for care for a nonservice-connected disability or condition covered by a health care plan held by the veteran, section 101 generally made the Department the secondary payer, with the veteran health plan bearing primary responsibility, as the Congressional Research Service explained in R43704. When the veteran held only Medicare, Medicaid, or TRICARE coverage, the Department served as primary payer for nonservice-connected care under the same statutory structure. Before receiving care, an eligible veteran was required to provide the Secretary with information about any insurance policy or contract the veteran held, so that the payer order could be established in advance, and the amount the Department paid for care outside a veteran plan coverage was capped at the Medicare rate or a negotiated rate. The design thus treated the program as a Department purchase of defined care from community providers, financed from a dedicated and finite fund, rather than as an insurance product carried by the veteran, and the distinction explained why an authorization, which committed fund money to a defined purchase, stood at the center of every transaction.

The misunderstanding that trailed the program from the first mailing grew directly out of the gap between the physical simplicity of a card and the administrative complexity behind the card. A card resembles an insurance card, fits the same slot in a wallet, and invites the same gesture at a reception desk, and the Congressional Research Service implementation report on the program, R44562, recorded that many veterans attempted to use the Choice Card as an insurance card, while the same report stated plainly that the card did not replace a veteran identification card, did not guarantee eligibility, and did not provide health insurance like benefits. Contemporary reporting captured the same gap from the veteran side of the counter. Military.com reported in November 2014 that veterans service organizations had cautioned for months that the card was not a golden key to unlimited care from any physician a veteran might choose, and Government Executive reported in April 2015 that the rollout had created confusion and that many veterans who believed in good faith that eligibility covered a planned visit were turned away. Eligibility was never automatic under the statute, possession of a card proved enrollment in the broadest sense and nothing more, and department authorization before private care was the hinge on which lawful use of the program swung.

## Forty Miles on Paper and Forty Miles on the Road

The statute fixed the distance at forty miles and stopped there, and the silence after the number became the most consequential silence in the program. Section 101 did not specify whether the forty miles ran in a straight line across the landscape or along the roads a veteran actually had to drive, and a number without a measurement method is an instruction with the decisive term left blank. The Department filled the blank in the interim final rule of November 5, 2014 by adopting straight line distance, described in the later Federal Register document as straight line or geodesic distance, measured from the residence of the veteran to the nearest Department medical facility. Geodesic measurement has genuine administrative virtues, since a straight line between two points can be computed uniformly and cheaply, and a national program processing millions of eligibility determinations has understandable reasons to prefer a metric that a computer can apply identically in every county. The defect of the metric was equally plain, since no veteran travels in a geodesic line, and the distance that governed access to care under the program bore no necessary relation to the distance that governed the burden of reaching care in a vehicle.

Road distance exceeds straight line distance in almost every landscape, and in broken country the gap between the two figures grows from a rounding difference into a different life. A residence measured at thirty five straight line miles from a facility might sit fifty or sixty road miles away where a river forced a long detour to a bridge, where a mountain range admitted passage through a single gap, where a peninsula required a drive around a bay, or where a rural road grid ran in long rectangular segments rather than toward the destination. Each of those geometries placed a veteran inside the forty mile line and outside any reasonable understanding of nearby care, and the veteran experience of the program in the first months reflected that inversion, since the population excluded by geodesic measurement overlapped heavily with the rural population whose travel burdens the distance route had been written to relieve. The measurement rule therefore selected against the very circumstances that made distance a burden, treating proximity on a map as a substitute for proximity on a road.

### Why did the way the department measured distance matter so much?

The Department first measured forty miles as a straight line, so a veteran separated from care by water, mountains, or winding roads could fall inside the line and remain ineligible, until the April 2015 change to driving distance roughly doubled eligibility, according to the Department.

Pressure for a different measurement arrived from several directions during the first months of 2015, and Government Executive reported on April 24, 2015 that the Department acted under pressure from veterans, lawmakers, and veteran advocates. At a Senate Committee on Veterans Affairs hearing on February 26, 2015, Senator Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut pressed Secretary McDonald on the forty mile rule and connected the measurement method to underuse of the program, as the office of Senator Blumenthal recorded in a statement dated April 24, 2015. Joseph A. Violante, national legislative director of Disabled American Veterans, argued in testimony before the Senate Committee on Veterans Affairs on March 24, 2015 that common sense and fairness required the distance criteria to be measured against a facility able to deliver the medical services the veteran was requesting. The argument shared a common structure across those sources, since each treated measurement as a policy decision disguised as arithmetic, and each insisted that a rule drawn on a map had redistributed a benefit that Congress had described in miles without specifying the kind of miles.

On April 24, 2015 the Department changed the definition by interim final rule, published in the Federal Register at volume 80, pages 22906 to 22909, amending title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part 17, and effective immediately on publication. Under the revised rule, eligibility based on place of residence turned on the distance the veteran had to drive to the nearest Department medical facility rather than the straight line or geodesic distance to that facility, and Government Executive reported that the Department used geographic information system software to calculate the driving distance. Secretary McDonald announced the change in a Department statement dated April 24, 2015 and framed the revision as an update that would allow more veterans to access care at the time and place of the veteran's choosing. The Department stated that the change from straight line to driving distance roughly doubled the number of eligible veterans, and letters went to the newly eligible veterans to notify each veteran of eligibility under the expansion. The same April 24, 2015 announcement aligned a related calculation by changing beneficiary travel mileage to the fastest route rather than the shortest route, a detail that underlined the larger point, since even driving distance contained choices about route, and every choice moved the boundary of eligibility for a definable group of households.

No word of the statute changed on April 24, 2015, and no number changed either, and that stability is precisely what gives the episode its analytical force. The forty mile figure stood untouched from enactment through the revision, the thirty day figure stood untouched beside the forty mile figure, and the population covered by the program roughly doubled, according to the Department, because a definition underneath one of the numbers changed. A veteran whose circumstances had failed the test on April 23, 2015 passed the test on April 24, 2015 without moving house, without a change in medical condition, and without any alteration in the road between home and facility, and the only fact that changed in that veteran case was the official meaning of distance. Eligibility under the Choice Program turned on two numbers and a definition, and the definition did more work than either number, because the numbers set the thresholds in the abstract while the definition decided which real journeys counted as long and which real waits counted as late. The clinically indicated date performed the same quiet labor inside the thirty day test that driving distance performed inside the forty mile test, defining the starting point from which the count ran, and the full service combination of hospital care, emergency services, and surgical care performed the same labor inside the state category, defining the kind of facility against which a state network was judged. Read closely, the Choice Program was less a pair of thresholds than a set of definitions wearing thresholds as an outer garment, and the April 2015 driving distance rule stands as the dated, documented moment when the Department conceded the point by rewriting a definition and doubling a program in a single Federal Register document.

## A Fixed Sum Attached to an Open Ended Promise

The money in the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 was arranged around a contrast that shaped everything that followed. On one side stood a promise defined by circumstance. A veteran who could not obtain an appointment within the statutory waiting period, or who lived beyond the statutory distance from a Department of Veterans Affairs medical facility, or who met another eligibility trigger in section 101, could receive an authorization for care outside the department, provided the department approved the episode in advance. On the other side stood a sum defined by a number, and nothing else. Section 802 established the Veterans Choice Fund in the Treasury, appropriated ten billion dollars to that fund, and made the money available only for carrying out section 101. The promise expanded or contracted with waiting lists, geography, clinical need, and administrative practice. The sum did neither. It waited in the fund, finite from the first day, and every authorized course of care drew it down.

That structure mattered for forecasting. A purchased care benefit inverts the control that the department exercises over care inside its own facilities. The department retained the authorization gate, and a Choice Card alone entitled a veteran to nothing, because departmental authorization had to precede private care. Yet once the statutory triggers were met and an authorization issued, the obligation belonged to the fund. The department could influence demand through the speed of its authorization decisions, the accuracy of its waiting time records, and the breadth of its provider networks. The department could not set the number of veterans who lived far from a facility, whose conditions required timely appointments, or whose local facilities lacked capacity at the moment of need. The fund therefore financed a demand driven benefit with the legal form of a capped account, and the cap was the feature that later produced repeated emergency legislation.

Congress placed limits inside the ten billion dollars as well as around it. The Congressional Research Service summary of section 802 recorded that no more than three hundred million dollars of the amount could be used for administrative expenses to implement section 101. The balance was for care itself, delivered by eligible non departmental providers and paid from the fund. The program was temporary in the original design. Section 101 was written to operate for three years or until the funds were exhausted, whichever came earlier, and the operative end date attached to that design was August 7, 2017. The fund language, by contrast, spoke in terms of money remaining available until expended. The two clocks, one calendrical and one financial, ran side by side from 2014 until Congress removed the calendar clock in April 2017, and much of the legislative activity described in this section consisted of adjusting one clock or the other as the balance moved.

The Choice appropriation was only the largest block in a larger emergency package. Title VIII, in section 801, authorized and appropriated five billion dollars for a different purpose, namely the repair of the departmental system whose failures had produced the demand for purchased care in the first place. The Congressional Research Service described section 801 as funding for the department to hire primary care and specialty care physicians and other medical staff, including nurses, social workers, and mental health professionals, and for the maintenance and operation of Veterans Health Administration facilities, including leases and minor construction. The Secretary had to submit a funding plan describing the intended use of the money and to report to Congress within one year of enactment on how the funds had been obligated. The hiring money was a defined investment in internal capacity, reported against a plan, and its logic ran in the opposite direction from the Choice Fund. Money spent successfully under section 801 would, over time, reduce the waiting times and capacity gaps that generated authorizations payable under section 802.

A third element of the package concerned physical space. Title VI, in section 601, authorized the department to carry out twenty seven major medical facility leases at specified locations, each for up to a specified amount, in communities distributed across the United States and including Ponce, Puerto Rico. Contemporary reporting on the conference agreement in July 2014 associated about one billion three hundred million dollars of the package with expanding the departmental footprint through those leases. Lease authorization of this kind is a slow instrument. A lease must be negotiated, a site fitted out, staff assigned, and a clinic opened before a single appointment can be offered. The leases therefore promised relief on a construction and contracting timetable, measured in years, while the Choice Fund was expected to relieve pressure on a clinical timetable, measured in days and weeks. The package thus combined an immediate purchasing mechanism, a medium term hiring program, and a long term facilities program, and asked all three to answer a crisis that had been documented in the spring of 2014 in terms of veterans waiting, in some reported cases, for months.

Added together, the elements of the act came to roughly sixteen billion three hundred million dollars, and Congress designated the package as emergency spending. Section 803 supplied the formal designations under the Statutory Pay As You Go Act of 2010 and the applicable budget resolution, with the effect, described by the Senate Republican Policy Committee, of waiving the mandatory spending restrictions that would ordinarily have applied. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the act would increase the federal deficit by twelve billion eight hundred million dollars over five years, a figure carried in the Senate Republican Policy Committee summary of the legislation and attributed there to the Congressional Budget Office. Congress had decided, in the summer of 2014, to pay for speed with borrowed money, accepting a deficit increase of a stated size rather than delaying the response until equivalent offsets could be assembled.

## From Slow Uptake to Repeated Shortfall

The first surprise produced by the fund was not exhaustion. It was the opposite. The program launched in November 2014, when the department began mailing Choice Cards to enrolled veterans, and early use fell far short of the level that a ten billion dollar appropriation implied. The Associated Press reported in February 2015, on the basis of a departmental report to Congress, that only about twenty seven thousand veterans had made appointments for private medical care since the mailing began, against about eight million six hundred thousand cards sent. The Government Accountability Office described the same pattern in institutional terms. Department officials had expected Choice to absorb much of the increased demand for care from non departmental providers after the disclosure of long waiting times. Instead, slow utilization left veterans receiving community care through previously established programs, which drew on the regular medical services appropriation rather than on the Choice Fund. A fund can be simultaneously underused for its stated purpose and inadequate for the system around it, and in the first half of 2015 the Choice Fund managed both conditions at once.

Confusion formed part of the explanation, and the confusion was structural rather than incidental. The card resembled an entitlement instrument. It arrived by mail, carried the language of choice, and was distributed to millions of veterans regardless of whether a particular veteran met a waiting time or distance trigger for a particular episode. The statute, however, made eligibility depend on facts that varied by episode and by facility, and required departmental authorization before care could be obtained at government expense. A veteran could hold a card and yet be ineligible for a desired appointment. A veteran could be eligible under the distance trigger as measured one way and ineligible as measured another. The original distance measurement used straight line distance, and reporting in February 2015 identified that measurement as a barrier for rural veterans whose road journeys far exceeded the distance as the crow flies. On April 24, 2015, the department announced and implemented the use of driving distance instead, and the department stated that the change would roughly double the number of eligible veterans. Every such correction widened the gate that the fund had to finance, and the financial consequences landed entirely on the same fixed balance.

The administration read the slow start as evidence that the money had been misallocated. In February 2015, as part of the fiscal year 2016 budget presentation, President Barack Obama stated that the administration would submit legislation to reallocate a portion of the Veterans Choice Program funding to support essential investments in departmental priorities. Helen Tierney, the departmental Assistant Secretary for Management, told reporters that veteran interest in obtaining care outside the department had not been as high as anticipated, that veterans had expressed a preference for care at departmental facilities from professionals known to them, and that the department sought transfer authority rather than additional money. The proposal met immediate resistance from the House Committee on Veterans Affairs. Representative Jeff Miller of Florida, the committee chairman, rejected the proposal in February 2015, called the idea a complete non starter, and stated that Congress and the President had made a promise to veterans in creating the Choice Program and that the administration attempt to reallocate its funding would renege on that promise. The dispute concerned who would decide the future size of the purchased care commitment. The administration treated an underused balance as a resource available for redeployment by budget proposal. The House committee leadership treated the appropriation as a commitment whose size only Congress could revise, and treated any reduction in the fund as a reduction in the promise itself.

Events in the spring of 2015 complicated the administration position and the committee position alike. The department faced a projected funding gap in its regular health care accounts even as the Choice Fund sat comparatively underused. The Government Accountability Office reported preliminary observations that the projected gap for fiscal year 2015 reached two billion seven hundred fifty million dollars, that higher than expected obligations for the longstanding care in the community programs accounted for about two billion three hundred forty million dollars of that gap, or eighty five percent of it, and that unanticipated obligations for hepatitis C drugs accounted for most of the remainder, at about four hundred eight million dollars. The department had failed to identify the projected gap until April 2015, six months into the fiscal year, according to the Government Accountability Office. The picture that emerged was of money in the wrong accounts rather than of money absent from the system. Purchased care demand existed in volume, flowing through older authorities with older appropriations, while the new authority attracted a fraction of the expected use because of administrative weakness, eligibility friction, and veteran unfamiliarity.

Congress answered that mismatch in July 2015 with the Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act, Public Law 114-41, signed on July 31, 2015. Despite a title dominated by highway programs, the act contained the first substantial revision of the Choice financing structure. Under section 4004 of that act, as described by the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service, the department was authorized to use approximately three billion three hundred fifty million dollars from the Veterans Choice Fund to pay for care provided to veterans through other specified non departmental care programs, for care furnished between May 1, 2015 and October 1, 2015. Within that amount, no more than five hundred million dollars was designated for costs associated with prescription medications for the treatment of hepatitis C. The authority expired on October 1, 2015. The act also widened Choice itself, removing the enrollment date restriction, clarifying the clinically indicated date as a basis for waiting time eligibility, and expanding the categories of eligible providers, according to the Congressional Research Service chronology. Public Law 114-41 therefore moved in two directions at once. It treated the Choice Fund as a reservoir from which other community care obligations could be paid during a defined window in 2015, and it broadened the eligibility rules that would govern draws on the same reservoir afterward. The February reallocation proposal had been resisted as a matter of principle when presented as an administration budget initiative. A congressionally enacted version of flexibility, narrower in time, capped in amount, and tied to identified obligations including hepatitis C treatment, passed within the same calendar year.

### Why did the Choice Fund keep running short?

The fund kept running short because Congress fixed the money in advance while eligibility turned on waits and distance that the department did not control. Once authorizations rose from hundreds of thousands to millions, each approved episode drew against the same finite balance without an automatic replenishment mechanism.

The surge that consumed the fund arrived after the administrative machinery matured. Secretary David Shulkin, in written testimony to the Senate Appropriations Subcommittee on Military Construction, Veterans Affairs, and Related Agencies on June 21, 2017, supplied the figures that documented the change. The department issued more than three hundred eighty thousand authorizations through the Choice Program in fiscal year 2015. In fiscal year 2016 the department issued more than two million authorizations, a more than fivefold increase over the fiscal year 2015 figure. In the first quarter of fiscal year 2017, authorizations rose by more than thirty percent over the same period of fiscal year 2016. Shulkin also reported that between March and May 2017 the department issued nearly eight hundred thousand authorizations, an increase of thirty two percent over the same three months of 2016, with March 2017 standing as the highest single month for authorizations since the program began. The provider network behind those authorizations had grown from approximately two hundred thousand providers and facilities in April 2015 to more than four hundred thirty thousand by March 2017, according to the same testimony. Each element of that growth was, in access terms, the program working. Each element was, in fund terms, an acceleration of the draw on a balance fixed in August 2014 on the basis of demand that nobody had measured, because the program that would generate the demand did not exist when the appropriation was written.

By the middle of June 2017 the arithmetic had become difficult to argue with. Shulkin testified that, as of June 9, 2017, nine billion two hundred million dollars of the Choice Fund had been obligated and seven billion one hundred million dollars had been expended. An authorization for an episode of care creates an obligation against the fund before a provider delivers the care, submits a claim, and receives payment. A fund can therefore be effectively committed well before its cash has moved, and program managers must plan against the obligated figure rather than the expended figure if authorizations are to be honored without interruption. Shulkin warned Congress in June 2017 that, at the pace of authorization then established, the fund faced exhaustion by January 2018 absent additional appropriations. In the same testimony he explained that the department had earlier expected an estimated one billion one hundred million dollars to remain unobligated on August 7, 2017, and that the spring 2017 acceleration had overtaken that expectation, eliminating the carryover balance on which the fiscal year 2018 budget request had partly relied. A departmental notice published in the Federal Register in December 2017 placed the expected exhaustion of all amounts deposited in the fund between January 2, 2018 and January 16, 2018, a window consistent with the June warning.

The calendar problem was solved before the money problem. On April 19, 2017, Public Law 115-26 was signed. That act removed the August 7, 2017 sunset from the Choice Program and authorized the program to continue until the funds in the Veterans Choice Fund were exhausted. Public Law 115-26 also made the department the primary payer in specified circumstances involving veterans with other health insurance, a coordination change with its own effects on the pace at which the fund paid for care. Removal of the sunset was necessary for a simple reason. An estimated balance of more than one billion dollars would otherwise have been stranded in a fund whose authorizing program had expired. The April 2017 act converted the program from a three year experiment with a hard end date into an open ended arrangement bounded only by money, and made the balance the sole governing limit on a benefit whose use the department could encourage, administer, and forecast, but could not ration by waiting list without recreating the conditions that the act of 2014 had been passed to end.

The money problem required a second statute in the same summer. On August 12, 2017, the VA Choice and Quality Employment Act of 2017, Public Law 115-46, authorized and appropriated two billion one hundred million dollars for deposit in the Veterans Choice Fund, to remain available until expended. Public Law 115-46 formed part of a larger measure of about three billion nine hundred million dollars that also authorized twenty eight major medical facility leases, pairing another infusion for purchased care with another round of investment in the departmental footprint, in a pattern that repeated the original structure of 2014 on a smaller scale. The two billion one hundred million dollars was presented as a bridge of roughly six months, intended to carry the program while Congress designed a consolidated community care system. The August appropriation averted an interruption of authorizations without altering the underlying design. The eligibility triggers were unchanged in their essentials. The authorization gate remained departmental. The fund remained a fixed sum, larger by two billion one hundred million dollars and subject to the same consumption dynamics that had consumed the original ten billion.

The final financing chapter covered by this profile belongs to the VA MISSION Act of 2018, Public Law 115-182, signed on June 6, 2018. The MISSION Act consolidated the departmental community care programs, including Choice, into a single Veterans Community Care Program scheduled to begin on June 6, 2019, and grandfathered Choice eligibility for specified veterans during the transition. Inside that redesign, the MISSION Act provided five billion two hundred million dollars in bridge funding for the Choice Program, so that authorizations under the outgoing structure could be honored while the consolidated program was built. The five billion two hundred million dollars was the largest single replenishment the fund received, and its placement inside a consolidation statute gave it a character that the emergency tranches of 2017 had lacked. The money was transition finance, sized against a defined migration from one program structure to another, with a scheduled start date for the successor arrangement. The bridge acknowledged the lesson of the preceding four years. A benefit defined by waiting times and distance generates obligations at a pace set by clinical need and departmental capacity, and any fixed sum attached to such a benefit is a forecast wearing the clothes of an appropriation. The Choice Fund began as ten billion dollars attached to a three year promise. It ended the period covered by this profile as a repeatedly replenished account whose successive balances recorded the distance between the demand Congress imagined in the summer of 2014 and the demand that eligible veterans presented once the machinery of authorization began to work.

## The Accountability Title Before the Courts

Section 707 of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, carried in Title VII, addressed a personnel problem rather than a medical one. The access provisions dealt with appointments, waiting times, and purchased care in the community. The accountability provisions dealt with the people who managed the system. Congress concluded, in the disclosures and hearings that followed the Phoenix reporting in the spring of 2014, that the ordinary machinery for disciplining senior managers in the Department of Veterans Affairs moved too slowly for a department in crisis, and that the Secretary required a faster instrument for removing or demoting senior executives whose performance or misconduct warranted that result. Title VII supplied that instrument in section 707, which added a new section 713 to title 38 of the United States Code. The litigation that followed tested one design choice within that instrument, the choice to make a hearing officer's decision final and unreviewable, and the test consumed the authority itself. Within three years, a court had severed the finality language, the Department of Justice had declined to defend it, the department had ceased to rely on the expedited track, and Congress had replaced the section with a different structure altogether.

The baseline was the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978. For members of the Senior Executive Service, the relevant disciplinary provisions were sections 7541 to 7543 of title 5. Section 7543(a) permitted a covered action only for misconduct, neglect of duty, malfeasance, or failure to accept a directed reassignment or to accompany a position in a transfer of function. Section 7543(b) attached procedural entitlements to any proposed action: at least thirty days of advance written notice stating specific reasons, subject to an exception where there was reasonable cause to believe the employee had committed a crime punishable by imprisonment; a reasonable time, and not less than seven days, to answer orally and in writing and to furnish affidavits and documentary evidence; representation by an attorney or another representative; and a written decision with specific reasons at the earliest practicable date. An agency could also provide a hearing by regulation. Section 7543(d) entitled an executive against whom an action was taken to appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board under section 7701 of title 5.

That appeal was layered, and each layer added time. Under the ordinary Board process described by the Congressional Research Service, an appeal could be heard by the Board or referred to an administrative law judge or an administrative judge, whose decision was an initial decision. An initial decision generally became the final decision of the Board unless a party, or the Director of the Office of Personnel Management, petitioned the full Board for review, or the Board reopened the case on its own motion. A final Board order was then subject to judicial review in the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit under section 7703 of title 5. A hearing officer therefore found facts and applied law in the first instance, the presidentially appointed members of the Board could correct the hearing officer, and a federal court could correct the Board. A contested removal remained reversible, with the remedies that followed reversal, for the whole of that period. The framework also split by basis. A career appointee removed from the Senior Executive Service for performance under the provisions associated with section 3592 of title 5 received an informal hearing before an official designated by the Board rather than a full section 7701 appeal, and the hearing did not delay removal. A career appointee removed for misconduct under sections 7541 to 7543 received the fuller appeal. The Board observed, in analysis discussed by the Congressional Research Service, that an appeals process did not itself delay a misconduct removal, which took effect before the appeal was heard. The congressional emphasis in 2014 fell on finality: on how long a removal remained reversible, and on the position of a Secretary who had decided an executive should go and then faced successive proceedings in which the decision could be undone. Section 713 compressed every stage that sections 7541 to 7543 and section 7701 had left open.

Section 707 of Public Law 113-146, enacted on August 7, 2014, created section 713 as a self contained authority for the department alone. Section 713(a) authorized the Secretary to remove an individual employed in a senior executive position if the Secretary determined that performance or misconduct warranted removal. The Secretary could remove the individual from federal service, or transfer the individual to a General Schedule position for which the individual was qualified and that the Secretary determined was appropriate, at the pay rate of that position. The definition reached beyond career appointees in the Senior Executive Service to departmental equivalents, namely health care professionals such as physicians and dentists holding administrative or executive positions in the Veterans Health Administration under appointments under sections 7306(a) or 7401(1) of title 38, as the Congressional Research Service summary recorded. The authority therefore reached directors of medical centers and health care systems, whether or not those managers were career members of the Senior Executive Service in the ordinary title 5 sense.

Compression operated at three points. Before the decision, section 713 stated no express notice and answer procedure of the kind in section 7543(b); departmental guidelines granted advance notice of five days and an opportunity to respond in writing, according to the Congressional Research Service report on the authority, in place of the thirty day notice guarantee in section 7543(b)(1). For appeal, sections 713(d)(2)(A) and 713(d)(2)(B) required filing with the Board not later than seven days after removal or transfer, far shorter than the thirty day period governing most Board appeals. The Board was required to refer the appeal to an administrative judge, who was required to expedite it and decide not later than twenty one days after filing. If the judge did not decide within that period, the decision of the Secretary became final by operation of the statute, and section 713(e)(4) barred the Board and the judge from staying a removal or transfer while the appeal proceeded. Finality completed the design. Section 713(e)(2) provided that, notwithstanding any other provision of law, including section 7703 of title 5, the decision of an administrative judge would be final and not subject to any further appeal. The full Board could not review its own judge, and the Federal Circuit could not review the decision. A judge who sustained a removal ended the matter, and a judge who reversed a removal likewise ended the matter. That single level finality set personnel appeals under section 713 apart from [the law governing how VA claims are administered](/2019/06/15/va-claims-process-law/), a field organized around successive levels of administrative decision and judicial scrutiny, and the contrast marked the place where a constitutional objection could attach.

The department used the authority within months, and the most prominent use produced the test case. Sharon Helman was director of the Phoenix Veterans Affairs Health Care System, the system at which the waiting list reporting of the spring of 2014 had originated. On November 10, 2014, Deputy Secretary Sloan Gibson notified Helman in writing of a pending action to remove her from federal service under section 707, codified at section 713. The charges, as the Board record summarised them, were lack of oversight, conduct unbecoming a senior executive, and failure to report gifts. The notice gave Helman five business days after receipt to submit a written response, in line with the departmental guidelines described by the Congressional Research Service. Helman responded through counsel on November 17, 2014. On November 24, 2014, the Deputy Secretary notified Helman that, after consideration of the reply and the evidence, removal from federal service would take effect immediately. The interval from notice to decision was fourteen days; under section 7543(b), the notice period alone would have run at least thirty days.

Helman appealed to the Board on December 1, 2014, within the seven day window in section 713, and requested a hearing. Helman raised a claim of harmful procedural error, withdrew the hearing request at the prehearing conference, and asked for a decision on the written record. On December 22, 2014, the administrative judge affirmed the removal within the twenty one day period in section 713(e)(1). The judge found that the department had failed to prove the specifications of lack of oversight, and had proved both specifications of conduct unbecoming a senior executive and both specifications of failure to report gifts. The judge rejected the defenses presented, including a due process contention and the harmful procedural error claim, and found that Helman had not overcome the presumption that removal was a reasonable penalty. The removal therefore stood on the conduct and gift reporting charges rather than on the oversight charge. Section 713(e)(2) made that divided merits decision conclusive on issuance. Helman sought more time to pursue review by the full Board, and the Clerk of the Board, citing finality under section 713(e)(2), indicated that the Board would take no further action. The Board, composed of members appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, thus declined to examine a decision issued in its name because the statute forbade examination. Helman petitioned the Federal Circuit for review. The department contended, in the posture recorded by the Congressional Research Service, that preclusion of judicial review followed from the language, objectives, and legislative history of section 713. Helman answered with constitutional claims under the Appointments Clause, the Due Process Clause and the Fifth Amendment, and Article III, and the Federal Circuit directed briefing on jurisdiction during 2015 in the case docketed as number 15-3086.

## Remand, Abandonment, and Legislative Repair

The defect lay in the status of the decision maker. Administrative judges at the Board were employees, hired as employees, rather than officers appointed in the manner Article II prescribes, and the Federal Circuit recorded agreement between the parties on that status. In an ordinary section 7701 appeal, that status presented no difficulty, because the decision of the judge was an initial decision subject to review by the Board and then by the Federal Circuit; supervision by appointed officers stood between the work of the employee and a conclusive judgment. Section 713 removed the supervision and left the work. A judge under section 713 weighed evidence, sustained or rejected specifications, resolved defenses, assessed the penalty, and issued a decision binding on the department, the executive, the Board, and the courts. The same judge could overturn a removal ordered by the Secretary of Veterans Affairs, a cabinet officer appointed by the President with Senate consent and accountable to the President, and no appointed officer could reverse that result. In the language the litigation developed from Buckley v. Valeo, the judge exercised significant authority pursuant to the laws of the United States. Under the Appointments Clause, significant authority of that character may be exercised only by an officer appointed as the Clause provides, as a principal officer through presidential nomination and Senate confirmation or as an inferior officer through appointment vested by Congress in the President alone, in a court of law, or in a head of a department. Hiring by the Board satisfied none of those methods.

The Department of Justice reached that conclusion before the court did. By letter dated May 31, 2016, the department advised the Speaker of the House of Representatives that the department would not defend section 713(e)(2) against the Appointments Clause challenge in the Helman case. The letter reasoned that section 707 required referral of an appeal to an administrative judge and committed resolution to the final and unreviewable discretion of that judge under sections 713(e)(1) and 713(e)(2). Administrative judges, the letter stated, were not appointed as officers, but were employees designated by the Board to administer oaths, examine witnesses, take depositions, and receive evidence, citing section 1204 of title 5 and section 1201.4(a) of title 5 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Section 707 thus vested an employee with final authority, unreviewable by any politically accountable officer, to uphold or overrule the removal decision of a cabinet level officer, a scheme the letter described as impairing presidential supervision of the civil service laws and as inconsistent with the Appointments Clause. The refusal was expressly narrow: the department would defend the bulk of the statute and would recommend invalidation and severance of section 713(e)(2) and provisions inextricably intertwined with it, while defending the remainder against the other constitutional arguments Helman had raised. The letter also recorded that Congress was considering legislation to address the infirmity, including a bill drafted in consultation with the department. The practical consequence followed during the summer of 2016. Reporting by Government Executive in July 2016 recorded the subsequent decision of the department to cease use of the expedited firing authority in the 2014 statute, and twelve military and veterans organizations sought leave to intervene in the Federal Circuit to defend provisions the government would no longer defend. Without reliance on the expedited track after May 31, 2016, senior executive personnel action fell back on sections 7541 to 7543 and other authorities available to the Secretary, and section 713 survived as text while the track it created had closed as an operating instrument.

### What did the Federal Circuit actually decide in the Helman case?

The Federal Circuit held that section 713(e)(2) violated the Appointments Clause by making an administrative judge's decision final and unreviewable, severed that provision and related finality language in section 713(e), left the remainder of section 713 intact, and remanded for review by the Merit Systems Protection Board.

Helman v. Department of Veterans Affairs, 856 F.3d 920, decided on May 9, 2017, by Chief Judge Prost for a panel that also included Circuit Judges Clevenger and Chen, set section 713 beside Freytag v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue, where special trial judges of the Tax Court conducted trials, exercised significant discretion, and rendered decisions of the Tax Court. The Federal Circuit concluded that authority to render a final decision affirming or overturning the removal decision of the Secretary was an important function and more than a ministerial task, and a significant duty that only officers of the United States could perform. A judge who could decide with no further review was, for that purpose, subordinate to no officer, because no officer could direct, supervise, or correct the result. Congress had purported to vest that authority in judges hired as employees, and the vesting was unconstitutional. The court declared invalid the portions of section 713 expressly keyed to finality: section 713(e)(2) in its entirety and portions of sections 713(e)(3) and 713(e)(5). Part of section 713(e)(3) fell because the word final created an indirect grant of final authority, under which a judge could decline to decide within twenty one days and thereby make a removal or transfer final without any appointed officer passing on the case. On severability, the court accepted the position of the government over the position of Helman, who had urged invalidation of section 713 in its entirety. The invalid portions were severable because the remainder remained fully operative as law and advanced the ends Congress had sought, and the excised statute operated as an expedited Board appeal in which the decision of the judge functioned as an initial decision subject to Board review. The court remanded for the Board to take appropriate action on the petition by Helman for review of that initial decision. Secretary David J. Shulkin stated on May 9, 2017, that the ruling directed Board review of the decision upholding the November 2014 removal of the Phoenix director, and that the removal remained in effect at that time.

Congress supplied the replacement six weeks after the decision. The Department of Veterans Affairs Accountability and Whistleblower Protection Act of 2017, Public Law 115-41, signed on June 23, 2017, rewrote section 713 in section 201 of that Act under a heading covering removal, demotion, or suspension of senior executives based on performance or misconduct. The substitution changed the forum for contesting a decision, the clock, and the route to a court, and each change answered the invalidated design. The Board disappeared from the senior executive procedure. Decisional authority rested with the Secretary, reconsideration took place through a grievance inside the department, and review outside the department took place in a court. A decision that was not grieved, and a grievance decision, were final and conclusive as a departmental matter, and the individual adversely affected could then obtain judicial review. No employee exercised conclusive authority over the action of the Secretary, and the Appointments Clause problem identified in Helman had no counterpart in that structure.

The terms preserved speed while relocating review. Under new section 713(a), the Secretary could reprimand or suspend, involuntarily reassign, demote, or remove a covered individual from a senior executive position if the Secretary determined that misconduct or performance warranted the action, and removal from the position could be accompanied by removal from the civil service. Under section 713(b)(1), the individual was entitled to advance notice and a file containing all evidence in support of the proposed action, to representation by an attorney or another representative of the choice of the individual, and to grieve the action under an internal grievance process that the Secretary, in consultation with the Assistant Secretary for Accountability and Whistleblower Protection, was required to establish. The Assistant Secretary headed the Office of Accountability and Whistleblower Protection created by Title I of the same Act, was appointed by the President under section 308(a) of title 38, and reported directly to the Secretary. Under section 713(b)(2), the aggregate period for notice, response, and decision could not exceed fifteen business days, the response period was seven business days, and a written decision with specific reasons was to issue not later than fifteen business days after notice. Under section 713(b)(3), the Secretary was required to ensure that the grievance process took fewer than twenty one days. Section 713(c) provided that section 3592(b)(1) of title 5 and the procedures under section 7543(b) of title 5 did not apply, and section 713(d) defined covered individuals to include career appointees as defined in section 3132(a)(4) of title 5 and individuals in administrative or executive positions appointed under sections 7306(a), 7401(1), or 7401(4) of title 38, with misconduct defined to include neglect of duty, malfeasance, and failure to accept a directed reassignment or to accompany a position in a transfer of function. Under sections 713(b)(5) and 713(b)(6), a covered individual adversely affected by an ungrieved decision or by a grievance decision could obtain judicial review, in which the court reviewed the record and could set aside action found to be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law; obtained without procedures required by law; or unsupported by substantial evidence. FedSmith analysis in June 2017 observed that the absence of Board review avoided the defect that had invalidated the 2014 finality provision. For the article record closed on April 1, 2019, the operative senior executive authority was the authority of the 2017 Act: a fifteen business day decision clock and a grievance measured in fewer than twenty one days, without the element that had made the 2014 clock conclusive.

## Implementation, Evidence, and the Search for a Durable Model

The implementation record began with a timetable that left little room for orderly construction. The statute was signed on August 7, 2014, and the Choice Program launched in November 2014, a span of roughly ninety days in which the Department of Veterans Affairs had to define eligibility, build or borrow provider networks, arrange a method for authorizing care, and explain an unfamiliar benefit to millions of enrolled veterans. The department did not build that machinery from nothing, because contracts for the Patient Centered Community Care program, known as PC3, were already in place, and the Choice contracts were modifications of that existing work. Even with that head start, the gap between the simplicity of the political promise and the complexity of the administrative task shaped almost everything that followed. A card suggested a straightforward transaction. The program behind the card required a determination that the veteran was enrolled, that a statutory trigger had been met, that the department had authorized care in advance, and that a network provider had accepted the appointment.

The card itself became the first source of confusion, and the confusion was built into the design that Congress had chosen. The department mailed Choice Cards on a wide scale, and the department figure for cards issued stood at about eight and a half million. Eligibility to receive a card and eligibility to use the program were different things, because the card went to enrolled veterans as a class while the right to obtain community care under section 101 depended on a specific trigger, such as a wait beyond thirty days or a residence beyond forty miles from the nearest department facility, together with authorization from the department before care was received. A veteran who presented the card to a provider without that authorization could therefore discover that the department would not pay for the visit, even though the physical card appeared to promise the opposite. The department adopted a phased mailing, sending cards first to veterans identified by distance, in an effort to keep the volume of inquiries within the capacity of the telephone systems built to answer them.

Call center confusion compounded the difficulty, and testimony during the first year made the problem a matter of congressional record. Deputy Secretary Sloan Gibson told the Senate Committee on Veterans Affairs in March 2015 that many veterans were frustrated with the Choice Program and that confusion of that kind led to lower use of the benefit. The frustration had concrete sources that Gibson identified in the same period, among them a distance rule that measured a veteran against the nearest department facility even when that facility did not offer the service the veteran needed, so that a veteran could live close to a small clinic and far from the relevant care and remain outside the program. Veterans who telephoned to test eligibility encountered a sequence rather than a single answer, because a call could confirm enrollment and yet leave open whether a wait time trigger had been calculated from a clinically indicated date and whether an authorization had issued. The department changed one of those variables on April 24, 2015, when it announced that distance would be measured by driving distance rather than straight line distance, and the department stated at the time that the change would roughly double the number of veterans eligible on distance grounds. A rule that changed within the first six months solved one inequity while confirming, for veterans who had been refused under the earlier method, that the answers given through the call centers depended on a definition that the department itself had replaced.

Scheduling fell largely to two third party administrators, TriWest Healthcare Alliance and Health Net Federal Services, and the division of labor between the department and those companies defined the lived experience of the program. The Congressional Research Service account of implementation described the two firms as the third party administrators for the program, with Health Net or TriWest authorizing services on behalf of the department, building and maintaining the networks of community providers from which appointments were made, and handling much of the contact with veterans and providers. The Government Accountability Office, in the report discussed below, described the same arrangement from the oversight side, noting that the department used two contractors to schedule most Choice Program appointments after receiving referrals from department medical centers. The administrators therefore stood between the medical center that identified a need for care and the community provider who would deliver that care, receiving the referral, contacting the veteran, finding a network provider, and setting the appointment. Each handoff added days that counted against the veteran even though no clinical encounter had occurred, and each created a separate record in a separate system.

Utilization grew steadily after the hesitant opening, and the figures require the same care about sources and periods that the wait time figures require. Secretary David Shulkin stated in June 2017 that the program had produced more than two million authorizations in fiscal year 2016, and that authorizations in the first quarter of fiscal year 2017 had risen by more than thirty percent over the corresponding quarter of the preceding year. Those figures measured authorizations rather than completed appointments, so the numbers established growth in the use of the authority without establishing how many discrete encounters followed. The contrast with the opening months was sharp. Department officials told the Senate Committee on Veterans Affairs in March 2015 that nearly eight million cards had been distributed since November 2014, while about forty six thousand requests for care had been approved, a pairing that senators and veterans organizations cited at that hearing as evidence that distribution had outrun use. By the period covered by the Shulkin statement, community care purchased under the Choice authority had become a routine component of department care rather than the exceptional release valve described during the passage debate in the summer of 2014.

The most systematic judgment on timeliness came from the Government Accountability Office in June 2018, in report GAO-18-281, titled Veterans Choice Program: Improvements Needed to Address Access-Related Challenges as VA Plans Consolidation of its Community Care Programs. The office found that a veteran referred to the Choice Program for routine care could potentially wait up to seventy calendar days for care if department medical centers and the third party administrators each took the maximum time the department allowed for their steps in the scheduling process, a structure that sat uneasily beside a statute built around care within thirty days of the clinically indicated date. The finding rested on two separate analyses of 2016 authorizations that the report set side by side. An analysis by the Veterans Health Administration of about five thousand authorizations created between July and September 2016 produced an average of fifty one calendar days for veterans to receive care, while an analysis by the Government Accountability Office of fifty five routine care authorizations created between January and April 2016 produced an average of at least sixty four calendar days. The report was careful about the limits of those samples, which were not generalizable to the whole program.

That data finding was that the department could not systematically monitor the timeliness of access to Choice Program care because the data available for the task were incomplete and unreliable. The Government Accountability Office identified three specific defects in GAO-18-281. The data did not capture the timeliness of referring veterans and opting veterans into the program, so the segment of the wait spent inside department medical centers before a referral reached an administrator fell outside the measurement. Clinically indicated dates, the dates from which timeliness was calculated, were sometimes changed by medical center personnel before referrals were sent to the administrators, which could mask the true length of a wait. Data on the timeliness of urgent care were unreliable, including inconsistencies in whether authorizations were categorized as routine or urgent. The office made ten recommendations directed at the consolidated community care program that the department was designing, among them the establishment of an achievable wait time goal for community care and the design of a scheduling process consistent with that goal. The department concurred with eight of the ten recommendations connected with those wait time findings, according to the summary the Government Accountability Office later provided in testimony designated GAO-19-571T. A second report, GAO-18-671, issued in September 2018 on timely payment to community providers, found that the department did not collect data or monitor compliance for certain requirements affecting how promptly providers were paid.

The Commission on Care supplied the other major institutional judgment of the period, and its final report of June 30, 2016 answered a different question from the one the auditors had asked. Created by the 2014 statute itself, the commission spent about ten months examining how veterans health care should be organized over the following two decades, and it produced eighteen recommendations for extensive organizational transformation. Its first recommendation proposed that the Veterans Health Administration establish high performing, integrated community based health care networks, to be known as the VHA Care System, combining department providers and facilities with Department of Defense and other federally funded providers and with community providers credentialed by the Veterans Health Administration, from which veterans would obtain care under a national delivery strategy shaped by local input. The commission coupled that network proposal with recommendations on governance, including a board of directors, and on facilities, information technology, and personnel. During its deliberations in early 2016, seven of its fifteen members proposed closing department facilities over time and converting the department primarily into a payer for private sector care. The final report did not adopt that course. Chair Nancy Schlichting warned against recommending a payer only role, and the report that twelve of fifteen commissioners endorsed instead proposed redesigning delivery inside a strengthened department acting as both payer and provider, integrating community capacity around a department core rather than dismantling that core.

## Succession Under a New Statute and the Questions Left Open

The succession arrived through legislation, because the Choice Program had been written as a temporary authority tied to a finite fund. Public Law 115-26, signed on April 19, 2017, removed the original August 2017 sunset and allowed the program to continue until the Choice Fund was exhausted, and later legislation in August 2017 added two point one billion dollars. Secretary Shulkin warned in June 2017 that the fund could be exhausted by January 2018 without additional money. Congress answered with a permanent structure in [the VA MISSION Act guide](/2019/05/01/va-mission-act-2018-guide/), which consolidated the authorities under which the department purchased care in the community and placed them inside a single program with its own eligibility rules. The MISSION Act also supplied five point two billion dollars in bridge funding for Choice during the transition, an acknowledgment in appropriations form that the old program had to be kept operating until the new one was ready to receive its patients.

### What replaced the thirty day and forty mile tests?

The VA MISSION Act consolidated community care into the Veterans Community Care Program, scheduled to begin on June 6, 2019. Secretary Robert Wilkie announced access standards based on average drive time and appointment wait time, replacing distance in miles and a single thirty day test with separate thresholds for different kinds of care.

The VA MISSION Act of 2018, Public Law 115-182, was signed on June 6, 2018. Its central community care provision directed the department to establish the Veterans Community Care Program, consolidating the Choice Program and the other community care authorities into that single program, with the consolidated program set to begin on June 6, 2019, one year after enactment. The statute grandfathered a defined class of veterans who had been eligible under the forty mile criterion of the Choice Program, preserving community care eligibility for veterans who met that distance test on the day before enactment and who continued to reside in a location that qualified under that criterion. The statute also directed the department to set access standards, and the choice of standards was left to the Secretary rather than fixed in the statutory text, which transferred the most politically sensitive judgment in the program, the point at which a wait or a journey becomes long enough to justify care outside the department, from Congress to the department that would administer the result.

Secretary Robert Wilkie announced those standards in January 2019, and the announced figures were stated as the standards for the June 2019 launch of the Veterans Community Care Program. For drive time, a veteran facing an average drive time of thirty minutes or more to obtain primary care or mental health care from the department would have the option of community care, while the corresponding figure for specialty care was an average drive time of sixty minutes. For appointment waits, a veteran facing a wait of twenty days or more for primary care or mental health care would have that option, while the wait standard for specialty care was twenty eight days. Distance in miles gave way to time in traffic, and a single threshold gave way to thresholds that varied by type of care. The announced standards were also more permissive than the tests they replaced, thirty minutes against forty miles and twenty days against thirty days, which meant that the population eligible for community care on access grounds was designed to grow as the temporary program became permanent.

An honest account of the 2014 statute has to record what the statute did not do, beginning with the failure that had produced the legislation. The Phoenix scandal, as the Office of Inspector General documented in 2014, rested on the manipulation of scheduling records and the maintenance of unofficial waiting lists against an internal fourteen day target, practices that concealed the length of real waits from the officials charged with correcting them. The 2014 act appropriated money for hiring and for facilities, created a purchased care authority, and imposed reporting requirements, but the act did not rebuild the scheduling systems whose manipulation had caused the scandal, and the June 2018 findings of the Government Accountability Office about altered clinically indicated dates showed that the integrity of the dates on which access was measured remained a live problem four years after enactment. The act also did not settle the structural question that ran underneath every eligibility debate, whether community care should function as a supplement that relieves a department system at points of demonstrated failure or as a substitute that a veteran may elect regardless of department performance. Every threshold in the statute implied an answer, because a thirty day test treats community care as a remedy for delay and a forty mile test treats community care as a remedy for distance, but neither test stated a principle from which the next threshold could be derived, and the MISSION Act inherited the same silence when it delegated the standards to the Secretary.

The accountability title supplied the third item in that honest account, because the authority most closely associated with consequences for the scandal survived only in rewritten form. Section 707 of the 2014 act, codified at 38 United States Code section 713, allowed the Secretary to remove or demote members of the Senior Executive Service through an expedited process in which an administrative judge of the Merit Systems Protection Board was to decide an appeal within twenty one days, with that decision final and not subject to further review by the Board or by a court. The department used the authority in November 2014 to remove Sharon Helman, director of the Phoenix VA Health Care System, and an administrative judge sustained that removal. The Department of Justice declined on May 31, 2016 to defend the finality provision, and on May 9, 2017 the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit held in Helman v. Department of Veterans Affairs, 856 F.3d 920, that the provision violated the Appointments Clause because administrative judges are employees rather than constitutionally appointed officers and could not be vested with final and unreviewable decision authority. Congress then replaced the mechanism in the Department of Veterans Affairs Accountability and Whistleblower Protection Act of 2017, Public Law 115-41, signed on June 23, 2017, whose section 201 amended section 713 to provide an internal grievance procedure and judicial review for senior executives.

The closing assessment therefore turns on two arguments that the record of implementation supports in different ways, and each deserves statement at full strength. The access argument, associated with the congressional sponsors of the 2014 act, Senator John McCain, Senator Bernie Sanders, and Representative Jeff Miller among them, and with veterans groups that pressed for wider eligibility, held that a veteran who has been told to wait beyond a defined interval or to travel beyond a defined distance has already received the department answer that matters, and that requiring that veteran to wait for the department to repair itself converts an institutional failure into a personal penalty. On that view, eligibility rules that measure proximity to any facility rather than to the needed service, or that measure distance in a straight line rather than by road, are not neutral administrative choices but devices that ration a benefit Congress intended to deliver, and the remedy is to widen the gate. Senator McCain carried the argument further in 2016, when legislation he introduced would have made the Choice Program permanent and opened eligibility to every veteran enrolled in department care. Concerned Veterans for America pressed a version of the same position through task force proposals for restructuring department care around community provision. The utilization figures lend the argument a factual footing, because authorizations above two million in fiscal year 2016 demonstrated that a substantial population of veterans, once the gate was reached, used community care rather than the department alternative on offer.

The integrated care argument, associated with the Commission on Care in its final report of June 30, 2016 and with veterans groups that warned against hollowing out the department, held that the value of the department system lies in coordination that purchased care fragments. Disabled American Veterans, through Executive Director Garry Augustine, opposed unfettered choice on the ground that giving every veteran a card and unrestricted access to community providers would drain resources from the department system and leave that system to wither, a result that would fall hardest on veterans with service connected disabilities who rely on department care most heavily. The commission made a cognate point in institutional rather than fiscal language, reporting that veterans who receive care exclusively through the Veterans Health Administration generally receive well coordinated care, while fragmentation across unconnected providers tends to produce lower quality and to shift costs among payers. On that view, community care belongs inside a network that the department credentials and manages, which was the design of the VHA Care System that the commission recommended. Later debates over toxic exposure care, addressed in [the PACT Act guide](/2019/04/15/pact-act-2022-guide/), would return to the same underlying question of where specialized, service connected care is best organized. The two arguments rest on different accounts of what the Phoenix scandal proved, one reading the scandal as evidence that a captive population needs an exit, the other reading the scandal as evidence that a neglected public system needs investment and competent management rather than a competitor financed from its own appropriation, and the statutes of 2014 and 2018 each enacted parts of both readings without adopting either reading in full.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Veterans Choice Act create?

The Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, Public Law 113-146, created the Veterans Choice Program, a temporary authority under section 101 for the Department of Veterans Affairs to furnish hospital care and medical services through private providers when department facilities could not deliver timely or nearby care. The act also created the Veterans Choice Fund with 10 billion dollars to pay for that care, appropriated 5 billion dollars for hiring and facilities inside the department, established the Commission on Care to study the delivery system, and created an expedited procedure for removing senior executives. President Barack Obama signed the act on August 7, 2014, and the Choice Program began operating in November 2014.

### Q: What scandal led to the Veterans Choice Act?

Reporting from Phoenix in the spring of 2014 alleged that schedulers at the Phoenix VA Health Care System kept unofficial waiting lists that concealed long delays from the department's official records, which were measured against an internal target of appointments within 14 days. The department's Office of Inspector General issued an interim report on May 28, 2014 substantiating that about 1,700 Phoenix veterans sat on unofficial lists, with delays of up to 115 days, and a department access audit in June 2014 reported more than 57,000 veterans waiting for appointments nationwide. Secretary Eric Shinseki resigned on May 30, 2014, and Congress enacted the statute roughly ninety days after the reporting broke.

### Q: What was the 40 mile rule in the Veterans Choice Act?

The forty mile rule was one of the eligibility triggers in section 101 of the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014. A veteran enrolled in department health care qualified for the Choice Program if the veteran resided more than forty miles from the nearest Department of Veterans Affairs medical facility. The statute set the distance but did not say how to measure it, and the department first measured straight line distance, which excluded veterans whose road journey was far longer than the map distance. On April 24, 2015, the department changed the measurement to driving distance and stated that the number of eligible veterans would roughly double.

### Q: What was the 30 day wait standard in the Veterans Choice Act?

The thirty day standard was the wait time trigger in section 101 of the 2014 act. A veteran qualified for care through the Veterans Choice Program when the department could not schedule an appointment within thirty days of the clinically indicated date, meaning the date a clinician judged appropriate for the care, or within thirty days of the date the veteran specified when no clinically indicated date applied. Meeting the standard did not send the veteran to a private provider automatically. The department first had to authorize the care, and the authorization process, rather than the standard itself, often determined how quickly community care began.

### Q: Did the Veterans Choice Act make it easier to fire senior executives?

Yes, by design. Section 707 of the act added section 713 to title 38 of the United States Code and gave the Secretary authority to remove or demote Senior Executive Service employees under an expedited procedure in which an administrative judge of the Merit Systems Protection Board was directed to decide any appeal within twenty one days, with the decision final and unreviewable. The design did not survive. In Helman v. Department of Veterans Affairs, decided May 9, 2017, the Federal Circuit held the finality provision unconstitutional under the Appointments Clause, and Congress replaced the structure in the Department of Veterans Affairs Accountability and Whistleblower Protection Act of 2017.

### Q: Why did the Veterans Choice Act run out of funding?

The act gave the Veterans Choice Program a fixed appropriation of 10 billion dollars while defining eligibility by circumstance, so spending rose and fell with demand the department did not control. Use began slowly in 2015, when confusion and narrow distance measurement suppressed authorizations, then grew steeply, passing two million authorizations in fiscal 2016 as eligibility widened and the provider network expanded. Congress removed the original August 2017 end date in April 2017, added 2.1 billion dollars in August 2017, and appropriated a further 5.2 billion dollars as bridge funding inside the VA MISSION Act of 2018 to carry the program toward consolidation.

### Q: What replaced the Veterans Choice Act?

The VA MISSION Act of 2018, Public Law 115-182, signed on June 6, 2018, replaced the Choice framework by consolidating the department's several community care authorities into a single Veterans Community Care Program, which was scheduled to begin on June 6, 2019. The successor program dropped the thirty day wait trigger and the forty mile distance rule in favor of access standards announced in January 2019: average drive times of thirty minutes for primary care and mental health and sixty minutes for specialty care, and appointment waits of twenty days for primary care and mental health and twenty eight days for specialty care. Veterans eligible under the forty mile rule were grandfathered.

### Q: How did Congress vote on the Veterans Choice Act?

The act passed both chambers by near unanimous margins at the end of July 2014. The House of Representatives agreed to the conference report on July 30, 2014 by 420 votes to 5, and the Senate agreed on July 31, 2014 by 91 votes to 3, with Senators Coburn, Corker, and Sessions in the negative. The conference had been co chaired by Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Jeff Miller after the Senate passed its version, S. 2450 substituted into H.R. 3230, by 93 votes to 3 on June 11, 2014. President Barack Obama signed the bill on August 7, 2014 as Public Law 113-146.

### Q: Did a veteran need approval before using the Veterans Choice Program?

Yes. Eligibility and authorization were separate steps, and confusing them was the most common misunderstanding of the program. A veteran could meet a statutory trigger, through a wait longer than thirty days or a residence beyond forty miles, and still require the department to authorize the episode of care before a private provider visit would be paid for. The Veterans Choice Card mailed from November 2014 did not itself authorize anything, and the card carried printed language to that effect. The department or one of its third party administrators confirmed eligibility, approved the provider, and issued the authorization, and care obtained without that step risked leaving the veteran with the bill.

### Q: What was the Veterans Choice Card?

The Veterans Choice Card was the physical card the Department of Veterans Affairs mailed to enrolled veterans from November 2014, reaching about 8.5 million veterans across the mailing phases. The card identified its holder as potentially eligible for the Veterans Choice Program and carried the telephone number used to request authorization for private care. It was not an insurance card, a payment card, or a guarantee of coverage, and the department's accompanying letter said so in direct terms. Many recipients treated the card as proof of entitlement, and the gap between what the card appeared to promise and what the authorization process delivered became a recurring source of complaint during the program's first year.

### Q: How much money did the Veterans Choice Act provide?

The act provided about 16.3 billion dollars in emergency designated funding. Section 802 established the Veterans Choice Fund in the Treasury with 10 billion dollars for the Choice Program, of which no more than 300 million dollars could pay for administering section 101. Section 801 appropriated 5 billion dollars to hire physicians, nurses, and other medical staff and to maintain and improve department facilities, including leases and minor construction. Section 601 separately authorized twenty seven major medical facility leases, which the Congressional Budget Office estimated would produce about 1.3 billion dollars of direct spending between 2017 and 2024. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the whole package would raise the deficit by 12.8 billion dollars over five years.

### Q: What was the Commission on Care?

The Commission on Care was a fifteen member body created by section 202 of the 2014 act to examine how the Department of Veterans Affairs delivered care and to recommend how the system should be organized over the following twenty years. Its members were appointed by the President and congressional leaders and drawn from health care delivery, management, and veterans service organizations. The commission issued its final report on June 30, 2016, with eighteen recommendations. Its central proposal was to build integrated networks combining department facilities with community partners under department coordination, and twelve of fifteen commissioners endorsed the report, rejecting a proposal from seven members that would have converted the department toward a payer only role.

### Q: What did the Federal Circuit decide about the Veterans Choice Act?

In Helman v. Department of Veterans Affairs, 856 F.3d 920, decided on May 9, 2017, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit held that the accountability appeal structure in 38 U.S.C. 713(e) violated the Appointments Clause of the Constitution. Section 707 of the 2014 act had made a Merit Systems Protection Board administrative judge the final decision maker on a senior executive's removal, with no review by the Board or the courts. The court reasoned that administrative judges are employees rather than constitutionally appointed officers and could not wield final, unreviewable authority of that significance. The court severed the offending provisions and remanded for Board review.

### Q: Who signed the Veterans Choice Act into law?

President Barack Obama signed the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014 on August 7, 2014, at Fort Belvoir in Virginia, an Army installation with a large military community. The signature came one week after the Senate vote of 91 to 3 and closed a legislative sequence that had begun with committee hearings in the spring of 2014, when reporting from Phoenix first alleged secret waiting lists. Secretary of Veterans Affairs Eric Shinseki, whose department the statute restructured, had resigned on May 30, 2014, and the confirmed successor, Robert A. McDonald, had taken office at the end of July, days before the act became law.

### Q: What is the public law number of the Veterans Choice Act?

The Veterans Choice Act is the common name for the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, enacted as Public Law 113-146 of the 113th Congress and printed at 128 Statutes at Large 1754. The bill vehicle was H.R. 3230, which had reached the Senate carrying a different subject before the Senate substituted the text of S. 2450 into it. The Choice Program itself was created by section 101 of the act and was administered under a statutory note associated with 38 U.S.C. 1703, while the accountability provisions of section 707 were codified directly at 38 U.S.C. 713.

### Q: How many veterans used the Choice Program?

Use grew from a slow start to a large volume within three years. In the program's first months the department had approved about 46,000 requests for care by March 2015, against nearly 8 million cards distributed. Secretary David Shulkin testified to the Senate Appropriations Committee on June 21, 2017 that authorizations exceeded 380,000 in fiscal 2015 and that more than two million authorizations were granted in fiscal 2016, with the months from March to May 2017 alone producing nearly 800,000, an increase of about thirty two percent over the comparable period. Authorizations count approved episodes of care, not individual veterans, and one veteran could receive several.

### Q: Did the Veterans Choice Act privatize veterans health care?

The act did not transfer the department's hospitals or its direct care mission to private operators, and its text described the Choice Program as a temporary supplement that paid private providers for authorized episodes of care. The privatization question was nonetheless the central argument about the statute. Supporters of wider eligibility, including Senator John McCain, who proposed making Choice permanent in 2016, treated community care as a durable route out of a failing queue. Organizations such as the Disabled American Veterans warned that unfettered choice would drain resources from department facilities and fragment care for veterans with complex, service connected conditions. The Commission on Care report of 2016 charted a middle position built on integrated networks.

### Q: What changed about the forty mile rule in 2015?

The number did not change; the measurement did. Section 101 of the 2014 act set eligibility at a residence more than forty miles from the nearest department medical facility without specifying how distance was to be calculated, and the department's first regulation measured straight line distance between the residence and the facility. Veterans separated from care by mountains, water, or indirect road networks were excluded despite long journeys. After sustained pressure from the Senate Veterans' Affairs Committee and veterans service organizations, the department published an interim final rule on April 24, 2015 measuring driving distance instead, and stated that the change would roughly double the number of eligible veterans.

### Q: When was the Choice Program scheduled to end, and what happened to that deadline?

As enacted in August 2014, the Choice Program was temporary twice over: section 101 authority ran for three years, and the 10 billion dollar Veterans Choice Fund would support the program only until the money was exhausted, whichever came first, placing the outer limit in August 2017. Congress dismantled both limits in stages. An act signed on April 19, 2017, Public Law 115-26, removed the August 2017 end date and let the program run until the fund was spent. The VA Choice and Quality Employment Act of August 2017, Public Law 115-46, then added 2.1 billion dollars, and the MISSION Act of 2018 added 5.2 billion dollars of bridge funding.

### Q: How did the Veterans Choice Act try to improve care inside department facilities?

Half of the statute's money pointed inward. Section 801 appropriated 5 billion dollars for the department to hire primary care and specialty physicians, nurses, social workers, mental health professionals, and other staff, and to fund maintenance, leases, and minor construction at Veterans Health Administration facilities. Title III addressed the workforce pipeline by targeting the five occupations with the largest staffing shortages and authorizing up to 1,500 additional residency positions. Section 601 authorized twenty seven major medical facility leases at specified locations. The internal investment reflected the conference compromise: outside care would relieve the queue in the short term while hiring and space were meant to shorten the queue itself.
