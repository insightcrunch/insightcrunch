The Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act 1956 rests on a distinction that almost every popular account of it gets wrong, and the distinction is the whole statute. No provision of the Act makes it an offence to sell sex. A person who, being an adult, exchanges sexual services for money commits no offence under this Act by that act alone, and a reader who works through the penal sections from beginning to end will search in vain for a clause that says otherwise. What the Act criminalises is everything around the act. It punishes the keeping of the premises where the exchange happens, the letting of those premises, the living on the earnings the exchange produces, the procuring of the person who performs it, the detaining of that person, the carrying on of the trade in or near a public place, and the soliciting by which a customer is found. The centre of the activity is left untouched and its entire circumference is fenced with offences, so that a person may lawfully do the thing itself and be arrested for nearly every step that doing it requires. That is the fact the reader needs, and everything else in the statute, its police machinery, its rescue powers, its protective homes and its sixty years of litigation and failed amendment, is a working out of that single structural choice.

The statute also has to be read for what it calls itself, because its two names record two different theories of the problem. It began life as the Suppression of Immoral Traffic in Women and Girls Act, 1956, a title that identified the traffic as something done to women and girls and promised its suppression. The amending Act of 1986 renamed it the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, substituted persons for women and girls throughout, and recast its central definition, so the statute a reader opens is a 1956 frame carrying a 1986 vocabulary and a set of penalties stiffened in two rounds of amendment. The delivered guide to [women's rights legislation in India](/2015/11/01/womens-rights-laws-india-guide/) maps this Act within the wider field of protective statutes, and this article takes the single statute apart: what it forbids, what it pointedly does not forbid, who enforces it, what happens to the person taken out of a brothel in a raid, how the courts have read it, and why every attempt to replace its settlement with a different one, whether abolitionist or decriminalising, has failed in Parliament. The argument is conducted in a legal register throughout, because the subject carries contested moral positions and affects a population that experiences criminalisation directly, and the two great positions in the field are stated later in this article at full strength in their own proponents' terms, with the article adopting neither.

## The law before 1956 and the convention that required a national statute

Prostitution was regulated in India long before it was legislated against at the centre, and the pre-1956 position explains the shape of the Act that replaced it. The colonial state had approached the subject through cantonment and contagious diseases legislation aimed at the health of soldiers, and through municipal and police powers aimed at public nuisance, rather than through any general prohibition. In the decades before independence the provinces began enacting suppression statutes of their own, among them the Madras Suppression of Immoral Traffic Act, 1930, and comparable provincial laws in Bombay, Bengal, the United Provinces, Punjab and elsewhere. These provincial Acts shared a family resemblance: they penalised brothel keeping, procuring and living on earnings, they armed the police with powers of entry, and they provided for the removal of women from brothels and their placement in institutions. They differed in detail, in penalty and in machinery, and they bound only their own territories, so that the trade the statutes pursued could cross a provincial boundary and change its legal character as it crossed. Section 25 of the 1956 Act would eventually repeal these State Acts as the central Act came into force in each State, with a savings clause preserving action already taken, and the repeal is itself a clue to the central Act's purpose: uniformity was half the point.

The Constitution supplied the second strand. Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings and begar and other similar forms of forced labour, and makes contravention an offence punishable in accordance with law; Article 35 reserves to Parliament, and denies to the State Legislatures, the power to make laws prescribing punishment for acts declared to be offences under Part III, which is why a subject that might otherwise have remained with the provinces became a parliamentary statute. Article 23 is a prohibition in search of a penal law, since it declares the wrong and leaves the definition of offences and penalties to legislation, and the 1956 Act is one of the statutes that answers it, though only in part, because the Act addresses traffic for one purpose, prostitution, and leaves traffic for labour, for servitude and for other exploitation to other provisions of the general criminal law. The relationship between the constitutional prohibition and this statute matters for reading the Act correctly. The Act is not the whole law of trafficking in India and never was; it is the law of one destination of trafficking, drafted before the modern vocabulary of trafficking existed in Indian law, and much of the confusion in public discussion comes from treating its title as a description of its contents.

The third strand was international. The Convention for the Suppression of the Traffic in Persons and of the Exploitation of the Prostitution of Others was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1949 and opened for signature at New York, consolidating a series of earlier international agreements reaching back to the beginning of the twentieth century. Its theory was abolitionist in a precise sense: it required parties to punish any person who procures, entices or leads away another person for the purposes of prostitution, any person who exploits the prostitution of another, and any person who keeps or manages a brothel or knowingly finances one, while it did not require parties to punish the person in prostitution, who appears in the Convention's scheme as the victim of the offences it creates. India signed and ratified that Convention at New York on 9 May 1950, and the long title of the Act that followed records the obligation in terms: the Act was made in pursuance of the International Convention signed at New York on the 9th day of May 1950. The fingerprint of the Convention is visible in the finished statute. The offences of the 1956 Act are the Convention's offences, directed at the procurer, the exploiter and the brothel keeper, and the absence of any offence of selling sex is not an oversight or a loophole but the deliberate reproduction of the Convention's design, in which the woman in prostitution was the person to be protected and rehabilitated and the persons around her were the persons to be punished. Whether that design describes the reality it governs is the question the rest of the statute's history keeps asking.

## Enactment, commencement and the two amendments that rebuilt the Act

The Suppression of Immoral Traffic in Women and Girls Act is Act 104 of 1956, dated 30 December 1956, and its commencement followed the pattern of statutes that depend on State machinery for their operation. Section 1 came into force at once, and the remaining provisions were left to be brought into force by notification of the Central Government; they came into force on 1 May 1958, by notification G.S.R. 269 of 16 April 1958. The gap between enactment and commencement is a small fact with a practical meaning, because the Act's machinery, special police officers, protective homes, corrective institutions and licensing, had to exist before its offences could be worked, and the Act extends to the whole of India while depending for nearly everything on the State Governments, which appoint the special police officers, establish and license the homes and make the rules under Section 23. A central statute with state machinery is a recurring Indian pattern, and in this field it means the Act's real content varies by State in ways its text does not show: the text is uniform, the homes and the enforcement are not.

The Act has been substantially amended twice, and a statute pillar has to keep the three texts distinct. The amending Act of 1978, Act 46 of 1978, in force from 2 October 1979, modernised the metric references, substituting two hundred metres for two hundred yards in the place offences and in the closure power, substituted the rescue and intermediate custody provisions of Sections 16 and 17 in the form in which they are principally worked, inserted Section 10A on detention in a corrective institution in its substituted form, recast Section 19, and inserted Section 22A empowering State Governments to establish special courts for speedy trial. The amending Act of 1986, Act 44 of 1986, in force from 26 January 1987, was the deeper reconstruction. It changed the name of the Act and of its subject, substituting the prevention of immoral traffic for the suppression of immoral traffic in women and girls and persons for women and girls throughout; it substituted the definition of prostitution so that the term means the sexual exploitation or abuse of persons for commercial purposes; it inserted the definitions of child, meaning a person who has not completed the age of sixteen years, of minor, meaning a person who has completed sixteen but not eighteen years, and of major, meaning a person who has completed eighteen years; it inserted sub-section (4) in Section 13 creating trafficking police officers appointed by the Central Government for offences committed in more than one State; it strengthened the safeguards and the removal powers in Section 15 and inserted Section 17A on the conditions to be observed before a rescued person is handed to parents or guardians; it omitted the old Sections 10 and 12, on probation and on security for good behaviour from habitual offenders; and it inserted Sections 21A, 22AA and 22B, on production of records by homes, on Central Government special courts, and on summary trial.

Two consequences of the 1986 reconstruction deserve emphasis at the outset because they govern how the Act is cited and searched. The first is that the statute's short title lost its date of reconstruction: a reader who meets the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act and assumes a 1986 statute will misplace it by thirty years, and a reader who meets references to SITA in older judgments is reading about the same statute under its first name. The second is that the 1986 Act made the statute formally indifferent to the sex of the person in prostitution and of the person proceeded against, so that the Act applies to the exploitation of men and transgender persons as it applies to women, even though its enforcement record, its institutions and its litigation have remained overwhelmingly concerned with women and girls, and its definitions of the places of detention and several of its operative provisions retain the imprint of the original scheme. The statute's text and the statute's life are two different things, and the distance between them is one of the subjects of this article.


## The definitions that decide who the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act reaches

Section 2 carries more of this statute's policy than any penal clause, because the definitions determine what counts as the organised activity the Act exists to suppress and what counts as an individual act it leaves alone. The definition of brothel is the load-bearing one. A brothel includes any house, room, conveyance or place, or any portion of any of them, which is used for purposes of sexual exploitation or abuse for the gain of another person or for the mutual gain of two or more prostitutes. Two elements in that definition decide most contested cases. The first is the gain element: premises used for prostitution become a brothel when the use is for the gain of another person, or for the mutual gain of two or more persons in prostitution, so a single person receiving clients alone in her own premises, for her own gain, is not keeping a brothel within the definition, while two persons working together from the same rooms for their shared gain are, a result that matters greatly to the safety argument examined later, since the definition penalises the arrangement by which persons in prostitution most commonly protect themselves, which is working together. The second is the breadth of place: a room, a portion of a house, a vehicle, any place at all, so that the prosecution does not have to prove a dedicated establishment and a rented room used repeatedly for the trade suffices.

### What does the Act mean by prostitution?

Prostitution, by Section 2(f), means the sexual exploitation or abuse of persons for commercial purposes, and prostitute is construed accordingly. The definition, substituted in 1986, is framed in the language of exploitation rather than of the transaction; it does not define selling sex as a wrong, but names a condition of exploitation to which the offences attach.

The remaining definitions build the Act's world. A public place is any place intended for use by, or accessible to, the public, and includes any public conveyance, which is the spatial trigger for the soliciting and vicinity offences. A child is a person who has not completed sixteen years, a minor a person who has completed sixteen but not eighteen, and a major a person who has completed eighteen, and these three bands drive the penalty structure, since offence after offence carries a severe mandatory range where the person in respect of whom it is committed is a child or a minor and an ordinary range where that person is an adult; the bands also decide which institution receives a person after rescue and which protective regime applies. A protective home is an institution established or licensed under Section 21 in which persons in need of care and protection may be kept, with technically qualified persons, equipment and facilities provided, and a corrective institution is an institution under the same section in which persons in need of correction may be detained, including a shelter for undertrials; the two definitions, between them, contain the statute's unresolved double attitude toward the person in prostitution, who is in one clause a person in need of care and protection and in the next a person in need of correction, and the machinery sections move individuals between the two descriptions without ever quite deciding which is true. A special police officer is a police officer appointed by or on behalf of the State Government to be in charge of police duties within a specified area for the purposes of the Act, and a trafficking police officer is one appointed by the Central Government under Section 13(4), and the magistrate for any purpose is the magistrate specified against that section in the Schedule to the Act, a detail that tells the practitioner which court exercises which power.

It is worth pausing on what Section 2 does not define. There is no definition of traffic or trafficking anywhere in the Act, despite the word standing in its title. The procurement offences of Section 5 do the work that a trafficking definition would later do elsewhere, but they are tied to prostitution as their destination, and the Act contains no concept of trafficking for forced labour, for domestic servitude, for organ removal or for any other exploitative purpose. When Parliament came to define trafficking generally, in the Penal Code in 2013, it legislated in a different statute, and the definitional gap in this Act is the formal reason the two bodies of law must be kept apart in analysis, however often they are run together in a charge sheet. Nor does the Act define the customer. The person who pays appears in the statute only at its edges, as the person with whom prostitution is carried on in the vicinity offence and as the person solicited, and that near-absence is the second great structural fact of the Act, because every serious reform proposal since, abolitionist and regulatory alike, has been an argument about whether the customer should be brought to the centre of the statute or kept out of it.

## The premises offences: keeping a brothel and letting premises

Section 3 is the Act's foundation offence, and it is drawn in two limbs. The first limb punishes any person who keeps or manages, or acts or assists in the keeping or management of, a brothel. On a first conviction the punishment is rigorous imprisonment for a term of not less than one year and not more than three years, with fine extending to two thousand rupees; on a second or subsequent conviction, rigorous imprisonment for not less than two years and not more than five years, with fine extending to two thousand rupees. The minimum terms matter. Parliament made brothel keeping an offence with a floor under the sentence, which is the drafting signal of an offence the legislature regarded as the organising wrong of the whole field, and the words acts or assists extend liability beyond the keeper to the manager, the person who collects the money, the person who runs the house day to day, and others who participate in the keeping, subject always to proof that the premises were being used as a brothel within the definition, gain and all. The second limb reaches the property interest. A tenant, lessee, occupier or person in charge of premises who uses them, or knowingly allows another to use them, as a brothel is punishable on first conviction with imprisonment extending to two years and fine extending to two thousand rupees, and on a later conviction with rigorous imprisonment extending to five years and fine; and an owner, lessor or landlord, or the agent of any of them, who lets premises with knowledge that they are intended to be used as a brothel, or is wilfully a party to that use, is punishable in the same way.

Knowledge is the contested element in the second limb, and the 1986 Act addressed it with a presumption that a practitioner must know. Under Section 3(2A), it is presumed, until the contrary is proved, that a tenant or owner knew of the use as a brothel if a report is published in a newspaper circulating in the area where that person resides to the effect that the premises have been found to be used for prostitution as a result of a search under the Act, or if a copy of the list of things found during such a search is given to that person. The device is characteristic of this statute, which repeatedly uses presumptions to bridge the evidential gap between a closed activity and the persons who profit from it at one remove. Section 3(3) completes the property scheme: on conviction of a tenant, occupier, owner or landlord under the second limb, any lease or agreement under which the premises are held becomes void and inoperative from the date of conviction, notwithstanding anything in any other law. The premises offences therefore do three things at once: they punish the organiser, they punish the knowing facilitator who supplies the space, and they destroy the tenancy that sustains the establishment. A reader who wants to understand why the trade, where it exists, so often operates from premises held informally and at the mercy of eviction, will find part of the answer here, in a provision that makes a formal lease a liability for everyone connected with the property.

The analytical point about Section 3 is the one the Supreme Court would underline many decades later in the directions discussed below: running a brothel is the unlawful activity at the core of the Act, and the illegality attaches to the establishment and its keepers, not to the individual act of the person working in it. That proposition is easy to state and hard to operate, because the establishment is usually constituted by the presence of the persons working in it, and a raid directed at the brothel necessarily sweeps up those persons, which is why the Act needed, and received, an elaborate machinery of removal, production before a magistrate and inquiry to sort the keeper from the kept. Whether that machinery in practice performs the sorting, or merely processes everyone it removes, is the enforcement question taken up after the offences are complete.

## The earnings offence and its presumption

Section 4 punishes living on the earnings of prostitution. Any person over the age of eighteen years who knowingly lives, wholly or in part, on the earnings of the prostitution of any other person is punishable with imprisonment extending to two years, or with fine extending to one thousand rupees, or with both; and where the earnings relate to the prostitution of a child or a minor, the punishment rises sharply, to imprisonment for a term of not less than seven years and not more than ten years. The offence is the statutory form of the figure the Convention aimed at, the person who is maintained by the trade without conducting it: the procurer who has become a dependent, the keeper's associate, and, in the drafting intention, the man who lives off a woman in prostitution. Its elements are deceptively simple. The accused must be an adult, must live wholly or in part on the earnings, must do so knowingly, and the earnings must be those of another person's prostitution. Each element has generated its own defence practice: that the accused has earnings of his own and does not live on hers, that support flowed the other way, that the money was shared household income rather than maintenance, that the accused did not know the source.

Section 4(2) supplies the presumption that makes the offence workable, and it is one of the most consequential evidentiary devices in the Act. Where a person over eighteen is proved to be living with, or to be habitually in the company of, a prostitute, or to have exercised control, direction or influence over the movements of a prostitute in such a manner as to show that he is aiding, abetting or compelling her prostitution, or to be acting as a tout or pimp on her behalf, it shall be presumed, until the contrary is proved, that he is knowingly living on the earnings of her prostitution. Association, control and touting are each made a platform from which the financial element is presumed. The presumption is rebuttable, and the courts require the platform facts to be proved first, but the practical burden it creates is the centre of the offence's controversy. Read one way, Section 4 is the only realistic instrument against the parasitic exploiter, who keeps no brothel in his name, signs nothing and receives cash. Read another way, its platform facts describe ordinary domestic life: an adult partner, a husband, a grown son, a brother who lives with a woman in prostitution is, on proof of cohabitation alone, presumed to live on her earnings until he proves otherwise, and the offence thereby reaches into the household of the person in prostitution and gives the police a standing lever over the people closest to her. Both readings are accurate descriptions of the text, and the choice between them is made in practice by the discretion of the officer who decides which households to enter, which is precisely the distribution of power the sex workers' organisations identify when they describe the earnings offence as an instrument used against families rather than against exploiters, and which its defenders answer by pointing to the rebuttable character of the presumption and the requirement that the prosecution first prove the association from which it springs.


## Procuring, detaining and seducing: the offences against the person

Sections 5, 6 and 9 form the group of offences that protect the person rather than the premises, and they carry the heaviest punishments in the Act. Section 5 punishes procuring, inducing or taking a person for the sake of prostitution. It is committed by procuring or attempting to procure a person, whether with or without that person's consent, for the purpose of prostitution; by inducing a person to go from any place with the intent that the person may become the inmate of, or frequent, a brothel; by taking or attempting to take a person, or causing a person to be taken, from one place to another with a view to that person's carrying on, or being brought up to carry on, prostitution; and by causing or inducing a person to carry on prostitution. The words with or without consent carry the section's philosophy: procurement is an offence even where the person procured agrees, because the wrong the section names is the recruitment of a person into the trade by another, and consent is relevant to sentence and to the aggravated form rather than to liability. The punishment on conviction is rigorous imprisonment for not less than three years and not more than seven years, with fine extending to two thousand rupees; where the offence is committed against the will of the person it rises to a term extending to fourteen years; and the proviso substitutes, where the person is a child, rigorous imprisonment of not less than seven years extending to life, and where the person is a minor, not less than seven years extending to fourteen years. The section is also triable either where the person was procured, induced or taken from, or where that person was taken to, a jurisdictional rule written for an offence whose essence is movement.

Section 5 is the closest this Act comes to a trafficking offence, and its limits are instructive. It requires prostitution as the destination, it requires no deception, coercion or abuse of vulnerability as an element, since procurement with consent is included, and it punishes the recruiter and the transporter rather than defining a status of victim. A modern trafficking provision, of the kind Parliament enacted in 2013, inverts the structure: it defines the offence by the means used, threats, force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power, inducement, and by the purpose of exploitation in all its forms, and it makes the consent of the victim immaterial where such means are used. Section 5, by contrast, is a prostitution-recruitment offence of the Convention era. Prosecutors who wish to charge the organised movement of persons for exploitation will ordinarily reach for the general code's trafficking sections, and Section 5 remains the charge for recruitment into prostitution where the trafficking elements are hard to prove or where the conduct predates the code provisions. The two are not interchangeable, and a careful article keeps their elements apart.

Section 6 punishes detaining a person in premises where prostitution is carried on. A person who detains any other person, with or without that person's consent, in a brothel, or in or upon any premises with intent that the detained person may have sexual intercourse with a person who is not that person's spouse, is punishable with imprisonment of either description for a term which shall not be less than seven years but which may be for life or may extend to ten years, with fine, the court being permitted for adequate and special reasons recorded in the judgment to impose less than seven years. Detention here is broader than locked doors. Section 6(3) presumes detention where a person, with intent to compel or induce a woman or girl to remain, withholds from her jewellery, wearing apparel, money or other property belonging to her, or threatens her with legal proceedings if she takes away property lent or supplied to her, and Section 6(4) bars the detainer from suing the detained person for the recovery of the property or money alleged to be lent or owed. These subsections describe a recognisable economy of control, in which debt and the withholding of belongings and identity of the person's own clothes and earnings do the work of walls, and the legislature of 1956 understood it well enough to draft directly against it. The presumptions continue in Section 6(2), under which a person found with a child in a brothel is presumed, unless the contrary is proved, to have committed the detention offence, and in Section 6(2A), under which a child or minor found in a brothel who on medical examination is detected to have been sexually abused is presumed to have been detained for the purposes of prostitution or sexually exploited for commercial purposes.

Section 9 is the custody offence: a person who, having the custody, charge or care of, or a position of authority over, another person, causes or aids or abets the seduction for prostitution of that person, is punishable with imprisonment of either description for not less than seven years, extending to life or to ten years, with fine, and again with the court's power, for adequate and special reasons recorded, to go below seven years. The section is aimed at the guardian, the institution keeper, the person entrusted with a child or a ward who delivers that person into prostitution, and its severity mirrors the breach of trust it punishes. Read together, Sections 5, 6 and 9 are the part of the Act that no participant in the modern debate proposes to repeal: abolitionists and sex workers' organisations alike treat procurement by deception, detention and the corruption of custody as the core wrongs, and the argument between them is not about these sections but about the premises, earnings, place and soliciting offences that surround them, and about the machinery that enforces the whole.

## The place offences: Section 7 and the two hundred metre rule

Section 7 is the provision under which the person in prostitution first becomes personally punishable, and it does so by geography. Any person who carries on prostitution, and the person with whom such prostitution is carried on, in any premises which are within an area notified by the State Government under Section 7(3), or which are within a distance of two hundred metres of any place of public religious worship, educational institution, hostel, hospital or nursing home, or of such other public place as the Commissioner of Police or magistrate notifies, is punishable with imprisonment extending to three months. Section 7(1A) transforms the offence where it is in respect of a child or minor, substituting imprisonment of not less than seven years, extending to life or to ten years, with fine. Section 7(2) punishes the keeper of a public place who knowingly permits prostitutes to resort to or remain in it for the purposes of their trade, the tenant or occupier who knowingly permits premises within the prohibited zone to be used for prostitution, and the owner or landlord who lets such premises knowing prostitution may be carried on there, with imprisonment extending to three months or fine extending to two hundred rupees or both on a first conviction, rising on a later conviction, and, where the premises are a hotel, with suspension of the hotel's licence for a period between three months and one year, or its cancellation where the offence is in respect of a child or minor in a hotel.

### What does the two hundred metre rule prohibit?

The rule in Section 7(1) prohibits carrying on prostitution in premises inside a government-notified area, or within two hundred metres of a place of public religious worship, an educational institution, a hostel, a hospital or a nursing home, or another notified public place, and it punishes both the person in prostitution and the customer with imprisonment extending to three months.

The notified-area limb deserves a moment, because Section 7(3) to (5) contains a small scheme of delegated spatial legislation. The State Government may, having regard to the kinds of persons frequenting an area, the nature and density of population and other relevant considerations, notify that prostitution shall not be carried on in specified areas; it must define the limits of such areas with reasonable certainty in the notification; and no notification may take effect earlier than ninety days after it is issued. The ninety day interval is a rare fairness device in this statute, a recognition that a prohibition which reclassifies where a person may work and live should not operate by surprise. In the notified areas and the two hundred metre zones, the Act's central distinction collapses in practice, because the act of carrying on prostitution is itself the offence, committed jointly by the seller and the person with whom it is carried on, who is the only appearance of the customer as a principal offender anywhere in the Act. Outside those zones the distinction survives. The geography of enforcement therefore matters more than the geography of the statute book: the same conduct is lawful in one lane and punishable in the next, and the practical criminalisation of the person selling sex under Section 7 depends on where she works, which is usually determined by poverty, policing history and the location of the trade she entered, rather than by any choice she made about the law.

## The soliciting offence: Section 8 and the arrest of the worker

Section 8 punishes seducing or soliciting for the purpose of prostitution, and it is the provision under which sex workers themselves are overwhelmingly proceeded against, the hinge on which the whole practical operation of the Act turns. Whoever, in any public place or within sight of, and in such manner as to be seen or heard from, any public place, whether from within any building or house or not, by words, gestures, wilful exposure of the person, whether by sitting by a window or on the balcony of a building or house or in any other way, or otherwise, tempts or endeavours to tempt, or attracts or endeavours to attract, the attention of any person for the purpose of prostitution; or solicits or molests any person, or loiters or acts in such manner as to cause obstruction or annoyance to persons residing nearby or passing by, or to offend against public decency, for the purpose of prostitution, is punishable on first conviction with imprisonment extending to six months, or fine extending to five hundred rupees, or both, and on a second or subsequent conviction with imprisonment extending to one year, with fine extending to five hundred rupees. A proviso added in 1986 provides that where the offence is committed by a man, he shall be punishable with imprisonment for not less than seven days and not more than three months, a provision whose drafting has drawn comment for prescribing a short mandatory term for a male solicitor while the principal clause carries no minimum for anyone.

Read the elements carefully, because their breadth is the point. The conduct includes tempting and endeavouring to tempt, attracting and endeavouring to attract attention, loitering, and acting in a manner that causes annoyance, each for the purpose of prostitution, and it may be committed from inside a building, from a window or a balcony, by gesture as much as by words. No completed transaction is required, no customer need be identified, no money need change hands. The offence is in substance an offence of visible availability in public space, and its proof ordinarily rests on the testimony of the police officers who observed the conduct, which is why Section 8 proceedings have the summary, repetitive character that magistrates' courts associate with them, and why the provision functions in practice as the standing charge available against any person in prostitution who works where she can be seen. Set Section 8 beside the absence of any offence of selling sex and the statute's true structure appears. Parliament did not punish the sale; it punished the search for the buyer, conducted in the only places where a person without premises, without advertising and without protection can conduct it. The person who can work invisibly, by introduction and by telephone, is outside Section 8; the person who must work visibly, on the street and at the brothel window, is inside it, and the offence therefore distributes its burden by class with a precision the text never announces.

Section 10A completes the penal scheme for the worker herself. Where a female offender is found guilty of an offence under Section 7 or Section 8, and the character, state of health and mental condition of the offender and the other circumstances of the case are such that it is expedient that she should undergo detention for instruction and discipline conducive to her correction, the court may, in lieu of a sentence of imprisonment, order her detention in a corrective institution for a term of not less than two years and not more than five years, having first heard her, considered any representation she makes and the report of a probation officer, and recorded its satisfaction that she is likely to benefit. The State Government may discharge her after six months if satisfied there is a reasonable probability she will lead a useful and industrious life, on conditions that may govern her residence and her activities. Consider what this means in the economy of the Act. A woman convicted of soliciting, an offence whose maximum sentence of imprisonment on a first conviction is six months, may instead be detained in an institution for up to five years, not as punishment, the section insists, but for her correction; and the detention is longer, by an order of magnitude, than the sentence it replaces. Section 10A is the clearest textual evidence for the reading of the Act advanced by its critics, that the statute treats the woman in prostitution as an object of reform to be managed, and equally the clearest evidence for the reading of its defenders, that the legislature preferred institutional correction, with training and supervision, to the revolving door of short prison sentences and fines that a poor woman cannot pay. The provision is seldom the subject of appellate discussion, which is itself part of the record: the persons it detains are the persons least likely to be represented on appeal.

Two further provisions round out the penal scheme. Section 11 permits a court sentencing a person who, within five years of release from a sentence of two years or more under this Act or under specified Penal Code offences including kidnapping and trafficking offences, is again convicted of such an offence, to order that the offender's residence, and any change of or absence from it after release, be notified for a period up to five years, a surveillance tail to the sentence. Section 14 makes every offence under the Act cognizable, but channels arrest without warrant through the special police officer, whose direction, guidance or prior approval is required, with a written order to a subordinate who arrests in his absence and a narrow emergency power for an authorised officer not below the rank of sub-inspector to arrest where delay would destroy evidence or permit escape, reporting the arrest and its circumstances to the special police officer as soon as may be. Section 22 confines trials of offences under Sections 3 to 8 to courts not inferior to a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, and Sections 22A, 22AA and 22B provide for special courts for speedy trial, established by State Governments and, for offences committed in more than one State, by the Central Government, and for summary trial where the State Government so directs, with sentence in summary trial capped at one year.

## The special police officer and the channelled power of arrest

The Act does not leave its enforcement to the ordinary police hierarchy, and the machinery it creates instead tells the reader a great deal about what the legislature of 1956 feared and hoped. Section 13(1) requires that there shall be, for each area specified by the State Government, a special police officer appointed by or on behalf of that Government for dealing with offences under the Act in that area, and Section 13(2) fixes the floor of the office: the special police officer shall not be below the rank of an Inspector of Police. The officer is assisted by subordinate police officers, including women police officers wherever practicable, and the State Government may associate with the officer a non-official advisory body of not more than five leading social welfare workers of the area, including women social welfare workers wherever practicable, to advise on questions of general importance regarding the working of the Act. Section 13(2A) permits the District Magistrate to confer the powers of a special police officer on a retired police officer who retired at or above the rank of Inspector, or a retired military officer who held a commissioned rank, for particular cases or classes of cases. The design is legible. Enforcement of this Act was to be senior, local and advised: senior, because the powers of entry, removal and arrest it confers are intrusive and were to be held at Inspector rank or above; local, because the officer is appointed for a specified area and works with knowledge of it; and advised, because the Act's operations touch welfare as much as crime and the advisory body of social welfare workers was written into the section as a standing counterweight to the police view of a raid.

### What is a trafficking police officer?

A trafficking police officer is a police officer appointed by the Central Government under Section 13(4), inserted by the amendment of 1986, to investigate offences under this Act and other laws on sexual exploitation committed in more than one State, exercising a special police officer's powers across the whole of India.

The existence of the trafficking police officer is an acknowledgement, made in 1986, that the trade the Act polices does not respect the areas for which special police officers are appointed, and that a purely local machinery will always be one jurisdiction behind an organised network. In practice the officer's significance is as much diagnostic as operational, since the cases that most need inter-State investigation, the recruitment in one State, the transport through a second and the exploitation in a third that Section 5 describes, are the cases in which the fragmentation of State enforcement is most costly, and in which the later general trafficking offences, with their own inter-State machinery, have largely taken over the ground. The channelled arrest scheme of Section 14, described with the offences, belongs with this machinery: arrest without warrant under the Act is lawful only when made by the special police officer or under his direction, guidance or prior approval, by a subordinate carrying a written order that names the person and the offence and must be shown on demand, or, in the narrow emergency case, by an authorised officer who must report at once. On paper, therefore, no constable may lawfully arrest under this Act on his own authority, and the distance between that paper position and the street-level experience reported by sex workers' organisations, in which the threat of arrest is a routine instrument of extortion unconnected with any written order, is one of the standing exhibits in the argument about whether the Act's problem is its text or its administration. A person facing proceedings under this Act is entitled to [legal aid through the legal services authorities](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/), and the general account in this article of provisions and procedures is legal information and not a substitute for advice on an individual case, in which the precise charge, the identity of the arresting officer and the place of the alleged conduct will usually decide the outcome.

## Search without warrant and the safeguards of Section 15

Section 15 confers the Act's most intrusive power and hedges it with its most detailed safeguards, and both halves deserve statement. Where the special police officer or the trafficking police officer has reasonable grounds for believing that an offence under the Act has been or is being committed in respect of a person living in any premises, and that search of the premises with a warrant cannot be made without undue delay, the officer may, after recording the grounds of the belief, enter and search the premises without a warrant, notwithstanding anything in any other law. Before making the search, the officer shall call upon two or more respectable inhabitants of the locality, at least one of whom shall be a woman, to attend and witness the search, and may issue a written order to them to do so; a person who without reasonable cause refuses or neglects to attend commits an offence under Section 187 of the Penal Code. The officer entering under the section is entitled to remove from the premises all the persons found there, and shall forthwith produce the persons removed before the appropriate magistrate. A person so produced shall be examined by a registered medical practitioner for the determination of age, or for the detection of injuries resulting from sexual abuse, or for the presence of sexually transmitted disease. The officer making a search shall be accompanied by at least two women police officers, and where any woman or girl removed is to be interrogated, the interrogation shall be done by a woman police officer or, if none is available, only in the presence of a woman member of a recognised welfare institution or organisation. The search provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure apply to a Section 15 search as they apply to a search under warrant, and the officer and those attending are protected from civil and criminal proceedings for anything lawfully done in connection with the search.

### Who may enter and search premises without a warrant under this Act?

Only the special police officer for the area, or a trafficking police officer, may enter and search premises without a warrant, and only on reasonable grounds, recorded in writing, for believing that an offence under the Act is being committed there and that a warrant cannot be obtained without undue delay.

The safeguards are not decoration. The requirements of recorded grounds, of respectable local witnesses with a woman among them, of women police officers in attendance and of immediate production before a magistrate convert the raid from a police operation into the first stage of a judicial proceeding, and a search conducted in defiance of them is vulnerable in the trial that follows, since the prosecution's account of what was found, and of who was found, depends on the integrity of the entry. The medical examination under Section 15(5A) serves the Act's sorting purpose, because age determines whether the person removed is a child or minor to whom the protective provisions and the aggravated offences apply, and because injuries and disease are evidence in the prosecutions of keepers and procurers, though the same examination, undergone by an adult woman who regards herself as a worker and not a victim, is experienced by her as part of the coercion of the raid, and sex workers' organisations have objected to compulsory examination as a violation of bodily autonomy performed under the colour of protection. Both descriptions of the same sub-section are, once again, accurate, and the tension between them is the tension of the Act itself. Organisations and advocates preparing or contesting the record of a raid work from registers, medical reports, production memos and inspection files, and keeping that documentary record complete and readable across the spreadsheet and document formats in which different authorities issue it is the unglamorous groundwork of accountability; the [ReportMedic office file viewer for reading Excel, DOCX and PPTX records](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) exists for exactly that kind of file, where a remand report or a home's inspection sheet arrives in whatever format the issuing office happened to use.

## Rescue, intermediate custody and the road to the protective home

Section 16 gives the magistrate a rescue power distinct from the police search. Where a magistrate has reason to believe, from information received from the police, from any person authorised by the State Government in that behalf, or otherwise, that any person is living in, or is carrying on, or is being made to carry on, prostitution in a brothel, the magistrate may direct a police officer not below the rank of sub-inspector to enter the brothel, remove the person and produce that person before the magistrate, which the officer shall forthwith do. Section 17 then governs intermediate custody, and its detail repays attention because this is the point at which the Act decides what a rescued person is. A person removed under Section 15 or rescued under Section 16 who cannot be produced at once before the appropriate magistrate must be produced before the nearest magistrate of any class, who passes orders for safe custody; no person may be detained under that head for more than ten days, and no person may be restored to or placed in the custody of a person who may exercise a harmful influence over that person. Before the appropriate magistrate, the person must be given an opportunity of being heard, and the magistrate must cause an inquiry into the correctness of the information, the age, character and antecedents of the person, the suitability of parents, guardian or husband to take charge, and the nature of the influence the home conditions are likely to have, and may direct a probation officer to inquire into those circumstances, into the personality of the person and into the prospects of rehabilitation. Pending the inquiry the magistrate may order safe custody, for not more than three weeks, and a child or minor may be placed in an institution under the Children Acts. In the inquiry the magistrate may summon a panel of five respectable persons, three of whom shall wherever practicable be women, to assist, drawing on a list of experienced social welfare workers in the field. Section 17A, inserted in 1986, adds that before handing a rescued person to parents, guardian or husband the magistrate may satisfy himself of their capacity and genuineness by an investigation through a recognised welfare institution or organisation.

If the magistrate is satisfied, after the inquiry, that the information received is correct and that the person is in need of care and protection, Section 17(4) empowers an order detaining the person in a protective home, or in such other custody as the magistrate for reasons recorded considers suitable, for a period of not less than one year and not more than three years, with an appeal lying to the Court of Session whose decision is final. The conditions of the order may include bonds from those entrusted with custody, with undertakings as to care, guardianship, education, training and medical and psychiatric treatment, and supervision by a person appointed by the court. Stand back from the detail and the structure is this: a person who has committed no offence, or whose offence is at most the place or soliciting offence, and who has been taken from a brothel in a search or rescue in which she may have been the intended beneficiary, can be detained by a magistrate's order in an institution for between one and three years, on the ground that she is in need of care and protection, following an inquiry at which she was heard but in which she was not tried for anything. The detention is civil in form and carceral in substance. Its supporters, in the tradition running from the Convention through the organisations that conduct rescues, describe it as the indispensable interval in which a person extracted from exploitation, often indebted, often controlled, sometimes a child, can be kept beyond the reach of the network that held her while education, training and family tracing proceed, and they point to the inquiry, the panel of social welfare workers, the bond conditions and the appeal to Sessions as the procedural substance that distinguishes protection from punishment. Its critics, led by the sex workers' collectives whose members have passed through the homes, describe the same order as detention without conviction imposed on the victim of the raid rather than on its target, point to the one to three year term as longer than the sentence for most offences in the Act, and observe that the homes' regimes of restricted movement, supervised communication and compulsory training reproduce, under a welfare name, the control the rescue was supposed to end. The statute's text supports both descriptions, because the text genuinely provides protection and genuinely provides detention, and does not choose between them.

Section 18 arms the magistrate against the premises once the persons have been dealt with. On information that a house, room, place or portion within two hundred metres of a public place of the Section 7 kind is being run or used as a brothel or used by prostitutes for carrying on their trade, the magistrate issues notice to the owner, lessor, landlord, tenant, lessee or occupier to show cause within seven days why the place should not be attached for improper user, and, if satisfied after hearing, may direct eviction of the occupier within seven days and direct that the place not be let again for one year, or for three years where a child or minor was found there in a search, without the magistrate's previous approval; a court convicting under Section 3 or Section 7 may pass the same order without fresh notice. The order is not subject to appeal and is not to be stayed or set aside by any court, civil or criminal, it lapses after the one or three year period, and on its passing any lease of the premises becomes void; an owner who was innocent of the improper user may have the premises restored, on a direction that they not be let to the person responsible. Section 19, by contrast, is the Act's one voluntary door: a person who is carrying on, or is being made to carry on, prostitution may apply to the magistrate for an order to be kept in a protective home or provided care and protection by the court, and the magistrate, after hearing the applicant and inquiring, including through a probation officer, into personality, home conditions and prospects of rehabilitation, may order that the applicant be kept in a protective home or a corrective institution, or under the supervision of a person appointed by the magistrate, for a specified period. Section 19 is seldom discussed, and it matters, because it shows the legislature knew the difference between custody sought and custody imposed, and provided for both.

## A statute of many magistrates

A reader tracing a single raid through the Act discovers that the judicial powers are deliberately fragmented, and the fragmentation has practical consequences that a pillar article should make explicit. The Act does not use the word magistrate in one sense. By Section 2(c), magistrate means the magistrate specified in the second column of the Schedule to the Act as competent to exercise the powers conferred by the section in question, so the identity of the judicial officer changes with the power being exercised: the magistrate who directs a rescue under Section 16, the magistrate who conducts the Section 17 inquiry and orders detention in a protective home, the magistrate who orders closure and eviction under Section 18, the magistrate who entertains a voluntary application under Section 19 and the magistrate who orders removal under Section 20 are exercising distinct grants, and the trial of the offences, by Section 22, belongs to a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, with the special courts under Sections 22A and 22AA and summary trial under Section 22B laid over the scheme. Appeals are distributed with the same particularity, the detention order under Section 17(4) going to the Court of Session whose decision is final, the closure order under Section 18 being expressly unappealable and unstayable, and revision lying in the ordinary way elsewhere. The design reflects the Act's hybrid character, part criminal code and part welfare jurisdiction, and its cost is borne by the person at the centre of the proceeding, who may be produced, in the course of one operation, before a nearest magistrate for interim custody, an appropriate magistrate for inquiry, and a trial magistrate for a Section 8 charge, with different files, different standards and, in any case where she is unrepresented, no single officer charged with seeing her whole position. Summary trial under Section 22B sharpens the point from the other side: where the State Government directs summary trial, the magistrate proceeds under the summary provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure and may not pass a sentence exceeding one year on a summary conviction, which fits the soliciting and place offences, whose maxima sit within that limit, and fits nothing else in the Act, since the exploitation offences begin at minimum terms of one, three or seven years and must be tried in the ordinary way whatever direction the Government issues; the summary power is thus, by the arithmetic of the punishments, a power over the worker's offences and not over the organisers', and its use for speedy disposal speeds the disposal of exactly the class of case that least needs the Act's machinery. The panel of respectable persons and social welfare workers that the inquiry sections summon into the process is the Act's attempt to compensate for that fragmentation with local knowledge, and its uneven use across districts is one more reason the same statutory text produces dissimilar justice in different places.

## Protective homes, corrective institutions and the licensing scheme

Section 21 supplies the institutions to which the custody sections lead. The State Government may establish as many protective homes and corrective institutions under the Act as it thinks fit, and no person or authority other than the State Government may establish or maintain one except under a licence issued by the State Government, on conditions it thinks fit to impose, with a proviso that the management of a home or institution shall wherever practicable be entrusted to women. Before issuing a licence the State Government may require a full and complete investigation of the application; a licence is not transferable, remains in force for the period specified and may be renewed on application made at least thirty days before expiry; and it may be revoked, after an opportunity to show cause, where the licensee breaches its conditions or the Act or rules, or where the Government is not satisfied with the condition, management or superintendence of the home, whereupon the home ceases to function. The State Government may transfer an inmate from one protective home to another, or between a protective home and a corrective institution, recording reasons, without extending the period the inmate was required to stay. Establishing or maintaining a home or institution without a licence is punishable, on a first offence with fine extending to one thousand rupees and on a later offence with imprisonment extending to one year, or fine extending to two thousand rupees, or both. Section 21A requires every licensee, whenever required by a court, to produce the records and documents maintained by the home or institution before the court. Section 23 gives the rule-making power to the State Government, and its enumerated heads are a map of the institutional life the Act imagines: custody and maintenance of persons in homes, discharge of offenders from corrective institutions, notification of residence by released convicts, the establishment, management, superintendence, staffing and discipline of homes, licensing forms and investigation, accounts and audit, registers, the care, treatment, training, instruction, control and discipline of inmates, visits and communication with inmates, transfers, discharge and arrest on breach of conditions, absence permissions and inspection.

The licensing scheme is the point at which the Act's ambitions meet administrative reality, and an honest pillar article must record the standing criticisms without inventing figures. The homes exist in the custody chain of every State, but their number, capacity, staffing and condition vary enormously, and the same statute that prescribes technically qualified persons, equipment and facilities in its definition of a protective home leaves the provision of all three to State budgets and State rules. Successive official and judicial exercises, including the directions of the Supreme Court in the proceedings examined below that the States survey all the homes under the Act, proceed from the premise that the register of homes is itself incomplete or out of date, which is a remarkable position for a licensing regime to reach: the State licenses the institutions in which its own magistrates detain people, and periodically has to be directed to find out how many there are. For the person inside, the distinction between the protective home and the corrective institution, protection in the one and correction in the other, is the distinction between two theories of her presence, and the transfer power, which moves an inmate between the two on recorded reasons of conduct and training, shows how readily the statute slides her from the first theory to the second without any new adjudication of anything she has done.


## Section 20: removal of a person from a place, and its constitutional career

One provision remains among the powers, and it is in some ways the most revealing in the Act. Under Section 20, a magistrate who receives information that any person residing in or frequenting any place within his jurisdiction is a prostitute may record the substance of the information, issue notice to that person to show cause why she should not be required to remove herself from the place and be prohibited from re-entering it, and, after inquiring into the truth of the information and giving the person an opportunity of adducing evidence, if satisfied that the person is a prostitute and that removal is necessary in the interests of the general public, order the person, after a date not less than seven days from the order, to remove herself to a specified place by a specified route and within a specified time, and prohibit re-entry without the magistrate's written permission. Failure to comply, re-entry without permission, and harbouring a person so removed are punishable with fines. No offence need be alleged. The trigger is a status, being a prostitute, joined to a judgment about the interests of the general public, and the order is an order of externment against a citizen in her own locality, of a kind otherwise associated with habitual offender and public order legislation.

Section 20 was challenged early and upheld, and the decision remains the foundation authority on the Act's coercive side. In State of Uttar Pradesh v Kaushaliya, decided in 1964 and reported at AIR 1964 SC 416, the Supreme Court rejected the attack on the section under Articles 14 and 19. The Court held that the magistrate's power is not unguided or unchannelised: he must act judicially, must determine whether the person is a prostitute and whether removal is necessary in the interests of the general public, the expression general public being neither vague nor arbitrary in the Court's reading, and there is a real and obvious differential between a prostitute who is a public nuisance and one who is not, which supports the classification. The restrictions on the freedoms of movement and residence were held to be reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public. The decision is a period piece in its reasoning, and its distinction between the prostitute who is a nuisance and the one who is not locates the constitutional question exactly where the statute leaves it, in the magistrate's assessment of a particular person's effect on a particular locality, with the person's own account of her life carrying such weight as the inquiry gives it. Section 20 survives, little used in some States and periodically rediscovered in others, and it was marked for omission by the amendment Bill of 2006, whose failure left it in force; a reader assessing the distance between the statute of 1956 and the directions of 2022 will find no sharper single marker than the continued existence of a power to expel a woman from a place for being what the Act elsewhere declines to make an offence.

## The courts and the children of the red light areas: Gaurav Jain

The Act's second great judicial chapter concerns not the woman but her children, and it began outside the Act's text altogether. In Gaurav Jain v Union of India, decided on 9 July 1997 by a bench of Justices K. Ramaswamy and D.P. Wadhwa, the Supreme Court dealt with a public interest petition founded on a magazine report on the lives of women in prostitution and their children, and directed attention to the condition of children growing up in red light areas: their exposure to the trade, their lack of schooling and their inheritance of their mothers' stigma. The Court treated the children of women in prostitution as entitled to equal opportunity, care and protection, and favoured arrangements, including juvenile homes and educational facilities, that would give them a route out of the environment of the trade; on review, directions framed in terms of the eradication of prostitution were recalled, while the directions for the protection of the children were maintained. The case is remembered in this field for two propositions. The first is that the children are rights holders in their own right, and that the State's duty to them does not depend on separating them from their mothers as a matter of course, a qualification the later proceedings would sharpen into a direction. The second is the case's characteristic blend of compassion and condemnation, in which the woman in prostitution appears simultaneously as a person failed by society and as the centre of an environment from which her children must be rescued; that blend is the judicial form of the same double attitude the definitions of protective and corrective institutions enact, and the sex workers' movement has criticised the judgment's framing while relying on its insistence that the children are citizens with claims on the State.

## Budhadev Karmaskar: a murder appeal that became the forum for a profession

The proceedings that define the modern law began as a criminal appeal of the most ordinary kind. Budhadev Karmaskar was convicted of the murder of a sex worker in Kolkata, the killing having taken place in September 1991, and his conviction and sentence of life imprisonment were upheld by the Supreme Court in February 2011 in Criminal Appeal No. 135 of 2010, reported as Budhadev Karmaskar v State of West Bengal, (2011) 10 SCC 283. In the course of that appeal the Court turned its attention from the appellant to the conditions of the community to which the woman he murdered had belonged, took up the question of the rehabilitation of sex workers and their right to live with dignity, and constituted a panel to examine the issues and make recommendations. The panel, chaired by the senior advocate Pradip Ghosh and including officials and organisations working in the field, worked through the following years on the prevention of trafficking, the rehabilitation of those who wish to leave sex work and the conditions of those who remain in it, and the proceedings continued as a standing matter in which the Court monitored the response of the Union and the States. The posture of the case is unusual and should be stated plainly: the substantive directions that govern the field were not made in a constitutional challenge to the Act, no provision of the Act was struck down, and the Act stands unamended; the directions were made in a continuing proceeding arising from a murder appeal, and their authority rests on the Court's power under Article 142 of the Constitution to do complete justice.

On 19 May 2022 a bench of Justices L. Nageswara Rao, B.R. Gavai and A.S. Bopanna, recording that the legislature had not acted on the panel's recommendations, issued directions in exercise of that Article 142 power, reported at 2022 SCC OnLine SC 704, and their substance must be given precisely because they are persistently misreported as having legalised sex work. The Court directed that sex workers are entitled to equal protection of the law, and that criminal law must apply equally in all cases on the basis of age and consent. Where it is clear that a sex worker is an adult and is participating with consent, the police must refrain from interfering or taking any criminal action. Where a brothel is raided, since voluntary sex work is not illegal and only running a brothel is unlawful, the sex workers concerned must not be arrested, penalised, harassed or victimised. The police should treat all sex workers with dignity and should not abuse them verbally or physically, subject them to violence or coerce them into any sexual activity, and should be sensitised to their rights. The measures sex workers use for their health and safety, including condoms, must not be construed as offences or treated as evidence of an offence. The Press Council of India was asked to call on the media to take utmost care not to reveal the identities of sex workers during arrest, raid and rescue operations, or to publish or telecast their photographs. The States were directed to survey all protective homes under the Act, so that persons detained there could be reviewed and released in accordance with law, and the directions addressed the issue of identity documentation, so that documents including ration cards and Aadhaar enrolment should not be withheld from sex workers or made conditional on disclosing their occupation, and the children of sex workers were not to be separated from their mothers merely on the ground of the mother's work. The Court described the basic protection of human decency and dignity as extending to sex workers and their children, who bear the brunt of the social stigma attached to the work.

The correct legal description of the 2022 order is therefore narrower and more interesting than the headlines it received. It changed no section of the Act. Soliciting under Section 8 remains an offence on the statute book, and the directions do not repeal it; what the directions do is discipline the exercise of police power against adult, consenting sex workers, insist on the Act's own distinction between the worker and the brothel keeper as the rule of raids, and attach constitutional dignity to a class of persons the statute had treated principally as objects of rescue or correction. The proceedings have the character of a continuing mandamus on the panel's recommendations, and the gap between the directions and their ground-level operation is the subject of continuing argument in the High Courts, which have applied the directions in quashing proceedings against women proceeded against in raids on the footing that voluntary adult sex work is not itself illegal. For the practitioner, the directions are the first authority to reach for when a client who sells sex is arrested in a raid; for the policy reader, they are Parliament's unfinished business performed by a court, and the abolitionist and rights positions both claim them, the one stressing that the Court protected only consenting adults and left every exploitation offence intact, the other stressing that the Court recognised sex work as work and its practitioners as citizens.

## The neighbouring code: trafficking under the Penal Code and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita

No account of this Act is complete without the separate body of law with which it is constantly confused, because the confusion decides real cases. Until 2013, the Penal Code contained offences of selling, buying and importing minors for prostitution and of habitual dealing in slaves, but no general offence of trafficking in persons. The Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, Act 13 of 2013, enacted on the recommendations that followed the Delhi gang rape of December 2012, [substituted Section 370 of the Penal Code and inserted Section 370A](/2016/03/15/criminal-law-amendments-women-compared/), creating for the first time a general trafficking offence defined by acts, recruiting, transporting, harbouring, transferring or receiving a person, committed by specified means, threats, force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or inducement, for the purpose of exploitation, exploitation being defined to include prostitution and other forms of sexual exploitation, forced labour and services, slavery and practices similar to slavery, servitude and the forced removal of organs; consent of the victim is immaterial where any such means are used. Section 370A punished the exploitation of a trafficked person, engaging a trafficked child or adult for sexual exploitation with knowledge or reason to believe that the person was trafficked. Those provisions have been carried into the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, in which [the trafficking offence is Section 143 and the exploitation of a trafficked person is Section 144](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/), with the gradations of punishment restated and, for the exploitation offence, the upper limits raised. The definitional structure is the point of contact with the Palermo Protocol, the Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons supplementing the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime, which India ratified in 2011, and the code's definition tracks the Protocol's acts, means and purpose.

The working division between the two statutes can be stated exactly. The 1956 Act is a prostitution statute: its offences are organised around premises, earnings, procurement for prostitution and solicitation, its machinery is the raid, the rescue and the protective home, and it contains no trafficking definition. The code provisions are trafficking provisions: they require the movement or harbouring of a person by prohibited means for exploitation, they reach every exploitative destination of which prostitution is only one, and their victim is defined by the means used against that person rather than by the place in which the person is found. A charge sheet in an organised case will commonly cite both, the code for the traffickers who recruited and transported, the 1956 Act for the keepers of the premises to which the person was delivered, and the prosecution must still prove each offence by its own elements, since Section 5 of the 1956 Act, which punishes procurement even with consent, and Section 143 of the Sanhita, which requires a prohibited means, are not the same offence wearing two numbers. The conflation of the two in public discussion is not harmless. It leads reformers to demand of the 1956 Act a general anti-trafficking performance it was never built to give, and it leads enforcement to treat every person found in a brothel as a trafficking victim for the purposes of rescue and detention, whether or not any prohibited means were used against her, which is the administrative form of the argument, examined next, about whether adult sex work and trafficking are one subject or two.


## The reforms that failed: 2006, 2018 and 2021

The Act's settlement has been challenged in Parliament three times in the modern period, and each failure teaches something different. The Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Amendment Bill, 2006, introduced in the Lok Sabha and examined by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, was the most surgical of the attempts, since it worked on this Act itself rather than replacing it. It proposed to raise the age of a child in the Act from sixteen to eighteen and to drop the minor and major distinctions; to enhance the punishment for brothel keeping and for detention; to insert a definition of trafficking in persons on the lines of the international Protocol, with punishments for trafficking and a new offence of visiting a brothel for the sexual exploitation of a trafficked victim; to omit Section 8, the soliciting offence, altogether; to omit Section 20, the removal power; to lower the floor for the special police officer from Inspector to Sub-Inspector; to create central and State authorities against trafficking; and to provide for in camera trial. The Bill thus contained, in one text, the two halves of the modern argument pulling in opposite directions: the deletion of the soliciting and removal provisions, which the sex workers' movement had demanded for decades, and the creation of a client-facing offence and a broader trafficking apparatus, which the same movement opposed as conflating sex work with trafficking and driving the trade underground, while health and HIV organisations warned that criminalising the client would destroy the outreach on which prevention depended. The Bill was not enacted, and lapsed with the dissolution of the Lok Sabha that had received it, leaving Sections 8 and 20 in force and the child definition at sixteen, where they remain.

The second attempt changed the instrument. The Trafficking of Persons (Prevention, Protection and Rehabilitation) Bill, 2018 proposed a comprehensive anti-trafficking code outside the 1956 Act, with a national anti-trafficking bureau, district and State machinery, a rehabilitation fund, forfeiture of the property of traffickers and aggravated offences. It was passed by the Lok Sabha on 26 July 2018, was not considered by the Rajya Sabha, and lapsed on the dissolution of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha. Its critics, prominently including the sex workers' networks and organisations working on labour migration, argued that its definitions swept consensual adult sex work and voluntary migration into a trafficking frame policed by raids and rewarded by rehabilitation counts, and that existing law, the code offences and this Act together, already covered the conduct and lacked only enforcement that distinguished the trafficked from the working. Its supporters, in the anti-trafficking movement, argued that a fragmented field of a prostitution statute, code sections and labour laws had demonstrably failed against organised networks and that a dedicated code with dedicated machinery was the precondition of serious enforcement. The third attempt, the draft Trafficking in Persons (Prevention, Care and Rehabilitation) Bill circulated by the Ministry of Women and Child Development for public comment on 30 June 2021, with comments invited until 14 July 2021, proposed a wider code still, extending to offences committed across borders and prescribing severe punishments including, for aggravated forms, the death penalty. It drew the same alignment of support and opposition, in sharper terms, and it was not introduced in Parliament. The legislative record therefore stands where the brief of any honest account must leave it: a dedicated anti-trafficking statute has been attempted repeatedly and enacted never, the Penal Code's and then the Sanhita's trafficking sections do the general work, and the statute of 1956, amended in 1978 and 1986, remains the governing law of prostitution, its soliciting offence and its protective homes intact.

A further structural fact belongs in this part of the account, because it explains what the failure of reform leaves untouched. Sex work stands outside every labour statute. The person who sells sex is not an employee under the industrial relations framework, has no employer against whom the code provisions on wages, social security or occupational safety can run, no recognised union bargaining structure under the trade union law, and no standing in the machinery that [the Indian labour law framework](/2013/05/01/indian-labour-law-framework-guide/) assembles for other informal workers, who are at least named as beneficiaries of welfare boards and funds. The sex workers' collectives have built substitutes of their own, cooperative banks, health clinics, peer education networks and crisis teams, of which the Durbar Mahila Samanwaya Committee's institutions in Kolkata are the best documented, but these are civil society constructions with no statutory footing, and their dependence on the tolerance of the local police, the same police armed with Sections 7, 8 and 20, is the practical form of the legal position: the work is not prohibited, and the worker is not recognised.

## The two positions, stated at full strength

The abolitionist position, in the terms of its own proponents, begins from a claim about what prostitution is. Organisations such as the Coalition Against Trafficking in Women, founded in 1988, and Apne Aap Worldwide, founded by Ruchika Gupta, hold that prostitution is itself a form of violence against women and a practice of sex inequality, that the distinction between forced and voluntary prostitution is unstable in a society of deep economic inequality, since the overwhelming run of entries into the trade are driven by poverty, by caste and family circumstance, by desertion and widowhood, or by trafficking and deception in childhood, and that consent given inside such constraints does not convert exploitation into work. On this view the customer is not a bystander but the cause: demand is what the trade supplies, the earnings offences and brothel offences fail because they leave the buyer untouched, and the law should therefore be reconstructed on the model first enacted in Sweden in 1999 and since adopted in Norway, Canada, France and Ireland, under which the purchase of sexual services is criminalised, the sale is decriminalised, and the person in prostitution is treated by the State exclusively as a person to be exited, supported and never prosecuted. Apne Aap's campaigns in India have pressed for precisely this asymmetry, the punishment of buyers and pimps and the protection of women and girls, and for the amendment of this Act to criminalise the client, and abolitionists read the rescue and protective home machinery, whatever its defects of administration, as the necessary protective arm of the State toward persons whose apparent consent they regard as the product of the exploitation itself. The position's strongest evidence, as its proponents present it, is the composition of the trade at its point of entry, where minors, deception and debt figure throughout the case records of the rescue organisations, and its strongest argument is that a legal order which punishes the keeper and the landlord but not the man whose money sustains both has misidentified the defendant.

The sex workers' rights position, also in its proponents' terms, begins from a claim about who should be heard. The Durbar Mahila Samanwaya Committee of Kolkata, a collective of sex workers organising in Sonagachi and across West Bengal since the early 1990s, the National Network of Sex Workers, and SANGRAM and its sex workers' collective VAMP in Maharashtra, associated with Meena Seshu, hold that adults who sell sex are workers and citizens, that the movement's own surveys and health work show a population far more various than the abolitionist account allows, including women who chose the trade over worse paid and more dangerous alternatives and who wish to remain in it with safety and dignity, and that the operative question is not whether the trade would exist in a just society but what the law does to the people in it in this one. On this view the 1956 Act is the problem in its daily operation: the soliciting offence is the charge under which workers are arrested, fined and extorted; the brothel definition punishes two women who share premises for safety by making their arrangement a brothel; the earnings presumption exposes partners and adult children to prosecution; the two hundred metre rule and Section 20 put residence itself at a magistrate's discretion; and the raid and rescue machinery subjects adult women to removal, medical examination and detention in homes they describe as prisons without trial, while driving the trade, wherever enforcement intensifies, into dispersed and unpoliced settings where violence goes unreported because the victim must confess her occupation to complain. The remedy proposed is full decriminalisation of consensual adult sex work, on the model of New Zealand's Prostitution Reform Act 2003, with the general criminal law, the trafficking offences of the code, the child protection statutes and ordinary offences against the person enforced vigorously against coercion, minors and exploitation, and with labour and health law extended to the trade as to other work. This position has institutional support of a specific kind: Amnesty International adopted a policy in 2016 favouring the decriminalisation of consensual sex work, and the World Health Organization, UNAIDS and the United Nations Population Fund have recommended the decriminalisation of sex work as a measure of health and rights protection, recommendations the Indian collectives cite as the considered view of the bodies that run the HIV programmes the Indian State itself depends on.

The two positions agree on more than their public collision suggests, and the agreement deserves exact statement because it marks the ground on which legislation could move. Both hold that trafficking, meaning the recruitment, movement and harbouring of persons by force, fraud, deception or abuse of vulnerability for exploitation, must be prosecuted vigorously, with the code offences as the instrument. Both hold that any involvement of a child or minor is a grave crime admitting no argument. Both hold that the person selling sex should not be criminalised for selling sex, and the abolitionist model decriminalises the seller as deliberately as the rights model does, differing on the buyer and the premises. Both condemn police violence and extortion against women in the trade, though they locate its remedy differently. And both, in their serious forms, are arguments about the same statute's circumference: the disagreement is whether criminalising more of the circumference, the customer above all, will shrink the trade toward extinction or merely toward danger, and whether decriminalising the circumference will dignify the worker or license the exploiter. That is an empirical and moral disagreement of the first order, and it will not be settled by the text of the 1956 Act, which embodies a third position, the Convention position, that neither camp holds: punish the keeper, the procurer and the landlord, ignore the customer, manage the woman.

## The child in the Act and the fault line at sixteen

The Act's treatment of the young exposes its most widely acknowledged drafting defect, and it is a defect of a single number. The aggravated offences, the rescue machinery's special routes and the presumptions around children all turn on the definition of child as a person who has not completed sixteen years, with minor covering the band from sixteen to eighteen. When the Act was reconstructed in 1986, sixteen was a defensible line in a legal order whose general age of majority for these purposes was unsettled. The surrounding law has since moved. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 defines a child as any person below eighteen years, and the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act proceeds on the same age; the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita's trafficking section likewise uses the category of the child on the eighteen year line. The result is a seam through the middle of a single case. A girl of seventeen found in a brothel is a child for the purposes of the sexual offences code and of the juvenile justice system, with its child welfare committees and its procedures, and a minor for the purposes of this Act, to which the aggravated ranges for minors do apply, so she is not unprotected; but the still graver ranges and presumptions keyed to the word child in Sections 5, 6 and 7, and the Section 6(2) presumption attaching to a person found with a child in a brothel, are keyed to the lower age, and the prosecution and the magistrate must keep two age maps open on the same file. The amendment Bill of 2006 proposed the obvious repair, raising the Act's child to eighteen and dissolving the minor and major categories, and the repair failed with the Bill. The Law Commission and the parliamentary committee examining that Bill both treated the misalignment as indefensible in principle, and its survival is a measure of the Act's legislative orphanhood: the point on which abolitionists, the rights movement, the committees and the drafting ministry have all agreed, at one time or another, is the point Parliament has never got round to enacting, because every vehicle that carried it also carried the client offence or the trafficking code on which agreement failed.

The seam is worked daily at the point of rescue, because a child taken from a brothel enters two systems at once. Under this Act the child is produced before a magistrate, medically examined for age and kept in intermediate custody pending the Section 17 inquiry, with the proviso to Section 17(3) permitting placement in an institution established or recognised under the Children Acts for safe custody. Under the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, the same child is a child in need of care and protection who must be produced before the Child Welfare Committee, the body charged with decisions on placement, restoration and rehabilitation for such children, and the committee's processes, its inquiry into the family, its individual care plan and its preference for restoration and non-institutional care where a safe family exists, run to a different logic from the magistrate's detention order of one to three years in a protective home. The two jurisdictions are not ranked against each other by any clean statutory rule, and in districts where both are active the child's destination depends on which door the rescue entered by, the police station's practice and the vigilance of the organisations accompanying the case. For the accused, the parallel systems matter differently, since the aggravated prosecution proceeds in the criminal court on the age the prosecution can prove, while the protective placement proceeds on the age the committee or magistrate accepts, and the two findings are made on different materials at different times, so that a trial may end in acquittal on the aggravated charge for failure of age proof in a case where the child has already spent a year in an institution on the footing that the age was established. No feature of the field better illustrates the cost of the Act's legislative isolation: it was written when no general child protection code existed, and every code enacted since has been drafted around it rather than with it.

The seam matters beyond symmetry. Age determination is the first forensic act of every rescue, under Section 15(5A), and in a population where birth registration is incomplete and documents are routinely absent or withheld by the very persons accused of detention, the medical estimation of age carries an error margin that straddles the statutory lines; a person estimated at seventeen may be sixteen or eighteen, and the estimate decides the charge against the accused, the institution that receives the rescued person and the regime under which that person is kept. The rights movement and the rescue organisations dispute the consequences in opposite directions, the one pointing to adult women detained as minors on contested estimates, the other to children released as adults into the hands of the networks that brought them, and both phenomena are documented in the litigation around the homes. A statute whose protective intensity changes discontinuously at an age that cannot be reliably measured, in the population least able to document it, has built an arbitrariness into its most sympathetic provisions, and the arbitrariness is the product not of the 1956 Parliament but of the subsequent Parliaments that amended everything around the definition and left the definition standing.

## Consent: the word the Act uses everywhere and defines nowhere

The organising idea of the modern argument is consent, and the Act's handling of it repays a section of its own, because the statute is neither as indifferent to consent as its critics say nor as respectful of it as the 2022 directions require. Section 5 punishes procurement whether with or without the consent of the person procured, and Section 6 punishes detention whether with or without consent; in both places the legislature deliberately wrote consent out of liability for the third party, on the Convention's reasoning that the procurer and the detainer are wrongdoers whatever the procured person says, since the saying may itself be the product of the procurement. Where the person is a child or a minor, the same philosophy runs through the aggravated ranges: the law treats the young person's agreement as legally worthless, as it does across the sexual offences code. For adults, however, the Act never asks the reciprocal question. It does not ask whether the woman in the brothel is there by her own choice for the purposes of any consequence that befalls her: she is removed under Section 15 or 16 either way, inquired into under Section 17 either way, and detained in a protective home, if the magistrate finds her in need of care and protection, either way, the finding of need being precisely the judgment that displaces her own account of her situation. The statute thus operates a one way valve on consent. The consent of the adult woman is irrelevant to excuse the man who procured her, which is protective, and equally irrelevant to entitle her to be left where she is, which is the opposite; it counts against third parties and never for her.

The 2022 directions can be read as the judicial correction of that asymmetry, and reading them so explains both their content and their limits. The bench's test, age and consent, takes the two facts the Act subordinates and makes them decisive for the conduct of the police: an adult participating with consent is not to be interfered with, and a raid on premises is to proceed against the running of the brothel and not against the women in it. The correction is real but it is a correction of enforcement, not of text, and it leaves the custody machinery of Sections 15 to 17 standing in its statutory form, available to any magistrate and any special police officer who takes the older view of need. The abolitionist reading of consent pulls the other way: on the account given by Apne Aap and the Coalition Against Trafficking in Women, the poverty and deception that stock the trade mean that the consent the bench's test looks for will be formally present and substantively empty in a large share of cases, and a test of age and consent will license the police to leave women in exploitation they were powerless to refuse. The rights movement answers that the alternative to asking the woman is asking anyone but her, the magistrate, the rescue organisation, the panel, and that every historical exercise of that substituted judgment has ended in the homes. The statute offers no resolution, because the statute was drafted in a framework, the Convention framework, in which the question was not asked: the woman in prostitution was a victim by definition, and victims are not consulted about the fact. Every later position in this field is an attempt to answer the question the Act refuses to pose, and the refusal is the deepest reason the Act satisfies nobody.

## The enforcement pattern and the evidence from Sonagachi

What the Act does in operation can be described without invented figures, because its pattern is structural and is confirmed by the architecture of the offences themselves. The charges that are cheap to prove are the charges that are brought. A Section 8 case requires observation of soliciting conduct in or near a public place and the word of the officers; a Section 7 case requires presence in the wrong premises; both are tried summarily in practice, both typically end in fines calibrated to another age, and both are brought against the person found on the spot, who is the worker. A Section 5 or Section 6 case requires a victim who will testify against the network that recruited or detained her, over months, against persons who may hold her debts, her documents and knowledge of her family; a Section 3 case requires proof of the gain element and of the keeping; and the prosecutions that matter most under the Act's own design are therefore the slowest, the rarest and the most dependent on the cooperation of the person the system has just removed, examined and detained. The predictable result, described in the same terms by the parliamentary committee that examined the 2006 Bill, by the panel in the Karmaskar proceedings and by the organisations on both sides of the policy argument, is an enforcement profile inverted against the statute's intention: heavy at the bottom, against soliciting and presence; light at the top, against procurement, detention and organised keeping; and experienced by the woman in prostitution not as protection punctuated by raids but as a standing condition of police discretion over her workplace, her residence and her liberty, exercised through charges the Supreme Court would eventually have to instruct the police not to bring against her in a raid at all.

The most consequential evidence about what changes that condition comes from Kolkata. The STD and HIV Intervention Programme begun in Sonagachi in 1992 under the direction of the public health physician Smarajit Jana, working through peer educators drawn from the sex workers of the district, organised the women of the brothels into the collective that became the Durbar Mahila Samanwaya Committee, and the programme's premise was the opposite of the raid's: that the women were the indispensable agents of health and safety in the trade, to be organised rather than removed. The Durbar's own institutions, its clinics, its cooperative credit society, its self-regulatory boards which screen new entrants to the district's brothels to identify and turn back minors and trafficked persons, are the standing practical exhibit of the rights position, and the boards in particular answer the abolitionist challenge on its own ground, since a screening mechanism run by the workers themselves claims to detect the trafficked entrant more reliably than a periodic raid, and to do so without detaining the entire population among whom the entrant hides. UNAIDS and the World Health Organization have cited the Sonagachi model in their guidance on community mobilisation among sex workers, and the Indian HIV programme's own targeted intervention structure, which works through peer outreach rather than enforcement, embodies the same concession by the State that funds it: the health of this population is achievable only with its cooperation, and its cooperation is not obtainable at the point of a Section 8 charge. The abolitionist response is also a practical one and should be recorded: that peer organisation, however effective for health, leaves the demand side and the economics of entry untouched, that self-regulation inside a brothel district presupposes the district, and that the women the boards turn away at the gate of Sonagachi are trafficked to districts with no boards. The evidence does not settle the argument. It establishes the narrower proposition on which the next decade of litigation and legislation will turn: that enforcement against the worker is not merely unjust in the rights movement's account but counterproductive in the State's own account, given by its health programme, of how this population is to be reached at all.

## The woman in the text: what the substitution of 1986 left behind

The reconstruction of 1986 proceeded, in large part, by substitution: women and girls came out, persons went in, and the statute emerged formally indifferent to sex. A careful reading of the text as it stands shows where the substitution stopped, and the residue is instructive, because it marks the places where the drafting imagination of the Act remained fixed on the female subject even as its formal scheme became neutral. Section 8 still describes the solicitor's conduct as wilful exposure of her person, by sitting by a window or on the balcony of a building or house, a pronoun that survived the general substitution and betrays the figure the draftsman saw when the clause was written. Section 6(3) still frames the presumption of detention in terms of a woman or girl, withholding her jewellery, apparel, money or property, and threatening her over property lent or supplied to her, and Section 6(4) still bars the detainer's suit against such woman or girl, although the operative subsections around them speak of persons. Section 10A still applies to a female offender, and only to a female offender: the corrective detention in lieu of sentence for offences under Sections 7 and 8 is a disposition the Act makes available for women and not for men, so that the one sentencing provision in the Act addressed to the person who sells sex is addressed, in terms, to women alone. The advisory body and the search provisions likewise retain their instruction that women be included wherever practicable, and the definition of the protective home's management proviso directs that management be entrusted to women wherever practicable, provisions benign in intention which nonetheless confirm the institutional picture: a statute administered by men and women over women, in buildings preferably managed by women, for the correction or protection of women.

The residue matters for more than antiquarian reasons. Male and transgender persons do sell sex in India, and since 1986 they are within the Act's offences and its machinery in the same terms as women; the 2022 directions in the Karmaskar proceedings, which speak of sex workers without qualification and were applied in argument to transgender workers as well, proceed on that footing. But the provisions that bite hardest on the worker, Section 8 and Section 10A, carry their original subject in their text or their structure, and a transgender or male worker proceeded against under them meets a statute that has not quite decided it is speaking to him. Courts read such provisions by their operative words rather than their pronouns, and no reported line of authority makes the pronoun a defence, yet the drafting residue has a rhetorical life of its own in the debate: for the rights movement it is evidence that the Act was written as an instrument for the management of women and never reconceived; for traditional defenders of the Act it is evidence of the opposite, that the legislature of 1956 and 1986 understood the trade's actual demography and drafted for the population it overwhelmingly contains. Both arguments are available because the text supports both, and the text supports both because Parliament, in 1986, chose substitution over reconception, the cheaper legislative operation, and left the seams visible.

## What a prosecution must prove: the elements assembled

A pillar article owes the practitioner one synthesis that the section by section treatment disperses, which is the shape of the prosecution's task across the Act, because the Act's enforcement pattern is explained by it. Every prosecution begins with the definition. The premises must be a brothel, which means proof of use for sexual exploitation or abuse for the gain of another, or for the mutual gain of two or more prostitutes; the activity must be prostitution, exploitation or abuse for commercial purposes; the person at the centre must be placed in the right age band, since the band selects the punishment range and sometimes the presumption. Against the keeper, the prosecution must then prove keeping, management or assistance in management, and against the landlord or tenant the additional element of knowledge, assisted by the Section 3(2A) presumption once a search report has been published in a local newspaper or the seizure list served. Against the person charged under Section 4, the prosecution proves the platform facts, living with, habitual company, control over movements, touting, and takes the benefit of the presumption of living on earnings, leaving the accused to displace it. Against the procurer under Section 5, the prosecution must prove one of the four acts, procuring, inducing to go, taking or causing to be taken, causing or inducing to carry on prostitution, with prostitution as the purpose, consent being no answer; against the detainer under Section 6, detention in a brothel or in premises with the specified intent, assisted by the presumptions that follow from withholding property or from the presence of a child; against the custodian under Section 9, the relationship of custody, charge, care or authority and the causing, aiding or abetting of seduction for prostitution. The place offences require the least: under Section 7, the premises, the distance or the notified area and the carrying on; under Section 8, the conduct of tempting, attracting, soliciting, molesting or loitering, in or within sight or hearing of a public place, for the purpose of prostitution.

Assembled in one view, the elements explain the charge sheet of an ordinary raid and the fate of an ordinary prosecution. The offences against the worker require proof of things the police can themselves observe in a single visit, and they can be completed, charged and tried with no witness but the raiding party. The offences against the organisers require proof of gain, of knowledge, of recruitment, of detention, against accused persons who keep no records the Act can seize and whose transactions are in cash, and they depend, for their human evidence, on the testimony of persons whom the same operation has classified as victims, removed to institutions and exposed to the character inquiry of Section 17. Defence practice answers in kind: it attacks the brothel definition where a single woman worked alone, attacks knowledge where the landlord let at arm's length, attacks the platform facts under Section 4 where the household was a family, attacks the search where the respectable witnesses were not of the locality or the grounds were not recorded, and attacks age where the medical estimate straddles a band. None of this is a defect of advocacy; it is the statute working as drafted, and it yields the distribution of outcomes the field knows, in which the convictions that accumulate are for soliciting and presence and the convictions that matter, for procurement and detention, are few, hard and slow. A legislature that wished to invert that distribution would have to change the elements, the presumptions or the machinery, which is what the amendment exercises of 2006 and the codes of 2018 and 2021 each, in their different ways, attempted and failed to do.

## Assessment: what the statute does well, what it does badly, and the verdict

Measured against its own design, the Act has real strengths, and a pillar article should record them before the indictment. Its exploitation offences, procuring, detaining and seducing a person in custody, are serious, well graded by the age of the victim, and supported by presumptions aimed at the actual techniques of control, the withheld property, the manufactured debt, the threat of legal process. Its machinery contains, on paper, safeguards that many later statutes omit: seniority of the enforcing officer, written grounds for warrantless search, local witnesses including a woman, women police officers at searches and interrogations, immediate production before a magistrate, an inquiry with a social welfare panel before detention, an appeal to Sessions. Its distinction between the seller and the establishment, vindicated by the Supreme Court in 2022, is more humane than the law of many jurisdictions that punish the woman directly. And its licensing scheme for homes, with investigation before licence, conditions, inspection and revocation, is a recognisable attempt to govern the institutions in which the State detains its own citizens without trial.

The indictment is longer, and it is structural rather than administrative. The Act's central distinction is defeated by its own place and soliciting offences, which between them criminalise the visible practice of the trade by the person the Act professes not to punish, and which fall, in the enforcement pattern described by the parliamentary committee that examined the 2006 Bill and by the panel in the Karmaskar proceedings, on the poorest and most visible workers rather than on the organised trade conducted indoors and by introduction. Its brothel definition punishes the safety practice of working in company. Its earnings presumption reverses the burden against the worker's household. Its custody machinery permits detention of an unconvicted person for one to three years in institutions whose own register the State has had to be ordered to compile, under a definition, in need of care and protection, that the same statute pairs with an institution for persons in need of correction and a power to detain a convicted solicitor for up to five years in lieu of a six month sentence. Its penalties carry the fines of another age, five hundred rupees for soliciting, two thousand for keeping a brothel, untouched while the imprisonment terms around them were raised, a small fact that measures how little sustained legislative attention the Act has received since 1986. Its client is invisible, so the one participant in every transaction who is never poor is the one participant the Act almost never reaches. And its title promises a law against trafficking that its contents do not contain, so that public argument perpetually indicts it for failing at a task assigned to it by its name and performed, since 2013, by another statute.

The namable claim of this article is the one with which it opened, and it can be given its full form. Indian law on the selling of sex is neither prohibition nor legalisation: it is a status without a name, in which the act is no offence and every condition of performing it, the premises, the partner in the premises, the sharing of earnings, the search for customers in public, the proximity to a school or hospital, the continued residence in a locality after a magistrate's order, is punishable, detainable or removable. Every serious participant in the field is, in the end, arguing about which direction to resolve that contradiction: the abolitionist would resolve it upward, extending criminalisation to the buyer until the trade itself withers; the rights movement would resolve it downward, withdrawing criminalisation from the circumference until the act's lawfulness becomes real; and the statute, unreformed since 1986 and unreplaced through three failed attempts, holds the contradiction in place, distributing its costs, as such contradictions usually do, to the person with the least power in the transaction. That distribution, and not the morality of the trade, is what the record of the Act's operation most clearly establishes, and it is the verdict this pillar article carries into the cluster: a statute may decline to punish a person and still govern her life entire, if it punishes everything she needs in order to live it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is sex work illegal in India?

No provision of the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956 makes it an offence for an adult to sell sex, and the Supreme Court proceeded on exactly that footing in its order of 19 May 2022 in Budhadev Karmaskar, where it recorded that voluntary sex work is not illegal and only running a brothel is unlawful. What the Act criminalises is the surrounding structure: keeping or letting a brothel, living on another person's earnings of prostitution, procuring or detaining a person, carrying on prostitution in a notified area or near specified public places, and soliciting in public. The practical position is therefore narrower than the formal one, since a person may lawfully sell sex and still be arrested for soliciting the customer or for working in premises that fall within the place offences.

### Q: What does the Immoral Traffic Prevention Act criminalise?

The Act criminalises the organisation and facilitation of prostitution rather than its sale. Section 3 punishes keeping, managing or assisting in a brothel, and letting premises for use as one. Section 4 punishes an adult who knowingly lives wholly or partly on the earnings of another person's prostitution. Section 5 punishes procuring, inducing or taking a person for the sake of prostitution, with or without that person's consent. Section 6 punishes detaining a person in premises where prostitution is carried on, and Section 9 punishes a person in custody, charge or authority who seduces the person in their care for prostitution. Sections 7 and 8 punish prostitution in or near specified public places and soliciting in public, and the Act adds machinery offences around unlicensed protective homes. Trafficking in persons generally is an offence under the criminal code, not under this Act.

### Q: What is the soliciting offence and who is arrested under it?

Section 8 punishes seducing or soliciting for the purpose of prostitution. It covers anyone who, in a public place, or within sight or hearing of one, whether from inside a building or not, by words, gestures, wilful exposure of the person or otherwise, tempts or attracts the attention of any person for the purpose of prostitution, or who solicits, molests, loiters or causes obstruction or annoyance for that purpose. A first conviction carries imprisonment extending to six months, or a fine extending to five hundred rupees, or both; a later conviction carries up to one year. No transaction, customer or payment need be proved, so the offence is in practice the charge of visible availability, and it is overwhelmingly the sex worker herself who is arrested under it, which is why the provision stands at the centre of the argument that the Act criminalises the worker by another route.

### Q: How does the Act deal with rescue and rehabilitation?

A special police officer may search premises without a warrant under Section 15 and remove the persons found there, and a magistrate may direct a rescue from a brothel under Section 16. The person removed is produced before a magistrate, may be medically examined for age, injury and disease, and is held in intermediate custody while the magistrate inquires into the information, the person's age and antecedents and the suitability of parents or guardians, assisted where needed by a probation officer and a panel of social welfare workers. If satisfied the person is in need of care and protection, the magistrate may order detention in a protective home for one to three years, subject to appeal to the Court of Session. A person may also apply voluntarily under Section 19. Protective homes and corrective institutions are established or licensed by State Governments under Section 21, and rehabilitation within them is framed as care, education and training.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court direct about sex workers in 2022?

In Budhadev Karmaskar v State of West Bengal, a bench of Justices L. Nageswara Rao, B.R. Gavai and A.S. Bopanna, by an order dated 19 May 2022 made under Article 142 of the Constitution, directed that sex workers are entitled to equal protection of the law and that criminal law must apply equally on the basis of age and consent. Where an adult sex worker is participating with consent, the police must refrain from interfering or taking criminal action; in a raid on a brothel, sex workers must not be arrested, penalised, harassed or victimised, since voluntary sex work is not illegal and only running a brothel is unlawful. The police were directed to treat sex workers with dignity and without abuse, the media were asked not to reveal identities during raids or rescues, condoms were not to be treated as evidence of an offence, identity documents were not to be withheld for non-disclosure of occupation, and the States were directed to survey all protective homes under the Act.

### Q: How does trafficking law differ from the 1956 Act?

The 1956 Act is a prostitution statute and contains no definition of trafficking. Its offences are organised around premises, earnings, procurement for prostitution and soliciting, and its machinery is the search, the rescue and the protective home. Trafficking in persons is defined by the general criminal code: Section 370 of the Penal Code, inserted by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013, and carried into Section 143 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, with the exploitation of a trafficked person in Section 144. The code offence requires recruiting, transporting, harbouring, transferring or receiving a person by threats, force, coercion, abduction, fraud, deception, abuse of power or inducement, for the purpose of exploitation, which includes sexual exploitation, forced labour, slavery, servitude and organ removal. It therefore covers every exploitative destination, makes consent immaterial where a prohibited means is used, and does not depend on premises or on prostitution at all.

### Q: What happened to the anti trafficking bill?

India has repeatedly drafted a dedicated anti-trafficking statute and has never enacted one. The Trafficking of Persons (Prevention, Protection and Rehabilitation) Bill, 2018, which proposed a national anti-trafficking bureau, district machinery, a rehabilitation fund and forfeiture of traffickers' property, was passed by the Lok Sabha on 26 July 2018 but was not considered by the Rajya Sabha, and it lapsed on the dissolution of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha. A revised draft, the Trafficking in Persons (Prevention, Care and Rehabilitation) Bill, was circulated by the Ministry of Women and Child Development for public comment on 30 June 2021, with a wider reach and severe punishments for aggravated forms. It drew strong opposition from sex workers' networks and migration organisations, who argued that it conflated consensual adult sex work and voluntary migration with trafficking, and it was not introduced in Parliament.

### Q: What are the arguments for and against decriminalising sex work?

The abolitionist argument, made by organisations such as Apne Aap Worldwide and the Coalition Against Trafficking in Women, is that prostitution is itself exploitation and violence, that entry into it is driven overwhelmingly by poverty, coercion or trafficking, often in childhood, and that consent within such constraints is not real choice; on this view the law should criminalise the buyer and the industry, decriminalise the seller and exit her, on the model Sweden adopted in 1999. The sex workers' rights argument, made by the Durbar Mahila Samanwaya Committee, the National Network of Sex Workers and SANGRAM's VAMP collective, is that adults who choose sex work are workers and citizens, that criminalising premises, earnings and soliciting drives the trade into unsafe, unpoliced settings, prevents organisation and the reporting of violence, and exposes workers to extortion, and that full decriminalisation with vigorous separate enforcement against trafficking and minors, on the New Zealand model of 2003, protects best. Both sides agree that trafficking and any involvement of minors must be prosecuted and that the seller should not be criminalised.

### Q: What counts as a brothel under the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act?

By Section 2(a), a brothel includes any house, room, conveyance or place, or any portion of one, which is used for purposes of sexual exploitation or abuse for the gain of another person or for the mutual gain of two or more prostitutes. The gain element is decisive. Premises used by one person alone, receiving clients for that person's own gain, are not a brothel within the definition, while premises shared by two or more persons in prostitution for their mutual gain are, even with no keeper, landlord or third party taking anything. That is why the definition is criticised by sex workers' organisations: the arrangement by which workers most commonly protect themselves, working together from shared premises, is the arrangement the definition converts into a brothel, exposing everyone in it to the Section 3 offences and to closure under Section 18.

### Q: What is the punishment for keeping a brothel or letting premises to be used as one?

Under Section 3(1), keeping or managing a brothel, or acting or assisting in its keeping or management, is punishable on first conviction with rigorous imprisonment for not less than one year and not more than three years, and a fine extending to two thousand rupees; on a second or subsequent conviction the term is not less than two years and not more than five years, with fine. Under Section 3(2), a tenant, lessee, occupier or person in charge who uses premises as a brothel or knowingly allows that use, and an owner, lessor, landlord or agent who lets premises knowing they are intended for such use or is wilfully a party to it, is punishable on first conviction with imprisonment extending to two years and fine, and on a later conviction with rigorous imprisonment extending to five years and fine. On conviction under Section 3(2), the lease of the premises becomes void.

### Q: How does the Act define a child, a minor and a major?

Section 2 defines a child as a person who has not completed the age of sixteen years, a minor as a person who has completed sixteen years but has not completed eighteen, and a major as a person who has completed eighteen years. The bands drive the penalty structure and the protective machinery. Offence after offence substitutes a severe mandatory range where the person in respect of whom it is committed is a child, a somewhat lower aggravated range for a minor, and an ordinary range for an adult; procuring a child, for example, carries rigorous imprisonment of seven years to life under Section 5. The bands also decide custody routes after a rescue. The sixteen year line is the Act's most criticised survival, since the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 and the juvenile justice law both set childhood at eighteen, and the amendment Bill of 2006 proposed raising the Act's line to eighteen, without success.

### Q: What presumption does Section 4 raise against a person who lives with a sex worker?

Section 4 punishes an adult who knowingly lives, wholly or in part, on the earnings of another person's prostitution. Section 4(2) provides that where a person over eighteen is proved to be living with, or to be habitually in the company of, a prostitute, or to have exercised control, direction or influence over her movements so as to show he is aiding, abetting or compelling her prostitution, or to be acting as a tout or pimp on her behalf, it shall be presumed, until the contrary is proved, that he is knowingly living on the earnings of her prostitution. The prosecution must prove the platform fact, cohabitation, habitual company, control or touting, and the financial element is then presumed. The presumption is rebuttable, but in practice it places the burden of explaining a household's finances on partners, husbands and adult relatives of women in prostitution, which is why the earnings offence is both defended as the only workable instrument against exploiters who receive cash and keep no brothel, and attacked as a lever over the worker's family.

### Q: Can a customer who pays for sex be punished under this Act?

The Act contains no offence of buying sex, and the customer is almost entirely absent from its text. He appears as a principal offender in one place only: Section 7(1) punishes any person who carries on prostitution, and the person with whom such prostitution is carried on, in premises within a notified area or within two hundred metres of a place of public religious worship, an educational institution, a hostel, a hospital or a nursing home, with imprisonment extending to three months. Outside those areas and that distance, paying an adult for sex is not an offence under this Act, though a customer may commit offences under other laws, for example where the person is a child or a trafficked person under the criminal code. Every proposal to punish the buyer as such, from the amendment Bill of 2006, which proposed an offence of visiting a brothel for the sexual exploitation of a trafficked victim, to abolitionist campaigns for a general purchase offence, has failed in Parliament.

### Q: Who is a special police officer, and what powers does that office carry?

A special police officer is a police officer appointed by or on behalf of the State Government under Section 13 for a specified area, who shall not be below the rank of an Inspector of Police, assisted by subordinate officers including women police officers wherever practicable, and advised by a non-official advisory body of not more than five leading social welfare workers of the area. The office is the channel for the Act's coercive powers. Arrest without warrant under the Act may be made only by the special police officer or under his direction, guidance or prior approval, with a written order naming the person and offence where a subordinate arrests in his absence. The special police officer may enter and search premises without a warrant under Section 15 on recorded grounds, remove the persons found there and produce them before a magistrate, and the District Magistrate may confer the office's powers on senior retired police or military officers for particular cases.

### Q: What safeguards govern a search of premises without a warrant under Section 15?

Section 15 permits the special police officer or a trafficking police officer to enter and search premises without a warrant only where there are reasonable grounds, which must be recorded, for believing an offence under the Act is being committed in respect of a person living there, and that a search with a warrant cannot be made without undue delay. Before searching, the officer must call upon two or more respectable inhabitants of the locality, at least one of whom shall be a woman, to attend and witness the search, and may order them in writing to do so. The officer must be accompanied by at least two women police officers, and a woman or girl removed may be interrogated only by a woman police officer or in the presence of a woman member of a recognised welfare institution. Persons removed must be produced forthwith before the appropriate magistrate, and the search provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure apply as they apply to a search under warrant.

### Q: What is the difference between a protective home and a corrective institution?

Both are institutions established under Section 21 by State Governments or maintained under their licence, but they embody opposite theories of the person inside. A protective home is an institution in which persons who are in need of care and protection may be kept, with technically qualified persons, equipment and facilities provided; a person rescued under Sections 15 or 16 and found after inquiry to be in need of care and protection may be detained there by a magistrate's order under Section 17(4) for one to three years, without any conviction. A corrective institution is an institution in which persons who are in need of correction may be detained; its principal intake under the Act is punitive in origin, since a court convicting a female offender under Section 7 or Section 8 may, under Section 10A, order her detention there for two to five years in lieu of imprisonment, for instruction and discipline conducive to her correction.

### Q: Can a magistrate order a sex worker to leave a place under Section 20?

Yes. Under Section 20, a magistrate who receives information that a person residing in or frequenting a place within his jurisdiction is a prostitute may issue notice to that person to show cause why she should not be required to remove herself from the place and be prohibited from re-entering it. After inquiry, at which the person may adduce evidence, if the magistrate is satisfied that the person is a prostitute and that removal is necessary in the interests of the general public, he may order removal to a specified place, by a specified route and time, not less than seven days from the order, and prohibit re-entry without his written permission. No offence need be alleged or proved. The Supreme Court upheld the provision against challenges under Articles 14 and 19 in State of Uttar Pradesh v Kaushaliya in 1964, holding the magistrate's power to be guided by the twin findings the section requires and the restrictions to be reasonable in the interests of the general public.

### Q: How did the amendment of 1986 change the statute enacted in 1956?

The amending Act of 1986, Act 44 of 1986, in force from 26 January 1987, reconstructed the statute without replacing it. It renamed the Suppression of Immoral Traffic in Women and Girls Act as the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act and substituted prevention language throughout. It made the Act formally neutral as to sex, substituting persons for women and girls in the operative provisions, and substituted the definition of prostitution as the sexual exploitation or abuse of persons for commercial purposes. It inserted the definitions of child, minor and major, created trafficking police officers appointed by the Central Government under Section 13(4) for offences committed in more than one State, strengthened the search and rescue provisions including medical examination and the conditions in Section 17A for handing rescued persons to parents or guardians, omitted the old probation and habitual offender sections, stiffened several punishments, and added provisions on records of homes, Central Government special courts and summary trial. The delivered Article 141 records the same Act number for the original statute, Act 104 of 1956.

### Q: What did the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Amendment Bill of 2006 propose, and what became of it?

The Bill, introduced in the Lok Sabha in 2006 and examined by the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, proposed to raise the age of a child in the Act from sixteen to eighteen and to drop the minor and major distinctions; to insert a definition of trafficking in persons on the lines of the international Protocol, with offences of trafficking and of visiting a brothel for the sexual exploitation of a trafficked victim; to enhance the punishments for brothel keeping and detention; to omit Section 8, the soliciting offence, and Section 20, the removal power, altogether; to lower the floor for the special police officer from Inspector to Sub-Inspector; to constitute central and State authorities against trafficking; and to require in camera trials. It united the demands of the sex workers' movement in its deletions with the client-facing offences that movement opposed, and it was never enacted, lapsing with the dissolution of the Lok Sabha that had received it, so Sections 8 and 20 and the sixteen year line all remain in force.

### Q: Why does Article 23 of the Constitution matter to this Act?

Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings and begar and other similar forms of forced labour, and declares that contravention of the prohibition shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law. The prohibition is fundamental but not self-executing as a penal code: it names the wrong and leaves the definition of offences and punishments to legislation. Article 35 reserves that legislation to Parliament, which is why the suppression of immoral traffic, a subject that might otherwise have belonged to the States, was enacted as a central statute in 1956, repealing the provincial suppression Acts as it came into force. The 1956 Act is therefore one of the statutes through which Article 23 operates, but only in part: it answers the prohibition for traffic into prostitution, while traffic for forced labour, servitude and other exploitation is answered by the trafficking sections of the criminal code and by statutes such as the bonded labour legislation. Article 23 also explains the Act's constitutional challenges, since its externment and restriction provisions had to be defended, successfully in Kaushaliya, as reasonable restrictions on the freedoms that Article 23 does not itself displace.
