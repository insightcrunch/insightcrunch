The Indian diaspora became a foreign policy constituency through deliberate statecraft rather than through demography alone. Tens of millions of people of Indian birth or descent live outside India, yet for the republic's first four decades its government addressed them mainly as a consular burden or an embarrassment to be managed at a distance. Between 1991 and 2023 a succession of governments built an architecture of crisis bonds, annual conventions, status cards and stadium rallies that converted emigrants and their grandchildren into an organised presence inside India's external relations and its election campaigns. The claim defended here is that the phase after 2014 operationalised that constituency at a scale no earlier phase approached.

## The Diaspora in Indian Foreign Policy: A Constituency Made, Not Found

A constituency, in the strict electoral sense, is a population that a political actor must answer to because it can reward or punish. The Indian diaspora holds no seats in the Lok Sabha and, with one narrow exception for citizens resident abroad, casts no ballots in Indian elections. The argument of this article is that the word nonetheless fits, because between 1991 and 2023 the Indian state learned to treat the diaspora as a body whose money, applause, lobbying and judgment it actively solicited, and whose reactions it factored into decisions taken in New Delhi. [The foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/) developed for the Non-Alignment era holds that Indian external policy is best read as domestic politics conducted by other means; the diaspora story carries that framework into a period when the domestic audience partly lived outside the territory of the state altogether.

Three framings compete for this material. The diaspora-studies framing, represented by the standard scholarly surveys of Indian migration, reads the story as a sociology of departure: indenture, chain migration, settlement, remittance and return. The event framing, favoured by journalism after 2014, reads the story as a sequence of spectacles: a prime minister at Madison Square Garden, a stadium in Houston shared with an American president, a rally outside Ahmedabad. The constituency framing defended here reads the same record institutionally. It asks what the Indian state built, in law and in ritual, to make emigrants usable: usable as lenders in a balance-of-payments crisis, usable as lobbyists in a foreign legislature, usable as applause that foreign governments could hear, and usable as volunteers and donors inside Indian campaigns. The five features that follow are the load-bearing parts of that construction: the crisis finance and voluntary economic embrace after 1991; the Pravasi Bharatiya Divas convention instituted in 2003; the Overseas Citizen of India status rolled out from 2005; the stadium events staged between 2014 and 2023; and the domestic-political content that the events carried back into Indian contests.

The evidentiary standard matters, because diaspora writing is prone to sentiment. The core documents are datable and checkable: the Report of the High Level Committee on the Indian Diaspora, forwarded by its chairman L. M. Singhvi in December 2001; the Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2003 and the amendments that followed it; the recorded proceedings of conventions and rallies; and the two scholarly accounts that anchor the adjudication, Latha Varadarajan's The Domestic Abroad (2010) and Devesh Kapur's Diaspora, Development, and Democracy (2010). Varadarajan, a political scientist, argues that the Indian state did not discover a pre-existing diaspora but discursively produced one, recasting emigrants from a problem of national loyalty into a strategic asset. Kapur, an economist of migration, tests the material channels through which emigration changed India itself: money flows, investment networks and the circulation of political ideas. The constituency reading borrows from both and adds the electoral observation they underplay: after 2014 the solicited applause of the diaspora was piped back into Indian domestic competition as evidence of national resurgence, so the constituency faced two ways at once, outward into foreign capitals and inward into Indian voting booths it did not itself occupy.

Numbers discipline the claim of unprecedented scale, and the numbers require attribution because the counters count different things. The United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, in its International Migrant Stock 2020, counted about 18 million Indian-born persons living outside India, the largest emigrant stock of any country. The Ministry of External Affairs, compiling the returns of Indian missions, has placed the wider population of overseas Indians, citizens and persons of Indian origin together, above 30 million; statements by ministers in Parliament during 2019 and 2020 used figures in the range of 31 to 32 million. The first series counts emigrants; the second counts a descent population across four generations in Mauritius, Fiji, Guyana and South Africa alongside last decade's software engineers in California. Both series agree on the political fact that matters here: no earlier Indian government disposed of an external population of this size, this wealth, or this concentration in the legislatures' districts of India's principal partners.

India was not the only state building such machinery, and the comparison sharpens what the Indian build achieved. The Philippines, the closest parallel as a labour-exporting democracy, created its Commission on Filipinos Overseas in 1980 and a welfare administration for overseas workers whose protection apparatus long predated India's, yet Manila's diaspora politics remained a politics of labour administration, without a status card of OCI's ambition or a stadium circuit. Mexico's Institute for Mexicans Abroad, created in 2003, courted a diaspora of comparable size in a single host country and built advisory councils that New Delhi's planners studied, but Mexico's emigrants remained outside any honours-and-convention ritual of the PBD's scale. Ireland appointed a minister for diaspora affairs in 2014 and China maintained its Overseas Chinese Affairs machinery across the same decades, each treating emigrants as a national asset in the Singhvi report's sense. The Indian construction is distinguished within this company by its combination: crisis finance, an honours convention, a quasi-citizenship card and a televised mass circuit, assembled by governments of rival parties over thirty years into a single architecture whose parts cite one another. It is the combination, not any single instrument, that the word constituency is needed to describe.

A framework that cannot state its own defeat conditions is a slogan, so the constituency reading states them. The reading would fail if the record showed the instruments unconnected, a finance ministry borrowing from emigrants while a foreign ministry ignored them and a prime minister's rallies drew only on sentiment; it would fail if the architecture's parts were dismantled by successor governments as partisan furniture; and it would fail if the diaspora's organised reactions demonstrably never entered any Indian decision, neither the pricing of a bond, the drafting of a statute, the staging of a visit nor the planning of a campaign. The record defeats each counterfactual in turn. The Singhvi report cites the bond issues as the constituency's proof of loyalty while recommending the convention that would honour the subscribers. The OCI statute's parliamentary history runs through the convention's demands. The stadium events announce legislation to the enrolled membership and harvest the applause for domestic broadcast in the same evening's news. And the continuity across three governments of opposed ideology, traced in the closing section, disposes of the partisan-furniture hypothesis. What survives is a construction with moving parts that mesh, which is the minimum condition for calling a population a constituency of a state that it does not elect.

## Before the Constituency: The Long Arc from 1947 to 1991

The architecture after 1991 is legible only against the doctrine it replaced. The Nehru government's stated position, repeated by Jawaharlal Nehru in Parliament and by Indian envoys abroad through the 1950s, was that Indians who had settled in Ceylon, Burma, Malaya or East Africa owed their loyalty to the countries that had received them and should seek their political futures there. India would protest gross injustice, as it protested South African racial legislation at the United Nations from 1946 onward, but it would not claim the settler populations as an Indian interest to be bargained over. The doctrine had honourable roots: a nationalist movement that had condemned indenture as a badge of colonial humiliation was reluctant to behave as a mother country with external clients, and the treatment of Indians in Ceylon under the Ceylon Citizenship Act 1948 and the Indian and Pakistani Residents (Citizenship) Act 1949, which left hundreds of thousands of plantation workers stateless, confirmed New Delhi's preference for negotiated repatriation over diaspora politics. The Sirimavo-Shastri Pact of October 1964 divided the stateless population of Indian Tamils between the two countries by quota, treating people as a bilateral ledger entry rather than as a constituency to be courted.

Varadarajan's The Domestic Abroad (2010) gives this phase its sharpest formulation: the emigrant, in the Nehruvian settlement, figured as a figure of suspicion, someone whose departure required explanation and whose wealth abroad attracted a moralising scrutiny at home. [Nehru's foreign policy as domestic politics](/2019/03/04/nehru-foreign-policy-domestic-politics/) had its own domestic uses, but the emigrant was not one of them; the diaspora did not appear in the Congress party's account of the nation it was assembling. The test of the doctrine came in August 1972, when Idi Amin ordered Uganda's Asian population expelled within ninety days. The expelled numbered in the tens of thousands, most of them of Gujarati origin, many holding British passports; India received a minority of the displaced, declined to mount the episode as a national cause, and watched the United Kingdom and Canada absorb the larger share. Historians of the episode, and the expelled families' own memoirs, record New Delhi's restraint as a compound of limited means and doctrinal consistency: a state that had told settlers to belong where they lived could not easily claim them when belonging failed. The expelled population's afterlife supplies the doctrine's long irony. Parminder Bhachu's Twice Migrants (1985) named the pattern the Ugandan and Kenyan Asians carried to Britain and Canada: communities twice displaced, first from India to East Africa and then from East Africa to the West, whose commercial discipline and family capital made them, within a generation, among the most prosperous segments of the entire diaspora. The population India declined to claim in 1972 became, by the 2000s, a population its prime ministers decorated at Pravasi Bharatiya Divas and courted at Wembley, and several of Britain's wealthiest business families of Indian origin trace their route through Kampala and Nairobi. The architecture's honours lists quietly reversed the old doctrine's judgment: the settlers who were told to belong where they lived are claimed, in their grandchildren's generation, as the diaspora's exhibit of success, and the claiming is done by the successor state of the very government that disclaimed them.

The economic wing of the old settlement was the brain-drain argument. Jagdish Bhagwati, then the most prominent Indian economist abroad, proposed with William Dellalfar in 1973 that developed countries tax the incomes of skilled emigrants from poor countries and remit the proceeds as compensation for publicly funded education exported without payment. The proposal, debated at the United Nations and in the pages of the Journal of Development Economics through the 1970s, framed the emigrant professional as a leakage from the national stock. Binod Khadria's The Migration of Knowledge Workers (1999) later documented the scale of the skilled outflow from the Indian Institutes of Technology and the medical colleges, giving the leakage thesis its empirical base. Against this background, the official embrace after 1991 was not a refinement of an existing policy; it was a reversal of the founding doctrine, executed by governments of three different party complexions, which is precisely why the reversal deserves to be read as architecture rather than as mood.

The hinge episode arrived in August 1990, when Iraq invaded Kuwait and roughly 170,000 Indians found themselves inside a war zone. The evacuation that followed, coordinated by the Ministry of External Affairs with Air India and Indian missions in the region, flew more than 110,000 people home over about two months in what was then among the largest civilian airlifts on record, while others crossed into Jordan and Saudi Arabia. The airlift entered Kerala's and Punjab's household memory as proof of what a state could owe its emigrants, and it arrived months before the balance-of-payments crisis of 1991 taught the same state what its emigrants could lend it. The two lessons, protection and finance, between them drafted the charter of the constituency that the next three decades would build.

The depth of the reversal becomes clearer when the emigration being reversed is dated honestly. The old diaspora was made by indenture: between 1834, when the first ships carried Indian labourers to Mauritius, and 1917, when the system was abolished under nationalist pressure, well over a million Indians were transported under contract to sugar colonies from Fiji and Natal to Trinidad, Guyana and Suriname. The girmitiyas, as the Fiji Indians named themselves from the word agreement, founded populations that remained politically exposed long after the contracts expired, and the nationalist movement that Gopal Krishna Gokhale and later Gandhi led against indenture treated their condition as a stain on India's standing in the world. Gandhi himself was the system's most consequential product: the young lawyer who reached Durban in 1893 and stayed twenty-one years built the first Indian political organisation abroad, the Natal Indian Congress of 1894, and returned to Bombay on 9 January 1915 carrying a method of politics learned among emigrants. The date the Singhvi Committee later chose for the diaspora's national day is therefore not a bureaucratic conceit. It marks the one moment in the pre-independence record when the diaspora generated the homeland's politics rather than merely suffering its own, and the committee's historians knew the resonance they were activating.

The post-1947 doctrine failed its later tests in ways the Uganda episode only partly shows. In Fiji, the coups of 1987 led by Sitiveni Rabuka overturned a government in which Indo-Fijians held the decisive share, and India responded with condemnation, trade measures and advocacy at the Commonwealth, pressing a diaspora cause with an energy the Nehru doctrine would have disowned; the episode is an early marker that the doctrine was eroding before the economics of 1991 buried it. In the Gulf, the oil boom after 1973 quietly created the population that would matter most, and the Indian state's instruments there remained those of a labour supplier: the Emigration Act 1983 regulated recruiters, the Protector of Emigrants stamped clearances, and the embassies ran welfare desks for a workforce already numbering in the hundreds of thousands by the 1980s. The two halves of the pre-1991 record thus point in opposite directions: a doctrine that disclaimed the settled diaspora, and a practice that managed the labour diaspora as an export industry. What neither half contained was the idea of the emigrant as a political resource of the Indian state, addressed in the language of honour and organised for effect. That idea waited for a finance ministry in crisis and a prime minister in search of foreign exchange, and its arrival in 1991 is the subject of the feature that follows.

## Feature One: Crisis Finance and the Voluntary Embrace After 1991

The first feature is financial, and its founding instruments were emergency instruments. In October 1991, with foreign exchange reserves reduced to a few weeks of imports, the State Bank of India issued India Development Bonds aimed at Non-Resident Indians and their Overseas Corporate Bodies; the issue raised about 1.6 billion US dollars. The device was repeated at each subsequent moment of external stress. After the Pokhran II tests of May 1998 brought sanctions from the United States and others, the State Bank's Resurgent India Bonds raised about 4.2 billion US dollars from subscribers in some forty countries. In October 2000 the India Millennium Deposits collected about 5.5 billion US dollars. Each issue was priced and marketed as a patriotic act as much as an investment: diaspora subscribers accepted returns at or below what pure market logic dictated, and ministers said so in public, converting a discount into a donation of confidence. Kapur's Diaspora, Development, and Democracy (2010) treats these episodes as the cleanest evidence that the diaspora had become an economic constituency of the state, one whose patriotic premium could be drawn upon when commercial creditors hesitated.

The standing apparatus beneath the emergency issues was the deposit architecture liberalised after 1991: Non-Resident External accounts, Foreign Currency Non-Resident accounts and the repatriable deposit schemes whose balances the Reserve Bank of India has reported, in its published series, as a stock well above one hundred billion US dollars through the second decade of the century. [The post-1991 institutional-political context](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/) explains why this embrace was voluntary on both sides: the reforms of the Rao government dismantled the suspicion regime of the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act era, under which the emigrant's money had been policed as a leakage risk, and replaced it with a soliciting regime in which the emigrant's money was courted as a national reserve. The political content of the feature lies in that substitution. A state that had taxed the idea of departure borrowed from the departed, and said thank you in the vocabulary of kinship.

### How much money do Indians abroad send home in a year?

The World Bank's migration briefs, prepared with the KNOMAD partnership, estimated India's remittance receipts at about 83 billion US dollars in 2020, the largest national total in the world. The Reserve Bank of India counts private transfers on a balance-of-payments basis and reports a lower figure for the same year; the two series measure different channels.

Remittances differ from deposits and bonds in one constituency-relevant way: they arrive household by household, constituency by constituency in the Indian political sense, and their geography writes a map of Indian state politics. The Kerala Migration Survey, conducted by K. C. Zachariah and S. Irudaya Rajan at the Centre for Development Studies in Thiruvananthapuram, estimated in its 2018 round that emigrants from Kerala numbered about 2.1 million and that remittances to the state reached some 85,000 crore rupees in that year, a flow equal to roughly a third of the state's domestic product on the survey's reckoning. Punjab, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar each carry their own survey literatures and their own politics of the absent earner: the household that receives Gulf money in Malappuram or Houston money in Hyderabad votes in Indian elections even though the earner does not, and every Kerala and Punjab party organisation maintains its informal channels to the emigrant associations whose donations and endorsements circulate in the home constituency. The constituency reading insists on this relay: the diaspora's financial weight becomes domestic political weight through the families who remain electors.

The third channel, investment, is the one where the constituency claim needs the most care. Kapur's verdict, echoed by AnnaLee Saxenian's The New Argonauts (2006) on the Silicon Valley engineers who built firms on both sides of the Pacific, is that the diaspora's decisive contribution to Indian investment ran through networks rather than through headline foreign direct investment totals: the founders and executives of the Indus Entrepreneurs network, founded in Silicon Valley in 1992, vouched for Indian software firms in American boardrooms at a moment when India's country risk kept institutional investors away. Direct diaspora investment in Indian industry remained modest beside the Mauritius and Singapore routes of the investment statistics, and honest adjudication records the point against the celebration literature. What the diaspora supplied after 1991 was credibility at the margin where credibility was scarce, and the margin, in 1991 and again in 1998, was where the Indian state's finances were decided.

Two comparisons fix the feature's distinctiveness. The first is with Israel Bonds, the instrument founded in 1951 through which the Israeli state borrowed from its diaspora for infrastructure across seven decades; Indian officials and the Singhvi report alike treated the Israeli precedent as proof that diaspora finance could be institutionalised rather than improvised. The Indian variant differed in one structural respect: India Development Bonds, Resurgent India Bonds and India Millennium Deposits were each issued by the State Bank of India as commercial paper with patriotic marketing, raised their billions in a matter of weeks, and were then retired, so the constituency's financial function stayed episodic until the deposit stock and the remittance flow made it permanent. The second comparison is with the gold the Indian state shipped abroad in 1991 as collateral for emergency loans, the transaction that seared the crisis into public memory. The diaspora bonds of the same autumn belong in the same ledger: a state pledging its metal in London simultaneously asked its emigrants for their savings, and the emigrants' answer, oversubscribed in the telling of the bank's own reports, was the first measurable act of the constituency the architecture would proceed to organise.

The deposit plumbing deserves a paragraph of its own, because it predates the crisis and reveals the embrace's gradual preparation. The Foreign Currency Non-Resident scheme dates from 1975, the Non-Resident External rupee accounts from 1970; both were devices of a control regime, designed to coax emigrant savings into official channels at administered rates and to deny the hawala market its clientele. After 1991 the same plumbing was re-purposed by deregulation: interest rates on the deposits were progressively freed, repatriation was eased, and the accounts were marketed by Indian banks' overseas branches as instruments of connection rather than of capture. The Reserve Bank's published stock of NRI deposits, which crossed one hundred billion US dollars in the middle of the following decade and stayed above that line, is the quietest and largest single proof of the first feature's success. Constituency, in this register, is a balance: tens of billions of dollars held in Indian banks by people who could move them elsewhere in a week, and whose decision to leave them in place constitutes a rolling vote of confidence that finance ministries on three continents have learned to read as political data.

The feature's last great demonstration came in 2013, and it is the episode sceptics of the constituency reading find hardest to answer. When the United States Federal Reserve's taper signals drove capital from emerging markets in the summer of 2013 and the rupee fell toward record lows, the Reserve Bank of India under its new governor, Raghuram Rajan, opened a concessional swap window in September 2013 for Foreign Currency Non-Resident deposits, offering banks a subsidised hedge that translated into premium interest rates for diaspora depositors. The window, open for three months, drew in about 34 billion US dollars, a sum the central bank's own accounts credit with stabilising the currency and rebuilding the reserves; a large share arrived as fresh NRI deposits from the Gulf and North America. No patriotic appeal was printed on the deposit certificates, and none was needed: the architecture had so normalised the diaspora's role as the state's reserve constituency that a central bank could address it through an interest rate and be answered within a quarter. The episode completes the feature's arc from the emergency patriotism of 1991 to the routine financial statecraft of 2013, and it is the strongest single evidence that the first feature built a constituency rather than a mailing list.

A fourth channel completed the feature's repertoire at the decade's turn: organised philanthropy. The Gujarat earthquake of 26 January 2001, which devastated Bhuj and its district, produced the first mass mobilisation of diaspora giving in the architecture's history, with relief funds assembled in Britain, North America and the Gulf within days and diaspora organisations chartering supply flights alongside the official relief effort. The American India Foundation, founded in New York in 2001 in the earthquake's aftermath with the encouragement of President Bill Clinton, institutionalised the channel, channelling diaspora wealth into Indian education, health and livelihood programmes across the following two decades. Philanthropy matters to the constituency reading for a structural reason. Giving at this scale is neither investment nor remittance; it purchases standing. The diaspora donor acquires a recognised place in the moral economy of the home country, schools and hospitals carry donor names from Toronto and Houston into Indian districts, and the Indian state, which regulates foreign contributions to its nonprofits under the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, stands as gatekeeper over the channel, licensing the constituency's generosity while reserving the right to refuse it. The gatekeeping, tightened by amendment in later years, is the philanthropic form of the OCI fence: connection welcomed, sovereignty reserved. The channel's organisational maturity showed in the giving campaigns of the decade's end: Indiaspora's ChaloGive initiative of the 2019 Diwali season coordinated diaspora donations to Indian nonprofits through a single platform, converting a festival's private generosity into a countable public act, and diaspora foundations' published accounts for the pandemic's first year recorded emergency grants raised for relief work on both sides of the ocean, in the emigrants' Indian districts and in their American neighbourhoods alike. Philanthropy on these rails is constituency behaviour in its purest voluntary form, the enrolled membership taxing itself for the collective good and publishing the receipts.

## Feature Two: A Calendar of Belonging: The Singhvi Committee and Pravasi Bharatiya Divas

The second feature gave the constituency a date, a venue and an honours list. In September 2000 the government of Atal Bihari Vajpayee constituted a High Level Committee on the Indian Diaspora under the chairmanship of L. M. Singhvi, jurist, parliamentarian and former High Commissioner to the United Kingdom. The committee travelled, took evidence across the emigrant world and organised its inquiry country by country, distinguishing the old diaspora of indenture and colonial settlement in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius and East and South Africa from the new diaspora of post-1965 professional migration to North America and post-1973 labour migration to the Gulf. Singhvi's forwarding letter of 19 December 2001 transmitted the report to the Minister of External Affairs, Jaswant Singh, and Prime Minister Vajpayee released the report publicly at Vigyan Bhavan on 8 January 2002. The report recommended, among much else, the celebration of a Pravasi Bharatiya Divas each year, the institution of a Pravasi Bharatiya Samman Award, a revision of the Person of Indian Origin Card scheme, dual citizenship for persons of Indian origin in specified countries, and a standing institutional machinery for diaspora affairs. The Ministry of External Affairs' own summary of action taken, placed before the Lok Sabha in 2002, recorded the acceptance of the day, the award and the card revision, and the reference of dual citizenship to legislation.

The report's own scale deserves record, because it set the constituency's official dimensions. The committee counted the diaspora at about twenty million persons across more than one hundred countries, organised its evidence in country studies that remain a reference for the population's geography, and framed the population in the vocabulary that governments have used since: a bridge, a resource, an asset whose connection to India required cultivation rather than command. Its dual citizenship recommendation was its boldest item and its clearest defeat. The committee proposed dual nationality for persons of Indian origin in a list of developed countries, with safeguards and without electoral rights. The government of the day, advised that the Constitution's framers had rejected dual citizenship and that the security establishment distrusted the device, delivered instead the OCI status examined in the next feature, a solution the committee's chairman publicly accepted as a beginning while diaspora organisations in North America recorded their disappointment in the convention proceedings of the following years. The episode is the architecture in miniature: a constituency organised enough to demand a status, a state willing to invent one, and a negotiation between the demand and the state's reservation of sovereignty that produced a card rather than a passport. Critics within the scholarly literature add the distributional point that the report's imagination ran to the diaspora of the West, its country studies and its citizenship proposal weighted toward the settled and the prosperous, while the Gulf's contract workers appeared in its pages chiefly as a welfare problem, a weighting the architecture inherited and the protection stack only partly corrected.

### Why does Pravasi Bharatiya Divas fall on 9 January?

The Singhvi Committee recommended 9 January because Mohandas Gandhi landed at Bombay from South Africa on 9 January 1915, and the date casts the emigrant's return as the founding gesture of the relationship. The first convention opened on that date in 2003 at New Delhi, and every later edition has kept the January anchor even as venues rotate.

The first Pravasi Bharatiya Divas ran from 9 to 11 January 2003 in New Delhi, inaugurated by Vajpayee, with delegates drawn from more than fifty countries and the first Pravasi Bharatiya Samman Awards conferred on diaspora figures in public life, science and commerce. The convention's design encoded the constituency thesis in ritual form: the emigrant was seated not as a supplicant at a consular window but as a guest of the republic, addressed by its prime minister and its ministers, and invited to inspect the investment climate of the states whose chief ministers competed for the delegates' attention in plenary sessions. Successive editions rotated through Mumbai, Hyderabad, Chennai, Jaipur, Kochi, Ahmedabad and Bengaluru, and the Gujarat edition of January 2015, held at Mahatma Mandir in Gandhinagar in the centenary year of Gandhi's return, announced the conversion of the convention from an annual to a biennial rhythm, with smaller regional editions in the intervening years. The Varanasi edition of 21 to 23 January 2019, its dates arranged to let delegates proceed to the Kumbh at Prayagraj, hosted the Prime Minister of Mauritius, Pravind Jugnauth, as chief guest, a choice that itself stated the constituency's map: a person of Indian origin holding the highest office of another state, received in India as kin.

The institutional machinery the Singhvi report demanded arrived in two stages. In May 2004 the incoming United Progressive Alliance government created a Ministry of Non-Resident Indians' Affairs, renamed within months the Ministry of Overseas Indian Affairs, with Jagdish Tytler as its first minister and Vayalar Ravi holding the portfolio for most of the decade after 2006. The ministry ran the conventions, the Know India Programme for diaspora youth from 2004, the Scholarship Programme for Diaspora Children, and the protection machinery for Gulf workers described below. In January 2016 the Modi government merged the ministry back into the Ministry of External Affairs, a decision defended by External Affairs Minister Sushma Swaraj, who held both portfolios, on the ground that diaspora work had become core diplomatic work rather than a welfare annex. Critics inside Parliament, and commentators in the diaspora press, read the merger as a downgrading; the subsequent Pravasi Bharatiya Kendra, a dedicated convention and research building in New Delhi inaugurated on 2 October 2016 and renamed the Sushma Swaraj Bhawan in February 2020, stands as the government's material answer. The adjudication offered here is that the merger, whatever its administrative merits, confirmed the constituency reading against the welfare reading: the diaspora file had migrated from the margins of labour welfare to the centre of foreign policy, which is where the spectacle events of the same years already located it.

The convention's working content, beneath the plenaries, is where the constituency transacted its business. State governments mounted investment sessions in which chief ministers and industry ministers pitched their jurisdictions to diaspora investors in scheduled competition, a format the Gujarat government's parallel Vibrant Gujarat summits from 2003 onward carried to its logical extreme by fusing the diaspora convention with the investment fair. Sector panels on health, education, philanthropy and knowledge networks paired diaspora professionals with Indian counterparts, and the Know India Programme, and later the Bharat Ko Janiye quiz for diaspora youth, addressed the third generation whose connection the architecture most feared losing. From 2007 the ministry carried smaller regional editions abroad, to New York, Singapore and other diaspora capitals, reversing the direction of travel: the state began visiting the constituency in its own cities between the January homecomings. The Samman lists repay reading as political documents. Alongside scientists, physicians and businessmen, the honours repeatedly reached the political class of the old diaspora, the prime ministers and presidents of Mauritius, Guyana, Trinidad and Tobago, Fiji and Suriname whose ancestors had left under indenture, so that the republic which in the 1950s told settlers to belong where they lived learned, fifty years on, to decorate their grandchildren's heads of government as its own distinguished kin.

## Feature Three: Paper Membership: The OCI Settlement and Its Limits

The third feature is legal. Its prehistory is the Person of Indian Origin Card, introduced by the Vajpayee government in 1999 as a purchasable identity document for persons of Indian origin holding foreign passports, offering visa-free travel and a measure of parity with Non-Resident Indians. The Singhvi Committee's central legal demand was larger: dual citizenship for the diaspora of the developed world. The Indian Constitution forbids dual citizenship, Article 9 and the Citizenship Act 1955 together requiring that an Indian who voluntarily acquires a foreign citizenship ceases to be Indian, and the government's legislative answer, the Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2003, therefore built a status deliberately short of citizenship. Sections 7A to 7D, inserted into the Citizenship Act by the 2003 amendment, created the Overseas Citizen of India: a registered status open to persons who had been eligible for Indian citizenship in 1950 or were descendants of such persons, subject to exclusions for those with Pakistani or Bangladeshi connections. Announced by Prime Minister Manmohan Singh at the 2005 convention, OCI registration opened in 2005, and the card that accompanied it became the single most demanded document in the diaspora's practical life.

The OCI settlement deserves precise statement, because public discussion habitually inflates it. The cardholder receives a lifelong, multiple-entry visa for India and exemption from the police reporting that the Foreigners Act regime imposes on other visitors. The cardholder holds parity with Non-Resident Indians in economic, financial and educational fields, with one standing exception, the purchase of agricultural or plantation land. The cardholder receives no political rights whatsoever: no vote, no eligibility for public employment, no access to constitutional office, and no Indian passport. The status is revocable by the central government on grounds stated in section 7D, which include fraud, disaffection toward the Constitution, and, after an amendment carried in 2019, violation of any law in force; the breadth of that cancellation clause, and its bearing on diaspora speech, is examined in [the architectural-substantive question reading](/2020/05/17/citizenship-amendment-act-debate/) of the Citizenship (Amendment) Act debate. OCI is thus best described as a membership of residence and sentiment, meticulously fenced off from the membership of the ballot box. The fence is the point: the Indian state wanted the diaspora's money, voice and presence, and was determined, across governments of opposed ideologies, that none of these should mature into a share of Indian sovereignty.

### Can an OCI cardholder vote in an Indian election?

No. The OCI card confers a lifelong, multiple-entry visa and parity with Non-Resident Indians in most economic and educational matters, and it expressly withholds political rights: no vote, no public employment, no constitutional office. Voting belongs to citizens, and since the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 2010 it includes citizens resident abroad who register and vote in person.

The narrow exception named in that answer marks the constituency's formal frontier. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 2010 inserted section 20A into the 1950 Act, permitting a citizen resident abroad, whose name is entered in the roll of the constituency of his Indian residence, to vote in person at that constituency's polling station. Registration figures reported to Parliament remained in the tens of thousands against a citizen-emigrant population in the millions, and the requirement of physical presence kept the overseas electorate a curiosity rather than a force; a proxy-voting amendment passed the Lok Sabha in 2018 and lapsed with that House. The contrast is structurally eloquent. The state extended the franchise to its absent citizens on paper and made its exercise nearly impracticable, while it extended to foreign citizens of Indian descent a card of real daily utility and no political content. Both halves of the arrangement serve the constituency design: the diaspora is to be bound to India by every tie except the one that would let it outvote the residents.

The architecture's tidying came in 2015. At Madison Square Garden in September 2014, as the next sections record, the Prime Minister promised the merger of the PIO and OCI schemes; an ordinance of 6 January 2015, followed by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2015, folded the PIO card into the OCI card, converted existing PIO cards into OCI cards, and ended the two-tier paper membership. By the end of the decade the Ministry of External Affairs reported OCI registrations in the region of four million, a figure that should be read as the constituency's enrolled membership: four million foreign citizens who had applied, paid and submitted to registration in order to hold a formal bond with a state in whose elections they cannot participate. No reading of the diaspora that ignores this enrolment, neither the sociology of migration nor the journalism of rallies, can explain what the Indian state had built by 2015: a legally defined, self-selected, dues-paying external membership, organised by a ministry, honoured by a national day, and armed with a card.

The enrolment has a shadow statistic that the celebration literature avoids. Each OCI registration presupposes a renunciation: the cardholder is a person who held, or whose parents held, Indian citizenship and exchanged it for another. The Ministry of Home Affairs' figures, placed before Parliament, put the number of Indians acquiring foreign citizenship at roughly 144,000 in 2019 alone, a flow concentrated among the professional emigration to North America, Britain and Australia. The architecture thus grows by converting citizens into cardholders at the very moment it celebrates their attachment, and the constituency's enrolled strength is partly an index of the citizenry's continuing departure. The pandemic year then stress-tested the card's legal content in public. On 13 March 2020 the government suspended the visa-free travel facility attached to OCI cards as part of its border closure, stranding cardholders abroad and separating some from Indian spouses and parents; the facility was restored in stages across the autumn of 2020 after sustained protest from diaspora organisations and litigation threats in Indian courts. The episode is a small parable of the settlement's terms: the card confers a privilege administered by the executive, not a right enforceable against it, and in the emergency the distinction governed. Constituency status, the cardholders learned, is membership at the pleasure of the state that issued the card.

## The Other Diaspora: Gulf Labour, Protection and Evacuation

Every celebratory account of the diaspora constituency meets its limit in the Gulf, and honest adjudication places the limit near the centre of the article. The architecture described so far was designed largely in the image of the North American professional: the bond subscriber, the card applicant, the stadium delegate. The largest bloc of the diaspora fits none of those images. Of the overseas Indian population counted by the Ministry of External Affairs, the greatest single concentration works in the six states of the Gulf Cooperation Council, building towers in Dubai and Doha, staffing hospitals and households in Riyadh and Kuwait City, and returning, after contracts measured in years, to villages in Kerala, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. This labour diaspora predates the architecture: the oil price rise of 1973 opened the construction economies of the Gulf to Indian workers a generation before the Singhvi Committee met, and Kerala's Gulf connection was already reshaping that state's economy and its party competition while New Delhi's official doctrine still treated emigration as leakage. The account of [Kerala's Left and Congress alternation](/2020/01/28/kerala-left-congress-alternation/) carries the domestic half of that story, the half in which Gulf remittances rebuilt the houses, schools and consumption patterns of the state's southern districts and quietly retired the old politics of scarcity.

### How many Indians live and work in the Gulf states?

The Ministry of External Affairs has repeatedly placed the Indian population of the six Gulf Cooperation Council states between eight and nine million, the largest single bloc of the diaspora and the source of roughly half of India's recorded remittances. The United Arab Emirates hosts the largest share, followed by Saudi Arabia and Kuwait.

The critique of the Gulf arrangement, stated at its strongest by the researchers who documented it and by the journalists who worked the labour camps, has three counts. The first is legal exposure: the kafala system of sponsorship, under which a worker's residence and exit depended for decades on a private sponsor, left Indian workers vulnerable to confiscated passports, unpaid wages and blocked departure, a vulnerability the Indian state's own Emigration Act 1983 tried to police through the Protector of Emigrants and the emigration-check-required passport category without reaching inside Gulf jurisdiction. The second count is asymmetry of honour: the Pravasi Bharatiya Samman and the stadium rallies celebrate the diaspora of achievement, while the worker who dies on a Doha construction site enters the record as a compensation file, and the number of Indian deaths in the Gulf states reported to Parliament each year runs into the thousands, most from natural causes by the official account, a framing the workers' advocates contest in individual cases. The third count is political silence: because the Gulf states supply India's crude oil and host its workers, New Delhi's advocacy for its nationals there proceeds by quiet consular pressure rather than public campaign, and the constituency that is courted with rallies in Houston is managed with discretion in Riyadh. Reporters who document abuse in the labour camps guard their informants accordingly, and [the methods journalists use to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) under such conditions belong to the practical infrastructure of this politics.

The state's protection stack, built piecemeal over two decades, is real even if its limits are real too. The Pravasi Bharatiya Bima Yojana, a compulsory insurance cover for emigration-check-required workers dating from 2003 and revised in 2017 to raise the insured sum to ten lakh rupees, attaches a financial floor to the riskiest migration stream. The Indian Community Welfare Fund, established in 2009 in Indian missions in the Gulf and other emigration-check-required countries and financed from consular service charges, pays for emergency repatriation, shelter for distressed household workers, and legal assistance. The eMigrate system, launched in June 2015, moved the clearance of recruitment into an online registry of foreign employers and Indian recruiting agents, and the Madad portal, opened in February 2015, gave workers and their families a tracked grievance channel into the mission network. None of this abolished the kafala asymmetry, and the Qatar labour reforms announced in 2020, which dismantled the exit permit for most workers, owed more to World Cup scrutiny than to Indian pressure. Around the protection stack grew a layer of bilateral labour diplomacy: memoranda of understanding on manpower with the Gulf states, joint working groups on labour, and the agreement signed with Saudi Arabia in January 2014 covering domestic workers, the category least protected by Gulf labour law and most exposed to abuse inside private households. The agreements' enforcement ran through the same missions that administered the welfare fund, and their texts were rarely public in full, a discretion that served the oil relationship and frustrated the workers' advocates in equal measure. The cultural record registers what the diplomacy could not say. The pravasi, the Gulf Malayali, became a stock figure of Kerala's cinema, fiction and popular music across these decades, the man whose earnings built the tiled house and whose absence structured the family's emotional economy, and the figure's familiarity in the home culture is the measure of how completely labour migration had been absorbed into the domestic imagination of at least one Indian state. A constituency that exists in a people's songs before it exists in its statutes is not an invention of the state; the architecture's achievement was to give the prior fact an institutional address. What the stack establishes is a service relationship between the Indian state and its least powerful emigrants, a relationship whose test comes not in conventions but in crises.

The crisis record is where the Gulf diaspora enters national politics as spectacle and obligation together. Operation Raahat in April 2015 lifted about 5,600 people from Yemen as the civil war closed over Sanaa, some 4,640 of them Indians and about 960 nationals of forty-one other countries, by Air India aircraft and Indian naval vessels working out of Djibouti; the operation was led on the ground by the Minister of State for External Affairs, General V. K. Singh, and foreign governments' requests for Indian help with their own nationals gave the episode its constituency charge, since the rescuer state displayed a competence its diaspora could cite abroad. Operation Sankat Mochan followed in July 2016, flying about 150 Indians out of Juba in South Sudan. The Vande Bharat Mission, beginning on 7 May 2020 under the pandemic's travel shutdown, ran the largest repatriation of the series: the Ministry of External Affairs recorded more than two million journeys on its flights and chartered arrangements by the autumn of 2020, a flow composed overwhelmingly of Gulf workers whose contracts the pandemic had cancelled. The constituency reading notes what these operations share with the stadium rallies: each is a staged demonstration, before domestic and foreign audiences, that the Indian state counts its emigrants as its own. That the demonstration matters most to the emigrants least able to attend a rally is the Gulf section's standing rebuke to the architecture's glamour, and the reason the adjudication below refuses to let the celebration case stand unqualified. The Gulf is also where the diaspora question meets [the maritime-architectural-substantive-domestic-political-content framework](/2020/10/29/india-indian-ocean-strategy-explained/): the sea lanes that carry India's oil carry its workers on the return leg, and the western Indian Ocean is a labour corridor before it is a strategy map.

The Gulf file also produced the architecture's most instructive piece of sub-national statecraft. Kerala, the state most completely remade by Gulf money, built its own diaspora machinery ahead of the Union: the Department of Non-Resident Keralites Affairs, NORKA, created in 1996, was the first government department in India devoted to emigrants, and its successor initiatives, from the Pravasi welfare fund to the Loka Kerala Sabha convened in Thiruvananthapuram in January 2018 as a standing assembly of the Malayali diaspora, treated the emigrant population as a constituency of the state government in the literal sense, with legislators, ministers and diaspora delegates debating policy in the assembly complex. When Saudi Arabia's Nitaqat labour nationalisation drive of 2013 threatened the jobs of tens of thousands of Malayali workers, it was Kerala's government that mounted the loudest public response, chartering relief and pressing New Delhi for a negotiated grace period, and the episode displayed the constituency operating through federal layers: the workers' home state claimed them as its own electorate in exile, and the Union's diplomacy followed where a state government's politics had already arrived. The 1990 Kuwait airlift belongs to this lineage too; its most honoured organiser on the ground was not a diplomat but Mathunny Mathews, a Malayali businessman in Kuwait whose network fed and sheltered the stranded, and whose felicitation by Kerala in later years recorded where the protection state's debts were actually owed.

The pandemic converted this federal constituency into an electoral question in the plainest sense. When the Vande Bharat flights began landing Gulf returnees in Kerala in May 2020, the state's politics reorganised itself around their reception: quarantine arrangements, the health minister's daily briefings, opposition charges about the treatment of returnees and the state government's counter-charges about the Union's flight schedules filled the Malayalam press for months, and the returnee figured in the campaign season that followed as a wronged voter with a fresh grievance and a Gulf reference point for judging governments. Pravasi organisations in the Gulf issued their own charters of demands addressed to both governments. The episode completed a circuit the architecture had long implied: the worker who could not vote from Sharjah returned to Kochi as an elector carrying a verdict on two states at once, the one that employed him and the one that fetched him home, and Kerala's parties canvassed him accordingly. Labour migration, administered for decades as a welfare file, had become indistinguishable from constituency politics, which is the Gulf form of the article's general claim.

## The Washington Test: A Constituency Inside Another Country's Politics

The nuclear agreement between India and the United States, negotiated between the joint statement of 18 July 2005 and the congressional approval of October 2008, subjected the constituency thesis to a controlled experiment. The Indian government needed a foreign legislature to change its law: the Hyde Act of December 2006, the 123 Agreement finalised in 2007, and the Nuclear Suppliers Group waiver of 6 September 2008 each required votes or consents in Washington and in the capitals of the supplier states. Inside the United States, the case for the agreement was argued in district after district by Indian American organisations whose members were constituents of the very members of Congress being lobbied. Varadarajan's The Domestic Abroad (2010), the most careful scholarly record of this episode, documents how the Indian embassy and the Indian American organisations worked in tandem: the United States India Political Action Committee, founded in 2002 under Sanjay Puri, organised briefings and fundraisers; business figures of the standing of Swadesh Chatterjee of North Carolina pressed the case in the Senate; and the argument that travelled was a constituency argument, that Indian Americans were model citizens whose ancestral country deserved a strategic exception. Members of Congress said as much on the record during the Hyde Act debates, praising the community in their districts as they explained their votes.

The domestic Indian half of the episode, [the 2008 nuclear deal's domestic crisis](/2020/10/18/2008-nuclear-deal-domestic-crisis/), supplies the control condition. Inside India the agreement nearly destroyed the government that signed it: the Left parties withdrew support in July 2008 over the same text that Indian Americans were defending in Washington, and the trust vote of 22 July 2008 turned on the charge that the government had mortgaged Indian sovereignty to American preference. The constituency reading draws the two halves together. The diaspora's Washington work was possible only because the post-1991 and post-2003 architecture had already taught Indian Americans to see themselves, and to be seen by the Indian state, as a political bridge population; and the ferocity of the Delhi contest shows what the bridge carried, the central foreign policy decision of the decade, contested simultaneously in two democracies by populations that the architecture had taught to regard the decision as theirs. Neither the diaspora-studies framing, which stops at the sociology of the lobbyists, nor the event framing, which prefers a rally to a whip count, captures the mechanism: an organised external population altering the legislative arithmetic of a foreign state in favour of the ancestral state, while the ancestral state's own legislature nearly fell over the same measure. It remains the single strongest exhibit for the constituency reading, and it predates the Modi era entirely, which is why the article's claim is about scale and systematisation after 2014 rather than about invention after 2014.

The infrastructure that made the mobilisation possible had been assembled over the preceding decade. The Congressional Caucus on India and Indian Americans, founded in the House of Representatives in 1993 with Frank Pallone among its founders, gave the population a standing address on Capitol Hill; its Senate counterpart followed in 2004. Indian American political giving had been climbing since the 1990s, and the population's concentration in New Jersey, California, Illinois, New York and Texas placed its donors and voters inside the districts and states where congressional careers are made. Varadarajan's account carries a critical edge that the adjudication must not flatten: she shows the Indian state's hand inside the diaspora's voice, embassy officials cultivating the organisations, talking points travelling from New Delhi to community podiums, so that the lobby's authenticity as an expression of American citizens was partly a construction. The point cuts both ways, and she intends it to. A constituency whose foreign-policy voice is ventriloquised by the ancestral state is still a constituency, indeed it is the purest form of the phenomenon this article names, a population organised to speak in one polity on behalf of another. But the construction carried a price that the Delhi debate of 2008 already hinted at and later controversies would collect: each public success of the Washington lobby taught American politics to ask whose interests Indian American advocacy served, a question the population's own pluralism, its party divisions, its regional and religious variety, its second generation's distance from New Delhi's scripts, makes harder to answer with every passing cohort. The constituency, in Washington as in Houston, is an achievement of organisation laid over a population that never speaks with one voice unless an organisation lends it one.

## Feature Four: The Stadium Circuit from 2014 to 2023

The fourth feature is the one the world's cameras recorded. On 28 September 2014, four months after taking office, Prime Minister Narendra Modi addressed the Indian American population at Madison Square Garden in New York, in an arena filled with about 18,000 people and organised by the Indian American Community Foundation, a vehicle created for the occasion by diaspora activists. The event's form was itself the message: a foreign head of government received in the register of an American rock concert, with members of the United States Congress in attendance, while giant screens carried the proceedings to a crowd in Times Square. The substance matched the form. Modi used the address to deliver policy to the constituency directly: the merger of the PIO and OCI schemes, lifelong visas for PIO cardholders, and eased visa arrangements for American tourists, announcements that the January 2015 ordinance then executed. A prime minister had converted a diaspora rally into a legislative occasion, and the constituency had been addressed, for the first time, as an audience entitled to policy rather than to sentiment.

### What did Narendra Modi announce at Madison Square Garden?

Speaking at Madison Square Garden on 28 September 2014, Narendra Modi announced that the PIO and OCI schemes would merge, that PIO cardholders would hold lifelong visas, and that visa procedures for American visitors would ease. The announcements converted a celebration rally into a policy occasion: the crowd heard concrete changes to the legal architecture of belonging.

The circuit that followed systematised the form. At Wembley Stadium in London on 13 November 2015, before a crowd reported at about 60,000 and assembled by the Europe India Forum, Modi was introduced by Prime Minister David Cameron, who attempted a line of Hindi for the audience and presented his guest as a partner in a renewed relationship; the spectacle of a British prime minister warming up a diaspora crowd for an Indian prime minister inverted the colonial geometry in a way the organisers fully intended. On 22 September 2019 at the NRG Stadium in Houston, the Howdy Modi event organised by the Texas India Forum drew more than 50,000 people and the attendance of President Donald Trump, who shared the stage, addressed the crowd and joined Modi in a lap of the stadium floor; Modi's endorsement phrase for Trump's re-election, delivered in Hindi to the cameras, and his invocation of the Article 370 decision to applause, carried the event's content back into Indian domestic argument within the hour. The staging repaid close reading. Modi told the Houston crowd that India had bid farewell to Article 370 and asked the audience to give a standing ovation to the Indian Parliament's decision, converting a foreign crowd's applause into a ratification ceremony for a domestic constitutional act performed for Indian cameras; the phrase he used for Trump, an adaptation of his own 2014 election slogan, performed the reciprocal service for the host. The two leaders entered holding hands raised, walked the stadium floor together, and addressed each other's publics over the heads of the assembled press, a grammar of mutual endorsement that no communique could have phrased as efficiently. American commentary divided between those who read the event as a foreign leader campaigning inside an American election and those who read it as the American president courting the fastest-growing electorate in the Texas suburbs; both readings conceded the constituency's existence, which was the event's purpose. On 24 February 2020 the direction of travel reversed: Trump came to India, and the Namaste Trump rally at the Motera stadium in Ahmedabad placed a crowd estimated at about 100,000 in front of the visiting president, with the diaspora's applause function performed by a domestic crowd for an American guest, the two governments trading stadiums as instruments of mutual legitimation.

The circuit's last documented station before the record closes belongs to June 2023, and it corrects the escalation story in an instructive direction. During Modi's state visit to Washington, the diaspora event was not a stadium at all: on the evening of 23 June 2023, at the Ronald Reagan Building and International Trade Center, the United States India Community Foundation hosted an invitation-only gathering of about one thousand diaspora leaders, at which the Prime Minister spoke on the role of the diaspora in India's growth. The date matters for the archive: the state visit's 22 June belonged to the address to the joint session of Congress and the state dinner at the White House, whose South Lawn welcome ceremony that morning had itself drawn several thousand Indian Americans. The sequence across nine years thus runs from mass rally to curated leadership gathering, and the constituency reading explains the modulation without embarrassment: a constituency that has been enrolled, carded and organised no longer needs to be assembled in a stadium to be counted, and its leadership can be addressed in a hall of one thousand with the cameras carrying the image to the enrolled millions. The same visit carried a second, business-facing diaspora occasion: an address on the partnership's economic promise at the Kennedy Center, hosted by Indiaspora and the US-India Strategic Partnership Forum, the two organisations that between them represent the constituency's corporate stratum in Washington. Indiaspora, founded in 2012 by the philanthropist M. R. Rangaswami as a network of Indian-origin leaders, had by 2023 become a standing fixture of the bilateral calendar, publishing lists of Indian-origin executives and officials that the two governments' speechwriters mined. The pairing of the two June 2023 occasions, the Reagan Building's community address and the Kennedy Center's business forum, displays the constituency's internal division of labour in its mature form: one event for belonging, one for capital, both inside a state visit whose communique recorded defence and technology agreements that the constituency's applause was assembled to bless. Coordination of this circuit ran from the Prime Minister's Office and the Ministry of External Affairs in tandem, an administrative fact illuminated by [the post-2014 PMO architecture context](/2019/08/11/pmo-centralisation-question-india/): the diaspora event became a standard module of the prime ministerial visit, budgeted and rehearsed like the business forum and the state banquet.

The circuit had a prehistory that its organisers never tired of recounting, because it converted the rallies from celebration into vindication. The United States had revoked Modi's visa in 2005 under the International Religious Freedom Act's provisions, after the Gujarat violence of 2002, and for a decade the denial stood as the diaspora's organising grievance: Indian American organisations campaigned against it in Washington, community newspapers tracked every rumour of its review, and the 2013 episode in which the Wharton School's India Economic Forum withdrew its invitation to Modi under faculty and activist pressure taught the organisers that the constituency's opponents commanded institutions too. When the Obama administration received the elected prime minister in September 2014, the Madison Square Garden rally staged the reversal before the constituency that had campaigned for it, and the Sydney gathering that followed on 17 November 2014, filling the Olympic Park arena with some 20,000 members of the Australian Indian population, extended the vindication circuit to a second continent within eight weeks. The Toronto address of April 2015 and the San Jose event of September 2015 repeated the form on the Pacific coast. Each station ran on the same organisational fuel: local community foundations incorporated for the purpose, volunteer armies in the thousands, waiting lists for seats, and a prime ministerial programme timed to the arena's clock rather than to the protocol of a state visit.

The rallies also generated their counter-record, which honest adjudication enters without discount. Outside Madison Square Garden in 2014, and again outside Wembley in 2015 and the NRG Stadium in 2019, diaspora opponents of the government, Kashmiri, Sikh, Muslim and left organisations among them, mounted protests that the cameras mostly declined to carry into India. In Houston the protests drew several thousand participants by the organisers' count and centred on the Kashmir lockdown then in force and the citizenship legislation moving through Parliament; a petition campaign against the visit ran in parallel in American media. The constituency reading absorbs the counter-record as evidence rather than embarrassment. A population that fills a stadium for a prime minister and fills the pavement outside against him is behaving exactly as a constituency behaves, divided, organised and worth courting, and the government's investment in the rallies is unintelligible unless the pavement outside them is also in view. What the broadcast images inside India edited out, the constituency's own politics kept in the frame for anyone who attended in person.

What the stadium circuit added to the older architecture was visibility as a political currency convertible in three markets at once. In the host country, a filled stadium priced the community's organisational strength for local politicians, several of whom attended precisely to be seen attending. In India, the broadcast images of foreign crowds chanting for an Indian prime minister entered election-season argument as evidence about the government's standing in the world, a transfer examined in the next section. In the constituency itself, attendance functioned as a rite of enrolment more binding than the OCI card, because it was performed before neighbours and cameras. The under-cited archive for this feature is the coordination correspondence of the Ministry of External Affairs' diaspora divisions and the organising committees' records, preserved in the ministry's files and in the community foundations' own papers; when historians work that archive, the constituency's construction will be traceable email by email, and the reading offered here stands or falls by what it shows.

One further transformation, visible across the same years, changed the constituency's weight in its host polity and therefore in New Delhi's calculations. The Indian American population became an electorate in its own right, courted by both American parties on calculations that American strategists published openly: a population of more than four million by the census estimates of the late 2010s, concentrated in swing suburbs of Texas, Georgia, Pennsylvania and Arizona, with the highest household incomes of any ethnic group in the American statistics. The group's arrival in American institutions ran on every track at once. Indian-born or Indian-descended executives held the chief executive's office at Microsoft from 2014, at Google from 2015 and at IBM from 2020, beside their counterparts at Adobe, Mastercard and a lengthening list of Fortune 500 firms. In Congress, the informal Samosa Caucus of Indian American members counted four representatives in the House after the 2018 elections, Ami Bera, Pramila Jayapal, Ro Khanna and Raja Krishnamoorthi, and Kamala Harris, the California senator whose mother came from Tamil Nadu, stood as the Democratic candidate for vice president in the election of 3 November 2020. The Indian state's interest in this ascent is the constituency's mature form: New Delhi no longer addressed only emigrants nostalgic for India, but a population embedded in the decision structures of its principal partner, whose goodwill the stadium circuit was designed to keep warm and whose presence in American politics raised the price any American administration paid for friction with India. The Howdy Modi stage-sharing of 2019, read against this demography, was not a favour granted to a visitor; it was an American president's own constituency arithmetic performed in public.

The constituency also built media of its own, and the media record supplies an independent gauge of its maturation. India Abroad, the New York weekly founded in 1970 by Gopal Raju, served as the Indian American population's paper of record for half a century, carrying the community's arguments with Washington and with New Delhi in its columns; its closure in 2020, announced as the pandemic broke the economics of ethnic print, marked the end of the constituency's first media age. The second age was already running on other rails: diaspora television channels broadcasting Indian politics into American and British living rooms in a dozen languages, community radio in Toronto and Houston, and the prime ministerial digital apparatus itself, the social media accounts and the NaMo application through which the Indian state's messaging reached diaspora telephones without passing through any editor. The stadium circuit and the digital circuit were one system: the rally supplied the images, the diaspora media supplied the commentary, and the family messaging groups supplied the distribution into India. A population that possesses its own media no longer needs the home country's newspapers to know what it thinks, and the Indian government's communications planners, who brief diaspora outlets as a recognised beat, conduct themselves accordingly.

## Feature Five: The Constituency at Home: Votes, Volunteers and Cultural Politics

The fifth feature is the one the brief for this article states as the post-2014 positioning of the diaspora as domestic political content, and it is best documented in the traffic running from the diaspora back into Indian elections. The Bharatiya Janata Party built the densest machinery for this traffic. Its overseas arm, the Overseas Friends of BJP, maintained chapters across North America, Europe and the Gulf; party figures including Vijay Chauthaiwale, who took charge of the party's foreign affairs department in 2014, coordinated diaspora campaigning as a recognised portfolio. During the 2014 and 2019 general elections, diaspora volunteers placed tens of thousands of phone calls into Indian constituencies, funded newspaper and hoarding campaigns through family networks, and travelled to India to canvass in person, a repertoire documented by journalists who followed the volunteer caravans of 2014 and by the party's own publicity. [The integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) situates this traffic inside the larger renegotiation of the Modi era, in which the diaspora figured as the external jury of a national story being retold at home. [The post-2019 election political-architectural context](/2019/08/16/2019-election-verdict-explained/) records the verdict toward which the traffic ran: in 2019 the stadium images, the Houston endorsement and the campaign calls formed one continuous argument that the world had ratified the government's first term.

The scholarly name for the sentiment this machinery organises is long-distance nationalism, Benedict Anderson's phrase for the politics of emigrants who remain passionately engaged with a homeland whose consequences they do not have to live with. Ingrid Therwath's essays on the Hindu nationalist movement abroad applied the concept to the Vishwa Hindu Parishad and the Hindu Swayamsevak Sangh networks that preceded the BJP's own overseas organisation by decades, tracing how fundraising and ideological formation in the diaspora fed movements inside India from the Ayodhya campaign onward. Therwath's work states, in effect, the complication this article is required to confront: the strongest counter to the constituency reading is that the post-2014 content runs through cultural and religious articulation, the diaspora addressed as Hindus and as bearers of a civilisational story, rather than through the civic and legal architecture of bonds, cards and conventions on which the constituency claim rests. The objection is serious and the article concedes its descriptive half. Modi's diaspora addresses do carry civilisational vocabulary, the overseas Sangh networks are older than the state's architecture, and the Houston crowd's response to the Article 370 passage was not the response of bond subscribers inspecting a yield.

The concession does not dissolve the constituency reading; it locates its mechanism. Cultural articulation, in the post-2014 record, travels through constituency infrastructure: the rally is booked by a community foundation, the volunteers are enrolled by a party's overseas chapter, the applause is harvested by a prime ministerial communications operation and replayed inside Indian campaigns. A civilisational appeal that required no organisation would dissipate as sentiment; the documented record shows organisation at every joint, from the Indian American Community Foundation's incorporation for the Madison Square Garden event to the Texas India Forum's registration of fifty thousand attendees. Anderson's own warning about long-distance nationalism, that its practitioners bear no accountability for its consequences at home, identifies the design flaw the architecture accepts rather than solves: the diaspora volunteer canvasses for outcomes whose costs fall on resident voters. Opposition parties made exactly this charge during the 2014 and 2019 campaigns, complaining of foreign money and foreign voices in Indian contests, and the Election Commission's disclosure framework, built for resident donors, reaches diaspora funding only where it crosses into the regulated channels of Indian law. The adjudication registers the charge as the constituency's democratic deficit, priced into the reading rather than wished away.

The mechanics of the traffic deserve the concreteness the scholarly literature sometimes skips. In the 2014 campaign, the BJP's overseas organisation and allied volunteer networks ran call centres in the United States whose volunteers worked from voter lists in target constituencies, telephoning households in Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh in the callers' own languages; the party's publicly stated target was to reach voters through the one channel Indian campaign regulation barely touched, the family telephone call from a son or brother in New Jersey. Volunteers funded their own travel to join constituency teams during the polling weeks, and the party's 2019 campaign repeated the system at greater scale, adding coordinated messaging groups that relayed campaign material to family networks in India within minutes of its release in Delhi. The Indian state's regulatory apparatus watched this traffic from the side: the Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act governs institutional donations, and the Representation of the People Act governs campaign expenditure, but neither instrument was written for a politics in which the marginal campaigner lives in Houston and the marginal donor is his cousin. The gap is not an oversight the constituency regrets. It is the structural advantage the fifth feature exists to exploit, and any future regulation of diaspora campaigning will have to begin by admitting that the constituency's domestic weight is real enough to be worth regulating.

## The Critique at Its Strongest: Brain Drain, Spectacle and the Worker Left Outside

The celebration case, assembled at its strongest, runs as follows. The diaspora is the largest and richest emigrant population in the world; its remittances exceed the foreign direct investment India receives in most years; its executives sit inside the commanding firms of the American technology industry; its lobbyists delivered a nuclear exception that eluded Indian diplomacy for three decades; and its applause, organised at scale, signals to host governments that India has friends inside their own electorates. On this account the architecture merely recognised a fact of power, and the Singhvi Committee's own phrase for the diaspora, a national resource, understates what the resource yielded. Governments that failed to build the architecture, the celebration case concludes, would have been negligent stewards of an asset lying idle.

The critique at its strongest begins with demography and ends with class. The brain-drain count, documented by Khadria for the knowledge workers and extended by public-health researchers to the tens of thousands of Indian-trained doctors and nurses practising abroad, holds that the sending country subsidises the receiving countries' skill stocks, and that celebratory policy converts a haemorrhage into a pageant. Kapur's answer, the most rigorous the celebration side possesses, is that the flows run both ways: remittances dwarf the education subsidy, diaspora networks lowered the cost of India's software entry into world markets, and the prospect of emigration raised the private return to education inside India, expanding the very institutions the drain was said to empty. Khadria's own later formulation, the diaspora option, concedes a version of the point: skilled emigration can be governed as circulation rather than loss. The adjudication accepts the economic answer for the professional diaspora while noting its silence about the Gulf, where circulation is compulsory, timed by contract, and stripped of the settlement rights that make the North American case benign.

The instrumentalisation count is sharper. Scholars of the post-2014 period, and the diaspora's own dissident organisations in the United States and Britain, have argued that the architecture treats emigrants as an instrument: a claque for the visiting prime minister, a fundraising base for one party's campaigns, a lobby on call for the government's positions, and a population whose internal minorities, Muslim, Sikh, Christian and Dalit Indians abroad, find the civilisational script of the rallies written without them. The documented protests that accompanied the Houston and London events, organised by South Asian diaspora groups opposed to the government's Kashmir and citizenship policies, are part of the record and are cited here as such: the constituency contains its own opposition, which is in fact a property of constituencies. The instrumentalisation charge nonetheless identifies a genuine asymmetry in the post-2014 phase, the fusion of state architecture with one party's overseas machinery, such that the line between the Indian state's diaspora and the BJP's diaspora blurred in the person of the organisers and the content of the events. A constituency courted by the state on behalf of a party is a constitutional impropriety in embryo, and the scholarly record, from Therwath's early warnings to the post-2019 critical literature, keeps the point standing.

A third vocabulary competes for the same material and should be answered on the record: soft power. Shashi Tharoor's Pax Indica (2012) gave the celebration case its most literate form, presenting the diaspora beside Indian cinema, cuisine and yoga as an asset of attraction that no ministry had to build because the emigrants carried it in their lives. The constituency reading answers that attraction without organisation is weather, not climate. The diaspora's restaurants and festivals existed in 1975, when they purchased India no bonds, no lobby and no leverage; what changed between 1975 and 2015 was not the supply of Indian culture abroad but the construction of instruments that converted diffuse goodwill into deposits, registrations, whip counts and rally crowds. Soft power names the constituency's fuel and mistakes it for the engine. The point matters for policy rather than for taxonomy: a government that believes the diaspora works by attraction will fund festivals, while a government that understands the constituency will maintain registries, welfare funds, evacuation fleets and campaign machinery, and the Indian record after 1991 shows each successive government, whatever its rhetoric, choosing the second list. The constituency reading survives the charge because it never claimed the architecture was innocent; it claimed the architecture was effective, and effectiveness in the service of a party is a fact about Indian politics, not a refutation of the framework that names it.

The constituency's internal opposition reached its own organisational maturity in the winter of 2019 and 2020, and the record of those months disciplines any temptation to read the architecture as a one-party enclosure of the diaspora. When the Citizenship (Amendment) Act passed Parliament in December 2019, diaspora protests assembled outside Indian missions and on university campuses across the United States, Britain, Canada, Germany and Australia, organised substantially by Indian students and professionals abroad and coordinated with the protest sites inside India whose movement politics the series treats elsewhere. The organisers were, by every demographic marker, members of the constituency the architecture had enrolled: OCI applicants, PBD's target generation, the professional stream of the new diaspora. Their claim on the diaspora's name was exactly as good as the rally crowds', and their existence demonstrates the constituency reading better than the rallies do, since a constituency is defined by the contest to speak for it. The harder case stands further from New Delhi's script altogether. The Khalistan referendum campaign conducted from 2020 by Sikhs for Justice in Canada, the United States, Britain and Australia sought to enrol a segment of the Sikh diaspora in a secessionist project directed against the Indian state, reviving the insurgency-era linkage between diaspora fundraising and homeland violence that the Indian security literature of the 1980s and 1990s had documented. The Indian state's response, bans, designations and sustained diplomatic pressure on host governments, treats that segment of the diaspora as a constituency too, an adversarial one, whose organisation must be answered with counter-organisation. Foreign policy constituency, the full record insists, is a field of contest, and the architecture of 1991 to 2023 is best understood as the Indian state's bid to hold the field against rivals for the diaspora's allegiance, including rivals drawn from the diaspora itself.

The spectacle's arithmetic deserves the same scrutiny as its politics, because the numbers were themselves political claims. Wembley's reported 60,000 filled two-thirds of a 90,000-seat stadium whose upper tiers the television framing discreetly avoided; the Ahmedabad rally's crowd was claimed by its organisers at figures running above the rebuilt Motera stadium's seated capacity, a discrepancy the Indian press noted at the time; and the Houston figure of 50,000 rested on the organising committee's registration count rather than on any independent turnstile audit. Attendance at a free, registered, transport-assisted rally measures organisational capacity more faithfully than spontaneous enthusiasm, and the constituency reading, which is a claim about organisation, has no need to inflate the numbers beyond that. What the figures establish, even discounted, is a mobilisation capacity without parallel in the history of diaspora politics anywhere: no other emigrant population has repeatedly filled the largest arenas of three continents for the leader of its ancestral state, and no other ancestral state has built its visit architecture around the fact. The sceptic who discounts every crowd by a third is left with the largest diaspora rallies ever staged, discounted.

## The Five-Feature Map

The article's findable artifact, the Five-Feature Map of the Diaspora Constituency, assembles the adjudication into a citable form. Read down the table, the features ascend from money to ritual to law to spectacle to electoral content; read across it, each feature names the instrument that made the constituency operable and the function the instrument performed. The map is the constituency reading in miniature, and it is offered as the reference other accounts of Indian diaspora politics will have to answer.

| Feature | Founding instrument or event | Date | Constituency function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Voluntary political-economic content | India Development Bonds, Resurgent India Bonds and India Millennium Deposits | 1991, 1998 and 2000 | Converts emigrant savings into state finance at moments of external stress |
| Pravasi Bharatiya Divas | First convention at New Delhi, on the Singhvi Committee's recommendation | 9 January 2003 | Gives the constituency a calendar, a venue and an honours list |
| OCI architecture | Citizenship (Amendment) Act and the opening of OCI registration | 2003 and 2005 | Converts descent into a durable legal status short of citizenship |
| Stadium events | Madison Square Garden, Wembley, Houston, Ahmedabad and Washington gatherings | 2014 to 2023 | Stages the constituency as visible political mass for three audiences |
| Domestic-political content | Diaspora mobilisation inside Indian election campaigns | 2014 and 2019 campaigns | Returns the constituency's energy to contests inside India |

The map also states, by its silences, the reading's limits. No row captures the Gulf worker's contract, the expelled Ugandan shopkeeper of 1972, or the doctor whose departure a district hospital still feels; the architecture's features are the features the state built, and a constituency defined by its instruments will always describe the governed relationship better than the lived one. The adjudication therefore closes with a bounded claim. Against the diaspora-studies framing, the constituency reading explains the state's behaviour, the budgets, the statutes and the stadiums, which the sociology of migration treats as backdrop. Against the event framing, it explains why the rallies worked, because enrolment preceded spectacle and law outlasted applause. The diaspora became a foreign policy constituency because five instruments made it one, and the instruments, dated and documented, remain open to inspection by any reader who doubts the conversion.

The durability test returns the strongest verdict available to the reading. The architecture was built by a BJP-led government between 1998 and 2004, which commissioned the Singhvi report, founded the convention and drafted the OCI statute; it was operated and extended for a decade by Congress-led governments, which created the diaspora ministry, opened OCI registration, won the Washington campaign and enfranchised the absent citizen on paper; and it was intensified after 2014 by a second BJP government whose prime minister made the diaspora a centrepiece of his statecraft. Three dispensations of opposed ideology handled the same instruments and none dismantled any of them, a cross-party continuity that distinguishes architecture from programme and constituency from clientele. The disagreements the record sustains are disagreements within the constituency form: whether the diaspora's honour should outrun its protection, whether its applause should serve a party or a state, whether its card should harden toward the citizenship its holders were promised in spirit in 2001. Those are the questions the next phase of the politics will argue, on terrain the five features built. The diaspora-studies scholar will go on counting the departures, and the cameras will go on finding the rallies. The constituency reading holds that the departures and the rallies are the surface of a construction, dated, documented and still under negotiation, by which the Indian republic acquired a population beyond its borders that its governments must answer to, and that answers, in money, in lobbying, in applause and in protest, with a voice no Indian election returns and no Indian foreign policy can any longer afford to ignore.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is OCI?

OCI, the Overseas Citizen of India status, is a registered legal status created by sections 7A to 7D of the Citizenship Act, inserted by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2003 and opened for registration in 2005. It is available to persons who were eligible for Indian citizenship in 1950, or their descendants, who hold foreign citizenship, with exclusions covering those connected to Pakistan or Bangladesh. The status grants a lifelong multiple-entry visa, exemption from police reporting, and parity with Non-Resident Indians in economic, financial and educational matters except the purchase of agricultural land. It grants no vote, no passport and no public employment, and it is revocable by the central government on stated grounds. OCI is therefore a durable membership of residence and sentiment, deliberately short of citizenship.

### Q: When was Pravasi Bharatiya Divas instituted?

Pravasi Bharatiya Divas was instituted in 2003, and the first convention ran from 9 to 11 January 2003 in New Delhi, inaugurated by Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee. The date of 9 January was chosen on the recommendation of the L. M. Singhvi Committee to commemorate Mohandas Gandhi's return to India from South Africa on 9 January 1915. The convention brought together delegates from more than fifty countries, and the first Pravasi Bharatiya Samman Awards were conferred at the inaugural edition. From 2015 the convention moved to a biennial rhythm, with editions at Gandhinagar in 2015, Bengaluru in 2017 and Varanasi in 2019, while smaller regional and thematic events filled the intervening years.

### Q: What was Madison Square Garden 28 September 2014?

The Madison Square Garden event of 28 September 2014 was the first stadium-scale diaspora rally of the Modi era, held four months after Narendra Modi took office as Prime Minister. About 18,000 members of the Indian American population filled the New York arena, in an event organised by the Indian American Community Foundation, with members of the United States Congress in attendance and screens relaying the proceedings to Times Square. Modi used the address to announce policy directly to the constituency: the merger of the PIO and OCI schemes, lifelong visas for PIO cardholders, and eased visa arrangements for American tourists, measures executed by ordinance in January 2015. The event fixed the template of the stadium circuit that followed.

### Q: What is the Howdy Modi 2019 event?

Howdy Modi was the diaspora rally held on 22 September 2019 at the NRG Stadium in Houston, Texas, organised by the Texas India Forum. More than 50,000 members of the Indian American population attended, and President Donald Trump shared the stage with Prime Minister Narendra Modi, addressed the crowd and joined him in a lap of the stadium floor. Modi's remarks included a phrase endorsing Trump's re-election and an invocation of the Article 370 decision that drew sustained applause, carrying the event's content into Indian domestic argument. The attendance of a sitting American president at another country's diaspora rally was without precedent, and the event is treated in this article as the stadium circuit's peak of mutual legitimation between the two governments.

### Q: How is the diaspora a foreign-policy constituency?

The diaspora operates as a foreign-policy constituency because the Indian state solicits its resources and factors its reactions into external decisions, even though it casts no votes in India. Its money arrives as remittances, deposits and crisis bonds that governments have drawn upon at moments of stress since 1991. Its organisations lobby foreign legislatures, most consequentially in Washington between 2005 and 2008, when Indian American advocacy helped carry the civil nuclear agreement through the United States Congress. Its applause, organised in stadium events from 2014, signals to host governments the electoral weight of their Indian-origin citizens. And its volunteers and funds flow back into Indian election campaigns, so external mobilisation converts into domestic political effect, which is the defining circuit of a constituency.

### Q: What is the L.M. Singhvi Committee on Diaspora?

The L. M. Singhvi Committee, formally the High Level Committee on the Indian Diaspora, was constituted by the Government of India in September 2000 under the chairmanship of L. M. Singhvi, jurist, parliamentarian and former High Commissioner to the United Kingdom. The committee surveyed the diaspora country by country, distinguishing the old diaspora of indenture and colonial settlement from the new diaspora of professional and labour migration. Singhvi forwarded the report in December 2001, and Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee released it publicly on 8 January 2002. Its recommendations included an annual Pravasi Bharatiya Divas, the Pravasi Bharatiya Samman Award, revision of the PIO card scheme, dual citizenship for specified countries, and standing machinery for diaspora affairs, most of which governments of both coalitions subsequently implemented.

### Q: How did the OCI architecture change in 2005?

In 2005 the OCI architecture moved from statute to operation. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2003 had inserted the legal provisions, sections 7A to 7D, creating the Overseas Citizen of India status. Prime Minister Manmohan Singh announced the scheme's launch at the Pravasi Bharatiya Divas of January 2005, and registration opened during that year, allowing persons of Indian origin holding foreign passports to apply for the card at Indian missions and through the Foreigners Regional Registration Offices. The 2005 rollout fixed the status's practical content: a lifelong multiple-entry visa pasted into the holder's record, exemption from police reporting, and parity with Non-Resident Indians in most economic and educational fields. The change converted the older PIO card's limited facilities into a permanent, inheritable framework of belonging short of citizenship.

### Q: What role did Indian Americans play in the passage of the India-US nuclear deal?

Indian American organisations lobbied the United States Congress for the civil nuclear agreement between the joint statement of July 2005 and the final congressional approval of October 2008. The United States India Political Action Committee, founded in 2002, organised briefings, testimony and fundraisers member by member, while business figures such as Swadesh Chatterjee pressed the case with senators, and the Indian embassy in Washington worked in open tandem with the organisations. Latha Varadarajan's The Domestic Abroad (2010) documents the campaign as the diaspora's most consequential intervention in a foreign legislature. The argument that travelled was a constituency argument: members of Congress repeatedly cited the standing of Indian Americans in their own districts while explaining their support for the Hyde Act and the 123 Agreement.

### Q: How large are remittances to India, and why do the counts differ?

The World Bank's migration briefs, prepared with the KNOMAD partnership, estimated India's remittance receipts at about 83 billion US dollars in 2020, keeping India at the head of the world table of receiving countries, with the Gulf states and the United States as the largest sources. The Reserve Bank of India reports a lower figure for the same period because it counts private transfers through the balance of payments, a series built from authorised dealers' returns that captures formal banking channels on a different basis. The two counts differ because they are built differently: the World Bank series models bilateral corridors and includes estimates for channels the banking returns miss, while the Reserve Bank series records what its reporting system sees. Scholars cite the pair together and state the basis of each.

### Q: What did the 2015 merger of the PIO card into the OCI card change?

The merger, promised by Prime Minister Narendra Modi at Madison Square Garden in September 2014 and executed by an ordinance of 6 January 2015 followed by the Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2015, abolished the two-tier paper membership of the diaspora. Every Person of Indian Origin card was deemed converted into an OCI card, and PIO cardholders were directed to exchange their documents for OCI cards at Indian missions. The practical gains for the converted holders were substantial: the PIO card's fifteen-year validity and its police reporting requirements gave way to the OCI card's lifelong visa and reporting exemption. The merger also unified the registry, so the state's enrolled external membership stood on a single list, which simplified consular administration and clarified who belonged to the constituency on paper.

### Q: What happened at Wembley Stadium on 13 November 2015?

On 13 November 2015 Prime Minister Narendra Modi addressed a diaspora gathering at Wembley Stadium in London, assembled by the Europe India Forum before a crowd reported at about 60,000, the largest diaspora audience of the stadium circuit. Prime Minister David Cameron introduced Modi, attempted a line of Hindi for the crowd, and presented the visit as the renewal of the relationship between Britain and India. The event's composition carried its own argument: a British prime minister performed the role of host and warm-up speaker for an Indian prime minister addressing British citizens of Indian origin in Britain's national stadium. Coming fourteen months after Madison Square Garden, Wembley confirmed that the 2014 New York event had been a template rather than an occasion, a repeatable instrument of constituency display.

### Q: What was the Namaste Trump event of February 2020?

Namaste Trump was the stadium rally held on 24 February 2020 at the Motera stadium in Ahmedabad, during President Donald Trump's state visit to India. A crowd estimated at about 100,000 filled the rebuilt stadium, the largest cricket ground in the world, after a roadshow from the airport lined by organised crowds. Trump addressed the gathering on the themes of the partnership, and Prime Minister Narendra Modi hosted him in his home state in a deliberate mirror of the Houston event of September 2019. Where Howdy Modi had placed an Indian prime minister before the diaspora on American soil, Namaste Trump placed an American president before a domestic Indian crowd, and the pairing completed the circuit: the two governments had learned to lend each other their crowds.

### Q: How do the Gulf and North American migrations differ within the diaspora?

The two migrations differ in skill, settlement and legal horizon. The North American stream, driven by the United States' 1965 immigration reforms and India's professional education system, consists largely of engineers, doctors and executives who settle permanently, naturalise, and hold the OCI cards, attend the conventions and staff the lobbies described in this article. The Gulf stream, opened by the oil boom of the 1970s, consists largely of contract workers in construction, services and households who cannot settle or naturalise under Gulf law, whose residence turns on renewable work permits, and who return to India at the end of their working lives. The first stream supplies the constituency's voice; the second supplies the larger share of its remittances and nearly all of its protection crises, and diaspora policy is the negotiation between the two.

### Q: What protections does India maintain for its workers in the Gulf?

India's protection machinery for Gulf workers rests on the Emigration Act 1983, which created the Protector of Emigrants offices and the emigration-check-required category of passports for workers travelling to listed countries. The Pravasi Bharatiya Bima Yojana provides compulsory insurance for such workers, with the cover raised to ten lakh rupees in the 2017 revision. The Indian Community Welfare Fund, established in 2009 in missions across the Gulf and financed from consular charges, pays for emergency repatriation, shelter for distressed workers and legal assistance. The eMigrate system of 2015 moved recruitment clearance onto an online registry of foreign employers and Indian agents, and the Madad portal of 2015 gave workers and families a tracked grievance channel. The machinery mitigates the kafala system's asymmetries without reaching inside Gulf jurisdiction.

### Q: What did Operation Raahat and the Vande Bharat Mission demonstrate?

Operation Raahat of April 2015 evacuated about 5,600 people from Yemen as the civil war closed over Sanaa, among them some 4,640 Indians and about 960 nationals of forty-one other countries, using Air India aircraft and naval vessels working from Djibouti. The Vande Bharat Mission, launched on 7 May 2020 under the pandemic shutdown, repatriated Indians on a far larger scale, with the Ministry of External Affairs recording more than two million journeys by the autumn of 2020, most of them Gulf workers whose contracts had collapsed. Together the operations demonstrated the protection half of the constituency bargain: the state that solicits the diaspora's money and applause displays, at moments of danger, a capacity and willingness to bring its emigrants home, and foreign governments' requests for Indian help in Yemen showed the display being credited abroad.

### Q: What is the brain drain critique of diaspora policy?

The brain drain critique holds that emigration transfers publicly subsidised skill from a poor country to rich ones, and that celebratory diaspora policy disguises the transfer as a triumph. Jagdish Bhagwati proposed in the 1970s that the incomes of skilled emigrants be taxed for the sending country's benefit, and Binod Khadria's The Migration of Knowledge Workers (1999) documented the outflow from India's premier technical and medical institutions. Devesh Kapur's answer, in Diaspora, Development, and Democracy (2010), is that return flows of money, networks and ideas, together with the incentive emigration gives to education inside India, outweigh the subsidy loss for the professional streams. The critique retains its force where the Kapur answer is weakest, in public health staffing and in the Gulf, where workers' skills and years are spent under contracts that permit no settlement.

### Q: What happened to the Ministry of Overseas Indian Affairs?

The Ministry of Overseas Indian Affairs was created in May 2004 by the United Progressive Alliance government, first as the Ministry of Non-Resident Indians' Affairs, with Jagdish Tytler as its first minister and Vayalar Ravi holding the portfolio for most of the following decade. It ran the Pravasi Bharatiya Divas conventions, the Know India Programme for diaspora youth, the diaspora scholarship scheme and the protection machinery for emigrant workers. In January 2016 the Modi government merged the ministry into the Ministry of External Affairs, a step defended by Sushma Swaraj, who held both portfolios, on the ground that diaspora work had become central diplomatic work. Parliamentary critics and diaspora commentators read the merger as a downgrading, though the diaspora divisions continued inside the foreign ministry.

### Q: What is the Pravasi Bharatiya Samman?

The Pravasi Bharatiya Samman is the highest honour the Indian state confers on members of the diaspora, instituted on the Singhvi Committee's recommendation and first conferred at the inaugural Pravasi Bharatiya Divas in January 2003. The award is presented by the President of India during the convention and recognises persons of Indian origin and Non-Resident Indians for distinction in fields ranging from public service, medicine, science and business to philanthropy and the promotion of India's standing abroad. Recipients have included heads of government of countries such as Mauritius, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana and Suriname, whose own careers embody the old diaspora's arrival at political power. Within the constituency architecture, the Samman performs the function of an honours list: it ranks the constituency's notables and binds them, by ceremony, to the state that honours them.

### Q: How do overseas organisations such as the Overseas Friends of BJP mobilise for Indian elections?

The Overseas Friends of BJP, the Bharatiya Janata Party's overseas arm, maintains chapters across North America, Europe and the Gulf and coordinates diaspora participation in Indian campaigns as a recognised party portfolio. During the 2014 and 2019 general elections, diaspora volunteers placed large volumes of phone calls into Indian constituencies from call centres and homes abroad, funded publicity through family networks, organised social media campaigns in Indian languages, and travelled to India to canvass in person. Ingrid Therwath's research on Hindu nationalist networks abroad traces the organisational ancestry of this mobilisation to the Vishwa Hindu Parishad and Hindu Swayamsevak Sangh chapters that preceded the party's own machinery. Opposition parties have protested the practice as foreign intervention in Indian contests, a charge that marks the democratic deficit priced into the constituency's design.

### Q: What took place at the Ronald Reagan Building in Washington in June 2023?

On the evening of 23 June 2023, during Prime Minister Narendra Modi's state visit to the United States, the United States India Community Foundation hosted an invitation-only gathering of about one thousand diaspora leaders at the Ronald Reagan Building and International Trade Center in Washington. Modi addressed the gathering on the role of the diaspora in India's growth, in the only community event of the visit. The previous day had belonged to the formal state programme: an address to the joint session of Congress, a state dinner at the White House, and a welcome ceremony on the South Lawn attended by several thousand Indian Americans. The Washington gathering marked a modulation of the stadium form: the constituency's leadership was addressed in a hall, with the cameras carrying the occasion to the enrolled membership beyond it.
