## Thirty public appearances in a whole career

On the evenings when he played at all, it was in a room like this one: a Paris salon with the curtains drawn, a Pleyel grand pushed toward the corner, and eight or ten listeners seated close enough to hear a single note decay into silence. There was no programme and no platform, because the playing was scaled to the furniture, soft enough that conversation could have continued over it and exact enough that nobody attempted conversation. It is the first fact of the Frederic Chopin life and career that the room, and not the hall, was his workplace. The platform career on which other virtuosos built their names barely exists in his record at all.

<!--more-->

![Frederic Chopin life and career, the pianist in the Paris salon world where he taught and played - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-99.webp)

The thesis of this account follows from that room. Chopin built the first major career in Western music that depended on neither public concerts nor institutional employment. He held no court post, no conservatory chair, no chapel appointment, and after his arrival in Paris in 1831 he almost never stood on a public stage. What he had instead was a teaching practice among the wealthiest families in Europe, a set of publishing contracts renewed country by country, and a reputation carried from drawing room to drawing room by the people who had heard him at close quarters. The intimacy listeners hear in the music is not a metaphor laid over that arrangement. It is the direct product of it. A man who plays for eight people in a carpeted room writes differently from a man who must fill a hall, and he earns differently, plans differently and ages differently too.

### How many concerts did Chopin give?

About thirty, by the count Jim Samson assembled from programmes, reviews and letters. The documented public concerts cluster in Warsaw and Vienna near the beginning and in Paris and Britain near the end. Between those clusters lie years in which he played only in private rooms, before listeners who bought no tickets.

The question his life poses, then, is how a pianist becomes world famous without playing in public. The answer is not a mystery of temperament, although temperament played its part, and it is not a single decision taken once. It is a sequence of arrangements, financial, social and medical, each of which closed the concert hall a little further and opened the lesson room a little wider. The fee structure of that lesson room, and the way it shaped the working week, belongs to the middle of this account. First comes the childhood that produced a musician for whom the salon was not a retreat from a career but the only career he was ever trained to imagine: a household in Warsaw where music was a trade practised at home, a father who earned his bread by teaching other people's children, and a boy whose earliest audiences sat in rooms no larger than the ones he would play in as a man.

## Warsaw, the family and a French father

The family into which Fryderyk Franciszek Chopin was born in 1810 was a teaching household before it was anything else. His father, Nicolas Chopin, had been born in 1771 at Marainville in Lorraine and had come to Poland as a sixteen year old in 1787, in the train of the Polish steward of the estate where his father worked as a wheelwright. When his employer's Polish venture collapsed, the young Frenchman stayed. He fought no battles and inherited nothing; what he did was learn Polish, attach himself to aristocratic households as a tutor, and build a life out of the one portable skill he possessed, his native language. In the household of the Skarbek family at Żelazowa Wola he met Justyna Krzyżanowska, a poor relation of the countess who kept house and played the piano and sang. They married in 1806. He went on to teach French at the Warsaw Lyceum, one of the best schools in the city, and later at the military academy of cadets, and the family lived first in rooms attached to the schools where he worked.

This matters to the biography because the son's career repeats the father's pattern at a higher level. Nicolas Chopin was an immigrant professional who converted a cultivated skill into a salary inside other people's houses, and who understood manners, discretion and languages as working tools. The household was bilingual and musical without being bohemian. Justyna played and sang; the children were brought up inside the sound of the piano as a domestic instrument rather than a theatrical one. Fryderyk was the second of four children and the only son. His elder sister Ludwika, born in 1807, would become the person closest to him in adult life, the keeper of his manuscripts after his death and the woman who carried his heart back to Warsaw. Izabela, born in 1811, married a teacher and lived a long Polish life. Emilia, the youngest, born in 1812, died in 1827 at the age of fourteen, and her death was the family's first experience of the chest illness that would shadow the household's memory of the son as well.

Żelazowa Wola, the village some fifty kilometres west of Warsaw where the Skarbek estate stood, was the family's home only briefly. The boy was born in the steward's house there while his father was employed as tutor to the Skarbek children, and within months the family had moved back to Warsaw, where Nicolas's posts were. The city, not the village, was the scene of the childhood: the apartments in the Saxon Palace and later in the Kazimierz Palace, where the Lyceum stood, put the family at the centre of Warsaw's educational world, in buildings that housed schools, lecture rooms and the apartments of professors. A child growing up there heard languages in the stairwell and music through the walls, and understood early that cultivated people earned their livings by being useful in rooms. It is difficult to overstate how completely the son's Paris career, the lessons in aristocratic apartments, the refusal of the platform, the dependence on patrons who were also employers, reproduces the structure of the father's Warsaw life. The scale changed. The arrangement did not.

## The birth-date discrepancy in the record

### When was Chopin actually born?

The record gives two answers and has never been reconciled. The baptismal register at Brochów records a birth on 22 February 1810 and a baptism on 22 April of that year. The family kept 1 March as the birthday, Chopin used 1 March himself, and some early printed accounts pushed the year back to 1809.

The discrepancy is small, nine days inside one year if the register's February date is set against the family's March one, but it opens a window onto the documentary condition of the whole life. The baptism took place two months after the birth, which was unremarkable in a country parish in winter, and the entry was written by a priest working from information brought to him. The family's own tradition was firm enough that the composer celebrated 1 March, wrote the date in his own hand, and was commemorated on it by the people who knew him. Adam Zamoyski, in his biography of Chopin, treats the register as the stronger document and the family date as a mistake that hardened into custom. Other students of the life note that registers were also written carelessly, that the entry names the child with a spelling of his Polish name that the family did not use, and that a document is not made infallible by being official. The honest position, and the one this account takes throughout, is that the birth date is unresolved: 22 February 1810 by the parish record, 1 March 1810 by the family, with the year itself occasionally misstated as 1809 in print during the nineteenth century, probably because a prodigy's age was computed carelessly by writers who had heard the wrong birthday and worked backwards.

A biographer learns from this opening problem how the rest of the life must be handled. Chopin left fewer documents than almost any comparable figure. He wrote letters, many of them vivid, but he kept no diary of his adult years, gave no interviews, published no memoir, and destroyed or lost much of what passed through his hands. The narrative tradition therefore leans heavily on other people's paper: his father's school records, his publishers' accounts, his pupils' notebooks, the memoirs of friends written years later, and one famous biography written by a rival. Where the documents conflict, as they do at the very first fact of the life, the conflict itself is the finding, and an account that irons it out into a single confident date is telling the reader something false about the state of the evidence. That discipline, holding disputed facts open at their documented strength, governs everything that follows here, from the counting of his concerts to the diagnosis of the illness that killed him.


The practical consequence of the muddle surfaced a century later, when the world came to celebrate his centenary. The festival year of 1910 was fixed by the 1810 of the register and the family alike, but the day kept in Warsaw was the family's 1 March, while documents printed in the same season gave 22 February, and the two dates were carved, in good faith, on different plaques. Biographers since have tended to print both and footnote the problem, which is the correct scholarly posture and an unsatisfying one for a reader who wants a birthday. The case is a useful first lesson in the evidence of this life: the documents are sparse enough that a nine day uncertainty at the start is not an embarrassment to be hidden but a measure of the archive, and the archive never gets much denser.
## The childhood performances and local celebrity

The boy's first audiences were exactly the audiences he would play to for the rest of his life: small rooms, well dressed listeners, and hosts who treated a musical child as an ornament of the evening. His first piano teacher, Wojciech Żywny, a Bohemian violinist and pianist settled in Warsaw, began teaching him when he was about six, and by February 1818, at the age of seven, the child had appeared in public at a charity concert and had a polonaise in print, a piece in G minor issued by a Warsaw music seller and dedicated to a countess. That sequence, print before platform, tells the story of the whole career in miniature. The reputation moved through salons and dedications; the concerts were occasional proofs of a fame that existed independently of them.

Warsaw in those years was a city that took its musical children seriously and displayed them. The young Chopin played in the palaces of the Czartoryski, Radziwiłł, Sapieha and Zamoyski families, the great names of the old Commonwealth aristocracy, and the anecdotes that gathered around those evenings were retold for decades: the child who, asked whether the audience had liked his playing, answered by asking what the ladies had said about his collar; the boy who performed before Grand Duke Constantine, the tsar's brother and the effective ruler of the kingdom, and improvised variations that the Grand Duke applauded. Such stories have to be handled as salon currency rather than court record, polished by retelling, but their existence is itself evidence. By the age of ten he was a recognised ornament of Warsaw drawing rooms, addressed in the press as a prodigy, and accustomed from childhood to the social mechanics of the room: arrive, be presented, play briefly, be talked about. The mechanics never changed. Only the city did.

What the childhood did not give him was equally formative. He was not dragged on tour by an impresario father in the manner of the Mozart children a generation earlier, and he was not exhibited across Europe as a travelling wonder. Nicolas Chopin, who knew the economics of households, protected the boy's schooling and his health, and Warsaw opinion, voiced in the press and in the family circle, treated him as a national asset to be educated rather than a commodity to be spent. He went to school at the Lyceum where his father taught, made friends he kept for life, spent summers on the estates of schoolfriends in the Polish countryside, at Szafarnia above all, where the teenage letters home are full of village dances, Jewish weddings, peasant music and jokes. The mazurkas and polonaises that would later carry Poland into every European drawing room were first heard in those summers, not as folklore collected by a scholar but as the working music of dances he watched from the doorway of the barn.


The Warsaw press followed the child with a pride that had a political colouring. The capital's newspapers reported his appearances as evidence of Polish cultivation under the partition, and the reports survive as the first layer of the public record, boosterish, repetitive and precious because so little else from those years was written down. His parents read them with a schoolmaster's caution. There was no question of the continental tour that a professional impresario would have demanded; the Lyceum timetable came first, the summers went to the country estates of friends, and the boy's health, already a subject of family management, was guarded against the exhibition circuit. Warsaw thus gave him the one childhood among the great virtuosos that was neither a business nor a tragedy: a local celebrity's childhood, applauded in drawing rooms, reported in newspapers, and left free to go to school.
## Teachers, the conservatory and what he was taught

Żywny's teaching was old fashioned and, for this pupil, nearly perfect. A pupil of a pupil of the Bach tradition, he fed the boy Bach and the Viennese classics, insisted on a singing line at the keyboard, and declined to force a system on a talent he recognised as original. Chopin remembered him with affection all his life, calling him by a Polish nickname in letters home decades later. When he was twelve, Żywny declared, in effect, that he had nothing more to teach him, and the boy passed without a second piano master of consequence. He never had another piano teacher. Everything he became as a pianist he worked out himself, at the keyboard and in the hearing of other players, which is one reason the playing described by his pupils later sounds so unlike any school of the period: it descends from no method, and it founded one.

Formal training came in composition. From 1826 to 1829 he studied at the Warsaw School of Music, the conservatory attached to the university, under Józef Elsner, a Silesian born composer who had become the central figure of Polish musical institution building: director of the conservatory, a producer of operas in Polish, and the most experienced composition teacher in the country. The wider world of such institutions, in which a conservatory education was becoming the normal gateway to a professional life across Europe, is charted in the series history of [the classical composers and their training](/2010/03/07/history-of-classical-composers/). Elsner's curriculum was thorough and contrapuntal, grounded in thoroughbass, fugue and the classical forms, and he had the wit to see what he had. His annual reports on the young student survive, and the last of them abandons the language of grades for a verdict: a musical genius. That phrase, written by a school director in an official document, is the strongest contemporary certificate the Warsaw years produced, and it was quoted for the rest of the century whenever the question arose of where the prodigy had come from.

The conservatory also taught him what institutional music felt like from inside, and the lesson cut both ways. He heard his own early orchestral works rehearsed and performed by student and theatre orchestras, learned the practical limits of provincial players, and acquired the habits of a professional: copying parts, meeting deadlines for nameday pieces and dedications, writing to order for the salons that fed his reputation. But he held no post at the end of it, applied for none, and showed no inclination toward the kapellmeister's ladder that Elsner's other pupils climbed. When a government scholarship for study abroad was discussed in 1829 and 1830, in reports that named him as the obvious candidate, the money did not materialise. The departure that followed was therefore self financed and self directed, a private venture by a twenty year old with letters of introduction, a small stock of published pieces and a reputation that stopped at the borders of the Russian partition of Poland.


His self education at the keyboard ran in parallel with Elsner's classroom and mattered as much. He practised in short, concentrated spells, telling correspondents in later years that he never played for more than three hours a day, and he learned by improvising for hours in the evenings at the houses of friends, testing harmonies and transitions that the treatises had not named for him. The organist Wilhelm Würfel, a Bohemian musician settled in Warsaw, gave him informal counsel and the run of the organ loft, and the contrapuntal solidity underneath the most rhapsodic of the mature works has its roots in those years of Bach with Żywny and fugue with Elsner. What no teacher gave him was a piano method, and the lack defined the teacher he became: the pupils in Paris were set to discover their hands as he had discovered his, by singing lines, by Bach, and by the refusal of all mechanical routine.
## Polish musical life before he left it

The Warsaw he grew up in was the capital of a kingdom that existed on a map drawn by other powers. The Congress of Vienna had made it a constitutional kingdom under the Russian tsar, with its own army, parliament and university, and through the 1820s that settlement was being steadily hollowed out by the tsar's officials and by Grand Duke Constantine's military regime. Polish cultural life responded by becoming intensely self conscious. The university, the learned societies, the Polish language press and the national theatre were not entertainments laid over politics. They were the places where a political nation that had lost its state rehearsed its continued existence, and music carried a share of that weight. Elsner's operas in Polish, the folk song collections of Oskar Kolberg, the patriotic songs sung in churches and at funerals, all belonged to a culture that understood art as evidence of survival.

Chopin's Warsaw career sat squarely inside that culture. His public concerts of 1830, in March and again in October, at the National Theatre, were national events as much as musical ones: a Polish trained pianist performing his own concertos, works that quoted the rhythms of Polish dances, before an audience that heard in them a statement about what Polish training could produce. The press reviews used the language of national pride as readily as the language of criticism. His circle was the circle of young Warsaw intellectuals and artists: the poet Stefan Witwicki, whose verses he set; the painter Wojciech Stattler; his schoolfriends Tytus Woyciechowski, a landowner's son with estates in the east, and Julian Fontana, the pianist who would later copy his manuscripts in Paris and edit his posthumous works. These friendships matter to the biography because they fixed his loyalties before exile could dilute them. In Paris he would earn his living from French and international pupils, but the emotional constituency he wrote home to, and measured himself against, remained the Warsaw circle of his youth.

It is worth being exact about what he left, because the later legend preferred a simpler story of a genius escaping a province. Warsaw in 1830 had an opera house, a conservatory, music publishers, a musical press and an aristocracy that employed musicians; it was not a backwater, and his formation there was complete and professional. What it could not offer was scale. The city had no market for a pianist composer of the first rank, no publishing industry with European distribution, no salon economy rich enough to support a teacher at the fees Paris would pay, and a political future that everyone in his circle could feel narrowing month by month. The young man who left in November 1830 was not fleeing a void. He was leaving a real musical culture that had made him, in order to find the economic structure his kind of talent required, and the distinction governed his feelings about Poland for the rest of his life: gratitude and homesickness without a trace of condescension, and a refusal, sustained for nineteen years, to turn the journey back into a return.

## The departure of 1830

### Why did Chopin leave Poland?

He left to build a career, not to escape a war. When his carriage crossed the border on 2 November 1830 he was twenty, with letters of introduction to Vienna and Paris and plans for Italy. The November Uprising against Russian rule began on 29 November, after he had gone, and it changed a study journey into an exile.

The farewell was staged by his friends as a piece of Warsaw theatre. At a gathering on the road out of the city, at Wola, Elsner and the conservatory students sang a cantata Elsner had written for the occasion, wishing the traveller glory abroad and a safe return, and his friends gave him a silver cup filled with Polish earth, to be kept as a pledge that wherever he prospered he would not forget the country. The cup, and the instruction attached to it, are documented by the participants and by the family, and the object did its work: he kept it all his life. He travelled with Woyciechowski as far as Vienna, through Kraków and the Austrian lands, playing where he was asked, hearing operas, and writing home the letters of a young professional taking the measure of a bigger world. The plan, as the letters show, was the standard grand tour of a rising musician: Vienna first, to be heard and to publish; then Italy, for the opera and the climate of reputation; then, perhaps, Paris and London. Nothing in the correspondence of those weeks anticipates permanence.

Why did he leave Poland, then, in the fuller sense the question deserves? The immediate answer is opportunity, and it is documented in the scholarship discussions of 1829 and 1830, in which officials who admired him considered and failed to fund a state stipend for foreign study. A private family of teachers could not finance a European launch. He went, therefore, as such young men went, on savings, introductions and hope. But under the opportunity sat a calculation his whole circle shared. The kingdom's autonomy was being dismantled piece by piece; censorship touched the press and the theatre; the university would be closed within two years, though nobody knew the date. His friends were the kind of young men who would be in the uprising when it came, and several of them were. He was not. The question of why he left cannot be separated from the question the next section takes up, of what happened in Warsaw four weeks after his departure, and why the journey of a few months became a sentence of nineteen years. That question opens here and closes in the next section: he left as a careerist, in the honourable sense. He stayed away as an exile, and those are two different biographies wearing the same name.


The mechanics of the leaving repay a moment's attention, because they show how the career was financed at the start. His father assembled the funds from the family's savings; patrons and friends added what they could; a passport was obtained for travel through Austria to Italy; and in the first days of November he played his second Warsaw farewell concert, performing the E minor concerto before an audience that understood it was applauding an export. He travelled by stagecoach through Silesia to Dresden, where he spent some days hearing the opera and being received in musical houses, and reached Vienna towards the end of the month. The whole apparatus, family savings, official passport, letters of introduction, a concerto under the arm, was the standard launching equipment of a young professional of his class, assembled this once for a passenger who would use it to leave his country forever.
## The uprising that began after he had gone

On the night of 29 November 1830, Polish cadets rose against the Russian garrison in Warsaw, and the kingdom was at war. The date is the hinge of the biography, and it must be set precisely against the departure: Chopin had been gone for twenty seven days. He had not fled the uprising, anticipated it in his travel plans, or been sent away to escape it. He was in Vienna, reading the news in stages as the couriers and the Austrian censorship allowed, writing letters home that swing between excitement, pride, homesickness and a gnawing guilt that he was safe while his schoolfriends were enlisting. Woyciechowski, who had travelled with him, turned back to join the Polish army. The composer did not. His father, in letters that combined affection with hard sense, advised him to stay where he was and continue the career; a sickly twenty year old pianist at a barricade served nobody, and the family needed the prospect of his earnings and his fame. That advice, given and taken, closed the loop opened at the departure: the journey had been a career move, and it remained one by the decision of the whole family, even as events converted it into exile.

The conversion was completed by the war's end. In September 1831, travelling from Vienna toward Paris by way of Munich and Stuttgart, he received the news that Warsaw had fallen to the Russian army after a siege, and the diary pages he wrote at Stuttgart, private pages never intended for print, record a young man's fury and grief at a distance he could not cross. The uprising's failure brought the expected settlement: the university and the conservatory closed, the constitution abolished, thousands of Poles exiled or fled, and a great emigration that reassembled Polish political life in Paris. Chopin's position inside that emigration was peculiar from the start. He was its most famous young artist and one of its least political members. He would play at Polish benefit evenings, keep Polish friends, set Polish poets and carry the cup of earth to his deathbed's room, but he never wrote the articles, signed the manifestos or joined the factions that consumed the exile community's leaders, and attempts by Russian diplomacy to draw him home, including feelers about a post as a pianist of the tsar, were declined without negotiation.

Adam Zamoyski's biography is clear eyed about the result: the exile that defines Chopin's reputation, the image of the patriot artist pining for a lost homeland, began as an ordinary professional journey and was hardened into permanence by a war he did not fight in and a settlement he could not accept. That is the revelation the sequence of dates delivers. The romantic version reverses cause and effect, making the departure a flight and the career a consolation. The documents run the other way. The career came first and was pursued single mindedly through the war's duration, in Vienna drawing rooms and Paris salons, while the homeland became unreachable behind him. What exile added was not the work but the weight: every mazurka he published in Paris was heard there, by French listeners and Polish ones alike, as a dispatch from a country that had ceased to exist as a state, and he understood that hearing, and priced it, and resented it, for the rest of his life.

## Vienna and the year of indecision

Vienna was the first test of the world outside Warsaw, and the city failed him as much as he failed it. He arrived at the end of November 1830 with genuine credentials: on an earlier visit in 1829, travelling as a tourist with family friends, he had given two concerts at the Kärntnertortheater, performed his variations on a theme from Don Giovanni to warm reviews, and been received as a prodigy of real promise. The Vienna of 1830 and 1831 was a harder place. The war in Poland made a Polish artist a complicated guest in the Austrian capital, where the authorities watched Polish sympathies closely. The publishers were polite and slow. His letters home complain of doors that opened halfway, of introductions that produced evenings rather than engagements, of a city whose musical life ran on cliques he had no capital to enter. He gave one further public concert, in April 1831, and the eight months of the stay produced no post, no contract and no plan.

The indecision of that year is documented in the letters with unusual honesty. Italy was the stated goal, and Italy receded: the Italian states were in their own ferment of revolts in 1831, money was short, and the war at home made every route uncertain. He considered staying, considered returning, considered London. What he did, in the event, was study the problem the way he would study everything afterwards, by watching how the trade worked. Vienna taught him the cost of the public career at close range: the intrigues of theatre managers, the dependence on orchestras and impresarios, the fragility of a reputation that had to be rewon in each city. It also taught him that his playing, intimate, detailed, built on nuance rather than power, was not the playing that filled Viennese halls, where the reigning virtuosos commanded a louder instrument and a louder manner. When he finally left in July 1831, travelling on a passport endorsed for London by way of Paris, the direction of his life was set by elimination. Paris was not yet a choice so much as the one great capital where the salon, not the hall, decided reputations, and where a Polish artist in 1831 arrived as a figure of public sympathy rather than suspicion.

The Munich concert on the way, a single charity appearance that August, gave him a success to carry into France, and Stuttgart gave him the news of Warsaw's fall and the diary pages already described. He reached the French capital at the end of September 1831, twenty one years old, with his health already unreliable, his funds modest, his German good, his French fluent from childhood, and a portfolio of works that included two concertos the Paris public had never heard. What he did not have was any idea that he would never leave again, or that the city he entered as a staging post would contain the whole of his adult life: the lessons, the contracts, the salons, the love affair, the illness and the grave.

## Arrival in Paris and the emigre community

Paris in the autumn of 1831 was absorbing the great emigration. After the fall of Warsaw, several thousand Poles, soldiers, deputies, aristocrats and artists, made their way west, and the French capital became the political headquarters of a nation without a state. Prince Adam Jerzy Czartoryski, once the tsar's foreign minister and now the uncrowned head of the emigration, held court at the Hôtel Lambert on the Île Saint-Louis, and Polish Paris organised itself around such poles: committees, newspapers, benefit concerts, anniversaries of the uprising kept like feast days. Into this world the young pianist arrived with advantages no other refugee possessed. He was not compromised by any faction, he was personally charming, he spoke the language of the host country like a native because it was his father's, and he had a trade the salons of the Faubourg Saint-Germain were eager to buy.

His first Parisian months were spent securing the ordinary machinery of survival: rooms, a piano, introductions. A letter to Ferdinand Paer, the Italian composer established at the Opéra, opened professional doors. The established pianist Friedrich Kalkbrenner heard him play, praised him extravagantly, and offered to take him as a pupil for three years, an offer that was half compliment and half annexation. Chopin, counselled by friends who saw the trap, declined the pupillage while keeping the friendship, a small masterpiece of the diplomacy his father's household had taught him. In February 1832 he gave his first Paris concert, in the salons of the Pleyel piano firm, playing his F minor concerto and other works with a small orchestra before an audience of the musical establishment. The review that mattered, by François-Joseph Fétis in the Revue musicale, recognised a new kind of pianism rather than a louder kind, and the phrase the evening fixed in the trade was that here was a talent of a wholly original order, unsuited to competing on the virtuosos' ground and under no obligation to try.

The decisive introduction was social rather than musical. Invited to play at a Rothschild evening, he was heard by the ladies of the great banking family and their circle, and the Rothschilds did what the Rothschilds did: they converted admiration into custom. Pupils came from that circle and from the Polish and French aristocracy connected to it, and by the end of his first Paris year the pattern of his life was visible in his appointment book. He would teach in the morning, in his own rooms or in the houses of pupils; he would dine among patrons who were also friends; he would play, late in the evening, in rooms where the audience numbered in single figures and included, in time, the most famous writers, painters and musicians in Europe. The emigre community gave him his emotional home, the Hôtel Lambert its occasions and its politics at one remove, but the living came from a wider Paris, and the double allegiance, Polish by loyalty and Parisian by trade, is the permanent structure of the eighteen years that followed.

## How a pianist without concerts earned a living

### How did Chopin earn his living?

From lessons and from publishing, and from nothing else. At his Paris rate of twenty francs a lesson, four or five lessons on a teaching day outearned a provincial concert tour, without the travel that damaged his health. Publishers in Paris, Leipzig and London paid him separately for the same works. He held no post and drew no salary.

The numbers repay attention because they explain the art. Twenty francs was the highest lesson fee in Paris, roughly the daily wage of a senior clerk multiplied several times over, and he charged it from his mid twenties onward, to pupils who came to his rooms and waited their turn in the season. Jean-Jacques Eigeldinger, whose study of Chopin as a teacher was built from the records and recollections of the pupils themselves, reconstructs the working day: lessons concentrated in the fashionable season, pupils arriving with prepared pieces, the master at a second piano or walking the room, correcting a fingering, demonstrating a phrase at a volume visitors described as barely above a whisper, and then sending the pupil home to work. In a good season the lessons alone produced an income that placed him among the prosperous professional men of Paris, and the income was steady in a way concert fees never were. Consider the contrast the previous cluster in this series sets out: Beethoven, a generation earlier, had been kept in Vienna by an annuity of four thousand florins paid by three princes precisely so that he would not have to earn in the open market, as the account of [the Beethoven life and career](/2011/01/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-life-and-career/) describes. Chopin needed no such instrument. The market itself, organised as lessons, paid him.

The point deserves underlining, because it is the shareable fact at the centre of this biography: Chopin performed in public roughly thirty times in his whole life, and his income came instead from teaching at twenty francs a lesson and from selling the same compositions to publishers in three countries. No salary, no patron's pension, no church or court or conservatory stands behind the most famous piano music of the nineteenth century. The second stream, publishing, is examined in its own sections below; the first, teaching, shaped his days, his health and his compositions, because a man who spends his mornings demonstrating phrases to aristocratic amateurs writes music that behaves well in a room, at close range, under the hands of a gifted amateur, and his published works are full of exactly that double character, drawing room scale carrying concert hall substance.

The income had a third, negative component that defined it as sharply: the money he refused. He refused most concert engagements, including lucrative ones. He refused a formal post more than once, and he refused the tsar's indirect offer of a titled position in St Petersburg, which would have paid him to be a trophy. And the arrangement had costs that the table below makes visible: it depended on his presence, his health and his social credit, all three of which were wasting assets, and it left no institution to catch him when the lessons stopped. But for eighteen years it worked, and it made him, in economic terms, something genuinely new: a composer of the first rank living as a private professional, closer in structure to a fashionable physician or advocate than to any musician of the generations before him.

## The teaching studio and the fee he charged

The lesson was his concert, his office and his shop floor at once. Pupils arrived at his rooms, in the sequence of Paris addresses he occupied through the 1830s and 1840s, each with music prepared; the very wealthy sometimes received him at their own houses, but the rule of the studio was that the pupil travelled, waited, and paid the twenty francs in advance of the teaching season. Eigeldinger's reconstruction, drawn from the annotated scores and memoirs the pupils left, shows a teacher of formidable precision. He worked from the score on the desk, marked fingerings in pencil, demanded that a phrase be sung before it was played, insisted on a supple wrist and an even, quiet hand, and treated rubato not as licence but as a strict accountancy in which time borrowed in one bar was repaid in another. He taught Bach, whom he set before every pupil; he taught the Viennese classics; and he taught his own works last and most severely of all, since those were the pieces the fashionable world wanted to hear his pupils play.

The fee itself is the revelation this section exists to land. Teaching at a high fee, not performance, was the financial basis of his life, and the lesson schedule shaped the music he wrote. Twenty francs a lesson, four or five lessons on a working day, five or six days in the week during the season: the arithmetic produces an income that required no orchestra, no impresario, no travel and no reviews, and it recurred every winter with the reliability of rent. But it also consumed the mornings of a man whose strength was limited and whose creative work had to be fitted around it, which is why the composing migrated to the summer months at Nohant and why the works arrive in clusters keyed to the teaching calendar. And it closed, at last, the question opened in the first section of this account. A pianist becomes world famous without playing in public by making the lesson room do the work of the concert hall: every pupil was an ambassador, carrying his name, his fingerings and his unpublished manner into houses the master himself might never enter, and paying for the privilege of doing so.

The studio also explains a feature of the music that listeners sense without naming. His published pieces are calibrated to be taught. Their hardest passages are short, local and unlockable by a technical hint given in a lesson: a fingering, a redistribution between the hands, a way of leaning on a harmony. The long spans ask for musicianship rather than athletic power. That is the profile of works designed to pass through the hands of the most gifted amateurs in Europe, and it is why the question of how the teaching shaped the technique itself is pursued at length in the companion study of [his musical style explained](/2011/04/07/frederic-chopin-musical-style-explained/). Here it is enough to fix the economic fact. The salon got the glory. The lesson paid for everything.

## Who his pupils were

The pupil list reads like a directory of the Europe that employed him, and its social range is the point. At its head stand the great names who came as much for association as for instruction: Princess Marcelina Czartoryska, of the Hôtel Lambert family, who became the most faithful custodian of his teaching and played his music for another half century; Countess Delphine Potocka, whose singing at his evenings and whose presence run through the correspondence; the Rothschild daughters and the ladies of the banking and diplomatic houses of the Faubourg Saint-Germain. These were the pupils who set the fee's meaning. Twenty francs bought the most exclusive musical instruction in Paris, and exclusivity was part of what was bought.

Below that glittering tier sat the pupils who carried his method into the profession, and Eigeldinger's work gives them their due. Karol Mikuli, a Polish student who became a conservatory director in Lwów, published an edition of the works marked with the master's fingerings and wrote down the principles of the teaching with a pupil's pedantic loyalty; his testimony is a foundation document of the Chopin tradition precisely because it comes from the lesson room and not from literature. Georges Mathias carried the teaching into the Paris Conservatoire as a professor and taught a generation of French pianists. Camille Dubois, later Madame O'Meara, left recollections of the lessons that preserve the atmosphere of the studio: the patience, the irony when a phrase was sentimentalised, the insistence that a pupil who could not sing a line had not yet earned the right to play it. Thomas Tellefsen came from Norway and stayed to become a keeper of the flame in Paris itself. The child prodigy Carl Filtsch, a Hungarian boy of extraordinary gifts whom Chopin taught in the early 1840s and praised without his usual reserve, died at fifteen, and the master's grief at that death is recorded by everyone who saw it.

The most consequential pupil, for the biography, is Jane Stirling. A wealthy Scottish spinster in her forties, she came to Paris for lessons in the mid 1840s, attached herself to the master with a devotion that organised itself into practical service, and after the separation from George Sand became the person who managed his survival: arranging the British journey of 1848, forwarding money, buying back furniture and effects, and preserving relics with a convert's thoroughness. Pupils of this kind cannot be counted in francs. They were the welfare system of a man with no institution behind him, and the fact that his survival in the final year depended on a pupil's charity is the clearest measure of both the strength and the fragility of the career he had built.


The social composition of the studio shaped the pedagogy that Eigeldinger recovered. Most of the pupils were women, because the piano in that world was a female accomplishment and the lesson a respectable call for a young lady to pay with a chaperone in the carriage; and most were amateurs, because the professional schools took the men. Chopin adjusted the teaching to the material without condescension that witnesses could detect: the lessons were long, the criticism minute, the repertoire chosen to stretch exactly the weakness in front of him, and the annotated copies his pupils carried away, their scores pencilled with his fingerings and marks, became relics that their families kept like manuscripts. A pupil's copy, in that world, was a certificate of contact with the master, and the trade in such contact, at twenty francs the hour, was the truest economy of the salon age.
## The publishing arrangements across three countries

If the lessons paid for his life, publishing paid for his name, and the arrangements were unlike those of any earlier composer because the legal world they operated in was unfinished. There was no international copyright. A work published in Paris could be reprinted in Leipzig or London by any firm that obtained a copy, and the composer would receive nothing. The defence, which Chopin practised from the mid 1830s with increasing sophistication, was simultaneity: sell the same work to a publisher in each country, arrange for publication on the same day in all three, and thereby establish the local right in each market before the pirates could move. His Paris publisher for most of his career was Maurice Schlesinger, whose shop in the rue de Richelieu was a centre of the trade, and later the firm of Brandus, which succeeded to the business. In Leipzig he dealt with Breitkopf and Härtel, the oldest music publishing house in Europe. In London, from 1836, his publisher was Wessel and Company, a firm whose commercial instincts he respected and whose presentation of his music he came to detest.

The money was real but it was not large, and it arrived work by work. He sold each piece or set outright for a fee, a few hundred francs for a group of nocturnes or a ballade in each country, which meant that a major work might earn him its fee three times over while a piece placed in only one country earned once. He negotiated hard, kept his own accounts, complained in letters about delays, proofs and misprints, and changed his arrangements when a firm displeased him. The paperwork of this trade, contracts, receipts, corrected proofs and angry letters, survives in quantity, and for the biographer it is gold: it dates compositions, fixes his movements between Paris and the country, and records a professional mind attending to business in a way the romantic portrait of the dreamy invalid never allowed. Readers assembling their own notes on the three editions and their differences can keep the collation in order in the [offline encrypted notes app obtained from the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/), a tool suited to private research material that should stay off public servers.

One further figure belongs in this account of the arrangements: Julius Fontana, the Warsaw schoolfriend who had followed him to Paris and served for years as copyist, agent and factotum. Fontana prepared manuscripts for the engravers, dealt with publishers in the master's absences, and knew the paperwork of the career better than anyone alive. It was Fontana who, after the composer's death, issued the posthumous works, including pieces Chopin had withheld, and the long shadow cast by those decisions belongs to the story of the estate. In the lifetime, the arrangement's meaning is simpler. Publishing in three countries was not vanity and not greed. It was the only legal technology available for converting a reputation in Paris drawing rooms into property, and he operated it with a competence that his legend has consistently underrated.

## Why he published in three countries at once

The simultaneity deserves a section of its own because it explains a permanent feature of the Chopin evidence: the sources disagree with each other, and they disagree because he arranged for them to. A work issued on the same morning in Paris, Leipzig and London went out in three editions prepared from the same manuscript at speed, corrected by different hands, proofed under different pressures, and in the English case dressed by Wessel with fanciful titles the composer had never given his pieces and raged against in letters home. The French edition might carry a fingering the German lacked; the German might preserve a reading the French engraver normalised away. Modern editors of Chopin therefore work like textual critics of a classical author, weighing three early prints of equal authority and no autograph to settle the matter, since he often gave the manuscript away, sold it, or left it with the first publisher who had used it.

The commercial logic underneath the textual chaos was exact. Copyright in the period was national, and it attached to first publication inside the territory. By selling the work in three capitals and synchronising the release, he sold the same notes three times at the full price of originality in each market, and he made piracy pointless, since any reprint would arrive after three authorised editions had stocked the shops. The system demanded a great deal of him: three negotiations, three sets of proofs, three relationships to manage, and a discipline of delivery that sits oddly beside the image of the improvising poet of the piano. It also put him ahead of his contemporaries in a way worth measuring. Tchaikovsky, a generation later, could rely on a single loyal publisher in Jurgenson and a patroness's subsidy, the arrangements set out in the account of [the Tchaikovsky life and career](/2011/06/07/pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky-life-and-career/); Chopin, without patroness or subsidy, had to build the equivalent security himself, out of contracts, and the building took the time other composers gave to the opera house and the road.

For the biography the consequence is this: the publishing system is the second pillar of the career, and it is as deliberate as the fee. A man who merely drifted into fame on the strength of salon charm does not maintain a three country copyright strategy for fifteen years, correct proofs on holiday, or keep publishers competing for his next set of pieces. The documents of the trade show an artist who understood his market with cold clarity, and they license the central claim of this article. The intimacy of the music was not the residue of a shy temperament left over after the career failed to happen. It was the product the career was designed, at twenty francs a lesson and three editions a work, to sell.


The English titles affair shows the system's costs in miniature. Wessel, who understood the English drawing room, issued the works with evocative French titles of his own invention, the sort of label that promised a mood to a purchaser who could not judge the notes, and Chopin's letters erupted: he had given the pieces numbers and genres, and the London shop had given them advertising copy. He threatened, complained and, characteristically, continued the arrangement, because the London fees were real and the piracy the system prevented was worse than the titles. In Brussels and elsewhere, meanwhile, unauthorised reprints circulated despite everything, struck from copies of the authorised editions, and the income they diverted is unrecoverable and uncountable. The three country system did not defeat the pirates. It merely ensured that the composer, and not only the pirates, was paid, in three currencies, on the same morning.
## The salon as his actual performance venue

To understand the career, the salon must be taken seriously as an institution, with its own economics, etiquette and acoustics. The Paris salon of the 1830s and 1840s was a room in a great house, kept by a hostess whose social credit drew the guests, lit for conversation, and furnished with a piano as a normal article of civilised life. Music there was not an interruption of the evening but one of its currencies. A celebrated pianist who could be persuaded to sit down after dinner raised the value of the house, and the pianist received, in return, the only payment the salon dealt in: access. Access to pupils, to patrons, to publishers' goodwill, to the audience whose names in a dedication sold editions. Chopin played in the houses of the Rothschilds, of the Czartoryskis at the Hôtel Lambert, of Countess Komar, Delphine Potocka's mother, of the diplomat and banker Auguste Léo, and in his own rooms, where on certain evenings a handful of chosen listeners were admitted to hear him in the setting he controlled completely.

His playing in those rooms became the stuff of testimony that later writers quarried for a century, and its consistent features can be recovered from the pupils and friends who described it independently of one another. The tone was small and endlessly shaded, produced by a hand his pupils described as boneless in appearance and exact in result. He accompanied himself in effect, the left hand keeping a strict pulse while the right sang with a freedom that his rubato teaching rationalised. He improvised, on themes given by the company, in a manner several witnesses called the summit of his art, and those improvisations are the great lost repertory of the life, since he wrote down only what the publishers could sell. He disliked playing the same celebrated piece on demand, disliked applause that treated music as athletics, and more than once, exhausted or ill, he played for ten minutes to a room that understood it had received the whole evening's worth.

This is the revelation the salon sections of the biography exist to deliver: the salon, not the concert hall, was his venue, and the scale of the rooms explains the dynamics the music actually asks for. His scores are full of markings at the quiet end of the scale, passages written to be heard across a carpet rather than across a parterre, and the fortissimos, when they come, strike with the force they do because the music around them whispers. Generations of pianists trained for halls have had to relearn that scale, and the argument about how he should sound, intimate or monumental, runs through the whole later history of his reputation, an argument the survey of [the greatest pianists of all time](/2025/05/07/greatest-pianists-of-all-time/) takes up from the performer's side. For the life, the point is structural. The salon was not where he hid from his career. It was where his career happened, nightly, for eighteen years, before audiences whose total number, summed across the whole life, may not equal the crowd at a single triumph of Liszt at the height of Lisztomania.

## The circle: Liszt, Bellini, Delacroix, Heine

The room described above had regular occupants, and the circle around Chopin in the 1830s is one of the best documented gatherings in the cultural history of the century, because several of its members wrote everything down. At its centre, in the years of arrival, stood Franz Liszt, two years his junior and in every public respect his opposite: a travelling virtuoso of volcanic energy who had conquered Paris as a boy and treated the city as a headquarters between campaigns. The two young men visited each other's rooms, played each other's music, appeared together in the charity concerts of the mid 1830s, and were painted by gossip as rivals in a contest neither of them had declared. Around them gathered Vincenzo Bellini, the Sicilian opera composer whose long melodic lines Chopin admired and absorbed, and whose early death in 1835, in a villa outside Paris, Chopin mourned with a grief his letters do not trouble to hide; he asked, at the end of his own life, to be buried near Bellini, and he was.

Eugène Delacroix, the painter, came slightly later and stayed closest. His journals record conversations about colour and music conducted with a seriousness neither man wasted on small talk, and his portrait of Chopin, painted in 1838 as part of a double portrait with George Sand and later cut in two, is the most truthful likeness that survives, because it catches the elegance and the exhaustion in the same face. Heinrich Heine, the German poet exiled in Paris, wrote about Chopin's playing in his reports for German readers with a poet's licence and a critic's ear, calling him the Raphael of the piano in a phrase that stuck; the compliment survives as criticism rather than as fact, which is where Heine's judgements belong. Add the Polish poet Adam Mickiewicz, improvising verses at emigre evenings; the cellist Auguste Franchomme, his collaborator and friend, with whom he wrote and played; the singer Pauline Viardot, who carried his music and his arrangements of the mazurkas into the opera world; and the picture is of a man whose sociability, often denied by the legend of the recluse, was in fact the operating system of his life. He was choosy, not solitary. The circle was small because he kept it small, and it was brilliant because Paris in that decade allowed a private room to hold, on a good night, a fair sample of European genius.


The circle also did practical work. Franchomme, the leading cellist in Paris and a professor at the Conservatoire, was the friend of the workshop no less than of the salon: the two men performed together, arranged together, and in the last Nohant summer worked side by side on the cello sonata that stands as the one major chamber work of the late years. Mickiewicz, the exiled Polish poet, presided over the emigre evenings at which Chopin's playing was received as a national rite, and the poet's improvisations and the pianist's were matched in the recollections of those present like two halves of a single performance. Viardot carried the connection into the professional opera world, championing the music in cities he never visited. A circle of this quality is not decoration on a biography. It was the medium in which reputations were manufactured in that Paris, and Chopin, who is so often described as shrinking from the world, had assembled around himself, by choice and by charm, one of the most effective publicity machines in Europe, and never once had to call it that.
## The relationship with Liszt and its cooling

The friendship with Liszt cooled slowly, and the reasons, where the documents show them, are the ordinary reasons magnified by fame. There was the professional asymmetry: Liszt's career grew louder, more public and more theatrical through the 1840s, while Chopin's contracted into the lesson room and the sickroom, and each man's trajectory could be read as an implicit criticism of the other's. There was the matter of Liszt's liaison with the Countess Marie d'Agoult and the gossip economy of Paris, in which Chopin's rooms and even his piano were, on a famous occasion in his absence, borrowed for purposes he had not sanctioned; the anecdote comes down through the circle's memoirs and must be weighed as memoir, but the offence it records, a violation of the private space on which his whole manner of life depended, fits the pattern of the cooling better than any quarrel over music. There was, finally, temperament: Liszt expansive, appropriative, generous with other men's music and other men's stories; Chopin reserved, ironic, and increasingly protective of the few rooms in which he felt safe.

What matters for the record is what the cooling left behind. The full career of the virtuoso, against whose public scale Chopin's private one has been measured ever since, is set out in the account of [Franz Liszt's life and music](/2018/09/07/franz-liszt-life-and-music/). When Chopin died in 1849, the surviving friend did the thing that has complicated his biography ever since: he wrote it. Liszt's book on Chopin, prepared with the Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein and published in 1852, is the founding text of the romantic portrait, eloquent, devoted and unreliable, and it is treated at the end of this article as what it is, a subject of the history rather than a source for it. The cooling therefore has a documentary sting. The man who knew the playing best, and had shared the rooms of the 1830s, became the author of the version of Chopin that the rest of the century believed, and the friendship's long fade into that book is one of the stranger fates in musical biography: the rival as memorialist, setting the terms on which posterity would misunderstand them both.


The joint years left documents that complicate any simple story of rivalry. In the mid 1830s the two appeared on the same programmes at Paris charity concerts, feats of collegiality the press reported with delight; Liszt performed Chopin's studies in his own concerts, an act of advocacy from the one platform in Europe that mattered; and when Chopin gave his rare Paris concert in 1841, Liszt reviewed it in the Gazette musicale with a generosity that included the famous observation that the audience had listened as if to a voice from another world. The cooling came later and came gradually, as Liszt's life moved into the orbit of Weimar and the European tour, and as the book of 1852 approached. What cooled, in the end, was not affection alone but trust in the use Liszt made of intimacy, and the biography of Chopin written after his death confirmed the fear in the act of refuting it.
## The engagement that collapsed

In the summer of 1836, travelling in Germany with his family, the twenty six year old Chopin renewed an acquaintance with a Polish family, the Wodzińskis, whose sons had been his schoolfellows in Warsaw, and fell in love with the daughter, Maria, then seventeen, a pianist of real talent whom he had taught as a child during a Dresden visit the year before. At Marienbad and Dresden that September, between the young people and with the knowledge of her mother, an engagement was formed, secret in form and conditional in substance: the family required a year of waiting, proof of his health, and the stabilising of his position, before anything was announced. He returned to Paris carrying her letters, sketches she had made of him, and a commitment he honoured; the correspondence of that winter is the happiest that survives from his adult life.

The collapse came by post and by degrees. Through 1837 her mother's letters cooled from conditions to discouragement, the health objection hardening into the real reason: the Wodzińskis, wealthy and rising, would not marry their daughter to a consumptive musician whose income depended on lessons, however famous. By the end of that year the engagement was dead, and Chopin kept the bundle of her letters tied with a ribbon, inscribed in his hand with two Polish words meaning my sorrow. The episode is sometimes treated as a decorative sadness in the life. Structurally it is more than that. It was the last point at which a conventional life, marriage into the Polish landed class, summers on an estate, a household of his own, stood open to him, and its closing by a mother's calculation about his lungs told him exactly what his illness meant in the world's accounting. Within a year of the final letters he had formed the connection that replaced that future: irregular, public, child encumbered, and with a woman who earned her own living by her pen. The contrast was not lost on Warsaw, and it was not lost on him.


Maria Wodzińska's own afterlife gives the episode its documentary footing. She kept the letters, the sketches and the album of the Dresden summer, and she married, in time, into the Skarbek family of Żelazowa Wola itself, so that the girl of the broken engagement ended as mistress of the very estate where the composer had been born; the marriage did not hold, and her long later life was spent largely in Italy and in the keeping of the relics of 1836. From the Paris side, the bundle tied with the ribbon and marked as his sorrow survived among his papers, which is why the episode is recoverable in such detail. Two archives, hers and his, kept the same summer from opposite ends, and the biographer who sets them side by side finds agreement on the facts and a perfect disagreement on nothing except the meaning, which is the usual condition of a broken engagement and no discredit to either witness.
## Meeting George Sand

He met Aurore Dupin, Baroness Dudevant, who wrote as George Sand, in the autumn of 1836, at a musical evening in Paris given by Liszt and Marie d'Agoult, and his first recorded reaction, in a letter to his family, was recoil from her masculine dress, her cigar and her fame. She, for her part, had heard his playing and made up her mind about the man with the decisiveness that governed her life. The courtship, if the word applies to a pursuit conducted largely through friends, invitations to her rooms and the slow demolition of his reserve, occupied the following year and a half, the very period in which the Wodzińska engagement was expiring by letter. By the summer of 1838 they were lovers, and by the autumn they had made the plan that produced the most famous winter in nineteenth century music: to travel south, to Majorca, for the sake of his health and her son's, and to live there cheaply and quietly while they both worked.

Sand requires introduction as a professional fact, not as a romantic accessory. In 1838 she was the most famous woman writer in Europe, earning large sums by novels produced at industrial speed, supporting a household that included her son Maurice and her daughter Solange, and living under the permanent scrutiny of a press that treated her life as copy. The connection, as she described it in her memoirs, written years afterwards and to be read as memoir, with a novelist's shaping hand, was on her side protective and possessive; on his, documented in letters that grow easier across 1838, it offered what his life had lacked since Warsaw: a household. She cooked, managed, nursed and organised; he composed, taught when in Paris, and was relieved of the logistics of survival. He also acquired, at a stroke, the most talked about domestic situation in France, a liaison neither married nor hidden, which the salons that employed him digested with remarkable speed. The two accounts of the relationship, hers in print and his in letters, are both partial, written by interested parties, and this article follows the stance the brief prescribes: report from the correspondence, attribute the competing readings, and decline to award the truth to either. What is not in dispute is the chronology. Majorca in the winter of 1838 and 1839; the summers at her estate at Nohant from 1839 to 1846; a separation in 1847 conducted through the grievances of her children; and one brief, accidental meeting on a staircase in March 1848, after which they never spoke again.


Her side of the household economy should be recorded, since the legend of the kept invalid depends on getting it wrong. Sand earned, by the pen, several times what Chopin earned by the lesson and the edition, and Nohant, the summers, the servants and the travel were paid from her income in the main. Chopin insisted on contributing his share to the housekeeping, kept his own accounts with her steward as with his publishers, and resented, in the precise way of a man raised in a schoolmaster's household, any suggestion that he lived on a woman. The friction is documented in both correspondences, and it matters because it marks the boundary of the dependence: materially he was sheltered, financially he was a paying guest, and the difference between those two conditions, invisible to the gossip of the time, was one he policed with some pride until the end.
## The Majorca winter

The party that left Paris in the autumn of 1838 consisted of Sand, her children Maurice, aged fifteen, and Solange, aged ten, and a composer whose chest trouble had worsened through the year and whose doctors had advised a mild climate. They travelled by way of Perpignan and Barcelona to Palma de Majorca, arriving in November, full of the optimism of people who had read about the Mediterranean rather than wintered in it. The first weeks went as the brochures promised: a rented house at Son Vent outside Palma, oranges, sun, letters home describing a paradise. Then the winter rains began, the house proved damp and unheated in the island manner, and Chopin's cough deepened into illness that frightened the household. A Palma doctor examined him, and word of the diagnosis the islanders feared, consumption, travelled through the town as such news does. The landlord demanded they leave and charged them for the disinfection and burning of furnishings, under local rules that treated the disease as a contamination of property.

In December they moved to the deserted Carthusian monastery at Valldemossa, in the mountains north west of Palma, renting cells from a dispossessed order's picturesque ruin: three rooms, a terrace, a view down a valley of almond trees, damp stone walls, and cold that the brazier could not master. It is one of the most visited musical shrines in Europe, and the reality recorded in the letters of both parties is of a beautiful prison. Sand's account, written later and published as a winter in Majorca, describes the islanders' hostility to an unmarried couple, the extortions of the servants, the difficulty of obtaining decent food, medicine or a piano. Chopin's letters to Fontana in Paris are terser and funnier than legend allows: complaints about the doctors, whose ministrations he mocked, about the weather, about the piano, an island instrument he found wretched, on which he waited for the Pleyel upright ordered from Paris, held up for weeks by customs duties at Palma before it reached the monastery in January 1839.

The medical reality under the romance has to be stated plainly, because the winter is routinely written as the moment the illness was contracted by damp and misfortune. It was not. The symptoms recorded before Majorca, the coughing, the fevers, the weakness that had already limited his teaching and cancelled journeys, belong to a condition established years earlier, and the island winter aggravated rather than created it. What the record supports is a man seriously ill in a cold stone building, attended by a woman who nursed him with formidable competence, examined by three local doctors whose treatments he distrusted, and working. Working is the fact the shrine and the letters agree on. The Pleyel, once freed from customs, stood in the cell, and the set of twenty four preludes that had occupied him for two years was completed at Valldemossa, in the worst weeks, alongside other pieces prepared for the publishers whose fees were paying for the expedition. In February 1839, with Chopin coughing blood and the household's money running down, they left the island for Barcelona and a French warship carried them to Marseille, where a good physician, rest and spring weather pulled him back from the edge his friends had feared he would cross on the island.

## What was written in Majorca

The Majorca works require a short section because they are the point at which the biography touches the catalogue and must hand over to it. The twenty four preludes, one in each major and minor key, were assembled and finished during the winter; some had been drafted in Paris, and the island's contribution was completion under pressure, which is perhaps why the set compresses states that a longer life might have spread across a shelf of volumes. A ballade, a scherzo and two polonaises were also in progress across the journey and its aftermath, polished at Marseille and in the first Nohant summer. The catalogue questions, which pieces belong to which room and which month, and what the famous association of a particular prelude with rain on the monastery roof is worth as evidence, belong to the detailed survey of [the greatest compositions and their making](/2011/04/21/frederic-chopin-greatest-compositions/), where the documentary record for each work is weighed on its own. Here the biographical fact is enough: the worst winter of his life was also one of its most productive seasons, and the productivity was not a defiance of the illness so much as a consequence of the household Sand ran, which freed him from every task except composition while the machinery of survival ground on around him.

There is a commercial footnote that belongs to the life rather than the works. The preludes, completed at such cost, were sold in the ordinary way, to the publishers in the three capitals, and the fees helped to settle the debts the expedition had run up, including the ruinous charges of the Palma landlord. The masterpiece of the winter was, among other things, an instrument for paying the winter's bills. Chopin knew the arithmetic and made it without self pity, in letters that move in a single page from the state of his lungs to the state of his accounts. The union of those two subjects, in that order, is the authentic signature of the career.


The preludes' first great reader understood what the winter had produced. Robert Schumann, reviewing the set in his journal in 1839, saluted the pieces as sketches of an eagle's flight, fragments of a genius recognisable in every bar; the review, written by the leading composer critic in Germany from the printed page alone, certified that the music written in the monastery cell travelled at full strength to readers who knew nothing of the circumstances. That is the biographical moral of the Majorca works. The romance of the winter, the rain, the smuggled piano and the cough, has been told so often that it threatens to stand in front of the music as its explanation. The documents support a duller and better story: a professional composer, seriously ill and badly housed, finished a set of pieces to meet his publishers' schedules and his expedition's debts, and the pieces turned out to be among the summits of his art. The mechanism was not suffering. It was the working routine of the Sand household, transplanted to a cell.
## The Nohant summers and the working routine

From 1839 the life settled into the rhythm that governed its remaining creative years: winters in Paris, teaching in the season, summers at Nohant, the estate in Berry, in the centre of France, that Sand had inherited from her grandmother and made the headquarters of her own industry. The journey south took a day by coach and train in improving stages; the household that received him there was large, noisy and organised around work. Sand wrote at night, at a pace her publishers could set clocks by, and slept through the mornings. Chopin composed in the afternoons and evenings in a room kept for him, played after dinner for the house and its guests, and observed the routine of a country house guest who had become, in every way except law, a member of the family.

The routine produced the music. Summer after summer at Nohant the large works of the middle and late career were drafted and finished: ballades, scherzos, the great polonaises, sonatas, the barcarolle, the polonaise-fantaisie, works whose scale and ambition grow in exact proportion as his public presence shrinks. The pattern is the inverse of the platform career and it is worth stating as the biography's central mechanism: as the rooms he played in grew fewer, the music he wrote in them grew larger, because composition had replaced performance as the act by which he addressed the world. Delacroix, a frequent guest, recorded in his journal the conversations of those evenings, Chopin at the piano analysing the logic of Mozart, or improvising while the painter watched the company listen, and his testimony has the ring of the place: a household in which art was not display but the family trade, practised at night by everyone in it.

The guests were the circle of Paris transplanted to the country: Viardot, singing his mazurkas in her own vocal arrangements to his accompaniment; Franchomme; pupils down from Paris; local Berry society kept at a managed distance. For seven summers the system worked, and its working conditions are visible in the output: seven summers, seven harvests of finished works, each delivered to the three publishers as the Paris season reopened. When the system broke down, as the next sections describe, it broke down through the household's internal politics rather than through the work, and the cessation of the Nohant summers after 1846 marks the true end of his creative prime, two and a half years before the end of his life.


The house also ran a theatre, and the theatre is part of the routine's evidence. Maurice built a puppet stage and later a stage for charades and plays, the young people of the house performed, neighbours were summoned as audience, and Chopin, who had a gift for mimicry that his letters display, was pressed into service as accompanist and occasional butt of the plots. Delacroix's journal and Sand's correspondence agree that these evenings were the house at its happiest, and they complicate, usefully, the portrait of the composer as a ghost at the feast: in the middle summers he was a working member of a theatrical family, improvising music for puppet tragedies in the evening and finishing a ballade at the desk the following afternoon. The last summer, 1846, was already shadowed; the guests noticed absences and silences, and the works completed that year, the barcarolle and the polonaise-fantaisie among them, have an autumnal reputation that the chronology of the household, read plainly, does much to explain.
## The household and his place in it

His place at Nohant was anomalous, and the anomaly was the price of the arrangement. He was not the master of the house, not a husband, not a servant and not quite a guest: he was the resident genius of a household ruled by a woman whose income, energy and fame exceeded his own, and whose children were growing into the ages at which such arrangements are judged. Maurice, the son, was artistic, idle and increasingly jealous of the composer who stood between him and his mother's attention; the surviving evidence of friction, quarrels over rooms, over the theatre the young people staged in the house, over Maurice's place in his own home, belongs to the mid 1840s and darkens steadily. Solange, the daughter, clever and difficult, attached herself to Chopin as a counterweight in the household's wars, and his partiality for her, documented in his letters and resented by Sand, became one of the charges in the final indictment of the relationship.

Chopin handled the anomaly the only way his formation allowed: by withdrawal into work and into correctness. He did not fight Sand's battles with her publishers, her neighbours or her family. He contributed money to the household when he had it, charmed the guests he was expected to charm, and retreated to the piano when the temperature rose. Friends who visited in the later summers noticed the strain, and the correspondence with his family in Warsaw, to whom he could not describe the household's true constitution, grows thinner on the subject of Nohant as the years pass. The stance this biography takes toward the household is the one the evidence supports: it was, for seven years, the most productive shelter a dying composer has ever been given, and it was, at the same time, a position that depended entirely on one woman's will, in a house full of her people, under arrangements that a single season of quarrels could dissolve. Both statements are true, and the dissolution, when it came, followed the second.


Money inside the household told the same ambiguous story. He paid for his keep, settled accounts for his rooms and his travelling, and gave presents on the scale his income allowed, and Sand's letters to third parties oscillate between pride in his independence and exasperation at his refusals of help. The position was untenable in the long run not because it was degrading but because it was undefined: a household can accommodate a husband, a guest, a patient or a lodger, and he was required, across nine years, to be all four in rotation, in a house where the children were growing old enough to audit the arrangement and the neighbours had never stopped. That he lasted nine years in it is a tribute to Sand's government of the house and to his own discipline of withdrawal. That it ended in a quarrel about the children rather than about music, money or another lover is the surest sign of what the household had actually been: a family, with a family's fault lines, and not the menage of the caricatures.
## The separation of 1847

The break came through the children, as the household's tensions had always promised it would. In the spring of 1847 Solange, aged eighteen, married a sculptor, Auguste Clésinger, a man of talent and violent debts, in a match Sand first resisted and then accepted; the young couple quarrelled with Maurice, came to blows in the Nohant drawing room in July of that year, and left the house under a cloud that Sand's letters describe as a scandal of ingratitude and violence. Chopin's offence, in Sand's final accounting, was that he had sided with Solange: he had received the couple in Paris, corresponded with the daughter, and declined to join the mother's anathema. Her letter of July 1847, which she later called her farewell, rehearsed nine years of devotion and dismissed him from the family with the completeness of a novelist ending a book. He answered briefly and with dignity, and he kept her letter, and his own copy of the facts, for the remaining two years of his life.

The competing readings must be given together, as the record preserves them. Sand's version, elaborated in her memoirs and her correspondence, is of a woman who had nursed an invalid through his worst years and was repaid with disloyalty in her own family war; her account is partial, shaped for posterity, and written by one of the great self dramatists of the century. The version that emerges from Chopin's letters and from witnesses such as Franchomme and his pupil circle is of a man who could not be made to hate a girl he had watched grow up, and who understood that the demand to choose was itself the dismissal. Modern biographers divide, and the division is instructive: Walker and Samson, reading the correspondence whole, find the fault distributed and the occasion disproportionate; Zamoyski finds Sand's conduct at the end colder than her own account admits. What no reading disputes is the consequence. He never returned to Nohant. The summers that had produced the music ended, and the two remaining years of his life were spent in Paris rooms and in a final, exhausting journey, without the household that had organised his survival since he was twenty eight. The last meeting, on a staircase in Paris in March 1848, when he held her briefly and asked after the child Solange had just borne, is reported by Sand herself, and it has the finality of a scene she knew she was writing.


The aftermath distributed the Paris circle along the fracture. Delacroix, who loved them both, kept a careful neutrality in his journal and his calls, and his entries for those months are the record of a man watching a continent divide inside one drawing room. Viardot and Franchomme stood with Chopin; the emigre Poles, whose darling he was, closed ranks around him with a partisanship that required no information; Sand's literary Paris took her side as a matter of professional solidarity. What neither party did was publish. The scandal lived in letters and in talk, and it is one of the cleaner features of a messy story that the two principals, who between them commanded the pens of Europe, kept the quarrel out of print in their lifetimes. Posterity has not observed the same restraint, and the modern biographer's task, separating the documented sequence from the memoirs' embroidery, is the one the stance of this article prescribes: the letters first, the memoirs second, and the novelist's version, for all its brilliance, last.
## Declining health through the final years

The illness must now be given its own section, handled as the record allows and no further. That his health was poor from youth is documented by the people around him rather than by medicine: the schoolboy who was excused exertions, the young man whose family letters report coughs and fevers as routine weather, the teacher who cancelled lessons through the winters of the 1830s and 1840s, the guest at Nohant whose hosts organised the house around his strength. In his lifetime the doctors who attended him treated a chronic disease of the chest, and the word his century used, and wrote on his death certificate, was consumption: tuberculosis. That remained the settled account for a hundred years, carried by every biography and consistent with the family history, since his sister Emilia had died at fourteen of a chest illness and his father had died in 1844 with lung disease recorded among the causes.

The modern debate, conducted in print since the 1980s, has proposed alternatives, and the stance charter requires them named with their proponents rather than smoothed away. John O'Shea, writing in 1987, proposed cystic fibrosis, pointing to the lifelong pattern of chest and digestive trouble, the survival pattern and the family cases. Jan Kuzemko, in 1994, proposed alpha 1 antitrypsin deficiency, a genetic condition affecting lungs and liver, and argued that the family history across generations fits its inheritance. Other physicians have answered both, holding that the recorded course, the haemoptyses, the wasting, the age at death and the diagnoses of the doctors who actually examined him, fit tuberculosis best, and that retrospective diagnosis across a century and a half, conducted without tissue and without any laboratory examination of the remains, is speculation wearing a white coat. The heart removed at his death and preserved in alcohol in Warsaw had not, in the scholarship available to this account, settled the question either way, and so the debate remains where this section leaves it: documented symptoms on one side, competing retrospective theories on the other, and the death certificate's word standing unrevised between them.

What the biography can say without choosing is the part that mattered to the life. The illness, whatever its name, set the terms of the career at every stage: it made the platform impossible and the lesson room necessary, sent him to Majorca and nearly killed him there, structured his dependence on Sand's household and his vulnerability when it ended, and shortened his working day year by year until the work stopped. He was not an artist ennobled by suffering, and this article refuses the romance that makes the disease a collaborator in the music. He was a professional with a progressive condition, managing it with the tools his century offered, which were rest, climate, nursing and money, and his achievement is better measured against the management than against the myth: the works were produced by a schedule, not by a fever.

## The London and Scotland journey

In the spring of 1848, revolution in Paris emptied the salons, scattered the pupils and cut off the income, and Chopin, who had played his last Paris concert in February, accepted the plan pressed on him by Jane Stirling: a journey to Britain, where her money, her family and her devotion would organise what his strength no longer could. He arrived in London in April 1848 to a city in the grip of the season, gave private performances in the great houses, played at Stafford House before Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, an engagement recorded in the court circular and remembered by the household as a courtesy between sovereigns of different realms, and appeared at two London matinées for the musical public, where the critics received him with a respect coloured, in the English manner, by condescension toward a style too quiet for their halls. He earned money. He also spent strength at a rate the letters home no longer bother to conceal.

In August the party moved to Scotland, to the houses of Stirling's family and connections: Calder House near Edinburgh, Keir House, Strachur on the Clyde. The Scotland of the letters is rain, enormous distances between houses, bagpipes he endured, and dinners at which the dying pianist was exhibited to county society with a kindness he found exhausting. He played where he was taken. In late August he travelled south to Manchester and gave a concert in the Gentlemen's Concert Hall to an audience of some twelve hundred, the largest room he ever played as a soloist, and the Manchester Guardian's critic, hearing the quietest pianist in Europe in a provincial hall, praised the delicacy and doubted the design. There were further appearances, in Glasgow in September and in Edinburgh in October, each organised by Stirling's network, each paid, and each costing him days of recovery. The journey's arithmetic was brutal and he stated it himself in a letter: he was earning his keep by spending the capital of his life.

He returned to London in November, and on 16 November 1848 he made his last public appearance, at the Guildhall, at a grand ball and concert for the benefit of Polish refugees, an occasion on which patriotism, charity and his own legend were combined into an obligation he could not decline. He played briefly, to a room arranged for dancing rather than listening, and the legend that the performance passed almost unnoticed is the fitting end of the platform career: thirty years after the Warsaw charity concert of his childhood, the public stage closed over him without ceremony. A week later he crossed to France, too ill to stand unsupported at the journey's stages, and reached Paris and his rooms as winter began.


The machinery of the British journey shows Stirling's organising genius at full stretch. Broadwood, the London piano maker, supplied instruments wherever he played, and their thicker, louder action troubled his fingers at first, a technical detail his letters record and his English hosts missed entirely. The fees were real: the London matinées and the private engagements of the season produced sums that, converted, compare with a good Paris teaching season, and the Scottish concerts added their share. But the letters' refrain is of cost, not income: the cold of the unheated great houses, the late hours, the distances by carriage between one country seat and the next, and the social requirement, which he met with the manners of his father's house, to be charming at dinner and then to play. He wrote to Paris that the Scots were kind, that the country was beautiful, and that he was paying for the tour with the substance of his remaining strength, in a sentence whose arithmetic nobody who read it misunderstood.
## The last public performance

The Guildhall evening deserves its own short accounting, because the biography of a performer should mark the exact end of the performing. He was thirty eight. He had been playing in public, on and off, since he was seven. The documented public appearances across those thirty one years number, by the count used throughout this article, roughly thirty: a rate of one a year for the most famous pianist in Europe. The final one was not a farewell, was not announced as anything special, and was given for Polish exiles whose cause had framed his whole adult life. He played a selection of his own pieces on a piano placed for a ball, in a hall full of dancers and talk, and those who knew what they were hearing were few enough to be named afterwards by the memoirists. Then the platform record simply stops, and the remaining eleven months of the life contain no public music at all.

It is worth pausing on how completely this ending fits the career's design. A platform virtuoso ends with a festival, a jubilee, a last tour marketed as such. Chopin ends as he began, as a guest in a room, playing for a cause and a company rather than a crowd, paid in obligation rather than fees. The pupils who gathered round him in Paris in the final winter understood that the studio was now the whole of the institution he had built, and they treated their lessons, while lessons could still be given, as audiences with a master who was rationing himself by the hour. When even the lessons stopped, in the spring and summer of 1849, the career did not conclude. It evaporated, in the manner of all enterprises that consist of one person's presence, and the final months belong to the biography of his friends as much as to his own.


The lists, examined closely, draw a distinction the counters of his concerts have had to respect. His last appearance as a billed soloist before a paying audience came in Edinburgh in October 1848, a matinée organised through the Stirling connection; the Guildhall evening in November was a benefit at which he played as a guest among other attractions, unbilled in any programme that survives, noticed by few and remembered, once its significance was understood, by everyone who had been within earshot. Samson's count of roughly thirty folds both kinds together, benefits, shared programmes, salon appearances announced in the press and the handful of full solo evenings, and the honesty of the approximation should be preserved: about thirty, across thirty one years, of which the true solo recitals, the evenings that were his alone, can be counted on the fingers of two hands.
## The final months in Paris

He came back to Paris at the end of November 1848 to a city under a new republic, an empty appointment book and an income that had ceased with the lessons. The winter and spring were managed by the network his career had built: Jane Stirling's money arrived in forms disguised to spare his pride, including a large anonymous gift of twenty five thousand francs that the evidence, assembled by his biographers from her papers, attributes to her; friends took furnished rooms for him when his own had to be given up; Franchomme, Gutmann and the faithful pupils rotated through the sickroom. He moved in June 1849 to Chaillot, then at the city's edge, for the air, and in August his sister Ludwika arrived from Warsaw with her husband, having obtained the passports and made the journey across a divided Europe to nurse him, and the presence of the person who had known him longest steadied the household of the last weeks. In September he was moved back into the centre, to rooms at 12 Place Vendôme, lent and arranged by friends, where the light was good and the callers could be received.

The callers came in procession, and their testimony, cross checked across memoirs, is unusually consistent: the rooms kept like a salon to the end, the piano open, the patient dressed and receiving when strength allowed, ironic about his doctors, tender with the pupils, and asking after music and after Poland. Delacroix visited and recorded the conversations. Viardot came and sang to him. The Polish emigration sent its princes and its priests. He made his will in the form the law allowed, settled what could be settled, expressed the wish recorded by those present that his unpublished manuscripts, the sketches and unfinished pieces no publisher had contracted, should be destroyed rather than issued, a wish his executors did not carry out, with consequences the next section must record. In the last days he was attended by Ludwika, by his pupil Adolf Gutmann, by Franchomme, by the Princess Czartoryska and a small company of the faithful, and the death, when it came in the early hours of 17 October 1849, was witnessed and documented by people whose accounts agree in substance because they were written by friends comparing notes in grief, not by strangers inventing a legend.


Almost nothing was composed in the final year, and the little that survives bears the marks of the sickroom. A mazurka in F minor, worked on during the summer at Chaillot, was left in a state that its posthumous editors had to complete from his sketches, and it has come down as his last piece, a circumstance its quiet character does nothing to contradict. The pen otherwise stopped. He corrected no proofs, negotiated no contracts, and for the first time since boyhood the publishers' stream ran dry, so that the income of the final months was the charity of friends wearing the disguises he required. It is the completion of the career's logic: an enterprise consisting of one man's mornings and one man's manuscripts had no reserves when the man failed, and the friends who stepped into the gap were repaying, in cash and in nursing, the exact social capital the salon years had accumulated.
## The death and the funeral arrangements he specified

He died on 17 October 1849, at the age of thirty nine, in the rooms at the Place Vendôme. The cause entered on the record was the disease of the chest under which he had lived for so long, named in the documents of the time as tuberculosis. This biography gives the fact its plain weight and no ornament: no deathbed sayings are retailed here as fact, since the famous ones come down through memoirs written to console, and no clinical particulars are set out, since they belong to the medical debate already summarised and not to a life. What he had specified, while he could still specify, was the music of his funeral: he asked for Mozart's Requiem, the work of the composer his own playing had most resembled in the judgement of his circle, and the request set his executors a problem, since the Madeleine, where the funeral was to be held, did not admit women's voices, and the Requiem requires them. Permission was negotiated, the funeral was delayed until 30 October to arrange it, and the delay allowed Paris to assemble.

The funeral at the Madeleine on 30 October 1849 was the largest public appearance of his career, eleven days after it ended. Some three thousand mourners filled the church, by the estimates of the press: the emigration in force, the pupils, the publishers, the artists of Paris, delegations from the world that had employed him in rooms and now claimed him in a cathedral. The Requiem was performed with Pauline Viardot and Rose Castellan among the soloists and Lablache, the great bass of the age, singing in the choir, a mourner as much as a musician. His own preludes in E minor and B minor were played on the organ, in an arrangement by the organist, so that the composer was present at his funeral in his own notes as well as in Mozart's. The coffin was carried to Père Lachaise by pallbearers drawn from his publishers, his pupils and his friends, and a handful of the Polish earth from the silver cup of 1830 was sprinkled into the grave, as his friends reported, so that the exile was buried, in token at least, in his own soil. The pattern his ending made, the artist of the small room mourned by a multitude, has drawn crowds of later interpreters, and the survey of [musicians who died young and the legends that grew from early graves](/2025/07/07/musicians-who-died-too-young/) examines that pattern across the century; here it is enough to note that Chopin, who had declined every large stage offered him in life, was given the largest in Paris in death, and had specified the music for it himself.


The grave itself completed the statement. In Père Lachaise he lies a short walk from the tomb of Bellini, as he had wished, in the division of the cemetery where Paris keeps its musicians, and the monument raised by subscription among his pupils and friends, a weeping muse by the sculptor Clésinger, Solange's husband, an irony the subscribers swallowed for the sake of the work, was unveiled a year after the funeral. Delacroix was among the mourners at the Madeleine and left the painter's record of the day; the emigration turned the ceremony into a national demonstration, with Polish banners among the mourners, a fact the French press noted and the Russian embassy noted in a different spirit. The funeral thus closed the double allegiance in the open: Paris gave the cathedral, the orchestra and the crowd, and Poland, present in the earth in the coffin and the banners above it, gave the meaning that the crowd had assembled to honour.
## The heart taken to Warsaw

### Why is Chopin's heart buried in Warsaw?

Because he asked, in effect, to be buried in two countries: his body in Paris among the artists who had been his company, his heart at home in the Poland he had not seen for nineteen years. Ludwika carried it out of France herself, and it was placed in a pillar of the Church of the Holy Cross in Warsaw.

The request belongs to the oldest stratum of his imagination, the world of divided burials of kings and saints that a Polish Catholic childhood supplied, and the fear recorded by his friends, that he might be buried alive, gave it a practical edge in an age that dreaded premature burial. Ludwika, who had closed his eyes, supervised the removal of the heart before the funeral, carried the sealed vessel with her when she returned to Warsaw in 1850, evading the attention of the Russian authorities who governed the city, and kept it in her own home for years, since there was no institution in occupied Warsaw ready to receive it. Only in 1879, thirty years after his death, was it installed with ceremony in a pillar of the Holy Cross Church on Krakowskie Przedmieście, the church of his youth, a short walk from the rooms where he had grown up, with an inscription drawn from the Gospel: where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. The pillar survived the destruction of the church in 1944 because the heart had been removed to safety during the Warsaw Uprising, and it was restored to the rebuilt church in the years after the war.

This closes the question opened at the account of his death: why is he buried in two countries? Because his life had been lived in two countries, and no single grave could state the fact. Paris holds the body, in Père Lachaise, in the city that paid him, published him and gave him his circle, his household and his fame. Warsaw holds the heart, in the city that formed him, taught him, and let him go. The division is usually offered as a romantic flourish. In the documentary light of this biography it reads as something plainer and stronger: the last arrangement of a man who arranged everything, the final contract of the career, drawn up between the two parties to his life and honoured by his sister at personal risk. Exile had divided him against his will for nineteen years. The burial divided him by his own instruction, permanently, and on his own terms.


The heart's twentieth century gave the arrangement its severest test. When the Germans destroyed the church in 1944, during the suppression of the Warsaw Uprising, the urn was taken out of the burning building by German officers and handed to the Polish church authorities, an episode documented on both sides, and it spent the war's remainder in safety at Milanówek outside the city. In October 1945 it was carried back through the ruins in procession and restored to the rebuilt pillar, where the inscription Ludwika's generation had chosen was cut again. The object has since been photographed, guarded and, in the disputes of scholars, argued over, but not moved; and the visitor who stands before it stands at the exact point where the two halves of this biography, the Warsaw child and the Paris master, were joined by the only person who had standing in both.
## The sister, the estate and the manuscripts

Ludwika outlived her brother by nine years, and they were spent largely as his first executor in fact, whatever the documents said. She sorted the rooms at the Place Vendôme, gathered the manuscripts, the letters, the annotated scores and the relics, and made the decisions on which the whole surviving Chopin archive depends. Some of what she kept reached Warsaw and perished there in the following century's wars, including letters whose loss scholars have mourned ever since; some stayed in Paris, passing in time to pupils, collectors and libraries. Jane Stirling, meanwhile, bought furniture, portraits and effects at the sales, with the same acquisitive devotion she had shown in life, and the dispersal of his physical world between two devoted women, each preserving a different half of it, is the reason the relics of Chopin are scattered from Warsaw to Paris to a Scottish castle.

The manuscripts carried the harder problem. On his deathbed he had asked that unpublished work be destroyed, and Ludwika, Fontana and the family declined to obey. Fontana, who knew the papers best, prepared an edition of posthumous works, issued in 1855 with opus numbers the composer had never assigned, including pieces of the first importance alongside pieces Chopin had withheld for good reason. The decision saved music the world would not willingly lose, and it violated the explicit instruction of the man who wrote it, and the tension between those two truths has never been resolved, only inherited. Every later estate has faced a version of the same choice, and the general questions it raises about executors, unfinished work and the ownership of a legacy are examined in the survey of [how estates manage musical legacies](/2026/10/07/how-estates-manage-musical-legacies/). In Chopin's case the papers' survival had one more consequence, quieter than the posthumous editions: the letters lived. Hundreds of his letters, to his family, to Fontana, to publishers and friends, were kept by their recipients and their recipients' heirs, and it is on that correspondence, more than on any narrative source, that the modern biography of Chopin is built. The estate, in short, refused him one wish and thereby made his biography possible.


One loss in the dispersal deserves naming, because it shaped the biography that could later be written. His letters to Sand, written across nine years in numbers that must have run to hundreds, have not survived as an archive; what remains of the correspondence between them is chiefly her side, preserved by her descendants and published in the great edition of her letters, so that the relationship reaches posterity edited by the party who outlived it by twenty seven years. His side survives where it was sent elsewhere: to his family in Warsaw, whose descendants kept the letters through occupation and war, and to Fontana, to Franchomme, to the publishers. The archive of the life is therefore shaped like the life itself, strongest in the documents of work and family, thinnest exactly where the romance was, and the modern biographer's discipline, visible in Samson's and Zamoyski's handling of the Sand years, is to narrate from the letters that exist and to flag, rather than fill, the silence where the others were.
## Liszt's biography and what it did to the record

Three years after the funeral, in 1852, there appeared the book that fixed Chopin's image for the rest of the century: a life by Franz Liszt, published first in serial form in a Paris musical journal and then as a volume, and written, as the scholarship of Alan Walker and others has established in detail, in close collaboration with the Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein, whose literary hand is present in its most purple passages and whose share in the text Walker demonstrated from the surviving papers. The book is beautiful, devoted and, as a record of fact, a romance. It invents scenes, supplies conversations no witness reported, dissolves chronology into atmosphere, and constructs the Chopin the romantic age required: a frail, ethereal creature of pure feeling, a Polish patriot bard, a being too fine for the world's commerce, composing as a medium rather than as a workman. Almost everything in it that can be checked against documents fails the check, and almost everything the general reader still believes about Chopin descends from it.

The custody problem must be stated exactly, because this article was written under its discipline. Liszt's book is never used here as a source for any event in the life. It cannot be. Its author was a subject of the life, a friend turned rival memorialist, writing with a collaborator who had never known the sitter, in a genre the pair of them were inventing as they went. What the book is evidence of is something else: the speed with which the documentary Chopin, the teacher at twenty francs, the contractor in three capitals, the negotiator and proof corrector, was replaced in public memory by the salon wraith of Liszt's prose. The reasons are not mysterious. Liszt's version was printed, translated and read; the documents were in drawers. And Liszt's version flattered everyone: the Poles got their bard, the salons got their angel, the pianists got a patron saint of sensitivity, and only the record lost. This is the final rung of the ladder this biography has climbed: his first major biographer was Liszt, a rival pianist writing with a collaborator and an agenda, and that book shaped the romantic portrait for a century. Every serious life since, Samson's, Zamoyski's, the documentary labours of Eigeldinger and Kallberg, has been written against it, document by document, and the argument between the portrait and the record is where the debate about his standing now lives, taken up in full in [the Chopin legacy debate](/2011/05/21/frederic-chopin-legacy-debate/).


The correction of the record began within the century itself. Frederick Niecks, a German born scholar settled in Scotland, published his life of Chopin in 1888 after interviewing surviving pupils and friends, and his two volumes, dry where Liszt's are perfumed, re-established the documentary floor: dates, documents, testimony taken down from witnesses who had sat in the lesson room. The twentieth century built on Niecks with the tools of the archive, the critical editions of the letters, the publishers' records exhumed by Kallberg and others, Eigeldinger's recovery of the pupils' testimony, until the workman of the contracts stood documented beside the wraith of the biography. The point for the reader of lives is general, and this article has applied it throughout: the first biography of a musician is rarely a source. It is the founding act of the reputation, and it must be audited like any other interested testimony, with the greater suspicion the more beautiful it is.
## Frederic Chopin life and career: a career made of lessons and contracts

The account can now be closed where it began, in the small room. A boy from a Warsaw teaching household, whose father had converted a cultivated skill into a living inside other people's houses, became the first composer of the first rank to live entirely as a private professional: no post, no pension, no platform, a diary of lessons at twenty francs, a portfolio of contracts in three capitals, and a reputation carried by hand from drawing room to drawing room until it covered the world. The arrangements were not the background of the art. They were its conditions, and they explain its scale, its address to the gifted amateur, its concentration in the short forms a publisher could sell and a pupil could master, and its survival, because music written for rooms does not date as music written for occasions does. The downstream of that achievement, the generations of pianists and composers who rebuilt their art on his, is traced in the study of [his influence on music](/2011/05/07/frederic-chopin-influence-on-music/), which begins where this life ends.

What remains open, and should remain open, is the judgement the documents will not deliver. Whether the career was a triumph of adaptation or a narrowing imposed by illness and exile, whether the music would have been greater or merely louder had he lived the platform life of Liszt, whether the household that sheltered him also confined him: the record supports serious versions of each answer, and the biographies named in this article divide along exactly those lines, Samson emphasising the workman's completeness, Zamoyski the exile's wound, Walker the treachery of the sources themselves. Anyone preparing a talk or a lecture from these materials can lay the two readings side by side in [the browser-based presentation viewer at ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), keeping the evidence and the interpretations in separate columns where they belong. For the life itself, the plainest summary is also the most accurate. He was given thirty nine years, a ruined chest, a closed homeland and a Paris lesson book, and out of those materials, without a single institutional support, he manufactured a permanence that the courts and academies of his century could not have given him and cannot take away.

One further fact completes the shape of the life, and it is the one his pupils carried out of the studio into the century. The career he improvised out of necessity, the professional musician living by lessons, editions and reputation rather than by appointment, became within a generation the ordinary career of the pianist composer, and the institutions he declined to join spent the following century competing to teach the method he had worked out alone. He did not live to see that normalisation, and nothing in the record suggests he wanted it. He wanted a room, a piano, a season of pupils, a summer of quiet, and the fees and contracts that made those things possible. The life and career of Frederic Chopin is the story of how completely he got what he wanted, and of what it cost everyone, beginning with himself, to keep it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Frederic Chopin?

Frederic Chopin was a Polish composer and pianist, born in 1810 at Żelazowa Wola near Warsaw and dead in Paris in 1849, at the age of thirty nine. He wrote almost entirely for the piano: nocturnes, mazurkas, waltzes, ballades, scherzos, polonaises, études, preludes, two concertos and three sonatas form the core of the catalogue. What distinguishes the life is its structure. Trained in Warsaw, he left Poland in November 1830, weeks before the November Uprising, and never returned. In Paris he built a career without precedent, living by private lessons at the highest fee in the city and by selling his works to publishers in three countries, while performing in public only about thirty times in his whole life.

### Q: Where was Chopin born?

He was born at Żelazowa Wola, a village some fifty kilometres west of Warsaw, in the steward's house of an estate belonging to the Skarbek family. His father, Nicolas Chopin, was employed there as tutor to the Skarbek children, and his mother, Justyna Krzyżanowska, was a relation of the countess who helped run the household. The family did not stay long. Within months of the birth they moved back to Warsaw, where Nicolas taught French at the Warsaw Lyceum, and the boy's childhood was spent in the capital, in apartments attached to the schools where his father worked. The birthplace house at Żelazowa Wola is preserved as a museum, set in a park, and the Warsaw rooms of his childhood stood in the Saxon Palace and the Kazimierz Palace.

### Q: Why do sources disagree about Chopin's birthday?

Because the two kinds of evidence were written by different hands and never corrected against each other. The baptismal register of the parish church at Brochów records his birth on 22 February 1810 and his baptism on 22 April of the same year, an entry made by a priest working from information brought to him two months after the event. The family kept 1 March as the birthday, the composer used 1 March himself in adult life, and his friends commemorated him on that day. A minority of early printed accounts gave the year as 1809, probably by careless arithmetic from a prodigy's stated age. No later document has settled the matter, so responsible biographies print both dates and leave the discrepancy open, as the record requires.

### Q: Was Chopin's father French?

Yes. Nicolas Chopin was born in 1771 at Marainville, a village in Lorraine, and came to Poland at the age of sixteen, in 1787, in the service of the Polish steward of the estate where his father worked. When that household's Polish venture ended, he stayed, learned the language, and made his living as a tutor in aristocratic houses and then as a teacher of French, first in private posts and then at the Warsaw Lyceum and the military school for cadets. He married Justyna Krzyżanowska, a Pole, in 1806, and the household their children grew up in was bilingual, French in its manners and Polish in its loyalties. The son inherited both halves: the French fluency that made Paris easy for him, and the Polish allegiance that exile never loosened.

### Q: Did Chopin ever return to Poland?

No. He left Warsaw on 2 November 1830 and never stood on Polish soil again, although he lived for nineteen more years and Poland was, in the first years, reachable. The reasons accumulated. The failure of the November Uprising in 1831 brought a Russian settlement that closed the university and the conservatory and made return a political act; his passport position as an emigre made travel into the Russian partition difficult and watched; feelers from the Russian side, including the prospect of a titled post at St Petersburg, were declined; and the career he had built in Paris could not be paused for a journey that would have satisfied homesickness at the price of everything else. He met Polish travellers, taught Polish pupils, and in 1849 received his sister from Warsaw at his deathbed. The country came to him. He did not go back.

### Q: How did Chopin support himself financially?

By two streams of income, both private. The first was teaching: in the Paris season he gave lessons in his rooms to pupils from the wealthiest families in Europe, at twenty francs a lesson, the highest fee in the city, with several lessons on a teaching day. The second was publishing: he sold each work outright to publishers in Paris, Leipzig and London, collecting a separate fee in each market for the same music. A third element was negative: the income he refused. He held no salaried post of any kind, declined most concert engagements, and turned away the offer of a court position in St Petersburg. In the final year, when illness stopped the lessons, the system had no reserves, and he lived on the disguised charity of his pupil Jane Stirling and the support of his friends.

### Q: How much did Chopin charge for a lesson?

Twenty francs a lesson, a rate he established in his mid twenties and held for the rest of his career. The figure needs context to be felt. Twenty francs was several times the daily wage of a skilled Paris workman and the highest lesson fee in the capital, which was precisely its function: it rationed his limited strength to a small number of wealthy pupils, it signalled rank in a market where price was read as quality, and at four or five lessons on a teaching day it produced an income that removed any need to perform in public. Pupils came to his rooms rather than receiving him at home, waited for their hour, and paid for the season in the manner of a professional retainer. Jean-Jacques Eigeldinger's study of the teaching, built from the pupils' own records, documents the rate and the system around it in detail.

### Q: Who were Chopin's students?

They fell into three groups. The first was the aristocracy of the lesson book: Princess Marcelina Czartoryska, who became the most faithful guardian of his teaching; Countess Delphine Potocka; and the daughters of the Rothschild and diplomatic houses of Paris, whose fees and connections sustained the practice. The second group carried the method into the profession: Karol Mikuli, who published the works with the master's fingerings and recorded the teaching principles; Georges Mathias, later a professor at the Paris Conservatoire; Camille Dubois, whose recollections preserve the studio's atmosphere; Thomas Tellefsen; and the prodigy Carl Filtsch, whose death at fifteen Chopin grieved openly. The third group is represented by Jane Stirling, the Scottish pupil whose devotion organised his final journey to Britain and whose money carried him through the last year of his life.

### Q: Why did Chopin publish in three countries simultaneously?

Because copyright was national and there was no international protection for music. A work first published in Paris could be reprinted freely in Leipzig or London, and the composer would receive nothing from those markets. His defence was simultaneity: he sold the same work to a publisher in each capital, arranged for the editions to appear on the same day, and thereby established his right in each territory before pirate reprints could be prepared. The system earned him three fees for one composition, and it explains a permanent puzzle of his music's sources. The French, German and English first editions were prepared in haste from the same manuscript by different engravers and proof readers, so they differ in details, and editors have weighed their conflicting readings ever since.

### Q: What was the salon and how did it work?

The salon was a private evening in a great Paris house, kept by a hostess whose social standing drew the guests, with music as one of the evening's currencies rather than its interruption. A celebrated pianist who played after dinner raised the value of the house; in return he received access, to pupils, patrons, dedications and publishers, which was the only payment the salon dealt in. Chopin's working life ran on this exchange. He played at the Rothschilds, at the Hôtel Lambert of the Czartoryskis, in the houses of bankers and countesses, and in his own rooms before a handful of chosen listeners, at a volume scaled to carpet and curtains. Witnesses describe the tone as small, shaded and exact, the left hand strict while the right hand sang, and the improvisations, never written down, as the summit of the art. The salon was not his refuge from a career. It was the career.

### Q: What was Chopin's relationship with George Sand?

It was a liaison of nine years, from 1838 to 1847, conducted without marriage and organised as a household. They met in Paris in the autumn of 1836, became lovers in 1838, spent the winter of 1838 and 1839 in Majorca with her children, and from 1839 to 1846 passed the summers at her estate at Nohant, where she wrote at night and he composed, in the most productive routine of his life. She nursed him through illnesses, managed the logistics of survival and gave him the domestic structure his life had lacked since Warsaw. The relationship ended in 1847 through a family quarrel in which he sided with her daughter Solange against her son Maurice, and her farewell letter of July 1847 closed the connection; they met once more, briefly, in March 1848, and never spoke again. Her memoir account and his letters are both partial, and the record is best read from both.

### Q: What happened during the winter in Majorca?

In November 1838 Chopin travelled with George Sand and her two children to Majorca, seeking a mild climate for his chest illness. The first weeks in a rented house near Palma went well; then the winter rains came, the house proved damp and cold, his cough worsened, and when the local fear of consumption attached to him, the landlord expelled the party and charged them for disinfecting and burning the furnishings. They moved in December to rented cells in the deserted Charterhouse of Valldemossa, where the cold defeated the braziers, the islanders distrusted the unmarried couple, and provisions, medicine and service were hard to obtain at any price. His ordered Pleyel piano was held for weeks by customs at Palma before reaching the monastery in January 1839. In February, with his health broken down, they left for Barcelona and Marseille, where rest and spring weather restored him.

### Q: How did Chopin die?

He died in Paris on 17 October 1849, at the age of thirty nine, of the chronic chest illness under which he had lived since youth. The cause recorded by his century, on the death certificate and in every early account, was tuberculosis, and his sister Emilia and his father had both died with lung disease in the record, which strengthened the diagnosis in the eyes of his biographers. The diagnosis has been questioned in print in later scholarship: John O'Shea proposed cystic fibrosis in 1987, and Jan Kuzemko proposed alpha 1 antitrypsin deficiency in 1994, while other physicians have defended the original account on the documented course of the illness, the haemoptyses, the wasting and the age at death. No laboratory evidence has settled the question. What is not disputed is the fact and the date, or that the illness, whatever its name, set the terms of his whole working life.

### Q: What happened to Chopin's heart after his death?

At his wish, his heart was removed before the funeral in Paris and kept by his sister Ludwika, who had been present at his death. She carried it back to Warsaw with her in 1850, preserved in alcohol in a sealed vessel, at some risk under the Russian authorities who governed the city, and kept it in her own home for years, since occupied Warsaw had no institution ready to receive it. In 1879 it was installed with ceremony in a pillar of the Church of the Holy Cross on Krakowskie Przedmieście, the church of his youth, beneath an inscription taken from the Gospel: where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. Removed to safety when the church was destroyed in 1944, it was restored to the rebuilt pillar in 1945, where it remains, while his body rests at Père Lachaise in Paris.

### Q: How reliable is Liszt's biography of Chopin?

As a record of fact, it is unreliable, and as a document of reputation it is indispensable. The book appeared in 1852, three years after the death, under Liszt's name and written in close collaboration with the Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein, whose share in the text Alan Walker established from the surviving papers. It invents scenes and conversations, dissolves chronology into atmosphere, and constructs the frail, ethereal patriot bard that the romantic age required, a portrait that shaped public belief for a century. Passages that can be checked against letters, contracts and pupils' testimony repeatedly fail the check. Modern biographers therefore treat the book as a subject of the history, the founding act of the legend, and never as evidence for events in the life. Where Liszt describes the playing of the 1830s he is a witness of genius writing literature, and he must be read with both halves of that sentence held firmly in mind.

### Q: Which Chopin biography should I read first?

Start with Jim Samson's life in the Master Musicians series, published in 1996. Samson writes as a scholar of the music and a sceptic of the legend, keeps the documentary questions open where the record leaves them open, and treats the career, the lessons, the contracts and the editions, as the substance of the life rather than its background. For a fuller narrative sweep, Adam Zamoyski's biography of 2010 brings the Polish evidence and the exile politics into the foreground and reads the life against the fate of the country he left. Alan Walker's essays and editions are the guide to the treacherous sources, above all to Liszt's book. Jean-Jacques Eigeldinger's study of Chopin as a teacher, assembled from the pupils' records, is the essential companion for anyone who wants the man his pupils actually met in the lesson room.

### Q: Should I read Chopin's letters or a biography first?

Read a biography first, and Samson's is the practical choice, because the letters assume a world the reader has not yet been given. Chopin wrote vividly but elliptically, to people who knew the pupils, publishers, prices and quarrels he mentions without naming, and a first reader without the map will mistake jokes for grievances and business for gossip. With the biography's framework in place, the letters become the most alive documents of the life: funny, hard headed about money, tender about his family, and ruthless about the doctors, landlords and publishers who crossed him. The correspondence is also uneven in survival. His letters to his family and to his friend Julius Fontana are rich; his letters to George Sand largely failed to survive, while hers to him are read in her collected correspondence. The biography tells the reader where those silences are and why they matter.

### Q: How do I separate fact from romance in Chopin's life story?

Work from a hierarchy of evidence and hold each story to its level. At the top stand documents produced at the time by disinterested hands: parish registers, publishers' contracts, concert programmes, reviews and the correspondence written for business. Next come the letters of the participants, vivid but addressed to particular readers with particular purposes. Below them stand the pupils' testimony, recorded close to the lessons and generally trustworthy on the studio. At the bottom, to be used as evidence about the legend rather than about the life, stand the memoirs written years later and the romantic biographies, Liszt's above all. Test any famous anecdote by asking who first printed it, when, and what they gained by it. The thirty concerts, the twenty franc fee and the three country editions survive the test. Most of the swooning invalid of legend does not.

### Q: How do I visit the Chopin sites in Warsaw and Paris?

In Warsaw, begin at Żelazowa Wola, the birthplace house in its park, reachable by road from the capital, and continue to the Church of the Holy Cross on Krakowskie Przedmieście, where the heart rests in its pillar; the Chopin Museum in the Ostrogski Palace holds manuscripts, letters, portraits and his last piano. In Paris, the grave at Père Lachaise, where he lies near Bellini as he wished, is the obligatory station; the rooms at 12 Place Vendôme, where he died, are marked by a plaque; and the salon world that employed him is best approached through the Hôtel Lambert on the Île Saint-Louis, visible from the street, and the Pleyel firm's surviving instruments in the city's music museum. Take the sites in that order and the geography of the life, Polish soil, Paris rooms, divided grave, reads itself off the map.

### Q: Which recordings suit a listener new to Chopin?

Choose pianists whose Chopin has the scale of the rooms the music was written for. Dinu Lipatti, recorded in the 1940s, plays with a clarity and restraint that let a new listener hear the counterpoint inside the poetry, and his waltzes and Barcarolle are a sound education in themselves. Arthur Rubinstein's Chopin, recorded across the middle of the twentieth century for RCA, offers the warmer, singing manner and remains the friendliest entry to the nocturnes and the concertos. Alfred Cortot's recordings from the 1930s preserve, with their period blemishes, a link to the French school that descended from the pupils. For modern sound and complete command, Krystian Zimerman's accounts of the ballades and the concertos set a benchmark. Begin with Lipatti and Rubinstein, and let the two manners argue the music's double nature, intimate and monumental, between them.
