The Hindu Code Bill is usually filed under family law, as if its nine-year passage from committee room to statute book were a technical exercise in tidying marriage, succession, adoption and guardianship rules for one community. That filing is a misreading, and it is the misreading this article contests. Between September 1947, when the Cabinet of the new Dominion resolved to carry the Bill forward, and December 1956, when the fourth and last of its successor statutes received assent, the Bill forced a question that the Constitution had deliberately left open: how far may a secular state reach inside the personal law of a religious community, and may it reach into one community's law while leaving another's untouched? Every later contest over personal law in India has been argued inside the answer that the Hindu Code Bill produced. The answer was asymmetric. Hindu personal law was reformed by parliamentary statute, in depth and against organised religious opposition. Muslim personal law was preserved, deliberately and on stated grounds of community autonomy. The claim defended throughout is the brief's own: the Hindu Code Bill's asymmetric outcome was the constitutive choice that defined Indian secularism, not an accidental incompleteness.

## The Hindu Code Bill and the Question It Actually Posed

A reform measure asks whether a rule should change. The Hindu Code Bill asked something prior and harder: who holds the authority to change it. When B. R. Ambedkar, as Law Minister, piloted the Bill through the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) from September 1948 onward, the opponents who rose against him rarely defended polygamy or the widow's limited estate on their merits alone. The deeper objection, heard from Seth Govind Das, from Thakur Das Bhargava and from President Rajendra Prasad in his correspondence of 1948, 1949 and September 1951, was jurisdictional: a legislature, and above all a legislature not yet elected on universal franchise, had no mandate to rewrite the sacred law of a community. Jawaharlal Nehru's government answered that personal law, however sanctified by custom, was civil law administered by state courts, and that a Parliament answerable to citizens could amend it as it amended any civil law. That exchange, repeated across the debates of 1949, 1950 and 1951, is why the Bill belongs to the history of secularism before it belongs to the history of the family.

The point connects directly to [the Constituent Assembly's secular-settlement tension that the Hindu Code Bill tested explicitly](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/). The Assembly had written a Constitution in which the state neither established a religion nor withdrew from religious life altogether. It guaranteed free profession, practice and propagation of religion, and in the same document it armed Parliament with power to legislate for social welfare and reform in matters entangled with religious practice. The Hindu Code Bill was the first occasion on which that second power was used at full scale against the personal law of the majority community. Its fate therefore settled, in practice, what the constitutional text had left as an abstraction: the Indian state would be a reforming state toward religion, but a selective one, and the pattern of its selectivity would be set by this episode.

### Why was the Hindu Code Bill a test of secularism rather than only a family law reform?

The Bill tested secularism because its central dispute concerned state authority over religious personal law, not the merits of any single rule. Opponents denied that a legislature could rewrite community law at all, while Nehru's government asserted that personal law was civil law open to parliamentary amendment, making the Bill's fate a verdict on the secular state's reach.

Measured against the standard textbook summary, which lists the Bill's provisions and its eventual division into four Acts, this framing changes what counts as the outcome. The outcome was not only that Hindu daughters gained inheritance rights or that Hindu marriage became monogamous in law. The outcome was a working definition of Indian secularism with three features that have persisted. First, the state claimed and exercised authority to reform the personal law of the majority community from within a parliamentary process. Second, the state declined, as a matter of stated policy rather than oversight, to exercise the same authority over the personal law of the principal minority community in the same period. Third, both moves were defended in a single vocabulary of constitutional principle, so that the asymmetry arrived dressed as doctrine rather than as expedient. Rajeev Bhargava, in The Promise of India's Secular Democracy (2010), would later give that doctrine its canonical name, principled distance. The events of 1947 to 1956 are where the doctrine was built, before it was named.

## The Rau Committee Blueprint: The 1947 Report Behind the Bill

The Bill that reached the legislature in the 1940s was not drafted in the heat of independence. Its architecture came from the Hindu Law Committee, first appointed by the colonial government in January 1941 under the chairmanship of B. N. Rau, the jurist who would later serve as Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly. The committee of 1941, whose members included the scholar Dwarka Nath Mitter, recommended that Hindu law be codified, and that the codification proceed by stages, beginning with the subjects where reform was most overdue. When the committee was revived in 1944, again with Rau in the chair, it toured the country, recorded evidence from lawyers, judges, religious authorities and women's organisations, and in 1947 produced both a report and a draft Code. That draft, published for public comment before independence, is the architectural template of everything that followed: a single code covering marriage, succession, minority and guardianship, adoption and maintenance, drafted as statute rather than left as commentary on sacred text.

### What did the Rau Committee recommend in its 1947 report?

The Rau Committee recommended that Hindu law be gathered into a single parliamentary code covering marriage, succession, guardianship, adoption and maintenance, that the code override divergent custom where the two conflicted, and that women's rights in marriage and property be placed on a statutory footing rather than left to the conflicting schools of classical interpretation.

The committee worked against a legal landscape that Parliament had already begun to alter in pieces. The Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act of 1937 had given a widow a limited estate in her husband's property, the first statutory breach in the classical rule that she could not inherit. G. V. Deshmukh's legislative efforts of the 1930s had pressed, with partial success, for women's rights to property and against child marriage's legal supports. The Rau draft absorbed these piecemeal gains into a comprehensive design and added what the piecemeal route could not deliver: a daughter's share in her father's estate, monogamy enforced by statute, divorce available to Hindu spouses for the first time as a general legal right, and adoption opened beyond the classical requirement of a son. Each of these provisions can be traced to a page of the committee's work, which is why the report of 1947, rarely cited in summary accounts, matters more than several of the louder debates that followed it. The politicians of 1948 inherited a finished instrument. Their fight was over whether to use it.

Rau himself embodied the continuity between the colonial codification project and the constitutional one. The same jurist who framed the draft Code spent 1947 to 1949 advising the Constituent Assembly on the Constitution's text, and the two projects shared a premise: that India's personal laws and its public law alike should be stated in enacted text, interpretable by courts, rather than in layered commentary whose authority rested on antiquity. When the Bill stalled, it was this premise, as much as any clause, that its religious opponents were resisting. A code can be amended by a majority. A sacred commentary cannot. The transfer of authority from commentator to legislator was the reform inside the reform.

## A Century of Statute Before the Code: The Precedents of 1829 to 1937

The claim that Parliament in 1948 was entering untouched sacred ground dissolves against the legislative record of the preceding century, and the reformers in the Assembly knew the record by heart. The Bengal Sati Regulation of 1829 had criminalised a practice its defenders grounded in scripture, and the colonial state had justified the prohibition as a matter of law and order and humanity rather than theology. The Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act of 1856 had legalised the remarriage that orthodox opinion held impossible, on the petitioning of Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, whose argument, like Ambedkar's a century later, was built from the shastric sources themselves. The Age of Consent legislation of 1891 and the Child Marriage Restraint Act of 1929, the latter carried by Harbilas Sharda against fierce orthodox resistance in the Central Legislative Assembly, had each placed a statutory boundary around a practice defended as religious. By the time the Rau Committee sat in 1941, the proposition that a legislature could amend Hindu domestic practice was not a proposal. It was a hundred years of enacted precedent.

Two features of that precedent shaped the Code's politics. The first is that every earlier intervention had been fragmentary, a single practice addressed in isolation, leaving the surrounding structure of marriage, inheritance and guardianship untouched and often producing anomalies that only a code could resolve: the remarried widow of 1856, for instance, forfeited rights in her deceased husband's property by the very Act that freed her to remarry. The Rau Committee's comprehensiveness was a response to this demonstrated failure of the fragmentary method. The second feature is the identity of the earlier legislators. The colonial state that enacted the precedents had, after 1857, publicly renounced interference in Indian religion as a matter of imperial policy, and its interventions were defended as exceptions. The government of 1948 laboured under no such renunciation and claimed the opposite mandate, yet it faced an opposition that quoted the colonial state's caution against the republic's ambition. The irony was structural and everyone in the chamber could see it: the empire had reformed Hindu law while disclaiming the right to do so, and the republic proposed to reform it while claiming the right as a democratic duty. Which of those two postures showed greater respect for the community being reformed became one of the debate's recurring subterranean questions, and the reformers' answer, that reform by one's own elected representatives is self government while reform by a foreign bureaucracy is tutelage, carried a force that purely legal argument could not have supplied.

The precedent that mattered most in the chamber, because it was within living legislative memory, was the Hindu Women's Rights to Property Act of 1937. Carried through the Central Legislative Assembly in the teeth of orthodox opposition, it gave the Hindu widow an interest in her husband's property equal to a son's share in the separate estate, though held as a limited estate that she could not freely alienate. B. N. Rau had advised on the drafting questions that Act raised, and its limitations were the committee's standing exhibit for the inadequacy of instalment reform: within a few years, litigation over the 1937 Act's meaning had filled the courts with conflicting interpretations, and the widow's limited estate had become a byword for a right granted in form and withheld in substance. When the Succession Act of 1956 converted that limited estate into absolute ownership, it was completing a sentence that the legislature of 1937 had begun and lacked the will, or the mandate, to finish. The Code's long gestation is therefore misdated when it is counted from 1947. Its true span runs from the first statutory breach in the classical order to the comprehensive settlement, and 1947 marks not its beginning but its acceleration under a government that finally possessed both the text and the electoral theory to finish it.

## From Cabinet Decision to Assembly Floor: September 1947 to September 1948

An early version of the Code had already reached the old Central Legislative Assembly in April 1947, in the last months of British rule, and had lapsed with the transfer of power. The decision that gave the measure its second life was taken in September 1947, when the Cabinet of independent India resolved to take up the Hindu Code as government business. The resolution mattered because of who executed it. The Law Ministry had passed on 15 August 1947 to B. R. Ambedkar, brought into the Cabinet by Nehru despite having led his own party against the Congress for two decades. Ambedkar took the Rau draft, revised it through the Law Ministry, and made it his own measure. The revival of the Code and the appointment of its most uncompromising advocate were a single act of policy.

That appointment is unintelligible without [Ambedkar's substantive authority that he carried into the Hindu Code Bill effort and resigned over](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/). As chairman of the Drafting Committee, Ambedkar was simultaneously steering the Constitution through the Assembly while his ministry prepared to reform the personal law of the majority community. The two tasks rested on one conviction, stated by Ambedkar in debate after debate: law in a republic derives its authority from enactment and consent, not from scripture, and a social order that scripture placed beyond amendment would place the republic itself beyond amendment. His opponents understood the connection as clearly as he did. To defeat the Code was to mark a boundary past which the new state's authority would not run.

The Bill was introduced in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) in September 1948 and referred for the scrutiny that consumed the following three years. The Assembly that received it sat in a double capacity, drafting a constitution in one set of sittings and legislating for the Dominion in another, and the Code's opponents exploited the duality from the start. A body elected in 1946 on a restricted franchise, they argued, first through Rajendra Prasad's interventions of 1948 and 1949 and then on the floor, was competent to frame a constitution by the extraordinary mandate of the freedom movement. It was not competent, they held, to rewrite the domestic law of three hundred million Hindus; that task, if it was to be done at all, belonged to a Parliament elected by the Hindus it would bind. The government's answer, that the Assembly's legislative competence was plenary and its mandate general, was legally sufficient and politically incomplete. The objection about the mandate never died. It waited, and in September 1951 it returned with a President behind it.

## The Assembly's Double Life: Constitution Maker as Legislature

A peculiarity of institutional form runs through the entire episode and explains several of its strangest turns. The body that debated the Hindu Code Bill between 1948 and 1951 was the Constituent Assembly sitting in its legislative capacity, the same persons meeting under a different presiding officer and a different authority: Rajendra Prasad presided over the constitution making sittings, while G. V. Mavalankar, as Speaker, presided over the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) when it transacted ordinary lawmaking for the Dominion. Until 26 January 1950 the Assembly legislated under the framework of the Government of India Act of 1935 as adapted; from that date it continued as the Provisional Parliament of the republic, unchanged in membership, until the first elected Lok Sabha replaced it in 1952. One room thus contained, in sequence, a constitution's authors, a dominion's legislature and a republic's provisional parliament, and the Code was debated under all three descriptions of its authority.

The doubling shaped the Bill's fate in ways a simple chronology hides. The members who had written the reform power into the religious freedom guarantee in the constitution making sittings were the members who then had to decide, in the legislative sittings, whether to use that power at full scale, and the opponents' competence objection drew its force from the doubling itself: the Assembly's undoubted mandate to constitute a republic did not obviously include a mandate to reconstruct the domestic law of its largest community before a single vote had been cast under the new Constitution. Prasad's interventions of 1948 and 1949 pressed exactly this seam, and his presidency of the constitution making body gave the objection an institutional weight that a backbench protest could not have carried. Conversely, the government could reply that the doubling was the founding generation's design rather than its accident: the Assembly had been convened to give India both a constitution and continuity of government, and its legislative competence had been exercised across the whole field of law, on taxation, on citizenship's precursors, on the integration of the princely states' legal orders, without anyone supposing that domestic law lay outside it. The Hindu Code was singled out for the competence objection not because the Assembly's authority was in general doubt, but because this measure touched religion, and the objection's selectivity is itself evidence for the article's thesis: the fight was never really about what the Assembly was. It was about whether the secular state the Assembly was building would be a reforming state, and the institutional doubling merely supplied the terrain on which that question could be contested for three years without a resolution neither side was willing to force.

The Provisional Parliament phase, from January 1950 to the spring of 1952, added a final twist. The Constitution the Assembly had made was in force, its reform clause available in the text, its Directive Principles declaring the uniform civil code an object of state endeavour, and still the Code stalled, because the objection had migrated from competence to prudence: an assembly whose members had been chosen in 1946, before independence, before Partition, before the Constitution itself, was being asked to bind generations on a question that divided its own majority party. Nehru's eventual acceptance of delay, formalised in his communication of January 1952, conceded the prudential objection while denying the competence one, a distinction that maps precisely onto the settlement the episode produced. Authority to reform was asserted without qualification and has never since been surrendered. The exercise of that authority was made to wait upon electoral sanction, and has carried that expectation ever since, so that every later government contemplating personal law reform has had to calculate, as Nehru calculated in 1951, not whether Parliament may act, but whether a Parliament that acts will be understood to have been entitled to.

## Nine Years of Reform: The Hindu Code Timeline from 1947 to 1956

The chronology below, the Hindu Code reform-attempt timeline from 1947 to 1956, compresses the episode into its legislative skeleton. It records the Bill's status at each stage, the opposition mobilisation that met it, and the position of the resignation that divides the story in two. Readers citing the episode should cite this timeline, because the sequence is the argument: a measure that an unelected assembly could not carry was carried, in divided form, by an elected Parliament, and the interval between those two facts is where Indian secularism acquired its shape.

Two features of the timeline deserve emphasis before the narrative resumes. The first is the sheer duration. Nine years for a measure whose text was substantially complete in 1947 indicates that the obstacle was never drafting. At every stage the obstacle was authority: the authority of an unelected assembly in 1948, of a Law Minister against his own Cabinet colleagues in 1951, of a President against his Prime Minister in the same month, and finally of an elected Parliament, which proved sufficient. The second feature is the change in the opposition's argument across the rows. Before 1952, opponents argued that the wrong body was legislating. After 1952, that argument being unavailable, they argued the merits and lost on most of them. The asymmetry at the heart of this article was produced inside this sequence, not imposed on it from outside.

## The Debates of 1949 to 1951: Two Arguments About One Bill

The floor debates in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative), running from 1949 into 1951, are usually summarised as a contest between reformers and traditionalists. The record supports a finer description. Two distinct arguments were conducted under one roof, and they were won by different sides. The first argument concerned the content of Hindu law: whether daughters should inherit, whether a Hindu marriage could be dissolved, whether a widow's estate should be absolute. On content, the reformers held the stronger brief, and Ambedkar defended it clause by clause with a command of the classical sources that disarmed the claim that codification was ignorant of them. The second argument concerned the location of authority: whether the power to settle these questions lay with Parliament, with religious authority, or with the community consulted through its own institutions. On authority, the opposition held the stronger politics, because every month of delay strengthened the demand that the question wait for a general election.

### Who opposed the Hindu Code Bill inside the Constituent Assembly (Legislative)?

The floor opposition was led by members speaking for orthodox Hindu opinion, among them Seth Govind Das and Thakur Das Bhargava, with Syama Prasad Mookerjee pressing the discrimination argument and Congress conservatives withholding support in division after division, a combination that denied the government the confident majority the Bill required between 1949 and 1951.

Ambedkar's own contributions to these debates repay attention because they fix the reformers' theory of the case. He did not present the Code as a concession extracted from a reluctant tradition. He presented it as the recovery, by statute, of an equality that the classical law's later interpreters had buried, and he rested the daughter's claim to inherit not on imported doctrine but on the demonstrable plurality of the schools themselves: a law that Mitakshara and Dayabhaga had already made different in Bengal and in the Deccan could not plausibly claim to be one immutable sacred order. The argument mattered for secularism's later defence. If Hindu law was already plural, regional and historically amended by commentary, then parliamentary codification was a change of instrument, not a violation of essence. Bhargava's principled-distance reading of 2010 is unintelligible without this prior move of Ambedkar's in 1949: the state could reform Hindu law because Hindu law had never stood outside history in the first place.

The women members of the Assembly supplied the debates with testimony that neither side could reproduce. Renuka Ray and Hansa Mehta, both veterans of the women's movement that had pressed the Rau Committee with evidence in the 1940s, spoke for provisions that the male leadership treated as bargaining counters, above all the daughter's share and the abolition of the widow's limited estate. Their interventions recorded, in the Assembly's own proceedings, that the constituency for reform was not a ministerial invention. Against them, opponents quoted scripture and cited the danger of breaking the joint family, an institution whose economic functions the Code's critics described as a welfare system that statute would destroy without replacing. The joint family argument was the opposition's strongest substantive card, and the government never fully answered it on its own terrain; the Succession Act of 1956 would eventually preserve the Mitakshara coparcenary for sons even while admitting daughters to a share of the father's separate property, a compromise whose genealogy runs straight back to these debates.

The opposition's most systematic parliamentary voice belonged to Syama Prasad Mookerjee, and his trajectory across the episode binds the founding debate to the political formations that inherited it. Mookerjee had sat in Nehru's first Cabinet as Minister for Industry and Supply, a Hindu Mahasabha figure included in the national government of 1947, and had resigned in April 1950 in protest against the Delhi Pact with Pakistan on the treatment of minorities in East Bengal. His speeches against the Code pressed the argument Prasad would repeat in his letter of September 1951: that to codify and reform the personal law of Hindus while leaving the personal law of Muslims untouched was discrimination dressed as reform, and that the honest alternatives were a common civil code for every citizen or the autonomy of each community's law. The argument's power was its form, an appeal to equality that the reformers could not dismiss as orthodoxy, and Nehru's replies were driven repeatedly to the sequencing defence, that Hindu law reform was the first step of a journey whose later stages circumstances would govern. In October 1951, weeks after Ambedkar's resignation, Mookerjee founded the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, and the discrimination argument travelled with him into the new party's founding vocabulary, where it has remained, through the party's later incarnation, as the standing indictment of the founding settlement from the Hindu Right. The article takes that indictment seriously as an argument and rejects it as a description: the settlement's asymmetry rested on the presence of an internal reform constituency and a century of legislative precedent within Hindu law, conditions whose absence elsewhere the founders stated on the record, and a sequencing that events interrupted is not the same thing as a symmetry that principle forbade. But the indictment's longevity is a fact of the record, and it confirms from the opposing side the claim this article defends from its own: that the outcome of 1947 to 1956 was constitutive, a choice whose terms set the argument for everyone who came after, including those who built a party on reversing it.

## The Clauses That Cut Deepest: Monogamy, Divorce and the Daughter's Share

Beneath the argument about authority ran a second argument about three clauses, and it is worth isolating them, because each one redistributed power inside the Hindu family and each drew a distinct species of opposition. The monogamy clause abolished the Hindu husband's legal capacity to take a second wife during the first marriage's subsistence, a capacity that classical law had permitted and that reformers documented in case after case of deserted first wives without remedy. Opposition to monogamy was rarely frontal; few members wished to defend polygamy in terms. It arrived instead as procedural caution, as the plea that social practice should change before law presumed to lead it, an argument whose effect was to preserve the practice by postponing its prohibition to a horizon that receded on schedule. The divorce clause cut deeper still. Classical Hindu marriage, in its dominant sacramental understanding, was an indissoluble union; the Code proposed dissolution by decree, on grounds including cruelty, desertion and adultery, available to wife and husband alike. For opponents this was the clause that converted a sacrament into a contract, and they said so in those words. For the women members and for Ambedkar, the indissolubility of marriage in a system that permitted the husband additional wives and unilateral desertion was not sanctity but a one sided trap, and the clause was the Code's moral centre.

The succession clauses redistributed not status but wealth, and they mobilised the most materially interested opposition. The draft gave the daughter a share in her father's property alongside the son, gave the widow an absolute rather than limited estate, and ranked the mother among the first heirs. Each provision struck the joint family at a different joint. The daughter's share threatened the doctrine that a daughter marries out of her natal family and takes her provision as dowry and maintenance; the widow's absolute estate threatened the reversioners, the male collaterals who had managed widows' property for generations in expectation of its return; the mother's rank threatened the agnatic principle that property descends through males. Landholders' lobbies and orthodox associations argued the provisions together as a design to fragment agricultural holdings and dissolve the family's economic unity, and the argument found hearers well beyond the orthodox camp, in a Congress Party whose rural base was the joint family itself. The government's answer, built from the Rau Committee's evidence, was that the joint family was already fragmenting under economic pressure, that the law's task was to distribute the fragments justly rather than to preserve an idealised unity at the price of women's destitution, and that the coparcenary's retention in the final Act showed the reform's respect for the institution's core. That the opponents of these clauses included propertied interests defending expectations of inheritance, and not only theologians defending texts, is a fact the debates record plainly, and it disciplines any account that treats the Code's opposition as purely religious in motivation. Religion supplied the vocabulary of the resistance. Property, in several of its most organised quarters, supplied the energy.

One further clause deserves record because its later career was the longest. The Code as drafted applied to Hindus defined expansively, gathering in Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs and every person not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew, and it overrode custom wherever custom and code conflicted, subject to savings the Acts would specify. The definitional clause made the Code an instrument of legal unification across a vast and varied population, many of whose members did not describe themselves as Hindus at all, and it drew objections from Sikh members and others that the settlement's champions answered with the constitutional text itself, whose explanation to the reform power adopted the same expansive reference. The episode prefigures a recurring feature of Indian personal law politics: the categories of the law are themselves legislative creations, older than the republic and stranger than the communities they name, and arguments conducted in the vocabulary of religious autonomy proceed within boundaries that statute drew.

## September 1951: A President, a March and a Resignation

The crisis of the Bill arrived in a single month, and its three events, read in sequence, expose the full machinery of the founding settlement under strain. The first event was epistolary. On 15 September 1951, President Rajendra Prasad wrote to Nehru a long note on the Hindu Code Bill, renewing the competence objection he had raised in 1948 and 1949 and adding two claims of consequence. The Bill, Prasad argued, was discriminatory, since it applied to one community alone; either the same law of marriage and property should govern every Indian, or the customary law of each community should be left alone. And if Parliament passed the measure regardless, the President proposed to watch its progress day by day and reserved his right to examine it on its merits before giving assent. Nehru replied the same day, in terms that left no room for the claim: the President had no power to go against the will of Parliament on a Bill it had considered and passed, and the whole conception of constitutional government stood against such an authority. The Attorney General, M. C. Setalvad, when his opinion was taken, supported the Prime Minister; the presidency created by the Constitution was bound by ministerial advice. Prasad accepted the position, and the republic's first great constitutional confrontation closed in correspondence rather than in crisis. These letters survive as primary evidence of a kind historians of the episode depend on, and the handling of such documentary material is a discipline in itself, of the sort set out for reporters and researchers in the account of [how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/): a document persuades only while its provenance and custody can be shown.

The second event took to the street. On 16 September 1951, Swami Karpatri, the ascetic founder of the Ram Rajya Parishad and the most formidable public campaigner against the Code, challenged Nehru to prove that even one section of the Bill accorded with the Shastras, offering to accept the entire Code if the government succeeded. The following day his followers marched on Parliament and were stopped by police at its approaches. The march's significance was presentational as much as numerical. It displayed, for newspaper readers across the country, a form of authority that Parliament could not vote down: saffron, ascetic and scriptural, claiming that the legislature had entered ground where its writ did not run. Between Prasad's note and Karpatri's procession, the Bill faced the same objection in constitutional dress and in religious dress within forty eight hours.

The third event ended the founding phase of the Code. Ambedkar tendered his resignation from the Cabinet by letter to the Prime Minister on 27 September 1951, and in the statement he laid before Parliament in October 1951 he placed the Bill's treatment among his stated grounds: the measure to which he had given years of labour had been allowed to stall, its marriage and divorce portions taken up in September and then abandoned without the government's full weight behind them. The resignation is commonly read as a personal rupture, and the personal element was real. Its structural meaning is larger. The Law Minister who embodied the state's reforming authority concluded that the authority would not in fact be exercised by the body that possessed it, and withdrew. Nehru, for his part, neither forced the Bill through the expiring Provisional Parliament nor abandoned it. His press communication of 15 January 1952 set out the government's position with care: the basic principles of the Bill stood accepted, the session had run out of time, no pending Bill could pass to the new Parliament, and it was possible and desirable that the measure be split into separate parts, each taken up on its own. The unified Code died in that communication. The four Acts of 1955 and 1956 were conceived in it.

## Beyond the Chamber: Petitions, Conferences and the Battle for Hindu Opinion

The parliamentary narrative understates the episode unless the country outside the chamber is kept in view, because both sides understood that the Assembly's hesitation could be moved by demonstrations of Hindu opinion and organised accordingly. The orthodox campaign built its machinery early. The Hindu Mahasabha, diminished as an electoral force after 1948, found in the Code an issue on which its conferences could again speak for a constituency, and its resolutions against the Bill charged the government with destroying Hindu religion under the forms of law. Swami Karpatri's Ram Rajya Parishad, founded in 1948, carried the campaign into the idiom of ascetic authority, holding that dharma was not the state's to codify and that a parliament of politicians could no more amend the shastras than it could amend the seasons. Sanatanist associations in the temple towns and the Hindi speaking north organised meetings, collected signatures and memorialised the Assembly in volumes that the record preserves, while sympathetic newspapers kept the controversy before readers between sessions. The campaign's theory was representational: to show that the community in whose name the government claimed to act rejected the act, and so to convert the competence objection from a lawyer's point into a demonstrated fact about Hindu India.

The government's supporters answered in kind, and their mobilisation is the half of the story that triumphalist accounts omit. The women's organisations that had given evidence to the Rau Committee collected their own signatures and sent their own deputations, and reformist Hindu opinion, in associations, universities and the press, argued that the orthodox claim to speak for the community was itself the fraud in the case, since the community's women, half of it, had never been consulted by the authorities who claimed its mandate. The contest over who represented Hindu opinion was unwinnable by either side in any form a returning officer could certify, which is precisely why the general election of 1951 to 1952 acquired its decisive importance in the story: it was the first occasion on which Hindu opinion, as part of the whole electorate, was actually counted on a programme containing the reform. The orthodox campaign's failure to convert its visibility into seats, and the Congress's retention of the reform in its manifesto through the campaign, together closed the representational argument that petitions could not. What remained for the opposition after 1952 was the merits, and on the merits, bill by bill, it lost. The sequence vindicates the article's structural reading once more. The asymmetry was not smuggled past an inattentive public. It was contested in the open, in conference and procession and print, by the most formidable religious mobilisation the early republic faced, and it survived because the reform's constituency, organised for two decades before the Bill existed, proved broader when counted than the constituency against it.

## The Resignation Letter of 27 September 1951, Read as a Constitutional Document

Ambedkar's letter of resignation, tendered to the Prime Minister on 27 September 1951, is usually excerpted for its sentences on the Hindu Code Bill and read as the climax of a personal story. Read whole, as a document of the founding, it is something more systematic: an inventory of the points at which its author judged the republic's practice to be falling short of its constitution, with the Bill's fate as the count he placed in the foreground of his public accounting. In the statement that accompanied and explained the resignation, laid before Parliament in October 1951, Ambedkar recorded that the Code, the labour of years and the measure by which he had hoped to leave his mark on the society the Constitution addressed, had been taken up in fragments, its most contested portions debated in September 1951 and then left without the government's decisive support, until the session's end made its abandonment a fact no assurance could disguise. He had stayed, the statement makes clear, through postponements he had protested, on the strength of expectations that the government would spend its authority on the measure. When the expectation failed, the office lost its purpose for him.

The resignation's other counts matter for the secularism argument because they show the same constitutional logic at work in its author across fields. Ambedkar charged the government with indifference to the condition and representation of the Scheduled Castes whose advancement he regarded as the republic's binding obligation, and with a foreign policy he believed had isolated India without securing it. The thread joining the counts is a theory of the state that the Code embodied in one field: that the Constitution's promises were mandates for action by the government of the day, not aspirations to be admired, and that a ministry which declined to act on them, from whatever prudential motive, had broken the instrument's terms as he understood them. Nehru's defenders answer, and Austin's institutional account supports them, that the prudential motives were themselves constitutional in character, since a reform carried by an expiring unelected assembly over a President's protest might have damaged the parliamentary authority on which all later reform depended. The disagreement between the two men in September 1951 was therefore not between principle and expediency. It was between two principles of democratic sequence: Ambedkar's, that a mandate to reform existed already in the freedom movement's commitments and the Constitution's text, and Nehru's, that a reform of this depth required the explicit sanction of a general election before it could bind. The election of 1951 to 1952 settled the sequence question in Nehru's favour as a matter of politics. It did not refute Ambedkar's reading, and the Code's eventual passage on the principles he had drafted stands as the record's own verdict that the substance had been his throughout.

There is a final reason the letter rewards close reading. It fixed, at the moment of rupture, the interpretation of the Bill that later scholarship inherited. Because the Law Minister resigned over the measure's stalling, the Bill entered public memory as a martyred reform, and its eventual passage in divided form could be narrated as a posthumous vindication rather than as the ordinary legislative success it also was. That framing, which the resignation created, has shaped every subsequent telling, including the scholarly disagreement this article adjudicates: Bhargava's defence reads the settlement philosophically, as the letter's author would have wished, while the political historians read the letter itself as a move within the game it protested. Both readings begin from the document of 27 September 1951, which is why the document, and not only the resignation it announced, belongs among the founding texts of Indian secularism.

## The Constitutional Logic: How Reform of One Community's Law Was Possible

The asymmetry that defines the episode was not a loophole. It was built from constitutional materials that the debates themselves cite. The freedom of religion guaranteed by the Constitution protects the practice of religion, and the same constitutional settlement empowers the state to regulate activity associated with religious practice and to legislate for social welfare and reform. The reform clause that mattered for the Code sits within the guarantee itself: nothing in the right to profess, practise and propagate religion prevents the state from making law providing for social welfare and reform, or for throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. The explanation appended to that guarantee counts Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists within the reference to Hindus for this purpose, which is why the four Acts of 1955 and 1956 govern those communities' family law as well. Parliament's power to enact the Code was thus textual, deliberate and debated; the founders wrote the reforming state into the article that protects religion, rather than leaving reform to implication.

### How did the Constitution empower Parliament to reform Hindu personal law?

The Constitution placed a social welfare and reform power inside the guarantee of religious freedom itself, so that legislation reforming practices associated with Hindu religious life, and opening Hindu religious institutions to all sections of Hindus, fell within Parliament's express competence, a textual basis the government's speakers cited throughout the debates of 1949 to 1951.

The companion decision, taken in the constitution making sittings, confirms that the abstention was designed rather than drifted into. When the Assembly debated the uniform civil code's place in the draft Constitution on 23 November 1948, members including Naziruddin Ahmad argued that a uniform code imposed on unwilling communities would violate the religious freedom the same document guaranteed, while K. M. Munshi and others defended the code as the necessary destination of equal citizenship. Ambedkar, replying for the Drafting Committee, declined to promise compulsion and pointed to a possible method: a future Parliament might begin with a code applicable only to those who declared themselves willing to be bound by it. The Assembly placed the code among the Directive Principles, binding as an endeavour and unenforceable as a right. The sequence of the two decisions, the Directive Principles placement of November 1948 beside the Code's introduction of September 1948, shows a legislature distinguishing deliberately between the community whose law it would reform at once, on the strength of that community's own organised demand, and the uniformity it would commend to a future it refused to coerce. Whatever else the asymmetry was, it was not inadvertence. Its authors debated the alternative in terms and chose.

Against Muslim personal law, no equivalent political path was cleared, and the reasons were stated on the record rather than concealed. The Constituent Assembly had declined to make a uniform civil code enforceable, placing it instead among the Directive Principles as an aspiration the state should endeavour to secure, a placement that registered both the ideal and the refusal to impose it on communities still raw from Partition. Nehru's government held, publicly and repeatedly, that reform of a community's personal law should come with that community's own demand behind it, and that a state which had just assured its largest minority of security in the aftermath of 1947 could not begin by rewriting that minority's family law under majoritarian pressure. Hindu law, by contrast, had been legislated on by colonial parliaments for a century, from the abolition of sati's legal protection to the widow remarriage legislation of 1856 and the property legislation of 1937; it arrived at independence already inside the state's legislative habit. The asymmetry, in short, had a doctrine (reform where the state already stood inside the law), a text (the reform power within the religious freedom guarantee), and a political ethic (no imposed reform on a vulnerable minority). Whether those three supports amount to a justification is the question the scholars took up, and it is to their disagreement that the argument turns.

## Principled Distance or Selective Reform: The Disagreement Adjudicated

Rajeev Bhargava's defence, developed in The Promise of India's Secular Democracy (2010), holds that Indian secularism was never a wall of separation. On Bhargava's reading, the Constitution equips the state with principled distance: it may engage religion or disengage from it, intervene or abstain, case by case, provided the engagement serves the constitutional values of freedom and equality rather than theological preference. Judged by that standard, the Hindu Code outcome is defensible in both directions at once. The state intervened in Hindu law because Hindu law was state-amenable, layered with a century of legislation and administered by state courts, and because intervention served equality for Hindu women. The state abstained on Muslim law because intervention there, in the conditions of the early 1950s, would have served neither freedom nor equality but majoritarian assertion against a minority whose consent had not been sought. Distance, on this account, is measured by principle, not by symmetry of treatment.

Pratap Bhanu Mehta's critique, stated in his essay "What is Constitutional Morality?" (Seminar, 2010) and sharpened in the commentary that followed 2014, presses the point where Bhargava's defence is thinnest. A doctrine that licenses intervention and abstention case by case supplies no discipline of its own; everything depends on the virtue of the intervener. Applied selectively, principled distance becomes a warrant for the state to reform where reform is popular with the majority and to discover restraint where reform is costly, and the asymmetry that began as a principled settlement hardens into an alibi. The post-2014 form of the critique adds that the Hindu Right's demand for a uniform civil code feeds on exactly this record: the state reformed the majority's law, froze the minority's, and thereby manufactured the grievance of unequal citizenship that the code's opponents of 1949, Mookerjee among them, had predicted on the floor.

The adjudication this article defends distinguishes the founding from its inheritance. At the founding, the asymmetry was constitutionally defensible, for the reasons Bhargava gives and for one he understates: the reform of Hindu law was carried against the majority community's own organised religious establishment, at real political cost, by a Parliament in which that community's representatives predominated. A state engaged in majoritarian favouritism does not ordinarily begin by defeating the majority's orthodoxy on marriage and inheritance. The abstention on Muslim law, in the shadow of Partition's violence and with the minority's leadership depleted by migration, was the reciprocal face of the same discipline. What has become harder to defend is the selective invocation of the settlement by later governments, which have treated Hindu law as permanently open to parliamentary revision while treating any judicial or legislative movement on minority personal law as a favour to be granted or withheld for electoral reasons. That selectivity is a failure of the settlement's heirs, not of the settlement. Mehta's critique lands on the inheritance; Bhargava's defence holds for the founding. The namable claim stands: the asymmetry was a constitutive choice, made for stated constitutional reasons, and its later abuse does not retrospectively convert it into an accident or a fraud.

The disagreement has a second layer that the adjudication should not flatten, because Mehta's chosen instrument, constitutional morality, is itself an inheritance from the founding debates. The phrase descends from Ambedkar's address to the Constituent Assembly on 4 November 1948, where, quoting the historian George Grote on the Greek republics, he warned that constitutional morality, a paramount reverence for the forms of the constitution and the habits that sustain them, is not a natural sentiment and must be cultivated. Mehta's essay of 2010 revives the phrase against a state whose secular practice he judges to have decayed into management of communities as blocs, each courted, each feared, none addressed as citizens. On that diagnosis, the asymmetry of 1956 is not the disease but its earliest symptom misread as health: the founding generation, Mehta's argument implies, bequeathed a form of secularism whose operation depended on statesmen willing to bear costs, Nehru absorbing a resignation and a presidential challenge rather than trimming, and a doctrine that requires virtue to function will fail in the hands of successors who practise secularism as arithmetic. Bhargava's rejoinder, implicit in his book's structure, is that no doctrine operates itself, and that the standard for judging a constitutional settlement is whether it supplies reasons that later actors can honour, not whether it can compel them. Principled distance supplied the reasons. The archive of 1947 to 1956 shows the reasons being used, in the chamber and in the correspondence, by actors who understood their cost.

A further consideration, visible only when the two positions are set against the primary record, is that the founding actors anticipated both critiques. Prasad's letter of 15 September 1951 is, in substance, the selective application objection stated sixty years before its scholarly form: a law for one community alone, he wrote, was discriminatory, and the state must either generalise its reform or abstain altogether. Ambedkar's resignation statement is, in substance, the constitutional morality objection: a government that will not spend its authority on its own measure has failed the constitution's discipline. The scholars of 2010 were therefore not discovering the settlement's tensions. They were inheriting an argument the founders had conducted in real time, with the stakes immediate and the outcome uncertain, and the durability of that argument across seven decades is the strongest evidence that the episode was constitutive rather than episodic. Settlements that merely end a quarrel do not generate a literature. Settlements that define a form do, and the Hindu Code settlement has generated one continuously, in seminar and courtroom and parliament, because the question it answered in one configuration returns in every generation in another: how a state committed to equal citizenship should comport itself toward the unequal laws its communities carry. The article's verdict follows the record. The founding answer was defensible, was defended at cost, and was framed in reasons that remain available. Its corruption in later selective use is an indictment of the users, and the distinction between a settlement and its abuse is the distinction on which any serious defence of Indian secularism must insist.

## The Political Explanation, Taken Seriously

No honest account can leave the asymmetry resting on doctrine alone, because the politicians who produced it left a record of calculation beside the record of principle. The Congress of 1949 to 1951 contained a large conservative bloc whose support Nehru needed on every other question, and the government's handling of the Bill bears the bloc's fingerprints: the repeated postponements, the decision to take the marriage and divorce portions first in September 1951 and then to let them drop, the preference for delay over division. The electoral asymmetry of resistance was also real, and participants named it. Hindu orthodoxy, however mobilised by Karpatri's campaign, was divided by region, sect and caste, and its political vehicles were weak; the Hindu Mahasabha was marginal and the Jana Sangh did not exist until October 1951. Muslim conservative resistance, by contrast, would have been read by the minority itself, four years after Partition, as a communal signal, and the Congress leadership priced that reading into every decision. Patel's caution in the correspondence of October 1948, his insistence that Hindu sentiment not be inflamed while the state was still consolidating, belongs to this register of the story: prudence about sequence, offered by a leader who shared neither Ambedkar's urgency nor Nehru's impatience.

Granville Austin's institutional reading, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), explains why the same measure that failed under these conditions succeeded once the conditions changed. Austin's account directs attention to machinery: the Congress's parliamentary majority after the election of 1951 to 1952, the manifesto commitment that converted the Code from a ministerial project into a party pledge, the whipping and committee management that carried the separate bills, and the accumulated authority of a Constitution whose text the opponents could no longer outflank. On Austin's telling, the Code's passage was a demonstration that the constitutional system could process a deeply divisive social question through ordinary legislative means, provided the electoral foundation was laid first. The reading is persuasive about mechanism and deliberately silent about meaning; it explains how the Acts passed without adjudicating whether the asymmetric settlement they completed was just.

The two explanations, political and doctrinal, are not rivals, and the article's verdict assigns each its weight. The political calculation determined the timing and the form: delay until an elected Parliament sat, division into four bills, compromise on the coparcenary. The constitutional doctrine determined the direction and the limit: reform of Hindu law by statute, abstention on Muslim law, both defended in the language of the text the Assembly had enacted. A purely cynical reading, in which doctrine was decoration on electoral arithmetic, fails against the record of September 1951, when the government absorbed a presidential confrontation and a Law Minister's resignation rather than either forcing the Bill or renouncing it. A purely doctrinal reading fails against the same month's evidence of postponement managed for party convenience. The settlement was principled in its architecture and political in its construction, and its durability comes from the first of those facts surviving the second.

## The Women Who Supplied the Evidence and the Votes

Summary accounts of the Code tend to cast Hindu women as the passive beneficiaries of a quarrel among men. The record contradicts the casting at every stage. The Rau Committee's inquiry of the 1940s received organised evidence from the All India Women's Conference, founded in 1927 and by then the principal national body pressing for codification of women's rights in marriage, inheritance and guardianship. The committee's draft bore the impress of that evidence; the daughter's share and the attack on the widow's limited estate entered the Code through channels that women's organisations had dug over two decades of resolutions, deputations and draft bills. Inside the Constituent Assembly, the argument was carried on the floor by members who were themselves veterans of that campaign. Hansa Mehta, who had worked on women's legal status since the 1930s, and Renuka Ray, who brought to the debates the authority of long organisational labour, spoke for the provisions that ministers treated as negotiable and refused the suggestion that the women's clauses could wait for a quieter session. Durgabai Deshmukh likewise aligned her considerable standing in the Assembly with the reform.

Their presence altered what the opposition had to say. Against a Law Minister, orthodox members could deploy the argument of the alien reformer imposing foreign notions on Hindu society, and they did. Against women members speaking Hindi in the chamber and citing the shastric plurality that Ambedkar had documented, the same argument lost its footing, because the demand for reform was visibly arising from within the community whose law was at issue. This matters for the secularism question that organises the article. The government's licence to reform Hindu law rested in part on a demonstrable internal constituency for reform, organised, articulate and present in the chamber. No comparable constituency for state-imposed reform of Muslim personal law presented itself to the Assembly in those years, and the women's organisations that supported the Code did not demand one. The asymmetry, viewed from this angle, tracks the sociology of demand as well as the theology of the two laws. Reform followed the community that had organised for it. That is a less elegant principle than principled distance, and the two together explain more than either alone.

The documentary trail of that constituency deserves a closer look, because it answers the charge that reform was a fashion imposed by a westernised ministry on an indifferent society. In 1946 the All India Women's Conference adopted an Indian Women's Charter of Rights and Duties, a document prepared under Hansa Mehta's leading hand, which demanded for women full equality in law, including rights in marriage, divorce, guardianship and inheritance that map, clause by clause, onto the Code the government introduced two years later. The charter was presented to the Constituent Assembly as the women's movement's brief for the constitution making itself, and its demands that the Assembly declined to entrench as fundamental rights reappeared as the programme of the Hindu Code, the statutory route being the one the Assembly's structure left open. When Mehta then sat in the Assembly and defended the Bill, she was not supporting a minister's initiative. She was prosecuting her own document, drafted before the ministry existed, through the only legislative channel available for it. Renuka Ray's interventions carried the same genealogy from the organisational side, the accumulated resolutions of the Conference through the 1930s and 1940s standing behind her speeches as their footnotes. This is the evidentiary basis on which the article rests its claim that the reform of Hindu law answered an internal constituency. The constituency left paper: charters, resolutions, evidence volumes before the Rau Committee, and speeches in the chamber. A government inventing a demand does not usually find the demand drafted, dated and delivered to it by the demanders in advance.

## The Election of 1951 to 1952: The Settlement's Second Founding

Between the resignation of September 1951 and the statutes of 1955 stands an event that accounts of the Code often treat as scenery and that this article treats as load bearing: the first general election, conducted across the winter of 1951 to 1952 on universal adult franchise. The Congress manifesto carried the substance of Hindu law reform among its commitments, so that the question the Provisional Parliament had declined to settle was placed, in general terms, before an electorate of a scale no Indian legislature had ever answered to. Nehru campaigned on the government's programme with the Bill's principles inside it, and his press communication of 15 January 1952, issued while the election was in progress, struck the balance the campaign required: the basic principles of the measure stood accepted by the party, the expiring Parliament had lacked the time and the standing to carry it, the new Parliament would receive it in divided and manageable parts. The formulation conceded the opposition's procedural case in full and surrendered nothing of the substance, an exchange whose wisdom the results appeared to ratify when the Congress returned with 364 of the Lok Sabha's 489 seats. The mandate objection that had shadowed the Bill since 1948 evaporated in that count. No member of the new House could argue that the body lacked the right to legislate for Hindus; the Hindus of India, voting alongside every other community in a common electorate, had returned the party pledged to the reform.

The election's crueller datum ran the other way. Ambedkar, standing in Bombay North, was defeated by the Congress candidate Narayan Sadoba Kajrolkar, and the author of the Code entered the Council of States in March 1952 as an opposition voice rather than returning to the Law Ministry whose work he had defined. The Acts of 1955 and 1956 were therefore piloted by other hands, through a Parliament in which their originator sat outside the government, and the fact disciplines any heroic telling in both directions. Against the telling in which Nehru betrayed the reform stands the electoral record: the reform was put to the country, endorsed, and enacted within one Parliament of the verdict. Against the telling in which the enactment was Nehru's personal triumph stands the personnel record: the minister who had made the measure unavoidable was excluded from its completion, and the completion bore the marks of his absence in the compromises the government accepted, above all the survival of the coparcenary, which a Law Ministry still driven by the Code's author might have contested harder. The settlement's second founding thus distributed credit and blame in a pattern the scholarly literature reproduces: Austin's institutional reading celebrates the machinery that the election supplied, while the tradition that reads the episode through Ambedkar's resignation records what the machinery cost and whom it excluded. Both are true, and the conjunction is the history.

## 1955 to 1956: The Code Completed as Four Acts

The second Parliament took the Code in the divided form Nehru's communication of January 1952 had foreshadowed, and the division proved to be a legislative masterstroke, because each part could gather its own majority and opponents had to defeat the reform four times rather than once. The Hindu Marriage Act, passed in 1955 and assented to on 18 May, made Hindu marriage monogamous, attached penalties to bigamy, admitted divorce and judicial separation to a sacramental institution that classical law had treated as indissoluble, and recognised customary rites, including saptapadi where it was customary, as sufficient solemnisation. Its Statement of Objects and Reasons presented the measure as completing, for marriage, the codification the Rau Committee had designed. The Hindu Succession Act followed on 17 June 1956, placing the widow, the daughter and the mother in the first class of heirs to a Hindu male's separate property and converting, by its fourteenth section, the limited estate of a Hindu woman into absolute ownership wherever she possessed property under that estate. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act of 25 August 1956 regularised the natural guardianship of minors, naming the father and after him the mother, and subordinated guardianship decisions to the welfare of the minor. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 21 December 1956 closed the set, validating the adoption of daughters, permitting a Hindu widow to adopt in her own right, and stating enforceable duties of maintenance toward wives, children, aged parents and widowed daughters in law.

### How did the Hindu Succession Act of 1956 change the property rights of Hindu widows?

The Succession Act converted the widow's limited estate, under which a Hindu widow could use her husband's property for life without power to alienate it freely, into absolute ownership of property she possessed, and placed her in the first class of heirs, transforming her from a maintenance claimant into a full owner in law.

The compromises inside the four Acts are as instructive as their victories. The Succession Act, while admitting daughters to the father's separate property and to a share computed on a notional partition, left the Mitakshara coparcenary intact: a son still acquired an interest in joint family property by birth, in a way a daughter did not, and the full equalisation of that birthright waited until the amendment of 2005, carried by a later Parliament under a different government. The Marriage Act retained the sacramental framing of the ceremony even while subjecting the union to registration, divorce and monogamy enforced by the criminal law. These retentions were the price the orthodox opposition extracted, not for passage, which it could no longer prevent, but for the acquiescence of the Congress's own conservative wing. They also mark the precise boundary of the founding settlement's ambition. The state reformed Hindu law as far as a parliamentary majority, assembled afresh for each bill, would carry it, and recorded the stopping point in the text. Anyone who wishes to argue that the Code was either a triumph or a betrayal must first explain why the stopping point fell where it did, in the divisions and committee rooms of 1955 and 1956.

A statute book victory is not a social fact, and the settlement's limits in enforcement belong to its honest description. The Marriage Act's monogamy depended on complaints reaching courts in a society where a second marriage could be solemnised in a village without any record the state would ever see, and prosecutions for bigamy under the new law were sparse in its first decades. Registration of Hindu marriages was left substantially to state rule making and remained patchy, weakening the evidentiary foundation on which a deserted wife's claim depended. The Succession Act's gifts to daughters were filtered through families' control of information and through the pressure on women to relinquish shares in favour of brothers, a pressure that social investigators documented for decades after 1956. None of this annuls the settlement, and the article does not present it as annulment. It locates the settlement correctly: what 1947 to 1956 decided was the location of authority and the content of the law's promise, the questions on which secularism's form turned. The conversion of promise into practice was assigned, by the same parliamentary method, to the slower agencies of courts, registrars, schools and the organised pressure of the women in whose name the promise had been made, and its incompleteness at any given later date measures the distance between a constitution's commitments and a society's habits, which is the distance Ambedkar's closing warnings to the Constituent Assembly in November 1949 had already marked as the republic's central problem.

## What the Code Did Not Do: The Unreformed Remainder

The settlement's asymmetry is usually stated as a contrast between two communities, but the map of what the Code left untouched is wider, and its full extent sharpens the argument. Christian personal law, governed substantially by the Indian Divorce Act of 1869 and the Indian Christian Marriage Act of 1872, remained unreformed in this period and for decades after, its divorce grounds so restrictive that Christian wives petitioned Parliament in vain well beyond 1956; the state's abstention there attracted a fraction of the commentary lavished on the Muslim case, because no organised political formation had made Christian law a symbol. Parsi personal law, codified in the Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act of 1936 through the community's own legislative initiative, illustrated the route the government professed to prefer for every community: reform drafted from within, carried by the community's own representatives, and enacted with consent, a route whose availability for the majority community in 1947, through the Rau process and the women's movement's two decades of work, was the factual premise of the whole enterprise. The Scheduled Tribes stood outside the Code's application altogether unless the state directed otherwise, a recognition written into the Acts that customary law in the tribal areas governed communities whose relation to the Hindu category was nominal at best. And the Special Marriage Act of 1954, enacted in the middle of the Code's passage, quietly constructed the settlement's other pillar: a civil marriage law open to any two Indians regardless of religion, through which a citizen could exit personal law by registration rather than by reform of the system itself.

Read together, these remainders describe a state proceeding community by community, instrument by instrument, at speeds set by internal demand and political capacity, and a uniform civil code receding into the Directive Principles' aspirational distance. That is either a principled pluralism or an abdication, and the scholarly disagreement rehearsed in this article is at bottom an argument about which description fits the whole map rather than its most contested corner. The article's judgement is that the map supports Bhargava on the founding period for a reason the two community comparison obscures: a state bent on majoritarian symbolism would not have bothered with the Parsi route's consent, the tribal exemption's restraint, or the civil marriage law's exit door, each of which limited the majority's law as an identity marker even while the Code reformed its content. The settlement's logic was the management of a plural legal order toward equality at the pace consent allowed, and its asymmetry between Hindu and Muslim law was the largest instance of a general method rather than a unique exception carved out for electoral reasons. Later governments inherited the method and, too often, only its convenience. The distinction between inheriting a method and exploiting it is the ground on which the settlement should be defended, and on which its exploiters should be judged.

## The Long Shadow: Shah Bano, Shayara Bano and the Uniform Civil Code

The settlement of 1956 did not freeze; it set the terms on which every later contest would be fought. When the Supreme Court decided Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum on 23 April 1985, holding that a divorced Muslim woman could claim maintenance under the general criminal procedure code, the judgment was received through the frame the Hindu Code had built: a state institution reforming minority personal law from outside, against the abstention the founding settlement had practised. The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act of 1986, by which the government of the day legislated the judgment's practical effect away, was defended and attacked in the same vocabulary. Defenders of the 1986 Act invoked community autonomy, the founding abstention's own ground. Its opponents invoked the Hindu Code precedent in reverse: a state that had reformed the majority's law by statute, they argued, owed the minority's women the same statutory protection, and the refusal to extend it was the asymmetry turned indefensible. Both sides were quoting 1956.

The Shayara Bano judgment of 22 August 2017, in which a Constitution Bench set aside instant triple talaq by a majority of three to two, and the statute of 2019 that criminalised its pronouncement, extended the pattern into territory the founders had marked as closed. Here the movement came through a court and then a Parliament acting on minority personal law with a reforming rationale drawn, in the government's public argument, from gender equality within the minority community itself, since the petitioners who carried the case were Muslim women. The episode fits the settlement's deeper logic better than its surface: intervention followed an organised internal constituency, as it had in the 1940s, and the state's abstention ended when the demand from within the community reached the courts. At the same time, the uniform civil code demand, descended from Mookerjee's floor argument of 1949 and from Prasad's letter of September 1951, continues to present the founding asymmetry as a standing injustice to be liquidated by uniformity. The article's analysis distinguishes that demand from Bhargava's defence on a precise ground: the code demand treats symmetry of treatment as itself the constitutional value, while principled distance treats equality and freedom as the values and symmetry as, at most, evidence. Which of those the Constitution enacted was decided, in practice, between 1947 and 1956, and decided for principled distance, by the only Parliament that has ever legislated the question in full.

The career of the 1986 Act supplies the pattern's most instructive variation, because it shows the settlement's vocabulary being used to reach a destination its authors would not have predicted. In Danial Latifi v. Union of India, decided on 28 September 2001, a Constitution Bench upheld the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act against the charge that it violated equality, but upheld it by construction: the reasonable and fair provision the Act required a former husband to make within the iddat period was read to extend to the divorced woman's future needs, so that the statute enacted to displace Shah Bano was interpreted to deliver much of what Shah Bano had ordered. The judgment is a small masterpiece of the founding settlement's method. The Court respected the form of parliamentary abstention, declined to strike down the minority's statute, and reached through interpretation for the equality value that principled distance names as the measure of legitimate engagement. Critics on one side called the construction an evasion of the confrontation the Constitution required; critics on the other called it reform by judiciary in defiance of the legislature's stated will. Both criticisms were available, in almost the same words, in the debates of 1949 to 1951, where Ambedkar had defended codification as the legislature's duty precisely because leaving reform to case by case adjudication delivered it unevenly and without democratic warrant. The wheel had come full circle: the argument for parliamentary reform of personal law, defeated as a comprehensive project for every community in the founding years, returned half a century later as the standard against which even the courts' ingenuity was found wanting.

## What the Nine Years Settled

The Hindu Code Bill began as a colonial committee's draft, survived a change of regime, a Law Minister's resignation, a President's constitutional challenge and an ascetic's march on Parliament, and emerged as four statutes that reordered marriage, inheritance, guardianship, adoption and maintenance for the majority of the population. Any of those facts would earn the episode its place in a chronicle. Its claim on the reader's attention is different. In settling the Bill, the founding generation also settled, without ever announcing a doctrine, the operating form of Indian secularism: a state authorised by its constitution to reform religion's civil law, exercising that authority where an internal constituency and a legislative history made reform an act of self government, and withholding it where reform would have arrived as conquest. The choice was constitutive because everything since has been negotiation within it. The Shah Bano reversal, the triple talaq litigation, the uniform civil code campaign and the scholarly argument between Bhargava's defence and Mehta's critique are not sequels to the Hindu Code Bill. They are its continuation by other means, conducted in the rooms it built. The Bill's opponents of 1949 feared that codification would transfer authority over Hindu life from tradition to Parliament. They were right about the transfer and wrong about its finality: authority transferred to Parliament can be argued with, amended and reversed, and the nine years from 1947 to 1956 are the record of a republic learning, clause by clause and crisis by crisis, how to argue.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Hindu Code Bill?

The Hindu Code Bill was a single draft statute, built on the Rau Committee's report of 1947 and introduced in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) by Law Minister B. R. Ambedkar in September 1948, which sought to codify and reform the personal law governing Hindus. It covered marriage, divorce, succession, adoption, maintenance, minority and guardianship in one measure, replacing the divergent schools of classical interpretation and regional custom with enacted text. Its leading provisions made Hindu marriage monogamous, introduced divorce, gave daughters a share in a father's property, converted the widow's limited estate into fuller rights, and permitted the adoption of daughters. The Bill was debated between 1949 and 1951 without being carried, stalled amid organised orthodox opposition and a presidential challenge, and lost its minister when Ambedkar resigned in September 1951. It was never enacted as one statute. After the first general election, Parliament took the measure in divided form and passed it as four Acts in 1955 and 1956, which together carried most of the original design into law.

### Q: Why did Ambedkar resign over the Hindu Code Bill?

B. R. Ambedkar tendered his resignation from the Cabinet by letter to Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru on 27 September 1951, and in the statement he laid before Parliament the following month he placed the Hindu Code Bill's treatment at the head of his public accounting. The measure had been the labour of his years as Law Minister, first in the Rau Committee's revision and then through three years of debate. In September 1951 the government took up the marriage and divorce portions and then allowed them to drop without spending its full authority, leaving the Bill to expire with the Provisional Parliament. Ambedkar had stayed through earlier postponements he had protested, on the expectation that the government would carry its own measure when the moment came. The September abandonment destroyed that expectation, and he concluded that his office had lost its purpose. His statement also recorded wider differences, over the condition of the Scheduled Castes and over foreign policy, but the Code was the ground he chose to make emblematic: a government that would not enact its own reform, in his judgement, had broken faith with the Constitution's promises.

### Q: When was the Hindu Code Bill passed?

The Hindu Code Bill was never passed as a single measure, and the dates of its passage are the dates of its four successor statutes. The unified Bill was introduced in September 1948, debated in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) and the Provisional Parliament between 1949 and 1951, and allowed to lapse when that body expired, after Ambedkar's resignation of 27 September 1951. Nehru's communication of 15 January 1952 announced the method of its revival: division into separate parts, each legislated on its own by the elected Parliament. The Hindu Marriage Act came first, receiving assent on 18 May 1955. The three remaining parts followed in a single year: the Hindu Succession Act on 17 June 1956, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act on 25 August 1956, and the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act on 21 December 1956. The nine years from the Cabinet's decision of September 1947 to the last assent of December 1956 therefore divide into three years of drafting and introduction, three years of stalemate, an electoral interval, and two years in which an elected Parliament enacted, in four statutes, the substance of the Code.

### Q: Who introduced the Hindu Code Bill?

The Hindu Code Bill was introduced in the Constituent Assembly (Legislative) in September 1948 by B. R. Ambedkar, the Law Minister in Jawaharlal Nehru's Cabinet, who had taken charge of the measure on entering the government on 15 August 1947 and had revised the draft through his ministry. The text he introduced was not written from nothing in that year. Its architecture came from the Hindu Law Committee chaired by B. N. Rau, whose report and draft Code of 1947 supplied the Bill's structure and most of its provisions, and an earlier version of the codification project had reached the Central Legislative Assembly in April 1947, in the last months of British rule, before lapsing at the transfer of power. The Cabinet's decision of September 1947 revived the project as government business, and Ambedkar made it his own measure, defending it clause by clause through the debates of 1949 to 1951 and staking his office on its passage. After his resignation, other hands completed the work: the four Acts of 1955 and 1956 were piloted through the elected Parliament by the Law Ministry of a government from which the Bill's author was absent.

### Q: Why was Muslim personal law not reformed alongside Hindu law?

Muslim personal law was left unreformed for reasons the founding generation stated on the record, in three connected parts. The first was assurance: a state formed in Partition's shadow, which had pledged security to the large Muslim minority that remained in India, judged that rewriting that minority's family law by a majority dominated Parliament would be read as conquest rather than reform, four years after the violence of 1947. The second was doctrine: Nehru's government held that reform of a community's personal law should answer an organised demand from within that community, as the women's movement and two decades of legislative effort had supplied for Hindu law, and no such constituency for imposed codification presented itself in those years. The third was constitutional design: the Constituent Assembly placed the uniform civil code among the Directive Principles on 23 November 1948, an endeavour commended to the state rather than a right enforceable against it, which recorded both the aspiration and the refusal of compulsion. Hindu law, by contrast, had been legislated on for a century and was administered as civil law by state courts. Rajeev Bhargava later named the resulting pattern principled distance.

### Q: What are the four Acts the Hindu Code Bill became?

The Hindu Code Bill became four statutes, enacted by the first elected Parliament after the unified measure had lapsed. The Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 made Hindu marriage monogamous, penalised bigamy, introduced divorce and judicial separation, and recognised customary ceremonies such as saptapadi as valid solemnisation. The Hindu Succession Act of 1956 governed inheritance, placing the widow, daughter and mother in the first class of heirs to a man's separate property and converting a Hindu woman's limited estate into absolute ownership by its fourteenth section, while retaining the Mitakshara coparcenary for sons. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act of 1956 regulated the custody and property of minors, naming the father and after him the mother as natural guardians and subordinating every guardianship question to the minor's welfare. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 1956 completed the set, validating the adoption of daughters, permitting a Hindu widow to adopt in her own right, and stating enforceable duties to maintain wives, children, aged parents and widowed daughters in law. Together the four Acts carried the Rau Committee's design into force.

### Q: How does the Hindu Code Bill connect to current Uniform Civil Code debates?

The Hindu Code Bill connects to uniform civil code debates because the argument for a single code for every citizen was first made, in its modern form, against the Bill itself. Syama Prasad Mookerjee pressed it on the floor of the Constituent Assembly (Legislative), and President Rajendra Prasad repeated it in his letter of 15 September 1951: a state that reformed one community's law while leaving another's untouched legislated unequally, and the honest courses were uniformity or abstention. The Constituent Assembly had already placed a uniform civil code among the Directive Principles in November 1948 as an endeavour rather than a command, so the founding settlement contained both the aspiration and the refusal to impose it, and the Hindu Code's asymmetric outcome became the standing exhibit in every later round of the argument. Supporters of a uniform code cite 1956 as proof that asymmetry was the founding generation's unfinished business. Defenders of the settlement, following Rajeev Bhargava, answer that the Constitution's measure is equality and freedom rather than symmetry of treatment, and that the founding choice was principled distance, deliberately taken.

### Q: What did the Hindu Code Bill change about a daughter's right to inherit her father's property?

Under the classical Mitakshara law that governed most of India before the Code, a daughter ranked far behind sons and male agnates in succession to her father's property, and in his separate estate she inherited, where she inherited at all, only in default of nearer male heirs, taking a limited interest hedged by reversion to his family. The reform that the Rau Committee drafted, Ambedkar defended, and the Hindu Succession Act of 1956 enacted, placed the daughter in the first class of heirs to her father's separate property, alongside his widow, his mother and his sons, entitled to an equal share as an absolute owner rather than a limited holder. The change was deliberately partial, and its boundary is the measure of the compromise of 1956: within the Mitakshara coparcenary, the joint family property in which a son took an interest by birth, the daughter acquired no birthright of her own, reaching the coparcenary only through a share computed on a notional partition at her father's death. Full equality inside the coparcenary waited for a later amendment. What 1956 settled was the daughter's standing as an heir in her own right.

### Q: How did the Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 change Hindu marriage law?

The Hindu Marriage Act, assented to on 18 May 1955, rebuilt Hindu marriage on statutory foundations in four principal moves. It imposed monogamy on a system that had permitted a Hindu husband plural wives, attaching the criminal law's penalties for bigamy to a second marriage during a subsisting first. It introduced divorce and judicial separation to a union that the dominant sacramental understanding had treated as indissoluble, making dissolution available to husband and wife on stated grounds such as cruelty, desertion and adultery. It preserved the community's own forms within the new frame, recognising the customary rites of either party, including saptapadi where that rite was customary, as sufficient to constitute a valid marriage, so that reform governed the institution's legal consequences without prescribing a single ceremony for a varied population. And it stated uniform conditions of capacity, including minimum ages and prohibited degrees, across the communities the Act covered, which included Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs as well as Hindus. The result kept marriage's sacramental clothing while subjecting the relationship to registration, monogamy and dissolution by decree, a characteristically negotiated product of the settlement this article describes.

### Q: What role did President Rajendra Prasad play in the opposition to the Hindu Code Bill?

Rajendra Prasad opposed the Hindu Code Bill from three positions, and his opposition supplied the resistance with its constitutional edge. As President of the Constituent Assembly he intervened in 1948 and 1949 with the competence objection: a body elected on a restricted franchise to frame a constitution lacked the mandate to rewrite the domestic law of the Hindu community, a task belonging to an elected Parliament. As the republic's first President he carried the argument into correspondence, and his letter to Nehru of 15 September 1951 added two claims. The Bill, he argued, was discriminatory, since it applied to one community alone, and the state must either apply the same law of marriage and property to every Indian or leave each community's law alone. And he reserved a presidential right to examine the measure on its merits before granting assent, proposing to watch its progress day by day. Nehru's reply of the same day denied that any such discretion existed, the Attorney General M. C. Setalvad supported the Prime Minister, and Prasad accepted the constitutional position. His assent was never tested, because the Bill lapsed, but his letter fixed the presidency's limits and the discrimination argument in the record.

### Q: Which organisations led the public campaign against the Hindu Code Bill?

The public campaign against the Hindu Code Bill was led by organisations of orthodox Hindu opinion, mobilising outside Parliament while their allies delayed within it. The Hindu Mahasabha, whose electoral strength had collapsed after 1948, recovered a public role through the controversy, its conferences passing resolutions that charged the government with destroying Hindu religion under legal forms. The Ram Rajya Parishad, founded by Swami Karpatri in 1948, supplied the campaign's ascetic authority and its street presence, arguing that dharma stood beyond legislative competence and carrying the agitation to its climax in the challenge and march of 16 and 17 September 1951. Sanatanist associations and religious bodies in the temple towns and across the Hindi speaking north organised meetings, gathered signatures and sent memorials to the Assembly, building the appearance and partly the reality of a community refusing its own reform. The campaign's strategy was representational throughout: to demonstrate that the Hindus in whose name the government acted rejected the act, and so to strengthen the competence objection in the chamber. Its failure is written in the election that followed, in which the Congress retained the reform in its programme and won 364 seats.

### Q: How did the first general election of 1951 to 1952 change the Bill's prospects in Parliament?

The first general election transformed the Hindu Code Bill's prospects by removing the objection that had stalled it for three years. From 1948 onward, opponents from Rajendra Prasad downward had argued that the Constituent Assembly and its successor, the Provisional Parliament, elected on a restricted franchise before independence, lacked the mandate to rewrite Hindu domestic law. The election of 1951 to 1952, conducted on universal adult franchise with the Congress manifesto carrying the substance of Hindu law reform among its commitments, answered the objection in the only currency it recognised. The Congress victory, 364 of the Lok Sabha's 489 seats, meant that the Hindus of India, voting in a common electorate, had returned the party pledged to the reform, and no member of the new House could deny its right to legislate. Nehru's communication of 15 January 1952 had prepared the method, division into separate parts, and the elected Parliament applied it, passing the four Acts in 1955 and 1956. The election also excluded the Bill's author from its completion, since Ambedkar lost his Bombay North seat, a conjunction that shaped the compromises, above all the coparcenary's survival, inside the final statutes.

### Q: How did the Hindu Code Bill define who counted as a Hindu for its purposes?

The Hindu Code Bill, following the draft of the Rau Committee and the pattern the four Acts retained, defined its subject population expansively rather than by belief. The Code applied to Hindus in the religious sense and equally to Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs, to converts to any of those religions, and by a residuary clause to every person domiciled in India who was not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew, unless it could be shown that such a person would not have been governed by Hindu law or custom. The definition gathered in communities that did not describe themselves as Hindu at all, and it drew objections from members who resisted the absorption of distinct traditions into a single legal category. The government's answer rested on the Constitution itself, whose explanation appended to the religious freedom guarantee adopted the same expansive reference to Hindus for the purposes of the reform power, counting Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists within it. The definitional clause therefore did constitutional work: it marked the territory across which Parliament's authority to reform personal law ran, and it fixed the founding settlement's boundary between the communities the state would legislate for and those it would leave alone.

### Q: How did Syama Prasad Mookerjee argue against the Hindu Code Bill in Parliament?

Syama Prasad Mookerjee's parliamentary case against the Hindu Code Bill rested on equality turned against the reformers. A member of Nehru's first Cabinet until his resignation in April 1950 over the Delhi Pact with Pakistan, Mookerjee spoke from standing that the government could not dismiss as obscurantist, and his argument avoided defending the classical law's content. The Bill, he contended, singled out one community for a discipline the state dared not apply to others: if Parliament held the authority and the duty to reform personal law in the name of equal citizenship, the honest measure was a civil code binding every Indian, and a code for Hindus alone was discrimination wearing reform's clothing. Where the orthodox opposition argued from scripture, Mookerjee argued from the Constitution's own promises, which made his intervention the one the government was obliged to answer in principle rather than in procedure. Nehru's replies were driven to the sequencing defence, that Hindu law reform was a first step whose later stages circumstance would govern. Mookerjee carried the argument into the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded in October 1951, and it has remained the Hindu Right's standing indictment of the founding settlement since.

### Q: Why was the unified Hindu Code split into four separate Acts in 1955 and 1956?

The unified Hindu Code was split into four separate Acts because division solved the three problems that had defeated the single measure. The first was parliamentary arithmetic: a nine year Bill touching marriage, succession, guardianship and adoption together allowed every opponent of any part to vote against the whole, while separate bills let each part gather its own majority among members who could support succession reform and reserve their doubts on divorce. The second was time: the experience of 1949 to 1951 had shown that a comprehensive code could be talked to death in an expiring assembly, whereas compact statutes could be carried within a session's discipline. The third was presentational: Nehru's communication of 15 January 1952 had committed the government to the method, describing division as possible and desirable, and the commitment reassured wavering Congress members that acceptance of one part bound them to nothing beyond it. The strategy's cost was the Code's unity and some of its coherence, since compromises could be struck part by part, most consequentially the coparcenary's survival in the Succession Act. Its benefit was enactment itself. The four Acts of 1955 and 1956 exist because the single Bill of 1948 does not.

### Q: What changes did the Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act of 1956 introduce?

The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, assented to on 21 December 1956, reformed the two subjects on which classical law had been most tightly bound to the son centred family. In adoption, it broke the classical rule that a daughter could not be adopted and that adoption existed to secure a male heir for spiritual and proprietary ends: the Act permitted the adoption of daughters on equal terms, allowed a Hindu widow to adopt in her own right rather than as her dead husband's instrument, required the adopted child's complete severance from the birth family and integration into the adoptive one, and attached the validity of every adoption to the child's welfare rather than to ritual qualifications. In maintenance, it converted obligations that classical law had left to conscience and custom into enforceable legal duties, stating the claims of a wife, of minor and dependent children, of aged and infirm parents and of the widowed daughter in law against the family's property and earners. The pairing was the Rau design's logic: adoption and maintenance, the mechanisms by which the joint family provided for its vulnerable members, were restated together as law.

### Q: How did women's organisations and women legislators support the Hindu Code Bill?

Women's organisations and women legislators supplied the Hindu Code Bill with its organised constituency, its evidence and several of its parliamentary voices. The All India Women's Conference, founded in 1927, had pressed for codified reform of women's rights through the 1930s, gave structured evidence to the Rau Committee in the 1940s, and adopted in 1946 an Indian Women's Charter of Rights and Duties, prepared under Hansa Mehta's leading hand, whose demands for equality in marriage, divorce, guardianship and inheritance map closely onto the Code introduced two years later. Inside the Constituent Assembly, the argument was carried by members who were veterans of that campaign. Hansa Mehta defended the Bill as the prosecution of her own document through the only legislative channel open to it, Renuka Ray brought the authority of the Conference's accumulated resolutions to the debates, and Durgabai Deshmukh aligned her standing with the reform. Their presence answered the orthodox claim that codification was a ministry's fashion imposed on an unwilling community: the demand was visible, organised and female, arising from within the community whose law was at issue. The founding settlement's defenders later rested the reform's legitimacy partly on exactly this record of internal demand.

### Q: How does Granville Austin explain the Hindu Code Bill's eventual passage?

Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), explains the Hindu Code Bill's eventual passage institutionally, as a demonstration that the constitutional system could process a deeply divisive social question through ordinary legislative means once its electoral foundation was laid. On Austin's account, the difference between the failure of 1948 to 1951 and the success of 1955 to 1956 was machinery rather than conviction: the first general election gave the Congress a commanding and freshly mandated majority, the party's manifesto converted the Code from a ministerial project into a pledge on which members had been elected, the division of the measure into four bills made majorities assembleable part by part, and the Constitution's text, with its reform power written into the guarantee of religious freedom, left opponents no procedural ground from which to outflank the government's competence. The reading is persuasive about mechanism and deliberately reserved about meaning. Austin shows how a Parliament so constructed could carry the Acts; he does not adjudicate whether the asymmetric settlement the Acts completed was just, a question he leaves to the argument between the settlement's philosophical defenders and its critics that this article takes up.

### Q: Why did the Mitakshara coparcenary survive the Hindu Succession Act of 1956?

The Mitakshara coparcenary survived the Hindu Succession Act of 1956 because it was the price of the Act's passage, exacted by the same coalition of propertied and orthodox resistance that had contested the Code since 1948. The coparcenary, the joint family property in which a son, grandson and great grandson acquired an interest by birth, was the economic core of the institution the reformers' critics accused them of dissolving, and its abolition would have united against the Bill landed members of the ruling party itself, whose rural base the joint family organised. The government of 1955 and 1956, working without Ambedkar and legislating part by part, chose a settlement that admitted the daughter, the widow and the mother to the first class of heirs in a man's separate property and converted the widow's limited estate into absolute ownership, while leaving the coparcenary's birthright intact for sons. A daughter reached joint family property only through a share computed on a notional partition at her father's death. The retention was understood at the time as a compromise rather than a principle, and the coparcenary's full equalisation waited until a later Parliament amended the Act in 2005.

### Q: What was Swami Karpatri's challenge to Nehru over the Hindu Code Bill in September 1951?

Swami Karpatri's challenge was the street theatre of the orthodox campaign at its September 1951 climax, staged in the same week as the presidential confrontation over the Bill. Karpatri, the ascetic founder of the Ram Rajya Parishad and the most formidable public campaigner against codification, addressed a meeting on 16 September 1951 and challenged the Prime Minister to a public debate on the measure's scriptural warrant: if Nehru and his colleagues could establish that even one section of the proposed Hindu Code accorded with the Shastras, Karpatri declared, he would accept the entire Code. The following day his followers marched on Parliament in support of the challenge and were stopped by police at its approaches, in a scuffle that the orthodox press reported with indignation. The episode's force was symbolic rather than numerical. It displayed, for newspaper readers across India, a form of authority that no division in the chamber could vote down, ascetic, scriptural and claiming that the legislature had entered ground where its writ did not run, and it confronted Nehru with a debating partner who could not be answered in Parliament because he denied Parliament's jurisdiction over the question.
