The punctuation and lineation of the second quarto of Romeo and Juliet, the 1599 volume printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby, are the least visible and most consequential layer of the play's text. Every reader who has ever met the lovers has met them through marks that the quarto's compositors set and that four centuries of editors have silently replaced: commas where the modern page prints exclamation points, colons where the modern page prints full stops, verse dissolved into prose in the middle of the play's most famous speech, prose dressed up as verse in a host's welcome, parentheses opened and never closed, a question mark standing where a command should end. The words of the play descend from that quarto with high fidelity. The marks arrive filtered, translated, and in places invented, and because a mark can decide how a line is breathed, parsed, and understood, the filter is not a cosmetic layer. It is a second authorship, exercised in the printing house first and in the editor's study ever since.

![The second quarto page of Romeo and Juliet with its original punctuation and lineation - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-83.webp)

The verdict argued through this study can be stated plainly at the outset. The pointing of the second quarto, Q2 in the shorthand of the trade, is neither the playwright's own score nor random noise from the press. It is print-shop punctuation, set by working compositors over authorial copy, in a period system that used stops to pace a voice rather than to diagram a sentence. Its lineation is likewise mechanical at exactly the places where it looks strangest: verse was set as prose to save space, prose was set as verse because the copy or the compositor's eye took it for verse, and a passage the author revised stands printed twice, in two versions, because nobody struck the first one out. An editor is therefore right to repunctuate for a modern reader and right to relineate where the quarto's breaks are accidents of the bench. The editor is wrong only when the substitution is silent at a place where the mark decides the meaning. At such places the quarto's stop is evidence, and evidence is owed to the reader in the note even when the editor overrules it in the text. The cruxes gathered here are the places where that duty bites.

That verdict cuts against two comfortable habits. The first treats the early pointing as sacred, a residue of the author's own phrasing that editing can only corrupt. The second treats it as beneath notice, pepper scattered by workmen who neither knew nor cared what the sentences meant. The documentary record supports neither. The same quarto that preserves the play's words with a fidelity no other early text approaches also prints a doubled word in the dawn parting, opens three parentheses in the balcony scene and closes none of them, and points one famous reproach as a question. A page capable of those accidents is not a score. A page whose colons and commas consistently serve pause and emphasis rather than grammar is not chaos either. Between reverence and contempt lies the working position this study defends: the marks are testimony, admissible, examinable, and sometimes contradicted, from witnesses who stood closer to the play's first life than any editor can, and who were also, demonstrably, fallible tradesmen in a hurry.

A word on method and scope. Quotations from the quarto in this study follow its text in original spelling, as the diplomatic transcriptions print it, because a discussion of marks that modernizes the marks in its own evidence would refute itself. Comparisons with modern practice name the Folger Shakespeare where a specific modern text is meant, since the Folger is the reading text in widest circulation, and speak of modern editions generally where the practice is common to the trade. Single cruxes that belong to single words, the rose line's missing question mark, the dagger line's variants, the possessive in the night speech, have their own studies in this series and are cited here at passage, as evidence for the system, without retrying their cases. The system is this study's property: the bench that set the marks, the copy that may or may not have carried them, the notation they formed, and the long editorial afterlife in which they were replaced, regretted, and at last made answerable.

## The Book on the Bench: How Q2 Was Made

The second quarto is a small book with a large claim on its title page. It announces itself as "The most excellent and lamentable Tragedie, of Romeo and Juliet. Newly corrected, augmented, and amended", printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby, to be sold at Burby's shop near the Exchange, in 1599. The standard bibliography counts forty-six leaves, gathered in fours and signed from A toward M, and some twelve copies survive. A quarto of that size was not printed page by page in reading order. The unit of work was the forme, the locked frame of type that printed one side of a sheet: four pages at a time for a quarto, imposed so that when the sheet was printed on both sides, folded, and gathered, the pages fell into sequence. Composition and presswork interleaved. While one forme stood at the press, the compositors set the next, and the type of an early forme had to be washed, broken up, and distributed back into the cases before its letters could serve again in a later one.

Those mechanics are not background colour. They are the generating conditions of nearly every crux in this study. A compositor who set type by the forme, working from manuscript copy he could not easily consult twice, made his decisions about stops and line breaks at speed, under the discipline of the measure: each line of type had to fill its width exactly, which meant that spelling stretched or shrank and spacing widened or tightened at the line's end, and that a verse line too long for the measure had to be turned, its overflow carried down and indented. Space was the shop's scarcest commodity after time. A page that ran long threatened the casting of the whole sheet, and the remedies were mechanical before they were literary: compress the setting, run speeches together, and, at the limit, set verse as prose, since prose fills a page without the white space that verse line endings waste. The reader who understands the bench understands why the quarto's accidents cluster where they do, at page ends, at forme boundaries, and inside long speeches, rather than distributing themselves evenly like errors of understanding would.

For a book of this size, the standard account of quarto production describes the setting as seriatim: the compositor followed his copy in order, page after page and forme after forme, rather than casting off the whole manuscript in advance and setting distant pages simultaneously, as the makers of a great folio were driven to do. Seriatim setting spares a small book the folio's characteristic disaster, the miscalculated cast-off that leaves a compositor with too much copy for the pages remaining and forces wholesale compression at a gathering's end. It does not spare the book the local pressures. Each page still had to end where the measure ended, whether or not a sentence ended with it; each forme's completion still released its type for redistribution while later formes were being set from the same dwindling cases; and a long speech crossing a forme boundary passed, mid-sentence, from one hour's work to another's, with the copy the only continuity. The second quarto's cruxes respect that geography. Its prose compression falls inside a single long speech on a single page. Its doubled passage straddles the junction of two scenes. Its stray parentheses scatter through one scene's stint. Nothing in the book requires the hypothesis of a shop in crisis. Everything in it is consistent with a shop in routine, which is the more damning and the more useful conclusion, since routine, not crisis, is what produced the text the world inherited.

Paper ruled the economics beneath the type. A copy of the quarto consumed eleven and a half sheets, and paper was the largest single cost in the manufacture of an early book, the expense against which every other economy was measured. An edition ran to hundreds of copies, and the sheets multiplied accordingly: a saving of a single page in the setting, repeated across a gathering and an edition, was real money, and a compositor who felt his page running long was spending his employer's paper with every line of white space he allowed. Verse is prodigal with white space by design, since a short line leaves its measure unfilled whether the sense has finished or not. Prose fills the measure to the last sort. The conversion of verse to prose at a tight place in the Queen Mab speech is thus not a mystery requiring a villain or a theory. It is the predictable act of a tradesman inside an economy, and the wonder is not that it happened once but that, in a book of three thousand lines set at speed, the legible instances are so few. The bench's routine respected the verse far oftener than it robbed it, and the pointing and lineation that survive are, by the same economy, the product of men who had no time to ruin what they were not required to touch.

Creede's shop was a busy and respectable house, and its most famous product has not wanted for hostile reviews of its workmanship. A verdict in The Library, preserved in Harry Hoppe's study of the first quarto, called the work of the second quarto's compositors "of the worst description", sprinkled with reversed letters and misreadings on every page. Hoppe's own catalogue of the quarto's misprints bears the gibe out at the level of the letter: turned sorts printing a name upside down, a "Nurce" for a Nurse, single letters swapped in common words. The gibe should be kept in proportion. Letter-level error is the ordinary weather of hand-press printing, and the second quarto's words, as distinct from its letters, transmit the play more completely and more accurately than any rival witness. The point for the study of its marks is narrower and more useful. The compositors were tradesmen working at trade speed, attentive to the look and fit of a page, intermittently attentive to sense. Their punctuation, which no one in the shop had been charged to make consistent, must be read as the residue of that attention: real, patterned, and nobody's considered design.

### What is a catchword doing at the foot of a Q2 page?

A catchword is the first word of the next page, printed at the foot of one page to guide the binder in gathering the sheets. It is shop furniture, not text. The second quarto's pages carry them throughout, and a transcription that prints them inline can make a page seem to stutter, repeating a word it has not reached.

The catchwords matter to a study of cruxes for one practical reason. They are the visible seams of the forme system, the proof on the page itself that the book was manufactured in units of four pages rather than composed as a continuous scroll. Where a modern reader sees a single flowing text, the quarto's own furniture keeps announcing the machinery: a running title across the top of each page, "The most excellent and lamentable Tragedie" on the left-hand page and "of Romeo and Juliet" on the right, a signature letter and number at the foot marking the gathering, and the catchword pointing forward to a page that, at the moment of printing, might not yet have been set. Verse lines break across those seams without apology. A sentence begun at the foot of one page finishes at the head of the next, and the compositor who set the second portion worked from the same copy but at a different hour, with a differently stocked case and a differently advanced sense of how much room remained. The crux student who ignores the seams will keep mistaking the shop's joints for the author's phrasing.

One further piece of furniture deserves notice because its absence is itself a datum. The quarto prints no act and scene divisions of the modern kind. Its landmarks are entrances, exits, and the two Chorus speeches, the first headed simply as the Prologue. The tidy grid of five acts and numbered scenes through which every modern reader navigates the play is editorial scaffolding, supplied by later centuries from the Folio's practice and from classical precedent. Line references, those indispensable coordinates of modern commentary, hang from that scaffolding. When this study cites a speech by act, scene, and line, the citation follows the modern editions' grid, and the grid's modernity is worth remembering once: the quarto's first readers found their way by who was on stage, not by numbered addresses, and the pointing and lineation they met served listeners and readers who navigated by ear.

## Copy and Hands: What Stood Between the Playwright and the Page

Every mark in the second quarto has a pedigree, and the pedigree has three generations: the copy the compositors set from, the hands that set it, and the presswork that fixed it in ink. The scholarly argument about the quarto's punctuation is, at bottom, an argument about the first two generations. If the copy was the author's own working draft, its pointing, however light, might carry authorial phrasing into print. If the copy was a transcript made by a careless scribe, the pointing might be the scribe's. If the compositors repunctuated as freely as compositors are known to have done, the pointing might belong to neither. The cruxes cannot be weighed until the pedigree is weighed, and the pedigree has been fought over for a century and a half.

The nineteenth century's most influential editors of the play, William George Clark and William Aldis Wright of the Cambridge Shakespeare, delivered the hostile version with unusual confidence. It was certain, they wrote, that the second quarto "was not printed from the author's MS., but from a transcript, the writer of which was not only careless, but thought fit to take unwarrantable liberties with the text. In passing through his hands, many passages were thus transmuted from poetry to prose." The transcript theory did heavy work for its holders. It explained the quarto's disorder without blaming the author for it, and it licensed the editor to repair the disorder freely, since the pointing and lineation being repaired were a meddling scribe's rather than the playwright's. Alexander Pope had felt the same licence a century earlier, adopting readings from the despised first quarto wherever the second offended him, and the Cambridge editors noted with disapproval that Pope felt it "too strongly". A theory about copy had become, as such theories always become, a permission slip for a style of editing.

The twentieth century replaced the scribe with the author. The prevailing account, argued by Brian Gibbons in his Arden edition of 1980 and carried forward by René Weis in the Arden Third Series edition of 2012, holds that the copy behind the second quarto was the playwright's foul papers: the working draft itself, complete with its false starts and unfinished revisions. The evidence is of the kind this study trades in. A revised passage stands printed twice, in its first and second forms, because the deletion that should have removed the first form was never made or never marked. Stage directions preserve the draft's looseness, naming entrances in terms a fair copy prepared for the playhouse would have tidied. Spellings and pointings vary with the freedom of a manuscript that answered to nobody's house style. On the foul-papers account, some of the quarto's accidentals could be the author's own, and the editor's duty of respect rises accordingly. Weis states the equation without flinching, identifying the second quarto's copy as Shakespeare's foul papers, while still importing most of the first quarto's stage directions into his text, a reminder that even the most copy-faithful editor edits.

Between the two accounts lies the question the cruxes actually turn on, and it is narrower than the debate's rhetoric suggests. Whichever manuscript stood on the compositor's bench, authorial draft or scribal transcript, the pointing that reached print passed through the hands of men who set type for a living, and the study of those hands has its own discipline, analytical bibliography, with its own triumphs and its own overreach. The triumph belongs to Charlton Hinman, whose Printing and Proof-Reading of the First Folio of Shakespeare, published in 1963, showed that the Folio's compositors could be told apart and their stints mapped. Hinman identified hundreds of distinctively damaged pieces of type and traced their recurrences through the volume, proving which pages were set from which cases and in what order, and he combined that physical evidence with the workmen's spelling habits, their preferences among variant forms of common words, and even their spacing habits around commas, to assign page after page to Compositor A, B, C, D, or E. The Folio's punctuation could thereafter be discussed with a precision that had been impossible before: a comma on a given page belonged, with measurable probability, to a named workman with known habits, and not to the author at all.

The method's prestige rests on that Folio foundation, and its application to a small quarto from another shop and another decade is an act of extrapolation that the scholarship itself teaches readers to grade carefully. Peter Blayney adapted Hinman's methods for quarto texts, and the adaptation works: type recurrence and press figures can order a quarto's formes and expose its presswork. Compositor identification inside a quarto is a harder matter. The discriminating spellings are fewer, the stints shorter, the copies of the book scarcer, and for the second quarto of this play no division of the setting between named or lettered hands has won general acceptance. Claims in this area properly carry the bibliographer's "possibly". The quarto's pages show the symptoms of more than one temperament, stretches of careful pointing against stretches of haste, but symptom is not attribution, and an honest account of Q2 says so. Later bibliographers have pressed the caution further. T. H. Howard-Hill revised several of Hinman's own Folio attributions with fresh evidence, and Pervez Rizvi argued in the Papers of the Bibliographical Society of America in 2016 that spelling evidence is weaker than its users assumed, because compositors demonstrably adjusted their spellings to justify their lines, bending the very habits by which scholars hoped to fingerprint them. Justification, the need to fill the measure, deforms spelling and spacing alike; the fingerprint smudges exactly where the work was tightest.

The pedigree that emerges is therefore layered, and the layers have different evidential values. The words of Q2 descend from copy very close to the author, foul papers on the prevailing account, and they carry the authority that closeness confers. The pointing descends from the same copy through at least one corrupting filter, the compositors' habits and haste, and possibly through a second, if any part of the transcript theory survives. The lineation descends through the same filters plus a third, the economics of the page, which could override both copy and compositor when space ran short. An editor weighing a Q2 comma is weighing a mark that is authorial at one or two removes and mechanical in its final form. An editor weighing a Q2 line break is weighing something more mechanical still. The disagreement between the transcript school and the foul-papers school matters for the history of editing, and this study awards it to the foul-papers account on the strength of the doubled passage and the draft-like directions. For the practical question, how much trust a stop deserves, the two schools converge more than either admitted: on both accounts, the stop reached print through hands that were not the author's, and its claim on a modern reader is the claim of early testimony, not of instruction.

The errors that pepper the quarto sort themselves, under the same analysis, into three kinds with three different evidential meanings, and a crux student should keep the kinds apart. Errors of the hand are the turned and wrong-fount sorts of Hoppe's catalogue, a letter inverted in the stick or drawn from the wrong box: they prove haste and nothing else. Errors of the eye are misreadings of the copy, where the compositor's glance converted the manuscript's letterforms into the wrong word: Romeo's boast that with love's light wings he did "orepearch" the orchard walls preserves such a glance, the copy's word for climbing over, misread into a form no lexicon knows. Errors of judgment, if the word judgment is not too grand, are the stops and line breaks themselves, set by rule of thumb at speed. The kinds matter because they fail independently. A page can be full of turned letters and pointed with care, or clean of misprints and pointed by rote, and the second quarto is both by turns. The critic who argues from a passage's pointing to the quality of its words, or from its words to its pointing, has confused the strata, and much confused writing about this text, in both the reverent and the contemptuous traditions, is exactly that confusion at work.

## A Grammar of Pauses: The Pointing System Q2 Actually Uses

To call the second quarto's punctuation chaotic, as generations of editors did before discarding it, is to measure it against the wrong standard. The modern system is grammatical: its marks diagram the syntax of a sentence for a silent reader's eye, nesting clause inside clause with a hierarchy of comma, semicolon, colon, and full stop. The system the quarto's compositors worked in was only partly that. M. B. Parkes, in Pause and Effect, his 1992 history of punctuation in the West, distinguishes the two great functions the marks have served: a rhetorical function, guiding a reader in the rhythm and timing of performance aloud, and a grammatical function, exposing syntactic structure to the eye. Earlier practice, Parkes shows, leaned toward the voice; modern practice leans toward the eye, and the balance was still shifting in 1599. A page of Q2 is pointed mainly for a reader who would hear the lines, in the head or aloud, and its stops are better understood as a notation of pauses than as a map of clauses. Judged as a map, the pointing fails constantly. Judged as a notation, much of it falls into place.

Percy Simpson made that case for Shakespeare's texts in Shakespearian Punctuation, published by the Clarendon Press in 1911, and his preface states the grievance with a schoolmaster's patience. "It is a common practice at the present day to treat the punctuation of seventeenth-century books as beneath serious notice", he wrote; editors, if they mentioned it at all, "describe it as chaotic and warn the reader that they have been driven to abandon it". Against the charge of chaos Simpson set a catalogue, forty-two heads of usage gathered from the early texts: light stopping as a positive style; the comma that marks a metrical pause rather than a syntactic one; the emphasizing comma, isolating a word for weight; the comma after a vocative, present or absent by rhythm rather than rule; the colon that introduces reported speech; the full stop set down in an incomplete sentence where the voice, not the grammar, had finished; and, most useful to this study, the use of the question mark in exclamations, the period's habit of letting the interrogative point do the work the exclamation mark performs on a modern page. Simpson's catalogue has worn in places, and where his case overreaches is weighed in this series' study of [punctuation and meaning in Romeo and Juliet](/2026/07/10/punctuation-and-meaning-analysis/), which argues the marks as interpretation in their own right. Its core finding stands. The early marks obey a different logic, and an editor who cannot state the logic will mistake its products for errors.

The working repertory of Q2 can be inventoried from its pages. The comma is the default joint, set wherever the voice might check, including places a grammarian would forbid one, between a subject and its verb when the subject is long. The colon is the system's workhorse stop, stronger than the comma and weaker than the full stop, and the quarto reaches for it constantly: it closes lines of the Prologue sonnet, punctuates Romeo's first sight of Juliet, and strings the Friar's testimony. The full stop is comparatively rare and comparatively final; sentences run long in Q2 because the stop that would end them keeps being deferred in favour of the colon's continuation. The question mark appears oftener than a modern reader expects and does double duty, closing commands and cries as readily as questions. The parenthesis marks matter set off from the main current of a speech, an aside, a qualification, a stage whisper of the syntax. The semicolon, a mainstay of the Folio page a generation later, is scarce to the point of absence in the passages collated for this study. The exclamation mark does not appear at all in those passages. A modern page, with its semicolons marshalling subordination and its exclamation points detonating at intervals, is not a cleaned version of Q2's page. It is a translation into another notation, and like every translation it decides things the original left open.

The Friar's first soliloquy, the speech that opens the second act's third scene in modern numbering, gives the colon's characteristic work in its purest form. The speech is a string of paradoxes about the earth and her children, and Q2 hangs the paradoxes on colons: "The earth that's natures mother is her tombe, / What is her burying graue, that is her wombe: / And from her wombe children of diuers kinde / We sucking on her naturall bosome finde: / Many for many, vertues excellent: / None but for some, and yet all different." Each colon suspends a proposition and lets its inversion swing from it, mother from tomb, excellence from difference, the mark functioning as the hinge of a balance rather than as a joint in an argument. A grammatical pointing must decide what the colon declines to decide, whether each clause explains, follows from, or merely succeeds the one before, and modern editions distribute full stops, semicolons, and commas across the speech according to each editor's resolution of that question. The quarto's notation holds the paradoxes in suspension, unresolved by design or by habit, and the speech's famous doubleness, virtue and vice in one rind, is enacted by its stops before it is stated by its words. Here the rhetorical system is not a defective version of the grammatical one. It is the better instrument for the matter in hand, and the editor who translates it should feel the loss as a loss, which is all that this study's verdict, at bottom, asks.

### Why does the Friar's Act 5 narrative run on commas where a modern page prints full stops?

The narrative runs on commas because Q2's stops measure breath, not syntax. The Friar's account is one long act of testimony, and the quarto chains its clauses with commas and the occasional colon, letting the sentence accumulate like speech before a judge. Modern full stops cut the testimony into findings, tidier and colder.

The Friar's examination speech in the tomb scene shows the system at full stretch. He stands, in his own words in the quarto's spelling, "both to impeach and purge / My selfe condemned, and my selfe excusde", and the narrative that follows runs for some forty lines of verse on a skeleton of commas, held together by the occasional colon: "Yet most suspected as the time and place / Doth make against me of this direfull murther". Clause rides on clause, the marriage, the potion, the letter, the miscarriage, each new fact joined to the last with the lightest of stops, because the speech is testimony and testimony does not pause for paragraphing. A modern edition breaks the run into sentences, and must: the grammatical reader needs the joints exposed. Something is gained, intelligibility at a glance, and something is spent, the accumulating pressure of a man talking for his life in one held breath. Neither page is wrong. They are pointed for different acts of reading, and the editor's choice between them is a choice about what the scene is.

The parenthesis deserves its paragraph, because Q2's handling of it is the repertory's most revealing habit. Used well, the mark does delicate work. In the Prologue, the second line of the sonnet is set inside parentheses, "(In faire Verona where we lay our Scene)", a locative aside bracketed off from the sentence it interrupts, and the bracket tells the voice to drop and hurry slightly, exactly as a modern comma pair would. In the lovers' first exchange, Romeo's plea carries a balanced parenthesis of the same kind: "They pray (grant thou) least faith turne to dispaire", the entreaty tucked inside the argument. Set against those courtesies, the balcony scene's parentheses are delinquent. Three times in that scene the quarto opens a parenthesis and never closes it: before "night" in Juliet's "What man art thou, that thus beschreend in (night", before "am" in Romeo's answer, "I know not how to tell thee who I (am", and before "walls" in "With loues light wings did I orepearch these (walls". A compositor's parenthesis sort, dropped into the line and never matched, is the likeliest mechanism, though a copyist's or author's stray mark cannot be excluded. Either way the lesson is the one this study keeps drawing. The mark is testimony about the shop and the copy, valuable precisely because its failures are so legible, and an editor deletes the strays without a murmur, as every editor has. What no editor should do is upgrade the same system's successes into the author's intentions. The page that cannot close a parenthesis has not earned that faith, and the page that points the Prologue's aside so deftly has not earned contempt. Both judgments at once are the discipline.

Two further features of the quarto's surface belong to the same accidental system and perish in the same silent translation. The first is italic. Q2 sets the dialogue in roman type but picks out proper names in italic, so that a page bristles with slanted signposts, Romeo, Juliet, Verona, Titan, each name flagged for the eye as the pointing flags pauses for the ear. The device is a sorting system, a typographic grammar of persons and places running parallel to the stops, and it does real work in a book whose readers might be following an unfamiliar Italian story at speed. Every modern edition drops it without a word, printing the names in the text's own roman, and the drop is so universal that few readers know a distinction existed to be dropped. The second feature is smaller: the circumflex that Q2 sets over the vocative O in places, "ô brawling loue", "ô be some other name", distinguishing the cry of address from the letter and the interjection at a glance. Modern pages print a bare O and let context sort the cry from the conjunction. Neither feature is a crux in itself. Together they measure the depth of the re-dressing every modern text performs: not merely stops and line breaks but the whole visual grammar of the page, replaced wholesale, as a matter of course, by editors who would hesitate to alter a single substantive word without a footnote. The accidentals are where editors live. The substantives are merely where they visit, and the tenancy leaves no forwarding address in most editions.

## Pointing Cruxes: Five Passages Where a Mark Decides

The system becomes concrete only at passages. Five exhibits follow, chosen because each turns on mechanism rather than taste: a parenthesis doing a comma's work, a catalogue stripped of its detonations, a vocative left unpointed, a chain of questions that thinks aloud, and a reproach that ends in a question mark. In each case the quarto's pointing is quoted first, in its own spelling, because the spelling and the pointing are one surface, and modernizing either in a quotation about the other falsifies the evidence.

The Prologue sonnet opens the book and announces its notation at once. Q2 prints: "Two housholds both alike in dignitie, / (In faire Verona where we lay our Scene) / From auncient grudge, breake to new mutinie, / Where ciuill bloud makes ciuill hands vncleane: / From forth the fatall loynes of these two foes, / A paire of starre-crost louers, take their life". The whole octave is one sentence. The parenthesis demotes the Verona line to an aside, a piece of stage geography whispered inside the sentence. Colons close the fourth and sixth lines, the period's hinge stop, holding the period open while grievance accumulates toward outcome, and the sentence does not reach its full stop until "their Parents strife" at the octave's end. The sestet repeats the architecture in miniature, a single period suspended across six lines to the closing promise that the players' toil shall mend what the summary missed. A modern text articulates the same words into shorter periods: the Folger Shakespeare, the most conservative of reading texts, keeps the quarto's parenthesis but sets a full stop after the fourth line and another after the eighth, making two sentences where the quarto suspends one. Other modern texts level the parenthesis to commas as well. The differences are small and the effect is not. Even the comma inside "A paire of starre-crost louers, take their life", set between subject and verb across the line break, does rhythmic work a grammatical system forbids, isolating "take their life" for the voice to land on. None of this proves the pointing authorial. It proves it functional, a set of decisions about emphasis and suspension that every modernization rearranges, whether or not the rearrangement is announced.

### Why does Romeo's oxymoron catalogue carry no exclamation marks in Q2?

Because the exclamation mark was not in the shop's working stock for such a passage. Q2 points the catalogue with commas and a single colon, letting the oxymorons accumulate in one level rush. The detonations a modern reader hears, phrase by phrase, are supplied by editors, and they change a sigh into a series of shouts.

Romeo's first set piece, the oxymoron catalogue of the opening scene, is the clearest casualty of the translation between notations. Q2 prints the speech as a single flowing accumulation: "Why then ô brawling loue, ô louing hate, / O any thing of nothing first created: / O heauie lightnes se, serious vanitie, / Mishapen Chaos of welseeing formes, / Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fier, sicke health, / Still waking sleepe that is not what it is". Commas carry the whole string; one colon, after "created", gives the voice its only full check; nowhere does the page raise its voice. A modern edition prints the same words as a volley of exclamations, each oxymoron detonated separately, and the speech that results is louder, wittier, and more performed. The quarto's Romeo is doing something else, reciting a litany in one breath, a young man showing off a rhetoric he has by heart, the phrases tumbling because the feeling behind them is second-hand. Editors who add the exclamation points are not vandalizing the text; they are obeying the modern notation's rule that apostrophe takes the mark of exclamation. They are also, inevitably, directing the speech. The crux in miniature is that both pages are internally consistent and mutually exclusive, and only one of them can be printed. The old pointing survives, for the reader who wants it, in facsimiles and old-spelling texts, and an honest modern note at the speech should say that the shouting is editorial.

The first-meeting sonnet turns on the opposite accident, a comma withheld. Juliet's reply to Romeo's pilgrim conceit begins in Q2 without any mark after the vocative: "Good Pilgrim you do wrong your hand too much". The modern page prints "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much", fencing the address with commas and slowing the line into courtesy before correction. Q2's unpointed run lets the address and the rebuke collide, and the collision is arguably the truer theatre: Juliet is fencing at speed, matching a stranger's conceit in his own form, and her first line has no time for the comma's bow. The claim should not be pressed into an intention. A missing comma is the commonest accident in any early book, and this one may mean nothing at all. That is precisely why it belongs in a survey of cruxes. It marks the boundary of what pointing evidence can do. Where the mark is present, it can be argued from; where it is absent, the argument must confess that absence in this period is the default state of the page, and build nothing on it.

Romeo's first sight of Juliet gives the colon a solo. Q2 points the quatrain: "O she doth teach the torches to burn bright: / It seemes she hangs vpon the cheeke of night: / As a rich Iewel in an Ethiops eare, / Bewtie too rich for vse, for earth too deare". Three colons in four lines, and not one of them joins two clauses in the grammatical sense. Each suspends the image at the moment of its completion and lets the next line's comparison swing from it, torch from cheek, jewel from beauty, the similes accumulating by suspension exactly as the Friar's paradoxes did. The modern page detonates the first line with an exclamation point, levels the second into the third with a line break and no stop at all in some texts, and closes the quatrain with the full stop the quarto's final colon refuses. The quarto's Romeo is held in the posture of looking, each perception arriving before the last has been discharged. The modern Romeo exclaims, compares, and concludes. Both are readable. Only one of them is the first printed record of how the speech was paced, and an edition that translates the colons owes the translation a note at least once in the scene, so that a reader meeting the speech in performance, where pacing is the whole art, knows a pacing existed before the one in hand.

Juliet's potion speech in the fourth act shows the question mark working at full capacity, and shows it working grammatically before it shows it working strangely. The speech is a chain of hypotheses, and Q2 points the chain with the interrogative throughout: "Come Violl, what if this mixture do not worke at all?" Each feared outcome arrives as a question the speaker puts to the empty room, and the pointing keeps the fear interrogative, a mind cross-examining itself, rather than declarative. The chain's pressure builds through subordinate clauses hung on commas, "theres a fearfull poynt" closed by a colon, until the speech's one moment of outward address breaks the pattern and breaks it oddly: "stay Tybalt, stay?" A command to a ghost, pointed with a question mark. Simpson's catalogue has a heading for exactly this, the period's use of the interrogative point in exclamations and commands, where the mark registers the voice rising rather than the sentence asking. Modern editions print an exclamation or a full stop, tidying the ghost's interruption into an order. The quarto's mark is stranger and better theatre, a cry that is half plea, and an editor who changes it should know, and say, that the change is a translation between notations and not the correction of an error. The speech then closes on the plainest stop in the book: "Romeo, Romeo, Romeo, heeres drinke, I drinke to thee". A full stop, after the triple name, as level as a door closing. The notation could do finality when finality was wanted.

The last exhibit is the play's last word of judgment. The Prince, surveying the tomb, opens his sentence of Verona in Q2 with a question: "See what a scourge is laide vpon your hate?" The interrogative hangs over the whole reckoning. Read with the quarto's mark, the Prince is not pronouncing but demanding, putting the evidence to the two fathers as a question they must answer; the lines that follow, heaven finding means to kill their joys with love, his own lost kinsmen, arrive as the answer the question extracts. Modern editions print a full stop or no stop at all at the line's end, and the speech settles into verdict, magisterial and closed. The difference is one sort of type, a curved mark against a point, and it reverses the posture of the play's highest authority at the moment of his greatest authority. No parallel passage and no compositor's habit settles which pointing the author would have chosen, because the choice lives in the accidental layer where certainty does not survive. This is the class of crux for which the verdict of this study was written. Print the modern stop, since the modern reader's grammar requires a settled sentence; record the quarto's question in the note, since a reader deciding how the Prince speaks is deciding something real about the play, and deserves to know the first printed page decided it the other way.

## Lineation Cruxes: Where the Verse Breaks, and Where It Only Seems To

Lineation is the second half of the quarto's accidental layer, and the half where the bench's economics speak loudest. A line break in verse is not decoration. It tells the voice where the metrical unit closes, tells the eye how speeches balance and answer one another, and in dialogue it can assign the halves of a shared line between two speakers like shares in a single breath. Q2's breaks descend from copy that was probably verse-lined by its author, but they passed through the same filters as the pointing, plus one more: the page itself, whose width fixed the measure and whose depth fixed the take. The cruxes of lineation are the places where a modern editor must decide whether a break in the quarto is a fact about the poem or a fact about the printing.

The famous case stands early, in Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, and this study cites it at passage because its allocation argument belongs to another article in this series. The facts of the page are these. The speech opens in verse: "O then I see Queene Mab hath bin with you". Then, at "She is the Fairies midwife", the verse gives out. For the whole descriptive core of the speech, the catalogue of the chariot, the traces, the whip, the dreams visited on courtiers, lawyers, ladies, parsons, and soldiers, Q2 sets prose, tight justified paragraphs that run on until "much misfortune bodes". At "This is the hag, when maides lie on their backs" the verse lineation resumes and holds to the speech's end. Nearly forty lines of a modern edition's verse stand in the copy-text as a prose block. The explanation favoured in the Arden tradition is compositorial compression: prose setting saves the white space that verse endings waste, and a compositor running long on his page reached for the saving. No editor prints the prose. Every modern text relineates the block as verse, and the relineation is certainly right, since the passage scans, rhymes in places, and resumes verse at both ends as though a frame had been lifted off it. But the episode fixes the correct attitude toward Q2's lineation in general. The quarto's most spectacular break is not a reading. It is a budget decision, visible as such, and the editor who reverses it silently is not concealing a doubt. The doubt begins only where the mechanism is not so legible.

### What happens to Capulet's welcome when prose is dressed as verse?

It gains a false dignity and loses its bustle. Set as verse, the welcome reads as ceremony, a host performing hospitality in measured lines. Printed as prose, as the first quarto and modern editions have it, the same words bustle and interrupt themselves, a man talking across a room filling with guests.

The reverse accident, prose set as verse, waits a page later in the same scene, and it is the more instructive crux because editors divide over it in silence. Capulet's welcome to his guests enters Q2 lineated as verse: "Welcome gentlemen, Ladies that haue their toes / Vnplagued with Cornes, will walke about with you". The lines scan loosely, in the four-stress swing of a man talking rhythmically, and the compositor, or the copy before him, took them for verse and set them as verse, line under line, through the welcome and into the exchange with his cousin about their dancing days. The first quarto prints the matter as prose, and modern editions print prose, restoring the bustle of a host working a room. The crux is real because the passage lives on the border: ceremonial prose in this period often moves in verse's shadow, and a case can be made that the copy itself wavered. What cannot be defended is the invisibility of the choice in most reading texts, where the prose simply appears, as obvious as daylight, and the quarto's verse dress goes unrecorded. The wider census of [lineation and lost verse in the quartos](/2026/07/03/lineation-lost-verse-quartos/) shows the same border disputed across the play's early texts: again and again, the line between verse and prose in these books is a judgment that print first made mechanically and editors have remade silently ever since.

The doubled dawn passage is the lineation crux that is also a window into the copy, and it too is cited here at passage, since its argument about revision belongs to another study in this series. At the close of the balcony scene Q2 gives Romeo a dawn quatrain of his own: "The grey eyde morne smiles on the frowning night, / Checkring the Easterne Clouds with streaks of light, / And darknes se fleckted like a drunkard reeles, / From forth daies pathway, made by Tytans wheeles", before his exit couplet, "Hence will I to my ghostly Friers close cell, / His helpe to craue, and my deare hap to tell". A page later the Friar enters with a basket and speaks the same dawn again: "The grey-eyed morne smiles on the frowning night, / Checking the Easterne clowdes with streaks of light: / And fleckeld darknes se like a drunkard reeles, / From forth daies path, and Titans burning wheeles". Editors print the Friar's version and delete Romeo's, judging the quatrain an authorial first thought that revision reassigned. For this study the treasure is smaller and stranger. The two settings of nearly the same words differ in their pointing, a comma after "light" in the first, a colon in the second, as they differ in their wording, "Checkring" against "Checking", "made by Tytans wheeles" against "Titans burning wheeles". Whatever stood in the copy, the stops did not stand still between one setting and the next, a few pages apart, in the same shop, in the same week. Pointing was being supplied fresh at the bench, passage by passage. No single exhibit in the quarto makes the mechanism plainer, and the systematic comparison of [Q1 against Q2 and what the differences reveal](/2026/04/10/q1-vs-q2-romeo-and-juliet-differences/) shows the same freshness at work wherever the two texts can be collated: accidentals are the least stable layer in transmission, the layer each new setting rewrites.

Speech division is lineation's quieter sibling, and the balcony scene holds its neatest crux. Twice, Juliet's lines in Q2 carry a second speaker hidden inside them. The quarto prints: "And follow thee my L. throughout the world. Madam." The "Madam" is the Nurse, calling from within, and the quarto gives her no speech prefix, no line of her own, nothing but the word standing at the end of Juliet's verse line like a stone in a shoe. A breath later the device repeats: "I do beseech thee (by and by I come) Madam." Modern editions extract the calls, assign them to the Nurse within, and set them as a separate speech, which is certainly what the theatre did with them. The repair is right and its cost is worth one sentence of notice. On Q2's page the interruption is typographically invisible, a summons absorbed into the heroine's own utterance, and a reader of the quarto experiences the calls as Juliet's thoughts made audible. Lineation and speech division, the humblest parts of an editor's work, decide even whose voice is speaking.

The feast scene's prose opening shows speech division working at its humblest and most exposed. Q2 distributes the servingmen's bustle among speakers identified by number alone, prefixes reading "1", "2", and "3" against the dialogue, with a generic "Ser." where the copy or the compositor declined to distinguish. The men banter about trenchers, joint stools, and the porter, and the quarto leaves their individuality at the level of a tally: three voices, interchangeable by design, since the scene's comedy is the household machine and not its parts. Modern editions stabilize the tally into a cast, printing First Servant, Second Servant, Third Servant, and assigning each speech with a consistency the copy-text does not attempt, occasionally redistributing a line where the sense seems to demand a different speaker from the one the number indicates. The editorial work is invisible and mostly harmless. It is also a decision, taken afresh in every edition, about whether the quarto's numbered anonymity is a fact about the scene, servants as functions in a great house, or an accident of a copy that never troubled to name them. Prose offers no metre to arbitrate the question, which is why the prose scenes' speech divisions wander more freely between editions than the verse scenes' do. In verse, the line itself testifies. In prose, the editor is the only witness, and the quarto's numerals are the closest thing to testimony the page provides.

### Why is Capulet's final answer one half-line short in Q2?

Because a half-line of the copy never reached print. Q2 gives Capulet "This is my daughters ioynture, for no more" and lets Montague answer as though a question had been asked. The missing demand, "Can I demand", enters the text from the later tradition, and editors print the repair without always flagging the seam.

The short line matters beyond its inches. It is the one class of lineation crux where the quarto is not ambiguous but defective, a place where the copy's verse measure itself fails and the failure can be proved from the dialogue's logic: Montague's "But I can giue thee more" answers a challenge that the printed text never issues. Every editor completes the exchange, and the completion descends through the later quarto and Folio tradition rather than from Q2 itself. Set beside the doubled dawn quatrain, the short line completes a matched pair of exhibits about the copy. In one place the copy carried more than the play needed, an unrevised alternative; in the other it carried less, a dropped half-line on the way to the press. Between surplus and deficit, the foul papers show their character as a working draft imperfectly tidied, and the compositor emerges as neither villain nor scribe but as the man who printed what was in front of him, whole where it was whole and broken where it was broken.

One letter-level exhibit closes the section, because it calibrates the shop's proof-reading better than any manifesto. In the dawn parting, where Juliet argues that the bird was the nightingale, Q2 prints her promise about the light: "Ile say yon gray is not the the mornings eye". The doubled article stands in the copy-text of the world's most edited love scene, uncorrected in the printing. Stop-press correction existed in Creede's shop, and copies of early books often differ at corrected points, but this doubling survived into every copy, a small fossil of the standard of attention the text actually received. An editor corrects it without a note, rightly. Its value here is as a control on every larger claim. A shop that shipped "the the" in its most careful book was not proof-reading for sense at the level of the phrase, and its punctuation, set by the same hands at the same speed, should be credited with exactly the authority that standard of attention can bear, no less and, crucially, no more.

A last lineation habit deserves record, because it governs every exchange of short speeches in the play. Where two speakers share a single pentameter, each contributing a half-line, Q2 simply prints each contribution as a line of its own, flush at the margin, indistinguishable in dress from a full line. The join between the halves exists only in the metre, and a reader of the quarto must hear it unaided: Capulet's short answer in the tomb and Montague's reply stand as two lines, and nothing on the page announces that they compose, between them, the verse's single measure. Modern editions supply the announcement typographically, indenting the completing half-line or aligning the shares so that the eye performs the completion the quarto left to the ear. The convention is a genuine service to silent readers, and it is also a pure editorial diagram, a piece of analysis printed as though it were text. Where the sharing is disputed, where scholars divide over whether two half-lines join into one measure or stand as broken verse in their own right, the modern page conceals the dispute inside its indentation, and only the collation, or a glance at the quarto's flush and undemonstrative lines, reveals that a rhythmic judgment was ever taken.

## One Opening of the Book: The Balcony Scene as Q2 Prints It

The system, the copy, and the bench meet on every page, but a single opening shows the meeting best, and the balcony scene's first page in Q2 can stand for the whole book. Romeo opens below the window in the quarto's level pointing: "He ieasts at scarres that neuer felt a wound, / But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the East, and Iuliet is the Sun." Comma, question mark, full stop: the three stops a modern reader would choose, deployed in the first three lines, a reminder that the notation is a system and not a sneeze. The sentence that follows shows the system's other register. "Arise faire Sun and kill the enuious Moone, / Who is alreadie sicke and pale with greefe, / That thou her maide art far more faire then she:" Six clauses ride on commas across three lines and land on a colon, the whole invocation suspended in one period that the modern page, the Folger included, closes with a full stop and punctuates with a thicket of commas the quarto does not supply. Nothing in the quarto's version is unclear. It is simply breathed differently, a single arc of address where the modern text walks in steps.

Juliet's first lines extend the lesson in both directions. "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" carries its question mark correctly by any century's standard, proof if proof were wanted that the shop's interrogative point was a working instrument and not a tic. The bargain that follows is hung on the system's hinge: "Denie thy father and refuse thy name: / Or if thou wilt not, be but sworne my loue, / And ile no longer be a Capulet." The colon suspends the first branch of the alternative while the second is stated, so that refusal and surrender hang in one grammatical breath, and the full stop falls only when both are on the table. A modern comma after "name" lightens the hinge, and a modern full stop severs the branches into separate decisions. The quarto's Juliet proposes a single act with two faces. The pointing is the interpretation, stated in one sort of type, and an editor who changes it has directed the scene's most consequential offer.

Then the page itself intervenes, as the bench section promised it would. Juliet's catalogue of what a Montague is not begins at the foot of a leaf: "Whats Mountague? it is nor hand nor foote," and there the page ends. At its foot stand the furniture of the shop, the signature "D 2" and the catchword "Nor", the first word of the page to come, set down to guide a binder who had not yet received that page from the press. Over the leaf the sentence resumes: "Nor arme nor face, ô be some other name / Belonging to a man." The compositor who set the second page worked from the same copy at a different hour, and the sentence he continued is not quite the sentence a modern reader knows: the quarto sets the plea "ô be some other name" before the phrase "Belonging to a man", and lacks the further item, "nor any other part", that the received text carries, a dislocation whose case belongs to the rose line's own study in this series and is noted here only as the page presents it. What the page presents, indisputably, is a sentence straddling a physical boundary in the book's manufacture, pointed continuously across the boundary as though the leaf were not there, by hands that treated the copy, and not the page, as the unit of their work. The line everyone knows follows within the same unbroken movement: "Whats in a name that which we call a rose, / By any other word would smell as sweete", a statement in the quarto's pointing, running on by comma, whose interrogative shape in modern texts is an editorial gift examined in that same study.

Even the scene's humble apparatus joins the exhibit. The speech prefixes wander within a few pages, "Iuli." and "Iu." for the heroine, "Ro." and "Rom." for the hero, swelling and shrinking as the line's room dictates, the shop's economy operating at the level of the label. The scene's delinquent parentheses wait a page further on, and its close carries the doubled dawn that the lineation section examined. A reader who has watched this one opening has seen every stratum of the accidental layer at work at once: a notation with its own logic, a copy with its own accidents, a bench with its own economics, and an editor, three centuries downstream, inheriting all three strata in every line and deciding, mostly in silence, which of them the next reader will be allowed to see. The opening is not exceptional. It is the book in miniature, and it is why the marks of Q2 repay the attention this study has spent on them: they are the only part of the text that shows the text being made.

## The Chain of Copies: Q2's Marks After 1599

The second quarto's accidentals did not wait for editors to start changing. They changed at every resetting, because a compositor who sets from printed copy repunctuates and relineates by the act of setting, consulting his habits and his measure before he consults his exemplar. The third quarto of 1609, printed for John Smethwick, is a reprint of Q2, and the Cambridge editors' verdict on it runs that it differs from its copy "by a few corrections, and more frequently by additional errors". The errors include the accidentals. S. W. Reid's collation of the Folio text against its quarto ancestors preserves a perfect miniature of the process at the play's most famous line of all. Q2 prints Juliet's resolve soundly: "This is thy sheath, there rust and let me dye". Q3's compositor, fiddling with the line to accommodate a turn-up forced by the long verse line that follows, produced "Tis is". The undated fourth quarto restored "This is", and the Folio's workman, setting from Q3, printed "'Tis in", a reading that stood in the world's Shakespeare for generations. The words of the line survived the chain, more or less. Its dress was rewritten at every link, once by a man solving a spacing problem with his fingers in the type. A transmission that treats accidentals this way cannot deliver any modern comma with a presumption of descent, and the miniature acquits and convicts the whole system at once: the chain preserved the play, and the chain is exactly why the play's marks must be verified rather than inherited.

The chain's last early link is the First Folio of 1623. W. W. Greg's summary of the descent has never been bettered for economy: Q2 was reprinted in 1609, and from that third quarto the Folio was set up without material alteration, the copy never compared against a playhouse manuscript for the substantives. Reid's study qualifies the picture at the edges, finding places where the Folio agrees with Q2 against Q3's errors, evidence that the Q3 copy used in the Folio shop had been corrected at points against some manuscript authority. For the accidentals the qualification hardly matters. The Folio's Romeo and Juliet was set in the main by the workman Hinman's scholarship letters E, judged in Reid's phrase, following the Folio's students, the least capable of the Jaggard shop's compositors, a setter whose pages transpose words, garble lines, and repunctuate at whim. Here the record offers a paradox that every student of pointing should be made to sit with. The Cambridge editors, no friends to slovenly printing, judged the Folio's punctuation, as a rule, more correct than the quartos'. Both judgments are true, and their conjunction is the lesson. The Folio's pointing is more correct by the grammatical standard, because a metropolitan shop of 1623 regularized as it set, bending the rhetorical notation toward grammar out of house habit. Correctness, in pointing, is a property of the system applied, not of the care taken, and a careless workman inside a grammatical system will outpoint a careful one inside a rhetorical system every time. The quarto's marks and the Folio's marks are not better and worse attempts at the same notation. They are different notations, and the descent from one to the other, accomplished without any editor deciding anything, is the largest silent repunctuation in the play's history.

One abstraction should be surrendered before the editors enter. "Q2's pointing" names a family, not an individual. Stop-press correction, the practice of halting the press to mend a sort or a word and then printing on, means that copies of the same edition can differ from one another at corrected points, the corrected and uncorrected sheets bound together indifferently into finished books. Twelve copies of the second quarto survive, and they do not all read identically at every point. The editor who prints "Q2" in a collation is citing an ideal object assembled by scholarship from a small crowd of near-identical witnesses. The crowd agrees on almost everything, which is why the abstraction works. At a crux, though, the honest citation names the state of the page in question, and the honest argument remembers that even the copy-text's authority is, at the level of the individual mark, statistical.

## The Editors' Four Centuries: Repunctuation as Policy

The early texts reached the eighteenth century in no fit state to be read for pleasure, and their first editors knew it. Nicholas Rowe gave the play act and scene divisions and regularized its presentation for the reader of 1709. Alexander Pope, persuaded that the second quarto's copy had been corrupted in transmission, dipped into the first quarto for readings and repunctuated as freely as he reworded. Lewis Theobald, Pope's enemy and the better scholar, took the opposite professional pride: where Pope swept, Theobald justified, defending individual decisions about pointing in print, treating a comma as a thing an editor might be called upon to explain. The contrast between the two men is the seed of everything this study has argued. From the beginning of the editorial tradition, punctuation was either an editor's silent prerogative or an editor's argued responsibility, and the tradition mostly chose the prerogative.

Samuel Johnson gave the prerogative its charter. In the Preface to his edition of 1765 he announced: "I have considered the punctuation as wholly in my power; for what could be their care of colons and commas, who corrupted words and sentences?" The sentence deserves its fame, because it states with perfect clarity the assumption under which Shakespeare was edited for two hundred years: the early printers cared nothing for stops, therefore stops are the editor's to dispose of without record or apology. Whatever could be done by adjusting points, Johnson added, was "silently performed". The adverb governed centuries. The Globe edition of Clark and Wright, the Cambridge text that put a standardized Shakespeare into the world's schoolrooms, could speak of punctuation "carefully regulated throughout" as a routine editorial service, regulation offered as a hygiene measure, its decisions no more itemized than a laundry list. Readers of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries read a pointing system invented by their editors, in the belief that they were reading the play's own, and the belief was never exactly false, because the words were the play's. It was the emphasis, the phrasing, the breathing, that had been quietly reassigned.

Against that long settlement stands the counter-tradition, and its founder is Edward Capell. Capell's edition, published in 1767 and 1768, was the first to be built on a systematic collation of the early quartos, undertaken because Capell believed the old copies, and not the modern editions, were where the text lived. His ten volumes are still modernized in their pointing; the conversion of practice took far longer than the conversion of principle. What Capell changed was the direction of an editor's attention, from the page being manufactured to the page that had survived, and the change propagated through George Steevens to Edmond Malone, whose edition of 1790 made the collation of quartos a scholarly duty and the recording of their variants a habit of the profession. The accidentals benefited last and least, since a collation records words more readily than stops, but the principle that would eventually protect them was Capell's: the early book is the evidence, the edition is the argument, and an argument that conceals its evidence has failed in its office. Every modern textual note that prints a quarto's comma for inspection descends from that sentence of method, whether the editor knows the descent or not.

### What did the Oxford Shakespeare's editors promise about repunctuation?

They promised restraint. Editing the Complete Works in the nineteen-eighties, Stanley Wells and Gary Taylor announced that they had repunctuated the early texts as sparingly as possible, and they directed readers who wanted the original marks to their old-spelling edition. The pledge made punctuation a stated policy, arguably for the first time.

The pledge marks the turn, and the turn has several causes that converge on this play's quarto. Simpson's catalogue, long ignored, was reread once scholars of performance began asking how early actors were cued by their texts. Parkes gave the rhetorical system its scholarly history. Hinman's bibliography taught editors to see a comma as a datable artefact of a specific shop rather than as a compositor's sneeze. And the play's own editors, working from Q2 with ever fuller collations, kept meeting places where the quarto's stop and the modern stop produced different lines, and the meetings accumulated into a professional conscience. The modern scholarly edition, Weis's Arden Third Series volume of 2012 among them, still modernizes: the Arden format is a modern-spelling text, and no reader is asked to parse "beschreend" or to supply missing parentheses. The change lies in the accounting. A modern edition's textual introduction states its policy on accidentals, its notes record the copy-text's pointing at places where the pointing signifies, and its collations let a reader audit the substitutions. The prerogative has become a responsibility, unevenly discharged, sometimes honoured in a single line of small print, but no longer invisible in principle.

The actors, in the end, reopened the question the scholars had closed. Simpson's central contention had always been a rehearsal-room claim before it was a bibliographical one: that the early pointing cues a voice, and that a text pointed grammatically makes an actor find the phrasing by other means or lose it. Twentieth-century practice editions mostly ignored the contention, printing the regulated text and coaching the verse by rule of thumb. A newer generation of editions has reversed the direction of travel. The Arden Shakespeare's Performance Editions, launched in 2017 for rehearsal rooms and classrooms, print the modern text with guidance on metre and punctuation as a stated feature, an editorial admission that the stops are directions for performance and that a reader benefits from being told so. The admission stops well short of restoring Q2's marks, and rightly, since the marks cannot bear that weight. But the wheel has come most of the way round: punctuation is once again something an edition explains, defends, and teaches, rather than something it performs in silence. Johnson's adverb has finally lost its jurisdiction.

One more development belongs to the record, because it has quietly changed who can check the editors' work. The quarto itself has become common property. Facsimiles and diplomatic transcriptions of Q2 circulate freely, and the pointing and lineation discussed in this study can be inspected by any reader with a browser, line by line, against the claims made for it here. The old settlement, in which the editor mediated between a rare book and a trusting public, is dissolving into a new one, in which the mediation is still needed, since few readers will parse the quarto's notation unaided, but can always be appealed against to the document. An edition's punctuation policy is therefore no longer merely a promise. It is a verifiable account, and the cruxes are where the verification happens.

## Original Pointing Versus Modern Clarity: The Verdict

The brief for this study names the complication honestly: original pointing versus modern clarity. The two goods are real and they conflict. What follows states the rule this study defends, in three clauses, and then applies it: repunctuate without apology, relineate where the mechanism is legible and record where it is not, and disclose, at the line itself, every place where the quarto's mark and the modern mark decide different meanings. Original pointing preserves the notation in which the play was first read, with its rhetorical pacing, its open constructions, its refusal to diagram what the voice was trusted to sort out. Modern clarity serves the reader the edition actually has, a silent reader, often a young one, meeting the play in a classroom, for whom a comma between subject and verb is not a metrical pause but a stumbling block, and for whom Q2's colon chains would misrepresent the syntax they struggle to follow. An edition must choose, and the choosing has usually been done by temperament: editors of a antiquarian bent keep the old marks and annotate; editors of a pedagogical bent replace them and move on. The evidence assembled here supports a rule rather than a temperament, and the rule has three clauses.

First, repunctuate. The case for printing Q2's pointing in a reading text collapses on the quarto's own evidence. A notation that opens three parentheses in one scene and closes none, that doubles a word in the dawn parting and leaves the doubling standing, that points the same quatrain two ways a few pages apart, is a working notation, not a finished one, and to fetishize it is to mistake the accidents of a week's presswork for the intentions of a playwright. Simpson's deeper point survives his case's weaker parts: the system was rhetorical, and a rhetorical notation cannot be honoured by being frozen, since its whole purpose was to cue a living voice. The modern editor who repunctuates grammatically is completing the system's work in the notation the age provides. The purchase is real and should be priced honestly. A reader meeting the play for the first time, in a classroom, under time pressure, needs the syntax exposed: which clause governs, where the sentence ends, who is being addressed. Grammatical pointing delivers that security on every line, and a text that withheld it in the name of authenticity would tax exactly the readers the edition exists to serve, taxing them, moreover, for a benefit most cannot yet spend, since the rhetorical notation rewards only a reader already fluent in the play. Clarity first is not philistinism. It is the correct order of operations for a reading text, and the case for the quarto's marks was never a case for printing them in the reading text. It is a case for keeping them in the edition's memory, in its notes and collations, at the places where they carry meaning, so that the fluent reader the classroom produces can graduate to them.

Second, relineate where the mechanism is legible, and record where it is not. The Queen Mab prose is a budget decision and may be reversed without ceremony. Capulet's verse-dressed welcome is a judgment call on a border passage, and the judgment should be visible in the notes, because another editor, weighing the same border, might lineate the other way, and a reader comparing editions deserves to find the fork marked. The doubled dawn quatrain and the dropped half-line are not pointing or lineation choices at all but states of the copy, and they belong in every textual introduction, as the clearest evidence of what kind of manuscript the compositors were given. The same clause governs the quiet typography of shared lines. Q2 leaves every half-line flush and lets the metre make the joins; the modern convention of indenting a completing half-line prints a rhythmic analysis as though it were the text itself. Where the join is certain the diagram is a kindness. Where scholars dispute whether two short speeches compose one measure or stand as broken verse, the indentation smuggles a verdict past the reader, and the note, not the margin, is where that verdict belongs.

Third, and this is the clause with teeth: where a Q2 mark decides a meaning, the edition owes the reader the mark. The class is small and identifiable. It includes the Prince's question mark, which reverses the posture of the play's final judgment; it includes every place where a comma's presence or absence attaches a phrase to the clause before it or the clause after; and it includes, in its purest form, a mark that is not there at all. The discipline has its test case in a single absent apostrophe: Q2 prints the possessive in Juliet's night speech without any mark, and every modern edition must choose, in type, between one sleeper and a crowd, as [the 'runaway's eyes' crux](/2031/05/09/runaways-eyes-crux-editorial-puzzle/) shows at the scale of a whole literature. An editor cannot avoid deciding such places. The duty is only to decide them in the open, with the quarto's evidence on the record. Original pointing and modern clarity are not, in the end, enemies. They are the two ends of a single obligation, to give the reader a text that can be read and the means of auditing what readability cost. The cruxes are the audit points, and a survey of them is the natural instrument of the audit.

The rule's cash value appears at the desk. A student who opens a modern edition at the tomb scene reads, in the space of a page, a girl alone with a vial pointed as a questioner, a friar's testimony sentenced into findings, a prince's demand flattened into a verdict, and a host's bustle, four acts earlier, dressed as ceremony by a compositor and undressed again by an editor, and none of it is marked as decided. The same student, given the edition this study argues for, reads the same modern text and finds, at the half dozen places where the marks carry meaning, a note that says what the quarto prints and lets the decision show. The second book is not less authoritative than the first. Its authority is of the only kind editing can honestly offer, the authority of an account that can be checked. Between Johnson's prerogative and that account lies the whole history this study has traced, from a bench in Creede's shop, where a man set a question mark because his copy or his instinct offered one, to a page where the mark at last has to explain itself.

No scene of the play reaches a modern reader unmediated at the level of the mark, and no class of passage is exempt: the most rhetorical set piece and the plainest narrative alike stand in type that a compositor chose and an editor replaced. Read down the last column and the stakes declare themselves. Not one row concerns a nicety. Each records a place where breathing, posture, ceremony, fear, or judgment itself changes with the mark, which is why the survey treats the accidental layer as a subject for a full study rather than a footnote to the substantives. The words of Romeo and Juliet have been edited for four centuries by scholars who printed their names on the title page. The marks were edited by everyone and credited to no one, and the survey is offered as their first collective title page: fifteen exhibits, three strata, one conclusion, that the smallest signs in the book are load-bearing, and that a reader who learns to see them has learned to see the book being made.

The survey's strata deserve one last word, because they are also a discipline for reading any early text, not a peculiarity of this play. Ask of any strange mark three questions in order: could the bench have made it, by rule of thumb, by haste, by the economics of the page? Could the copy have carried it, from an author's foul papers, a scribe's transcript, a playhouse hand? Could the tradition have preserved it, through reprints that regularized as they copied and editors who regulated as they printed? A mark that fails all three tests, explicable by no stratum, is a crux in the strong sense, and the survey's hardest rows are of that kind. A mark that passes one test belongs to that stratum's story, bench or copy or tradition, and the editor's duty differs accordingly: forgive the bench, interrogate the copy, audit the tradition. The discipline is portable. It works on any quarto, any folio, any text that reached print through hands, which is to say on every text the period has left behind.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What pattern do the fifteen exhibits of the Q2 crux survey share, and what does that pattern require of an editor?

The survey is the table in this study that sets fifteen passages side by side: what the second quarto prints, what modern editions print, and what the difference decides. Its pattern is that no divergence in the table is random. Each one resolves into a mechanism, a compositor saving space, a notation translating rhetoric into grammar, a copy carrying a draft's surplus or deficit, or an editor choosing silently between two defensible pages. The survey exists to make those decisions visible at the exact lines where they were taken, so that a reader can audit an edition passage by passage rather than trusting its title page. The pattern the rows share is what sets the editor's obligation: because the quarto's marks carry meaning at these points, an edition that replaces one must show its working.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Q2 crux survey to argue that every modern line of the play is partly an editor's work?

An essay should build the argument from three rows of different classes, so that the mediation is shown to be systematic rather than anecdotal. The Prince's question mark proves that pointing can reverse a speech's posture. The Queen Mab prose proves that lineation can be a budget decision invisible in every modern text. Capulet's missing half-line proves that even the words can arrive through editorial completion. Set against Johnson's claim of punctuation as the editor's property and the Oxford editors' later pledge of restraint, the rows support a precise conclusion: the modern text is a collaboration between a playwright, a printing house, and four centuries of editors, and scholarship begins where the collaboration is acknowledged.

### Q: How was a quarto the size of the second quarto imposed and printed, forme by forme?

A quarto sheet carries eight pages, four on each side, and the unit of presswork was the forme, one side of the sheet locked in a frame. Pages were imposed out of reading order, so that folding the printed sheet brought them into sequence, and the compositors set the formes in an order dictated by the press rather than by the narrative. After a forme was printed, its type was distributed back into the cases for reuse in later formes. Signatures at the foot of pages and catchwords repeating the next page's first word guided the binder through the gatherings. The cruxes cluster at the system's joints, page ends and forme boundaries, because those were the points where measurement, memory, and space all ran short at once.

### Q: What did the Cambridge editors blame for the second quarto's disorder, and why has their culprit lost favour?

Clark and Wright blamed a transcript. Their preface holds it certain that Q2 was printed not from the author's manuscript but from a copy made by a careless scribe who took unwarrantable liberties and transmuted passages from poetry into prose. The culprit has lost favour because the quarto's strangest features point the other way. A scribe copying a finished text does not print a revised passage twice, in both its forms, and does not leave stage directions in the loose, anticipatory state of a working draft. Gibbons and Weis argue from those features to the author's foul papers, and their account explains the disorder as the draft's own, which the transcript theory never could.

### Q: Why has no compositor stint-map of the second quarto won general acceptance?

The Folio method that produced stint-maps needs long stretches of setting, discriminating spellings in quantity, and enough surviving copies to separate one workman's habits from chance. The second quarto offers forty-six leaves, a short stint by any division, some twelve surviving copies, and a spelling record too thin to bear the weight of attribution. Pervez Rizvi's critique cuts deeper still: compositors adjusted spellings to justify their lines, so the fingerprint evidence deforms exactly where the work was tightest. The quarto's pages plainly show more than one temperament, careful stretches against hasty ones, but temperament is not attribution, and the honest verdict remains the bibliographer's "possibly".

### Q: What did Hinman's Folio work establish about accidentals as evidence, and where did later bibliographers press back?

Charlton Hinman's Printing and Proof-Reading of the First Folio, published in 1963, established that accidentals are evidence with an address. By tracing damaged types through the volume and charting the compositors' spelling and spacing habits, he assigned pages to individual workmen and showed that a comma on a Folio page could be credited to a named man's practice rather than to the author. The pressback came in two waves. T. H. Howard-Hill revised several of the attributions themselves with fresh evidence, and Rizvi argued that the spelling method is unsound at its root, since justification bends spellings toward the line's needs. Peter Blayney's adaptation of the method to quartos secures presswork and order more readily than hands.

### Q: What is the difference between an accidental and a substantive, and why does the difference govern an editor's freedom?

Substantives are the words themselves, the readings that carry sense. Accidentals are the dress the words wear, spelling, punctuation, lineation, the features a copy transmits least reliably and a printing house regenerates most freely. Editorial theory grants the editor wide freedom over accidentals because the copy-text's claim to preserve them is weak, while substantives command conservation, since changing a word changes the work. The difference governs freedom because it allocates doubt: an editor may modernize a comma on policy but must justify a changed word by argument. The boundary frays exactly at the cruxes, where an accidental, a question mark, a line break, starts deciding sense and borrowing a substantive's rights.

### Q: Which marks make up the second quarto's working repertory of stops, and which familiar modern mark is missing from it?

The quarto works with five marks. The comma is its default joint, set wherever the voice might check. The colon is its hinge, stronger than a comma and weaker than a stop, closing lines of the Prologue and stringing testimony in the tomb scene. The full stop ends sentences sparingly, after long accumulations. The question mark closes questions and, by the period's habit, commands and cries as well. The parenthesis brackets asides and qualifications. The semicolon is scarce in the passages collated for this study, and the exclamation mark is missing from them altogether, although the modern page scatters it through speeches the quarto leaves level.

### Q: How does the second quarto point the Prologue sonnet, and what does modern pointing flatten there?

Q2 points the sonnet as a piece of voiced rhetoric. The second line, the Verona line, sits inside parentheses, demoted to an aside. Colons close the fourth and sixth lines, hinging the sentence open so that grievance hangs suspended before consequence. A comma stands between the subject and its verb in "A paire of starre-crost louers, take their life", isolating the verb phrase for the voice to land on. Modern pointing flattens the hierarchy the marks create. The long suspension is broken into shorter sentences, the hinges lightened into joints or hardened into full stops, the aside levelled to commas in many texts, and the metrical comma normalized away, leaving a sonnet that parses more cleanly and breathes less deliberately.

### Q: Why does the balcony scene open three parentheses in the second quarto and close none of them?

The openings stand before "night" in Juliet's question about the man below, before "am" in Romeo's answer about his name, and before "walls" in his boast about love's light wings. No closing mark follows any of them. The likeliest mechanism is a compositor's parenthesis sort dropped into the line and never matched, although a stray mark in the copy cannot be ruled out. Authorial design can be excluded on the quarto's own evidence, since the same book closes parentheses neatly in the Prologue and the first-meeting sonnet. Editors delete the strays without ceremony, and the episode's value is calibration: a system capable of this lapse must have its successes credited modestly too.

### Q: What do the parentheses around "grant thou" ask a voice to do in the first-meeting sonnet?

The parenthesis asks the voice to drop in pitch and quicken slightly, the way a speaker lowers into an aside without leaving the sentence. Romeo's line argues that saints grant prayers lest faith turn to despair, and the plea itself, "grant thou", is tucked inside the argument in brackets, a whisper of entreaty embedded in a piece of casuistry. The mark is a piece of stage direction for the throat. Modern editions replace the brackets with commas, and the words survive entire, but the instruction to the voice is one of the things the translation between notations spends without recording.

### Q: Why does Juliet's command to Tybalt's ghost end with a question mark in the second quarto?

Because the period's interrogative point did work that the exclamation mark performs on a modern page. Percy Simpson's catalogue of early usage gives the habit its own heading, the use of the question mark in exclamations and commands, where the mark registers a voice rising rather than a sentence inquiring. Juliet, alone with the vial, sees her cousin's ghost hunting Romeo and cries "stay Tybalt, stay", and the quarto's mark keeps the cry suspended between order and appeal, a command addressed to something that may not be there and may not obey. The modern exclamation point settles the cry into an order, and the fear loses its upward inflection.

### Q: Why does the Prince's reproach to the fathers end with a question mark in the second quarto?

The quarto prints "See what a scourge is laide vpon your hate" and closes the line with an interrogative point, so that the sentence of Verona opens as a demand rather than a verdict. With the question mark standing, the Prince puts the evidence to the two fathers and makes the lines that follow, the lost joys and the lost kinsmen, arrive as the answer his question extracts. Modern editions print a full stop or nothing, and the speech settles into magisterial closure. One sort of type reverses the posture of the play's highest authority at the moment of judgment, and no evidence survives that can settle which posture the author intended.

### Q: Which passage of the second quarto lets a reader watch the same lines pointed twice, and what does the difference prove?

The dawn quatrain at the junction of the balcony scene and the Friar's cell is set twice within a few pages, once as Romeo's exit lines and once as the Friar's entrance speech. The words vary, "Checkring" against "Checking", "made by Tytans wheeles" against "Titans burning wheeles", and the pointing varies with them, a comma after "light" in the first setting and a colon in the second. The doublet proves that pointing was supplied fresh at the bench for each setting, since a fixed pointing in the copy, faithfully followed, would have had to survive two settings in the same shop in the same week, and it did not.

### Q: What does the doubled word in Juliet's dawn speech reveal about proof-reading in the printing house?

In the parting scene Juliet, arguing that the bird was the nightingale, promises to call the grey of dawn anything Romeo wishes: "Ile say yon gray is not the the mornings eye". The doubled article stands uncorrected in the copy-text of the most scrutinized love scene in the language. Stop-press correction existed in Creede's shop, and early books often preserve corrected and uncorrected states of a page side by side, but this doubling passed into every copy. The exhibit calibrates the house's standard of attention. A shop that shipped the doubled word was not proof-reading at the level of the phrase, and its punctuation, set by the same hands, can claim only the authority that such attention earns.

### Q: How do editors free the Nurse's buried "Madam" calls from Juliet's lines in the balcony scene?

Q2 prints the calls inside Juliet's verse, "And follow thee my L. throughout the world. Madam.", with no speech prefix and no line of their own, and repeats the device a breath later. Editors extract the word, assign it to the Nurse speaking from within, and set it as a separate speech, usually with a stage direction marking the voice as offstage. The repair restores what the theatre must always have done with the moment, a summons cutting across a vow. It also reverses the page's own effect, in which the call reads as a thought inside Juliet's utterance, and the better editions record the quarto's arrangement in a note.

### Q: Why do the second quarto's speech prefixes for the two mothers keep changing shape?

The prefixes wander because nobody in the shop was charged with their consistency. In the stretches of Q2 examined for this study, the Capulet parents appear under a shifting set of short forms, a wife as "Wife" in one place and compressed initials in another, Lady Capulet as "La." and "Mo.", Capulet as "Fa.", "Fat.", and "Ca.", the abbreviations swelling or shrinking with the room on the line and the copy's own looseness. Modern editions regularize the cast into stable identities, and Weis, following Jill Levenson's Oxford edition, prints "Capulet's Wife" and "Montague's Wife", a considered decision about who these women are, taken at the humblest level of the page.

### Q: How can justifying a line of type change the spelling and pointing inside it?

A line of hand-set type must fill its measure exactly, neither slack nor overfull, and the compositor met the requirement with the materials nearest to hand. Spellings stretched or shrank, a "do" becoming "doe" or the reverse, spaces after commas widened or closed, and a verse line that overran was turned, its tail carried down and indented. The accidentals of a tight line are therefore partly typography rather than testimony, which is why Rizvi's critique of spelling-based attribution matters: the evidence scholars use to fingerprint a compositor was itself deformed by the measure the compositor was filling, most heavily exactly where his page ran tight.

### Q: What licence did Samuel Johnson claim over Shakespeare's punctuation, and how long did editors work under it?

In the Preface to his edition of 1765, Johnson announced that he had considered the punctuation as wholly in his power, asking what care of colons and commas could be expected from the men who corrupted words and sentences, and adding that whatever could be done by adjusting points was silently performed. The licence governed editing for roughly two centuries. Through the Globe era and deep into the schoolroom tradition, pointing was regulated, modernized, and replaced without record, on the assumption that the early printers had cared nothing for stops. The assumption held until Simpson's catalogue was reread, Parkes had written the system's history, and the Oxford editors replaced the licence with a pledge of sparing, stated, auditable repunctuation.

### Q: When a modern comma decides a meaning, what does an editor owe the reader about the second quarto's mark?

The editor owes the reader the mark itself, printed in the note at the line. The modern text may keep the comma the reader's grammar requires, since a reading edition serves a silent reader and clarity is a real good. What it may not do is let the substitution pass as the author's own pointing, because at such a place the comma is no longer furniture but a decision about sense, and a silent decision hardens into a false fact about the play. The note costs a line of small print. What it buys is the reader's right to audit the mediation everywhere else, on the evidence of the one place where the mediation confessed itself.
