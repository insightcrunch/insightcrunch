## The Cut Taken Before a Developer Is Paid

An app store commission looks simple on a receipt, yet the thirty percent figure hides a bundle that explains why the charge survives long after cheaper payment tools appear. A store joins distribution, payment handling, trust screening, and access to a large installed base of devices into one toll, and a developer cannot purchase those parts separately inside the same storefront. When Apple opened the App Store in 2008, a developer kept 70 percent of the price and the store kept 30 percent, and that single deduction arrived before any tax bill, salary, or server invoice faced the maker of the software. The order of payment matters because the commission is not a fee added after success. The commission is the gate through which every paid download and every in-app purchase must pass, so the store shares in gross revenue rather than in profit after costs.

![A smartphone screen showing a grid of application icons beside a price tag - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That distinction shapes business decisions from the first sketch of a product. A subscription service with heavy content licensing costs feels a 30 percent deduction very differently from a small game built by two people with almost no marginal expense. Both pay the same headline rate on the same kind of transaction, even though one business surrenders a slice of revenue it barely marks up while the other surrenders a slice of revenue that is nearly all margin. Pricing follows from that arithmetic. Developers set a price point that still works after the deduction, choose which features sit behind a paywall, and decide whether a free version can carry enough users toward paid tiers to cover the toll on the minority who pay. The commission therefore influences design, not only accounting.

The deduction also arrives with a kind of certainty that other costs lack. Advertising prices swing, server bills rise with usage, and contract staff charge by the hour, but the store percentage stays fixed and predictable across millions of small transactions. For Apple and for Google, that predictability turns an enormous flow of modest purchases into steady services revenue reported quarter after quarter. For a developer, the same predictability removes negotiation. A large publisher and a first-time creator face the same published schedule in the standard program, with lower tiers offered only under defined conditions such as small business programs or long-held subscriptions. The rate is a rule of the marketplace rather than a bargain struck between two parties of equal weight.

Understanding the cut requires separating three questions that public argument often merges into one. The first question asks what the percentage buys, because a toll that funds real services differs from a toll that funds only permission. The second question asks how the percentage was set, because a figure inherited from earlier media retail carries a history that a figure built from cost accounting would not. The third question asks whether a developer can reach the same customers through another route at a lower total cost, because an expensive door matters less when a second door stands open beside it. Each later section of the store system, from hosting files to screening fraud, answers part of one of those questions, and the full picture emerges only when price, history, and alternatives sit on the same page.

## What an App Store Actually Sells

A store sells far more than a download button, and the breadth of the bundle is the strongest defense of the app store commission in its standard form. The first item in the bundle is placement inside a catalog that a customer already trusts enough to visit with a payment credential on file. When the App Store launched in July 2008 with about 500 applications, the value lay less in shelf space than in the habit Apple had already built through iTunes, where millions of accounts held card details ready for one-tap purchase. A developer listing a product in that catalog bought instant reach into pockets and homes that no independent website of that era could match at any advertising price a small team could pay.

The second item is transaction trust. A customer handing card details to an unknown developer website faces friction and doubt, while the same customer buying through a store account faces a familiar receipt, a stored credential, and a refund path administered by a known company. Apple and Google operate that trust layer with fraud screening, receipt validation, and account recovery systems that run across billions of transactions. The third item is delivery machinery. Store servers host the binary file, push updated versions, verify device compatibility, and restore past purchases when a customer replaces a phone. The fourth item is discovery, through search rankings, category charts, editorial collections, and recommendation surfaces that can lift an unknown product into public view without a separate marketing contract.

A fifth item, often overlooked, is the installed base itself. A store arrives preinstalled on every device its owner ships, signed in with the account created during device setup, and tied to an operating system that directs software installation through that single channel. On an iPhone, that position has no parallel rival inside the device, so the bundle includes something no outside vendor can sell at all, namely default access to the entire active fleet. Google Play holds a similar default position on most Android devices outside China, even though Android permits other installation routes in a way the iPhone system historically restricted. The bundle therefore mixes services a developer could in theory buy elsewhere, such as payment handling and file hosting, with a privilege no market supplies twice, namely presence at the only storefront most customers ever open.

Pricing a bundle as one percentage creates winners and losers inside the same schedule. A developer who needs every part, who lacks a brand, a billing relationship, and a support desk, receives substantial value for 30 percent. A developer with a famous brand, an existing billing relationship, and customers who would follow that brand anywhere receives far less new value, yet pays the same rate on transactions the store rules require to pass through store billing. That mismatch explains why the loudest disputes over commissions have come from large services such as Spotify and Epic Games rather than from small creators, and why small creators often defend a system that large firms attack. The bundle is generous to the unknown and expensive to the established, and a single rate cannot fit both experiences at once.

## The Unit of Sale Inside a Store

Every commission needs a defined unit to tax, and a store defines that unit with care. The taxable event is not the creation of software, the ownership of a device, or the attention of a user. The taxable event is a digital transaction completed through store billing, either a paid download at a chosen price tier or an in-app purchase of digital content and features consumed inside the application. A ten dollar game download and a ten dollar pack of game currency both pass through the same deduction, even though one buys a complete product and the other buys an item that exists only inside a product already installed. Apple built the in-app purchase system in 2009, a year after the store opened, and that addition quietly enlarged the commission base from the price of entry to nearly every digital sale that followed entry.

Physical goods and real world services sit outside that base by design. A ride ordered through an application, a meal delivered to a door, or a jacket shipped from a warehouse does not pay the store percentage, because the store claims its toll on digital value delivered through the device rather than on commerce merely arranged by software. That boundary explains the strange shape of many product decisions. A meditation service selling access inside its application pays the commission, while the same company selling a printed workbook through a shop does not. A reading service must weigh selling ebooks inside an application against directing customers to a website, because the first route carries the toll and the second route carries friction, extra steps, and rules about how plainly an application may point outward.

Subscriptions stretched the unit further across time. A monthly plan renews again and again, and each renewal historically paid the full headline rate until store policies added reduced rates for subscribers retained beyond one year, with Apple moving long-held subscriptions to a 15 percent store share in 2016. The change conceded a principle worth naming, namely that the store contribution to a loyal subscriber in month 14 differs from its contribution on the day of discovery. Yet the renewal still flows through store billing, still depends on the stored credential, and still ends if a customer cancels inside store settings. The unit of sale thus became a continuing relationship administered by the store, not a single moment at a checkout.

Free applications complicate the picture in a useful way. A free download pays no commission at the moment of installation, and advertising supported products can earn revenue outside store billing altogether. Stores accept that gap because free products enlarge the catalog, sell devices, and create the audience that paid products monetize. The commission system therefore rests on a minority of transactions inside a vast free economy, which is one reason the headline rate stays high. When only paid digital transactions carry the load of funding catalog operations, trust systems, and delivery networks used by everyone, the rate on those transactions must cover costs spread far beyond the paying few. Whether that cross subsidy is fair depends on which side of the transaction a judge, a developer, or a customer happens to stand.

## Where the Seventy Thirty Split Came From

The seventy thirty split did not begin as a measurement of store costs, and its origin explains much of its staying power. When Steve Jobs introduced the App Store in March 2008 and opened it that July, Apple offered developers 70 percent of the sale price and kept 30 percent, presenting the division as generous against the deals software makers knew from earlier channels. The figure echoed the structure Apple had already used in digital media, where the iTunes Store, opened in 2003, paid record labels the larger share of each 99 cent song download while Apple kept the smaller share and treated hardware sales as the main profit engine. A familiar division lowered the argument at launch. Developers heard a number that resembled a music business wholesale split rather than a new tax invented for a new device.

Inheritance mattered because nobody in 2008 possessed reliable data on what mobile software distribution should cost at scale. The smartphone market was small, the iPhone had sold about 6,000,000 units in its first year, and paid applications for earlier phones moved through carrier controlled decks with far harsher terms. Against that background, a clear 70 percent share with no listing fee and no charge for free applications read as an opening rather than a burden. Apple also set the price tiers, handled the card transaction, and paid developers monthly across many countries, removing chores that had consumed small software firms in the era of boxed products and regional distributors. The split solved an adoption problem first and a cost problem second, if it solved a cost problem at all.

Once a number governs millions of contracts, the number acquires defenders beyond its original logic. Store accounting, developer business plans, price tiers, and customer expectations all settled around the expectation that a digital storefront keeps roughly one third at the top rate and less under special programs. Later entrants copied the figure because copying signaled legitimacy. Google used a similar 70 and 30 division for Android Market and later Google Play, Microsoft used comparable terms for its store, and Valve already operated with a similar headline share on Steam for computer games. Each copy strengthened the sense that 30 percent was a natural constant of digital retail rather than a choice made in one boardroom in Cupertino during one product launch.

A cost based figure would drift as technology changed. Bandwidth prices fell, storage prices fell, and payment networks matured between 2008 and the following decade, yet the headline split held steady for years across the largest stores. That stability reveals the real foundation of the number. The split prices access to a marketplace and to a customer relationship at least as much as it prices any measurable service, and access prices follow bargaining power rather than server invoices. When courts and regulators later pressed Apple and Google to justify the figure, the defense leaned on the whole bundle of services and on intellectual property in the platform itself, not on a ledger showing that each transaction consumed 30 percent of its price in costs. The origin story therefore carries the argument inside it, because a number born as an inheritance can be defended as tradition long after the conditions that made it attractive have faded from view.

## Physical Software Retail and the Margin It Took

Software reached customers through cardboard, plastic, and negotiation long before any download store existed, and that older system took margins that make a 30 percent commission look mild by comparison. In the boxed software era of the 1980s and 1990s, a publisher sold a product to a distributor at a wholesale discount, the distributor sold to a retail chain, and the chain sold to a customer at list price, with each layer keeping a share. Retailers such as CompUSA and Egghead Software expected margins that could consume 40 percent or more of the shelf price once distribution, returns, and unsold inventory entered the calculation. The publisher also paid for packaging, manuals, disks, shipping, cooperative advertising, and the prized placement of a box at eye level near the front of the store.

Shelf space was scarce in a way a digital catalog never is. A store could stock only a few thousand titles, so buyers for retail chains acted as gatekeepers with power over which products lived and which vanished. A small developer without a publisher often could not reach the shelf at all, and a publisher agreement could take a further large share of the remaining revenue in exchange for manufacturing and access. Payment arrived slowly, sometimes months after a sale, and returned boxes came back as a cost rather than as a reusable file on a server. Against that record, a digital store offering 70 percent of the price, monthly payment, and unlimited catalog room represented a genuine transfer of income toward creators, whatever complaints later attached to the same figure.

Mobile software before 2008 faced an even tighter gate. On feature phones, carriers such as Verizon and NTT DoCoMo controlled the decks, the menus where customers found games and ringtones, and placement on a deck could decide whether a product sold at all. Developers and content aggregators often surrendered half or more of revenue under carrier arrangements, paid for testing and certification across dozens of handset models, and waited through slow settlement cycles. The deck system taught the industry a durable lesson about default menus. Whoever controlled the first screen a customer saw controlled the economics of everything sold behind that screen, a lesson the smartphone stores inherited and refined rather than invented.

The physical era also explains why later debates about [how optical discs work](/2026/11/19/how-optical-discs-work/) belong inside a discussion of margins, because pressed discs, printed boxes, and truck delivery turned distribution into a chain of paid middlemen that download stores collapsed into a single toll. A compact disc cost money to press, warehouse, and ship, and every unsold copy destroyed value, while a hosted file copies at near zero marginal cost and never goes out of stock. When distribution cost collapsed, the margin built to cover distribution should in theory have collapsed with it. Instead, the margin migrated. The savings from ending physical production flowed partly to developers through the 70 percent share and partly to store owners who kept charging a retail style percentage for a wholesale style service. That migration sits at the center of every later argument about whether the commission pays for work performed or for position held.

## The Download Store That Reset the Split

One store above all others reset expectations for digital software, and it did so before the smartphone existed in its modern form. Valve released Steam in 2003 as a delivery and update system for its own games, then opened it to outside publishers in 2005, offering online distribution without pressing discs, printing boxes, or bargaining for shelf space. Steam handled hosting, patching, payment, and community features, and its revenue division settled near the same shape the App Store later made famous, with the publisher keeping the larger share near 70 percent and the store keeping the remainder. A generation of game developers learned through Steam that a download store could replace an entire physical supply chain while leaving the creator with more of each sale than boxed retail ever allowed.

The iTunes Store performed the same reset for music five years before the App Store applied the lesson to software. Opening in April 2003 with about 200,000 songs at 99 cents each, iTunes proved that customers would pay for downloads inside a clean storefront tied to a device they loved, and that labels would accept a wholesale style split for a channel that reduced piracy by convenience rather than by punishment. When Apple extended the model from songs to applications in 2008, the company reused the store account, the billing relationship, the receipt system, and the customer habit in one motion. The App Store was therefore not an experiment built from nothing. The App Store was a proven retail machine pointed at a new category of goods, and the 70 and 30 split traveled with the machine as part of its standard equipment.

Resetting the split changed who could enter the market. A developer in 2009 needed a computer, a developer account that cost 99 dollars per year with Apple, and a product that passed review, rather than a publishing contract, a manufacturing budget, and a retail buyer willing to gamble on shelf space. The catalog grew from about 500 applications at launch to more than 100,000 within about 16 months, a pace no physical channel could have housed. Entry at that scale created the modern shape of software competition, where discovery replaced distribution as the scarce resource. Getting into the store became easy. Getting noticed inside the store became the hard and expensive problem, and that shift quietly raised the value of the chart positions, search results, and editorial slots the store itself controlled.

Later stores inherited both the reset and its limits. Console makers Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo operated download stores for PlayStation, Xbox, and Nintendo systems with headline shares in the same broad territory, because platform owners viewed digital sales through the lens of hardware ecosystems they had funded. Epic Games challenged the inherited figure directly in 2018 by launching its games store at an 88 and 12 split, arguing that distribution and payment in a mature download market cost far less than 30 percent. The Epic move proved the older split was a choice rather than a law of nature, yet the largest stores kept their headline rates and answered with selective reductions instead. A reset, once complete, becomes the new tradition, and traditions in retail economics fall only when a rival channel grows large enough to make the old toll optional rather than merely resented.

## How a Transaction Moves Through the Store

A purchase that feels instant travels through a chain of checks, records, and settlements before any money reaches a developer. The chain begins when a customer taps a price button and the device asks the store account for authorization, often confirmed through a password, a fingerprint, or a face scan tied to the account. The store server verifies the account, confirms the payment method on file, checks the product identifier and price tier against the catalog record, and screens the attempt against fraud rules built from account history and device signals. Only after those checks pass does the store create the entitlement, the durable record stating that this account owns this product or holds this subscription, and that entitlement matters more than the file itself because the entitlement governs re-downloads, family sharing where offered, and restoration on a new device.

### What does a developer actually receive from a ten dollar sale?

On a standard ten dollar sale priced at 9 dollars and 99 cents in a common tier, the store keeps about 30 percent and the developer receives about 6 dollars and 99 cents before taxes and any other business costs reduce that amount further still.

Payment capture follows authorization through the card networks or other billing rails attached to the account, including carrier billing in some markets and stored balance in others. The store, not the developer, stands as merchant of record in the standard model, which means the store name appears on the customer statement, the store handles sales tax and value added tax calculation in many jurisdictions, and the store absorbs the first line of chargeback handling when a customer disputes a charge. Apple took the merchant role from the first App Store sale in 2008, following the iTunes pattern, and Google adopted the same posture for Google Play.

Settlement closes the chain on a slower clock than the tap that opened it. Stores aggregate transactions across a fiscal period, convert currencies, deduct the commission and applicable taxes, and pay developers in batches, with Apple paying on a monthly rhythm tied to its fiscal calendar and minimum thresholds in each currency. A developer in Berlin, a studio in Austin, and a solo creator in Seoul all read the same style of report, showing units, proceeds, and adjustments by territory. Refunds reverse part of the flow. When a store grants a refund, the corresponding proceeds leave the developer balance under rules that changed over time, and subscription cancellations stop future renewals rather than clawing back months already consumed. The paperwork of the chain, receipts, tax invoices, and entitlement records, forms an audit trail that a small developer selling alone would need to build, buy, or rent from several vendors at once.

Fraud control threads through every step because digital goods invite particular abuse. Stolen card testing, account takeover, refund abuse, and receipt manipulation all target instant delivery products that cannot be recovered after use. Stores invest in detection systems at a scale no individual developer can match, and a blocked fraudulent purchase saves the developer a chargeback fee and a reversed sale. The protection is imperfect and sometimes blocks honest customers, yet the cost of operating without it lands somewhere, either in a store commission or in separate contracts with payment and risk vendors. The transaction chain therefore shows the commission in motion, part toll, part outsourced back office, moving money and records through systems that stay invisible until one link fails in public.

## Payment Processing Inside the App Store Commission

Payment processing is the easiest part of the bundle to price against outside alternatives, and that ease makes this section the sharpest test of the app store commission as a whole. Card acceptance through a mainstream processor such as Stripe carried a standard United States price of 2.9 percent plus 30 cents per successful charge for many years, a schedule quoted openly and used by millions of businesses. Against that yardstick, a 30 percent store share looks less like payment pricing and more like a bundle price in which payment forms only a small ingredient. Any honest account of the commission must start there, because a developer comparing only the act of moving money will always find the store expensive by a factor near ten on small transactions.

The comparison grows fairer when the full stack around a raw card charge enters the ledger. A developer selling alone must also handle sales tax and value added tax across jurisdictions, a burden that grew heavier after the United States Supreme Court decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair in 2018 expanded state power to tax remote sales. Add currency conversion across more than 170 territories, local payment methods from iDEAL in the Netherlands to convenience store payment in Japan, fraud screening, chargeback fees, refund administration, receipt and invoice generation, and the customer support burden when a payment fails at midnight. Each item can be rented from a specialist vendor, and companies such as FastSpring and Paddle built businesses as merchant of record providers doing exactly that bundle for software firms, at prices well above raw card rates and well below 30 percent for most transaction sizes.

Scale changes the arithmetic inside the store as well. Apple and Google process transaction volumes that command favorable network terms, spread fixed compliance costs across billions of charges, and pool fraud losses across a catalog where no single product can sink the system. Their internal cost per transaction sits far below the retail price a small developer pays a processor, which means the margin between store cost and store charge widens as volume grows. Defenders of the commission answer that pricing a platform by its marginal cost mistakes how ecosystems fund themselves, since the same revenue pool supports review staff, developer tools, operating system development, and the retail presence that creates demand. Critics answer that a fee justified by everything ends up justified by nothing in particular, because no single service in the bundle can be tested against its price.

Small transactions expose the tension most clearly, and anyone comparing store fees with [how contactless payments work](/2026/10/04/how-contactless-payments-work/) in physical retail sees the same fixed cost problem in miniature, since a percentage plus a fixed fee falls hardest on the smallest basket. A one dollar in-app purchase surrendering 30 cents leaves a developer with 70 cents before any other cost, while card network economics on a one dollar charge consume a large fixed slice no matter who runs the transaction. Stores answer the small transaction problem partly through aggregation, batching several small purchases into fewer card charges behind the scenes. The device in a pocket thus runs two payment stories at once, a simple percentage presented to developers and a complicated cost structure hidden beneath it, and the gap between those two stories is where most arguments about fairness in payment processing properly live.

## Distribution, Hosting and Delivery Costs

Distribution was the original promise of the download store, and its cost curve explains both the early gratitude toward the commission and the later skepticism toward the same figure. Hosting an application means storing the binary file, serving it to millions of devices, delivering updates that can run to hundreds of megabytes, and doing so quickly in every region where the device is sold. In 2008, content delivery at that scale required serious infrastructure, and Apple already operated delivery systems for iTunes media, including music files and the video catalog added in 2005, that a young application market could ride on from the first day. A developer listing a product received global delivery without signing a single contract with a content delivery network, without forecasting bandwidth, and without paying when a product suddenly turned popular and traffic multiplied overnight.

Popularity insurance is a real economic good that flat percentage pricing quietly provides. A boxed publisher feared success in one specific way, because a hit product meant emergency manufacturing, air freight, and empty shelves during the hottest weeks of demand. A download store removes that failure mode entirely. The file copies perfectly, the store scales the delivery, and the developer pays the same percentage on the millionth download as on the first. Services built around user content extend the same logic further, since store review and hosting also cover the updates that keep a product compatible as operating systems change each year. When Apple required 64-bit support for applications in 2015 and later pressed the move from 32-bit catalogs, the store machinery of notices, deadlines, and binary checks carried part of the transition cost that developers would otherwise have managed alone.

Yet the honest ledger must record how far delivery costs fell while the headline commission stood still. Cloud storage and bandwidth prices declined steeply across the decade after 2010 as Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud competed for exactly the workloads a store operates, and content delivery networks such as Cloudflare pushed caching toward commodity pricing. A gigabyte delivered in the early store era cost a store far more than the same gigabyte delivered later, and application files, while larger than songs, remain small beside the video streams that platforms deliver at advertising supported prices of zero to the viewer. If distribution alone set the commission, the rate would have glided downward with its cost base, and the failure to glide is evidence that distribution functions as one ingredient in a bundle priced by access rather than by expense.

Delivery also carries duties beyond moving bytes. Stores scan uploads for malware, sign code so devices can verify integrity, stage rollouts so a faulty update reaches 1 percent of users before it reaches everyone, and pull harmful products from circulation when a threat appears. Those protective functions resemble the quality control a good physical retailer once provided by refusing defective stock. A developer benefits from a delivery system customers regard as safe, because safety raises willingness to install unknown software at all. The commission debate returns, at the end of the delivery question, to the same fork found in payment and in placement. Priced as a utility, store distribution looks expensive and grows more expensive looking each year. Priced as admission to a guarded marketplace attached to more than 1,000,000,000 active devices in the Apple ecosystem alone, the same percentage describes something no utility vendor sells, and the argument over which pricing frame should govern has outlasted every cost curve offered to settle it.

## Review as a Cost and as a Gate

Review is the point where distribution stops being automatic and becomes permission. A developer builds an application, signs it with a certificate, uploads a binary through a store console, and then waits while another organisation decides whether the work may reach customers at all. That pause is not incidental to the commission. It is one of the services the commission is said to pay for, and it is also the mechanism that makes the toll enforceable, because a store that reviews every submission controls the doorway through which every sale must pass. The two descriptions coexist without cancelling each other. A gate can protect users and still confer power on the gatekeeper.

The protective account begins with scale and risk. Apple has described its App Store review operation as examining tens of thousands of submissions and updates in a typical week, combining automated checks with human reviewers who test for crashes, deceptive behaviour, privacy violations and prohibited content. Google Play applies a comparable mix through its own review and policy systems. The work catches real harm. Reviewers reject applications that harvest contacts without disclosure, that disguise gambling behind a puzzle interface, or that imitate a banking application in order to steal credentials. Parents who allow a child to download from a storefront rely in part on that screening, even if they never see it operate.

The cost account begins with delay and opacity. A rejection returns a guideline citation, often a numbered rule rather than a detailed explanation, and the developer must guess which element of a complex application triggered it. A small team can lose a launch window tied to a conference, a seasonal sale or a partner announcement while an appeal moves through a queue. The Hey email application dispute in 2020 made the friction visible to a wide audience when Basecamp described a rejection over payment rules for an application its developers believed complied. Whatever the merits in that case, the episode showed how review converts a commercial disagreement into an existential one, because the sanction is not a fine but absence from the shelf.

Review also shapes design before any reviewer sees the work. Developers read the guidelines as a kind of building code and remove features that might attract a rejection, including alternative payment prompts, links to web sign-up pages or content categories that sit near a prohibited line. Economists call the wider pattern a chilling effect, and it is difficult to measure precisely because the applications that were never submitted leave no record. The measurable part is simpler. Every guideline narrows the distance between what a developer might build in an open distribution channel and what a developer builds when a single reviewer stands between the code and the market.

That narrowing is why review belongs inside an account of the commission rather than beside it. If the fee bought only payment processing, review would be an unrelated burden imposed by a separate department. In the bundled model, review is presented as part of what the fee funds, alongside security scanning, hosting and fraud prevention. The developer cannot decline the review and pay a lower rate, just as the developer cannot decline the storefront search index or the payment rail and assemble a cheaper package. The bundle holds together because each element reinforces the others, and review is the element that makes the bundle compulsory rather than merely convenient.

## Discovery and the Search Shelf

Discovery is the least tangible element in the bundle and, for many developers, the most valuable. An installed base of more than a billion devices means little to an independent developer if none of those device owners ever learns that an application exists. The storefront converts a vast population into a searchable shelf. A user types a task into a search field, compares a row of icons, screenshots and ratings, and installs within a minute. That sequence feels effortless from the user side of the glass. From the developer side, placement within it determines whether months of work produce revenue or silence.

Search inside the stores behaves like search elsewhere on the internet, with ranking signals that developers study and attempt to influence. Apple has stated that search drives a large share of App Store downloads, and its ranking weighs text relevance, ratings, download velocity and user behaviour after a result appears. Google Play applies its own signals across search and browse surfaces. A cottage practice called app store optimisation grew around those signals, adjusting titles, subtitles, keyword fields and screenshots in the same spirit as search engine optimisation for web pages. The existence of that practice is evidence that the shelf is not neutral storage. It is an allocation system for attention, and attention is scarce even when shelf space is infinite.

Editorial featuring adds a second allocation layer on top of algorithmic ranking. A place on a store home screen, a collection card or a seasonal list can multiply downloads for a small application in a way its developer could not purchase through ordinary advertising at a comparable cost. Apple employs editors who select applications for featuring, and Google Play runs its own collections and awards. Featuring carries no separate invoice, which makes it easy to overlook in an account of the fee. Yet it functions as part of the return on the commission for the applications chosen, while applications never chosen pay the same rate for a shelf position deep in the search results where few users scroll.

Paid placement complicates the picture further. Apple Search Ads, launched in 2016, allows a developer to bid for the top position in search results for chosen keywords, and Google offers comparable advertising across Play surfaces. A developer therefore pays the commission on revenue and may also pay for advertising simply to be seen by users searching for the very category the application serves. Large publishers treat that advertising as a normal customer acquisition cost alongside the commission. Small publishers experience the combination more sharply, because the free shelf and the paid shelf occupy the same screen and the boundary between them is a small label that many users never notice.

Ratings and reviews form the trust layer of discovery. A prospective user who has never heard of a developer can still judge an application by a star average built from thousands of strangers, by written reviews describing failures and strengths, and by the developer responses attached to complaints. Building an equivalent reputation system on an independent website would take years and would still lack the decisive advantage of appearing at the exact moment of decision. The store collapses reputation, comparison and transaction into one screen. That collapse is a genuine service, and it is one a developer cannot reproduce alone at any reasonable price.

The shelf metaphor also reveals the dependency.

## What the Small Business Rate Changed in Structure

The Apple Small Business Program, announced in November 2020 and effective from the start of 2021, cut the commission to fifteen percent for developers earning under one million dollars in proceeds during a calendar year. Google announced a parallel reduction for Google Play in 2021, applying fifteen percent to the first one million dollars of earnings each year. The headline in both cases was generosity toward small firms, and for an individual developer near the start of a business the difference between thirty percent and fifteen percent is material. A rate cut of that size can fund a part time contractor, a year of server costs or the margin that keeps a niche application alive.

The structural meaning of the change lies less in the number than in what the number admitted. For more than a decade the thirty percent rate had been presented as a uniform term of the storefront, a standard commission comparable across digital marketplaces. A tiered rate broke that uniformity. Once a platform concedes that a lower rate is sustainable for one class of developer, the rate stops looking like a law of nature and starts looking like a pricing decision that can be revisited for other classes. Spotify, Epic Games and other large critics made exactly that argument in public and regulatory settings, treating the small business tier as evidence that the standard rate reflected market power rather than cost.

The threshold design created its own incentives at the boundary. Under the Apple program, a developer whose proceeds pass one million dollars during a year moves to the standard rate for the remainder of that year, and eligibility in a later year depends on the earlier year total. A business approaching the line therefore faces a cliff in its unit economics rather than a gentle slope. Accountants call such a design a notch, and notches distort behaviour around the threshold, encouraging firms to defer launches, split catalogues across entities where rules permit, or simply accept that a successful December carries a higher toll than a quiet January. Google softened its version by applying the lower rate to the first million for every developer, large or small, which removed the cliff while keeping the headline gesture.

Administration added a second structural feature. The lower rate was not automatic in the Apple program at its launch. A developer had to enrol, declare associated accounts and accept that the platform would judge eligibility from its own sales records. That enrolment requirement matters because it kept the concession inside the existing system of control. The platform remained the accountant of the threshold, the judge of associated developer accounts and the party able to remove a participant. A discount granted through enrolment differs from a lower market price discovered through competition, even when the amount saved on a given sale is identical.

The program also clarified which developers the standard rate actually burdens. A firm earning nine hundred thousand dollars pays fifteen percent. A firm earning nine million pays thirty percent on the same kinds of transactions, through the same payment rail, delivered by the same servers, discovered through the same search shelf. The cost of serving the larger firm is higher in absolute terms but not proportionally higher per sale, since payment processing and delivery scale at low marginal cost. The tier therefore exposed the standard rate as a share of success rather than a recovery of expense.

## Subscriptions and the Second Year Rate

Subscriptions changed the commission from a tax on a moment into a share of a relationship. A single purchase ends when the transaction clears, but a subscription renews month after month or year after year, and the platform share renews with it. For a developer, that repetition transforms the arithmetic. Thirty percent of one sale is a cost of distribution. Thirty percent of every renewal, for as long as a customer remains subscribed, is a permanent partner in the business, present in every forecast and every valuation. The second year rate emerged from that tension, as platforms recognised that an indefinite full share would push their most valuable developers toward the web.

Apple announced in 2016 that the subscription share would fall to fifteen percent after a subscriber completes twelve paid months, and Google Play adopted a similar reduction for subscribers retained beyond twelve months before later broadening its subscription rate. The change rewarded retention, which both sides want, and it aligned the platform take with the argument that the store contributes most at the moment of discovery and initial conversion. After a year, a subscriber who renews through habit, loyalty to the service and satisfaction with its content is harder to describe as a customer the store delivers anew with each payment.

The mechanics reward continuity and punish interruption. Paid months accumulate under rules that account for pauses, upgrades, downgrades and movement between subscription tiers within a group. A subscriber who lapses and returns can reset or complicate the count depending on the timing and the store policy in force. Developers therefore study retention curves with the rate change in mind, designing annual plans, grace periods and win back offers partly to carry subscribers across the twelve month line where the margin improves. A pricing decision by the platform becomes, in that way, a design decision inside hundreds of thousands of independent businesses.

The improved second year margin also reshaped how subscription businesses are valued and sold. An acquirer examining a subscription application looks at the age profile of its subscriber base, because a base dominated by subscribers beyond twelve months yields more net revenue per dollar of gross sales than a young base paying the full rate. Two applications with identical gross subscription revenue can therefore differ in value on the strength of subscriber age alone. The commission schedule, written by the platform, reaches into private transactions between third parties and alters the price of assets the platform does not own.

Streaming media exposed the limits of the concession. Spotify argued for years that paying thirty percent, or even fifteen percent, on subscriptions sold inside the application placed it at a disadvantage against Apple Music, a rival service owned by the platform that pays no equivalent toll to a third party. Netflix responded to the economics by removing sign-up from its iOS application in 2018, directing prospective members to the web instead. The second year rate softened the burden for established subscribers but did nothing about the first year problem, the period when a service spends most heavily to acquire customers and can least afford to surrender nearly a third of the revenue those customers bring.

Developers also learned to build around the subscription clock. Introductory offers, free trials and promotional pricing all interact with the paid month count, and a poorly designed trial can delay the point at which full paid months begin to accumulate.

## Digital Goods, Physical Goods and the Boundary

The commission applies to digital goods and services consumed inside an application, and it stops at the boundary of the physical world. A game selling a sword, a streaming service selling a subscription and a dating application selling a boost all fall inside the toll. A ride across a city, a delivered meal and a pair of shoes ordered through a retail application fall outside it. That line, more than any other in store policy, reveals what the commission actually taxes. It does not tax commerce conducted through an application. It taxes content and features that the platform classifies as part of the application itself.

### Why are physical goods outside the app store commission?

Physical goods sit outside the commission because the platform supplies only the software doorway, while fulfilment, payment risk, delivery, returns and inventory belong to the merchant in the physical world beyond the application itself, where drivers, kitchens, warehouses and shops perform the work being sold.

The reasoning behind the boundary has a practical core. When Uber arranges a ride, the valuable service is a driver, a car, fuel and insurance moving through real streets, and the application is an ordering and dispatch layer. When DoorDash arranges a meal, a restaurant cooks food and a courier carries it, and disputes concern cold dishes and wrong addresses rather than software features. Platforms have long treated such transactions as the merchant business, processed through the merchant own payment arrangements, often through processors such as Stripe or Braintree. Extending a thirty percent toll to the full price of a ride or a meal would consume the entire margin of businesses that operate on thin percentages, and it would invite those businesses to abandon native applications altogether.

Digital goods present the opposite economics. A virtual currency pack, an unlocked level or a premium filter has near zero marginal cost once built, and delivery occurs inside the software the platform hosts and reviews. From the platform perspective, the application and the purchase are one product, so the store payment system becomes compulsory and the commission attaches. From the developer perspective, the same fact can look arbitrary, because a meditation application selling a guided session pays the toll while a marketplace selling a physical book of meditations does not, even though both sales begin with the same tap on the same device.

Boundary cases test the classification constantly. An application selling tickets to a live event, a service booking a human tutor by video, or a marketplace for handmade goods each mixes digital arrangement with real world fulfilment, and store guidelines devote detailed language to sorting those cases. Apple guidelines have treated real world services consumed outside the application as outside the purchase system, while features consumed inside it remain inside. The distinction sounds clean until a service is delivered through the screen itself, as with a live lesson or a streamed class, where lawyers and developers can argue both sides of the line from the same facts.

The boundary also explains a pattern in application design that users notice without naming it. Retail and transport applications ask for a payment card directly and keep it on file, while media and game applications route the same user through the store payment sheet with its familiar confirmation. The difference is not a design preference. It is the visible edge of the toll. Where the commission applies, the platform insists on owning the transaction.

## Readers, Media and the Categories Outside the Cut

Not every digital sale inside an application pays the toll in the ordinary way, because the stores carved out categories where a compulsory purchase system would break the business it tried to tax. Reader applications form the clearest carve-out. An application whose purpose is to let a user read or watch content bought elsewhere, such as an ebook library, a magazine archive or a video catalogue, may allow access to that earlier purchase without offering a new sale inside the application at all. The Kindle application became the familiar example for many users, who buy books on a website and read them in an application that contains no bookshop.

The carve-out exists because of how media retail developed before the smartphone. Publishers and retailers sold books, music and video through many channels, at prices set in a wider market, often under agency or wholesale arrangements that left no thirty percent margin to surrender. A toll at the standard rate would have forced media sellers either to raise prices inside one channel, creating different prices for the same book depending on the device used to buy it, or to withdraw from the storefront. Amazon chose withdrawal of the purchase function rather than the toll, and the reader rule ratified that outcome as policy. The application remains, the library remains, and the transaction moves to a browser where the platform takes nothing.

Multiplatform services received related treatment. A service available on the web, on a television and on rival devices may let subscribers who joined elsewhere use an application without buying through the store, provided the application follows rules about what it may say and link to. Those rules historically restricted even a mention that sign-up was possible on a website, which produced the strange user experience of an application that demanded an account it could not create and could not explain. Netflix and Spotify both lived inside that silence for long periods, relying on users to work out the missing step unaided.

The categories outside the cut widen further in specific verticals. Enterprise applications distributed to employees, applications sold with hardware, person to person experiences and certain educational and medical services each sit under particular rules that vary between stores and across jurisdictions. Apple also created arrangements over time for video partners and for news publishers, with conditions tied to participation in platform programs such as Apple News. Each arrangement is defensible on its own facts. Together they form a patchwork in which the commission depends not only on what is sold but on the history, leverage and regulatory exposure of the category selling it.

Patchwork pricing carries a lesson about the standard rate. A platform that can sustain fifteen percent for a news publisher, nothing on a book bought elsewhere, and a negotiated arrangement for a major video service is not recovering a uniform cost. It is setting prices category by category according to what each category will bear and what each category can do if refused. Economists recognise that pattern as price discrimination, a normal practice in many industries and a revealing one here, because it shifts debate away from whether the services have value and toward whether a single gatekeeper should set the discriminating prices for an entire device ecosystem.

For developers outside the favoured categories, the carve-outs can feel less like policy than like a map of power.

## Alternative Payments and Steering Rules

A developer who accepts the commission rate may still ask a separate question. May the developer tell users that the same purchase costs less somewhere else, or offer another payment route altogether. Steering rules govern that question, and for many years the major stores answered it largely in the negative. Anti-steering provisions in Apple guidelines barred applications from directing users to purchase mechanisms outside the application, including through buttons, external links and calls to action. Google Play maintained its own payments policy requiring its billing system for digital goods sold in applications distributed through Play, with exceptions that grew only gradually.

The commercial logic from the platform side is straightforward. A store that builds the shelf, hosts the binary and processes the payment regards a link that diverts the transaction to a website as an attempt to take the benefit of distribution while escaping the toll that funds it. Free riding is the term usually applied. From the developer side, the same link looks like ordinary price communication, the digital equivalent of a shopkeeper telling a customer that an item costs less at the shop own counter. The dispute is therefore not really about links. It is about whether the platform owns the customer relationship that its storefront helped create, or merely rents access to that relationship transaction by transaction.

Spotify carried the argument to regulators, filing a complaint with the European Commission in 2019 that accused Apple of acting as both player and referee, taxing a rival music service while favouring its own. The complaint focused attention on steering because the practical injury was communicative as much as financial. Spotify could sell subscriptions on its own website at a price untouched by the commission, but inside its application it could not tell users plainly that the option existed or what it cost. A rule about speech inside an interface became the mechanism that protected a rule about money.

Litigation sharpened the issue in the United States. In Epic Games v. Apple, decided in 2021 after a trial in the Northern District of California, the court rejected most of Epic claims under federal antitrust law but found the anti-steering provisions unlawful under California unfair competition law, and it issued an injunction against their enforcement in the covered form. The judgment distinguished between the platform right to charge for its services and a right to silence developers about alternatives. Later proceedings over the design and commission attached to external purchase links showed how contested compliance could become, as Apple introduced an entitlement system for external links alongside a reduced but still substantial commission on linked purchases, and Epic challenged that response as inadequate.

### Can a developer use another payment system inside an app?

In many cases a developer may offer another payment route only under store rules, an entitlement, a regional law or a specific program, while the default rule for digital goods still requires the store billing system for purchases made inside the application on the platform in question.

Regulators outside the United States moved on parallel tracks. The Netherlands Authority for Consumers and Markets ordered changes for dating applications in a dispute centred on payment choice, and South Korea amended its Telecommunications Business Act in 2021 to bar app market operators from forcing a particular payment system, a first statute of its kind. The European Union Digital Markets Act later imposed obligations on designated gatekeepers that reached payment and steering practices across the region.

## The Developer Side of the Ledger

The developer ledger begins with the gross price a user agrees to pay and then subtracts line after line before any money reaches the business that built the product. A ten dollar purchase at the standard rate yields seven dollars before tax treatment, refunds, chargebacks and the developer own costs. From the seven dollars come server bills, salaries, support, analytics, advertising and the next version of the application itself. None of that arithmetic is hidden, and developers accept many deductions in ordinary commerce. The distinctive feature of the store deduction is that it scales with revenue rather than with use, so success enlarges the payment to the platform faster than it enlarges any cost the platform bears in serving that success.

Payment processing supplies the comparison developers reach for first. Card networks and processors such as Stripe charge in the region of three percent for a comparable transaction, including interchange and processing margin, though exact pricing varies by volume, geography and risk. A developer comparing thirty percent with three percent is not claiming the store does nothing beyond processing. The comparison isolates the one component with a clear competitive price and asks what the remaining twenty seven points purchase. Distribution, review, discovery and trust are the usual answers, and each has value, but none is priced separately anywhere in the system, so the developer cannot test whether the bundle price beats the sum of alternatives obtainable in an open market.

Scale changes the emotional register of the ledger without changing its structure. For a hobbyist earning a few hundred dollars a year, the commission is an abstraction inside a hobby that also brings delight, learning and an audience that would otherwise be unreachable. For a company with fifty employees whose entire revenue passes through two storefronts, the commission is the largest single line after payroll, larger than rent, larger than cloud infrastructure, and payable to a counterparty that also competes with the company in adjacent categories and writes the rules of its marketplace. Epic Games, with Fortnite revenue at a scale few applications reach, could afford to force the issue in court in 2020 by inserting its own payment option and accepting removal from the store. Most developers cannot treat delisting as a litigation strategy.

The ledger also contains costs that never appear as percentages. Guideline compliance consumes engineering time whenever rules change, as they did around privacy labels, account deletion and sign-in options. A rejection near a launch date can waste a marketing spend committed months earlier. Support teams handle users who believe the developer controls refunds that the platform actually administers, and finance teams reconcile store reports that arrive on platform schedules in platform formats. These frictions are individually small and collectively constant, and they fall on the developer whether the application earns millions or barely covers its developer program fee.

Against those entries stand real credits that an honest ledger must record. The store supplies a payment relationship with users who would never enter card details on an unknown developer website. It supplies updates delivered silently, licences verified without developer effort, tax collection and remittance across dozens of jurisdictions, and protection against a meaningful share of piracy and fraud. A developer selling software from a personal website in the era before the stores handled every one of those burdens personally or paid separate vendors for each. The bundle solved genuine problems, and nostalgia for an unbundled world should not pretend those problems were small.

## The Platform Side of the Ledger

The platform ledger starts from a different first line. Before any commission exists, a company designs a device, builds an operating system, develops programming tools, maintains documentation, runs developer programs and persuades hundreds of millions of people to buy the hardware and carry it daily. Apple spent decades and enormous capital reaching the position where an iPhone owner trusts the device with payment credentials and personal data. Google built Android and gave the operating system to device makers, then built Play as the commercial centre of that ecosystem. From that side of the table, the commission is not a toll on someone else business. It is the return on the platform itself, the mechanism by which the software marketplace pays for the world that made it possible.

Operating the store costs real money at real scale. Data centres store and deliver application binaries and updates for a catalogue measured in millions of titles. Review teams, automated scanning systems, fraud investigators and developer support staff all draw salaries. Payment operations carry interchange fees, fraud losses, refund handling and the cost of collecting and remitting taxes and value added taxes across jurisdictions with conflicting rules. Apple has cited figures in legal proceedings for its investment in tools and distribution, and the Epic trial record included extensive testimony about App Store costs and profitability contested by both sides. The exact margin remained disputed in public argument, which itself shows that the platform ledger is not a simple cost sheet open for inspection.

Intellectual property supplies the platform strongest legal framing. In Epic Games v. Apple, the court accepted that Apple held rights in its intellectual property and could charge a licence for its use, even while ruling against the anti-steering provisions under California law. That distinction matters for the ledger because it relocates the commission from a service fee, which invites cost comparison, to a royalty, which invites a different question about what access to a protected system is worth to the party seeking entry. A royalty is judged by the value of what is licensed and the alternatives available to the licensee, not by the licensor marginal cost of permitting one more use.

The platform also carries risks that developers rarely price. A fraudulent application that steals money damages trust in every application on the device, including those of honest developers who had no part in the fraud. A privacy scandal involving one popular title can trigger regulatory attention for the whole ecosystem and depress user willingness to install anything new. The store operator absorbs that reputational exposure because users blame the device in their hand before they blame a developer whose name they barely registered. Curation, however imperfect, is partly an insurance system against collective damage, and the commission is partly its premium.

Competition forms the most contested entry. Platform defenders point to rival stores, rival devices, the open web and, on Android, alternative stores and direct installation as evidence that no developer faces a single unavoidable gate. Samsung operates its own Galaxy Store, Amazon runs an Android app store, and a user dissatisfied with one phone ecosystem can buy another. Developer critics answer that switching costs, user habits and the concentration of spending power make those alternatives weak in practice for reaching high value customers, particularly on iOS where no rival store historically operated.

## Two-Sided Market Logic and Network Effects

The economic frame that organises both ledgers comes from the theory of two-sided markets, developed most influentially by Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole in Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets, published in 2003. A two-sided platform serves two groups whose value to each other runs in both directions, and it prospers by getting the pricing structure right, not merely the pricing level. Rochet and Tirole showed that a platform may rationally subsidise the side whose participation attracts the other side, while collecting most of its revenue from the side that values access to the first group more highly. Price, in that analysis, follows the pattern of cross-side attraction rather than the cost of serving either group alone.

The application store fits the model with unusual neatness. Users receive the storefront, the payment system and much of the discovery machinery without a separate charge, because a large and confident user base is the asset that draws developers. Developers pay, through the commission, because users are present, equipped with stored payment credentials and habituated to installing software from one trusted place. The subsidy and the toll are two halves of one design. Charging users directly for the store would shrink the audience that makes the store worth paying for, while charging developers nothing would leave the platform to recover its return from hardware margins alone, a model closer to a traditional computer market than to the storefront economy that emerged after 2008.

Network effects strengthen the arrangement with every additional participant. Each new application makes a device marginally more useful to its owner, and each new device owner makes the platform marginally more attractive to developers deciding where to spend limited engineering time. Economists distinguish the direct effect within a group from the indirect effect across groups, and the store runs on the indirect kind. A games developer does not benefit from the presence of other games developers, and may suffer from their competition, but benefits greatly from the presence of millions of players. A player benefits from the crowd of developers competing to serve the player. The platform sits at the crossing point of those benefits and charges the side positioned to pay.

The delivery machinery underneath the crossing point is easy to forget because it works silently. An update published in one country must reach devices on every continent, intact, authenticated and fast, whether the file is a small utility or a game measured in gigabytes. Understanding [how cascading failures work in cloud systems](/2026/11/14/how-cascading-failures-work-in-cloud-systems/) clarifies why that reliability is an achievement rather than a default, since a store serving a billion devices concentrates demand in ways that can turn a local fault into a global outage. The platform operates that infrastructure, absorbs its failures publicly and prices its dependability into the same commission that pays for everything else in the bundle.

Two-sided logic also explains why the fiercest disputes concern rules rather than the percentage alone. In a two-sided market, the platform governs the terms on which the sides meet, and those terms decide how the total value created by their meeting is divided. Steering rules decide whether a developer may carry a relationship formed on the platform into a cheaper channel. Review rules decide which products may meet users at all. Category carve-outs decide which sellers pay for the meeting and which attend free. A change of three points in the rate moves money.

## Console, PC and Rival Stores Compared

A commission rate means little until it sits beside the rates charged by other channels for a comparable sale. Console stores, personal computer storefronts, and mobile stores grew up under different hardware economics, different degrees of openness, and different expectations about who pays for the platform itself. Yet their headline shares cluster in a narrow band, and that clustering tells a developer more than any single rate card. A console maker sells hardware, often at thin margins, and treats software sales as the place where the platform recovers its investment. A personal computer store sells no hardware at all and competes against direct sale and rival launchers on the same machine. A mobile store sits between those poles, tied to a device the customer bought for many purposes beyond software.

The comparison settles what a headline percentage actually buys in each channel and where a lower figure reflects a narrower bundle rather than a cheaper version of the same one. Console storefronts operated by Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo have long used a thirty percent share as their standard for digital game sales, a figure documented across developer accounts and industry reporting over many console generations. That share sits on top of licensing arrangements, certification testing, and development kit access that have no direct equivalent in mobile stores. The console toll therefore pays for entry to a closed system in a stronger sense than a phone store does, because no rival storefront can operate on the same console at all. A developer who refuses the console terms has no alternative route to that installed base, not even a cumbersome one.

Personal computer distribution shows a wider spread, and the spread maps closely onto how much each channel does beyond moving a file. Valve built Steam into a storefront with community forums, workshop support for modifications, cloud saves, matchmaking services, and a review system that shapes buying decisions, and Steam kept a thirty percent headline share long after rival launchers appeared. The Epic Games Store entered with a twelve percent share and funded exclusive releases to build an audience, a strategy that showed both the attraction of a lower toll and the cost of assembling the audience that makes any toll worth paying. Microsoft set a twelve percent share for personal computer games in its own store in 2021, using price to draw developers toward a storefront that competed for attention on machines where Steam already held the audience.

A parallel from another industry clarifies the pattern, because cinemas faced the same bundling question when distribution moved from physical prints to encrypted digital files: [how digital cinema packages work](/2026/10/21/how-digital-cinema-packages-work/) describes a system where delivery, security, and playback authorization arrive as one package rather than as separable services. App stores follow that same logic. The file, the payment, the licence check, and the storefront shelf arrive together, and the comparison table records the price of the package in each channel rather than the price of any single part inside it.

| Channel | Share taken by the store | Condition for a lower share | What the share principally covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple App Store standard rate | Thirty percent | None at the standard tier | Distribution, payment processing, review and storefront placement |
| Apple App Store small business rate | Fifteen percent | Developer proceeds under one million dollars in a calendar year | The same bundle at a reduced toll for small sellers |
| Google Play standard rate | Thirty percent | None at the standard tier | Distribution, payment processing, review and storefront placement |
| Google Play subscription rate | Fifteen percent | A subscriber retained beyond twelve months | Billing, account handling and delivery for a retained subscriber |
| Steam | Thirty percent, falling with revenue | Twenty five percent past ten million dollars and twenty percent past fifty million dollars | Distribution, payment processing, community features and storefront placement |
| Microsoft Store for PC games | Twelve percent | Applies to PC game sales in that store | Distribution and payment processing for PC games |
| Direct web sale | No store share | The developer runs its own storefront | Whatever the developer separately buys for payment, hosting and discovery |

Read across the rows, the pattern is consistent. Where a store commands an audience that a developer cannot reach another way, the share stays high even under competitive pressure. Where a store must persuade developers to bring supply to an audience still being built, the share falls, and the store absorbs costs elsewhere or accepts thinner returns while it builds position. The percentage alone never settles whether a channel is expensive. That judgment needs the size and buying habits of the audience behind the percentage, which is the subject the next sections take up in detail.

## Why Rival Stores Settled on Similar Rates

Rival stores did not arrive at similar rates by accident, and they did not arrive there by a single agreement either. The thirty percent figure carried a history before phones entered the story. Console licensing in the era of physical cartridges and discs accustomed publishers to surrendering a substantial share for manufacture, distribution, and access to a controlled platform. Retail distribution of boxed software also took a large slice of the purchase price once retailer margins, manufacturing, shipping, and returns were counted. When digital storefronts replaced boxes, platform owners kept a share in that familiar range and presented it as continuity rather than invention, even though the underlying costs of delivery had fallen sharply.

Imitation did much of the remaining work. A new storefront choosing a rate looks sideways at the rate customers and developers already treat as normal, because a familiar figure needs no explanation and a novel figure invites suspicion about what has been left out. Apple set thirty percent for the App Store in 2008, and Google adopted the same headline figure for Android distribution, and each company could point to the other as evidence that the rate reflected a market rather than a whim. Steam held thirty percent on personal computers for years before its revenue tiers arrived, and console stores held the same line, so a developer moving between platforms met the same number in room after room. Repetition turned a business choice into an apparent fact of nature.

Costs pushed in the same direction, though they explain less than platform owners suggest. Every large store pays for payment processing, fraud screening, refund handling, content delivery networks, review staff, developer tooling, and the engineering that keeps a storefront running across thousands of device models. Yet those costs do not add up to thirty percent of every sale for most transactions, which is why the rate persisted through imitation and bargaining power rather than through cost accounting alone. The share is a price for access, and access prices settle where the holder of the audience can sustain them.

Bargaining power explains why the figure bent in some places and held in others. Large publishers with audiences of their own negotiated privately or built their own launchers, as Electronic Arts and Ubisoft did on personal computers, and their ability to walk away gave them leverage a small studio never holds. Subscription services changed the calculation again, because a subscriber retained over time produces predictable revenue, and Google and Apple both cut subscription shares to fifteen percent after the first year to keep developers inside the system. Small business programmes cut the rate for developers under a proceeds threshold, which reduced pressure from the most sympathetic critics while leaving the standard rate untouched for the largest earners.

Convergence also survived direct challenge, which is the strongest evidence that the rate rests on structure rather than habit alone. The Epic Games Store offered twelve percent from its launch in 2018 and spent heavily on exclusives and free game giveaways, yet Steam retained its dominant position and its higher headline rate, because players kept libraries, friends lists, and habits inside Steam that a lower developer share could not move. A rate cut aimed at developers does not automatically move the customers whose presence gives the rate its value. Rival stores settled on similar rates because each store with a locked in audience faces the same temptation and the same defence, and each store without one discovers that cutting the rate is the easiest part of competing.

## The Antitrust Cases and What They Tested

Antitrust law gave the argument over commissions a courtroom, a record, and a set of definitions that commercial debate had avoided. Two cases brought by Epic Games, one against Apple and one against Google, tested the same underlying complaint through different legal routes and produced different outcomes, and the difference between those outcomes is as instructive as either result alone. Epic Games, maker of Fortnite, deliberately provoked both disputes in August 2020 by adding a direct payment option inside Fortnite that bypassed the store payment systems, and both platform owners removed the game from their stores on the same day.
The Apple case went to a bench trial in the Northern District of California, and Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers issued judgment in 2021. The court defined the relevant market as digital mobile game transactions, a definition narrower than the market for all mobile software and broader than the market for Fortnite alone. On that definition, Apple held a large share but was held not a monopolist under federal law, and most of Epic Games claims failed. One claim succeeded under California unfair competition law. The court struck down the anti-steering provision that barred developers from telling customers about cheaper purchasing options outside the app, and it ordered Apple to allow developers to communicate those options and link toward them. The judgment therefore separated two questions that public debate had merged. Market power in a defined market was one question, and a specific contractual restraint on communication was another, and the answers differed.

The Google case followed a different path and reached a different destination. A jury trial in 2023 considered Google Play and the Google Play billing system, and the jury found that Google had maintained an unlawful monopoly in Android app distribution and in app billing markets. Evidence about agreements with device makers, payments to developers under arrangements such as Project Hug, and the treatment of rival stores persuaded the jury in a way that the Apple record had not persuaded a judge. The contrast matters for anyone reading the commission debate through the cases. The same thirty percent figure appeared in both courtrooms. The surrounding conduct, the openness of the underlying operating system, and the definition of the market decided the outcomes, not the percentage standing alone.

A further chapter reached the highest court as a dispute about compliance rather than about the original liability findings. In November 2026, thirty two states and the District of Columbia filed a brief at the Supreme Court of the United States backing Epic Games in Apple Inc v. Epic Games Inc, No. 25-1311, over compliance with the anti-steering injunction. The brief addressed whether the remedy ordered in 2021 had been honoured in substance, a narrower question than whether the commission itself was lawful.

Taken together, the cases tested four distinct propositions. A store may hold a dominant share of a carefully defined market without violating federal monopoly law. A store may still lose on a specific restraint that silences developers about outside options. A jury may treat payments and agreements that suppress rival stores as monopoly maintenance. And a remedy may generate years of further dispute about fees charged on linked purchases and the design of compliance itself. None of the courts set a lawful commission percentage, and none ordered a store to unbundle its services at a regulated price. The litigation moved the boundaries of conduct around the toll. It did not abolish the toll, and it did not price it.

## Regulation and the Designated Gatekeepers

Regulators in Europe chose a route that courts in the United States had not taken, moving from case by case adjudication toward rules written in advance for firms designated in advance. The European Union adopted the Digital Markets Act in 2022, and the statute named a category of gatekeepers, large platform firms whose core services sit between businesses and consumers at a scale that makes avoidance impractical. The European Commission designated Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, and Microsoft as gatekeepers in September 2023, covering twenty two core platform services between them. App stores operated by Apple and Google fell squarely inside that framework, and the obligations attached to designation rather than to any finding in a single lawsuit.

The obligations changed the practical terms on which a store operates in Europe. A gatekeeper store must allow third party app stores and sideloading under the conditions the statute sets, must permit developers to steer customers toward offers outside the store, and must allow alternative payment processing inside apps. Apple responded with a revised structure for Europe that introduced alternative terms, a reduced commission for developers who adopted them, and a core technology fee charged per install beyond a threshold, a design that itself drew argument about whether the new structure honoured the statute in substance. The Commission opened proceedings and issued preliminary findings on steering, and the argument shifted from whether rules should exist to whether a particular fee design complied with rules already in force.

That shift in the argument is the deepest change regulation produced. Under antitrust litigation, a developer had to prove a market, prove power inside it, and prove harm, a burden that consumed years and millions in legal costs before any remedy arrived. Under the Digital Markets Act, the obligations arrive with designation, and the argument concerns compliance, exemptions, and the technical design of choice screens, prompts, and fee schedules. Regulation therefore reaches conduct that litigation leaves alone, including fee structures applied uniformly to developers too small to sue. It also creates a different risk, because a rule written for a gatekeeper may fit one store model well and another badly, and compliance design can reproduce the original barrier in a new form, as the dispute over the core technology fee illustrated.

Other jurisdictions watched the European experiment and adapted parts of it to their own legal traditions. South Korea amended its Telecommunications Business Act in 2021 to bar app stores from forcing a single payment system, a measure aimed at the same chokepoint the European statute addressed. Japan adopted legislation for large mobile platform operators that addressed steering and rival stores. The United Kingdom built a digital markets regime giving its competition authority power to designate firms with strategic market status and impose conduct requirements.
For the commission itself, regulation trimmed edges rather than reset the headline figure. No major regulator set a maximum percentage for app store commissions. Instead, regulators attacked the conditions that protect the percentage, the bans on steering, the bans on rival stores, and the requirement to use the store payment system. That choice reflects a judgment shared across several legal systems, that competition over the bundle is a safer instrument than a price cap on the bundle. Whether that judgment holds depends on whether rival stores and outside payment routes actually win customers once the legal barriers fall, which returns the question to the audience and the habits that made the original stores valuable.

## The Case Against the Bundle, Given Its Best Form

The strongest case against the commission is not that stores do nothing. It is that stores charge for several things as one indivisible price, and forbid the customer from declining parts that customer does not need. Epic Games built its public and legal campaign on exactly that distinction. Tim Sweeney, the founder of Epic Games, argued that payment processing is a commodity service that trades at a small fraction of thirty percent, that distribution of a digital file costs little at the margin, and that a developer with an audience of its own gains nothing from storefront discovery it never uses.

Spotify pressed a parallel version of the same argument from the music market, and its complaint carried weight because Spotify competes directly with a store owner service. Daniel Ek and Spotify legal leadership argued over many years, including in a complaint to the European Commission in 2019, that Apple charged Spotify a thirty percent share on subscriptions sold through the store while Apple Music paid no equivalent toll, and that anti-steering rules prevented Spotify from telling subscribers about cheaper routes. The European Commission fined Apple in March 2024 in the music streaming case, a decision centred on steering restrictions. The Coalition for App Fairness, founded in 2020 by Epic Games, Spotify, Match Group, and other developers, organised that argument into a shared platform, contending that the bundle is forced, that the rate is uniform regardless of value received, and that store owners compete against their own tenants under rules the tenants cannot match.

The payment comparison gives the argument its sharpest edge. Card payments carry interchange and processing costs that, even with fraud screening and chargeback handling added, sit far below thirty percent of a transaction for ordinary digital sales. A developer selling a ten dollar item surrenders three dollars to the store, while the payment component of that surrender, priced on its own in competitive markets, would account for only a portion of the three dollars. The remainder pays for distribution, review, discovery, and access, whether the developer values those services at that price or values them at nothing.

Uniformity strengthens the complaint further. A bundle priced identically for a small utility and a global game treats unlike sellers as if they consumed identical services, and the store refusal to sell the parts separately blocks the market test that would reveal what each part is worth. The refusal to unbundle, backed by rules against steering and rival stores, is therefore the centre of the case. The percentage is the symptom. The compulsion is the charge.

The thesis survives that case in one place and narrows in another, and honesty requires naming both. It survives on access. No critic priced the installed base itself, the billion devices already configured with accounts, payment credentials, and buying habits, and that base is the asset the toll principally rents. A payment button supplies none of it. The thesis narrows on compulsion and on uniformity, because a toll for access does not justify every condition attached to access, and the steering bans and payment mandates that courts and regulators struck down were defences of the bundle rather than parts of the bundle itself. A store can charge for its shelf without gagging the tenant about other shelves. The best form of the opposing case therefore does not dissolve the commission. It separates the defensible rent for audience and infrastructure from the indefensible restraints that protected that rent from comparison, and it leaves the percentage to be argued as a price, openly, rather than as a condition of silence.

## What a Store Does That a Payment Button Cannot

A payment button performs one act well. It takes money from a buyer, moves it to a seller, handles the authorisation, and records the transaction. A store surrounds that single act with a system that begins before the buyer arrives and continues long after the money moves. Comparing the store commission with a payment processing fee alone mistakes one component for the whole machine, in the same way that comparing a rent payment with a locksmith fee mistakes one part of a shop for the shop itself. The difference shows most clearly in the work a buyer never sees.

Discovery is the first layer. A storefront ranks, categorises, features, and recommends, and its search function answers the buyer who knows a need but not a product name. Most software is found rather than sought by brand, especially outside games, so placement inside store search and category charts decides whether a product exists commercially at all. Editorial collections, seasonal features, and personalised recommendations built from purchase history steer demand toward products that advertising alone would struggle to surface, because the recommendation arrives at the moment of intent inside the place where buying happens. A payment button waits at the end of a journey that the store itself largely creates.

Trust is the second layer, and it is built from unglamorous machinery. Store review examines submitted software for malware, for deceptive behaviour, for privacy practices, and for compliance with content rules, and while review misses problems and rejects innocent developers in error, the existence of a gate changes buyer behaviour. A buyer installs from a store with a confidence that a download from an unfamiliar website rarely earns, because the store account, the refund route, and the removal power stand behind the transaction. Parental controls, age ratings, permission disclosures, and privacy labels extend that trust to buyers who cannot evaluate software directly. None of those functions appears in a payment authorisation, and all of them raise the conversion rate that the commission is ultimately charged against.

Delivery and aftercare form the third layer. A store hosts the file, serves it quickly in the buyer country, manages updates across millions of devices, supports beta testing channels, handles licence restoration when a buyer replaces a phone, and processes refunds and subscription cancellations through an account the buyer already holds. Family sharing, gift purchases, promotional codes, staged rollouts, and crash reporting belong to the same infrastructure. Each function is small in isolation. Assembled, they remove friction at every step where a sale or a renewal might otherwise fail, and friction removed at scale is worth money to a seller in a way that a per transaction fee comparison never captures.

The honest limit of the argument deserves equal space. A store does these things, but a store does not do all of them equally well for every seller, and a famous product with its own audience uses the discovery layer lightly while paying for it fully. The store case proves that the bundle contains real services beyond payment. It does not prove that the bundle price equals the value of those services for every developer, and it does not prove that the services must be sold as an indivisible set. What a payment button cannot do is reproduce the audience, the trust, and the delivery system on the day a developer needs them. Building equivalents is possible, as large publishers have shown with their own launchers and account systems, but the cost of building them is exactly the cost the commission lets a smaller seller rent instead of own.

## How Analysts Judge a Channel by Its Whole Toll

Judging a distribution channel by its headline percentage is the most common error in the commission debate, because the percentage is the one figure every channel publishes and the one figure that captures the least about what a sale actually costs. A disciplined comparison starts with the whole toll, meaning every charge and every lost sale that a channel imposes or prevents, set against every cost the channel removes from the seller ledger. Developers who run the same product through several channels learn the arithmetic quickly, because the channel with the higher share sometimes returns more money per user acquired than the channel with the lower share, and the difference lies in conversion, retention, and reach rather than in the rate card.

### How can a developer compare channels by total cost rather than headline rate?

A developer compares channels by adding every charge the channel imposes or makes necessary, the store share, payment fees outside the store, the cost of billing support, refund handling, tax collection work and the discovery the developer must buy elsewhere, then dividing that total by the revenue the channel delivers.

Conversion comes first in the calculation. A store account with stored payment credentials, biometric authorisation, and a familiar purchase flow completes transactions that an unfamiliar web checkout loses at the card entry screen. The gap between a one tap purchase and a form demanding card numbers, billing addresses, and account creation is measured in abandoned sales, and each abandoned sale is a cost of the cheaper channel that never appears on its invoice.

Costs avoided form the second column. A direct channel charges no store share, but it charges for payment processing, sales tax and value added tax handling across jurisdictions, fraud screening, chargebacks, hosting, content delivery, customer support for billing disputes, and the engineering time to keep a checkout compliant and secure. Tax compliance alone has driven small developers toward intermediary sellers, because digital sales tax rules differ by country and sometimes by state, and a store that acts as merchant of record absorbs that burden in exchange for its share. The comparison that matters is therefore not thirty percent against a payment fee. It is thirty percent against the full stack of fees, salaries, and lost sales that the alternative quietly assembles.

Audience quality forms the third column, and it resists simple measurement, which is why headline comparisons omit it. A store delivers buyers who arrived intending to spend inside a software marketplace, carrying accounts and habits formed over years. A web channel delivers whoever the developer marketing can reach, at advertising prices set by auction against every other advertiser. Customer acquisition cost belongs inside the channel comparison for exactly that reason. A developer paying thirty percent on sales to buyers the store delivered may spend less, in total, than a developer paying nothing on sales to buyers the developer paid an advertising platform to find. The toll and the advertising bill are substitutes in part, and judging one without the other flatters whichever channel hides its cost in the other column.

The method also disciplines the opposite conclusion. When a product already owns its audience, through a brand, a community, or a subscription relationship formed elsewhere, the store columns empty out. Conversion gains shrink because loyal buyers tolerate friction, discovery adds little because buyers arrive by name, and the whole toll calculation then condemns the full share as payment for services the sale never used. That is why the same arithmetic that defends the commission for a new developer indicts it for an established one, and why uniform pricing across both cases draws fire from informed critics rather than from critics who misunderstand the bundle. The whole toll method does not pick a side in advance. It forces each side to count everything, including the costs its preferred channel prefers to leave off the page.

## What the Commission Buys the Customer

Discussion of the commission usually casts the customer as a bystander, a person whose money passes through an argument between a platform and a developer. That framing misses what the customer receives from the arrangement, because the store share funds services the customer uses directly and would notice immediately if those services vanished. The customer buys an app, but the customer also relies on the store as an account, a library, a safety check, and a returns desk, and those functions exist because transaction revenue pays for them rather than because the device maker supplies them from goodwill. A companion [reference point](https://vaultbook.net) is available for readers comparing the figures in prose of their own.

Safety screening is the most concrete benefit. Store review, imperfect as developers rightly describe it, screens out a large volume of malicious and fraudulent software before it reaches a phone, and the store power to remove a harmful app from every device at once has no equivalent in open web distribution. Permission controls let a customer see and revoke what an app may access, privacy labels summarise data practices in a form a non specialist can read, and age ratings and parental controls let a household govern purchases by children.

Purchase protection forms the second benefit, and customers feel it at the worst moments. A store account gives the buyer a single place to demand a refund, cancel a subscription started by accident, dispute a charge made by a child, and restore every past purchase onto a new device without contacting each developer separately. Subscription management inside one account ended the older pattern in which cancelling required hunting through a seller website for a buried option, a pattern regulators had criticised in direct selling for years. Family sharing extends one purchase across a household, and purchase history provides a record that survives lost receipts and changed email addresses. Each protection is a consumer service funded from the same pool the commission fills.

Curation and continuity form the third benefit, quieter than the other two. Store search, ratings, and reviews let a customer choose between rival apps with information gathered from millions of earlier buyers, and update systems keep installed software patched without any action by the owner, which closes security holes that unpatched software would leave open. Compatibility signalling warns a customer when an app will not run on a device, and regional storefronts handle currency, language, and local consumer law in a single flow. A customer who buys directly from a developer website gains none of that by default and gains each piece only where the individual seller chose to build it.

The customer side of the ledger also carries the costs, and a balanced account names them. Commission costs may pass through into higher prices where a developer prices to a target margin, and steering restrictions historically prevented customers from learning that the same purchase cost less elsewhere, which is why courts and regulators treated those restrictions as a consumer harm rather than a private contract term. The customer therefore stands on both sides of the argument at once. The commission buys the customer a safer, simpler, better organised marketplace, and the conditions that once protected the commission also kept the customer in the dark about alternatives. Separating the service from the silence is the reform that serves the customer best, because the customer can value the bundle honestly only when the price of leaving it is visible.

## The Toll and the Shelf It Rents

Every marketplace in commercial history has charged for the same underlying asset, a place where buyers already gather. A market stall rent buys foot traffic. A department store concession buys the reputation and the flow of the host store. A shopping centre lease buys position among other tenants who draw the crowd that each tenant alone could not assemble. The app store commission belongs to that lineage, updated for a marketplace where the shelf is infinite, the stock never spoils, and the crowd arrives carrying payment credentials and a decade of habits. The percentage startles because the goods are weightless. The logic underneath it is old.

The argument over the commission was never really an argument about whether the shelf has value. Epic Games built stores of its own, Spotify sells through channels that charge for access, and every critic of the thirty percent rate operates happily inside other marketplaces that take a share for audience and trust. The live argument concerns the terms attached to the rent, whether the tenant may tell customers about cheaper premises, whether a rival landlord may build on the same street, and whether the rent stays fixed while the services it buys grow cheaper to provide. Courts and regulators, from the judgment of Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in 2021 to the European Union gatekeeper rules of 2022, have mostly answered those narrower questions, trimming the conditions while leaving the shelf and its rent standing.

That pattern suggests the commission will change the way rents usually change, through pressure at the edges rather than through abolition. If buyers follow developers to cheaper channels, the toll was rent for a captive audience. If buyers stay where their accounts, libraries, and habits live, the toll was rent for something buyers themselves value.

A developer deciding where a product belongs, and a customer deciding where to buy it, both face the same final question in different dress. What does the toll buy, for this product and this buyer, that the alternatives do not supply at a lower total cost. Asked that way, the commission becomes a price, open to comparison, negotiation, and refusal where refusal is genuinely possible. The shelf remains. The rent remains. What the long fight over steering, rival stores, and payment choice has changed is the landlord power to forbid the tenant from ever pricing the shelf against the street outside.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is an app store commission?
An app store commission is the portion of a digital purchase price that the store operator retains before passing the remainder to the developer. It applies to paid downloads, in app purchases of digital content, and subscriptions sold through the store billing system. The retained portion functions as the price of selling through that storefront rather than as a separate invoice for each service involved. Rates are set in the developer agreement for each store, and the deduction happens automatically at the point of settlement, so a developer receives net proceeds rather than collecting the full price and paying a bill afterward. The structure resembles a consignment arrangement, where the seller of shelf space takes an agreed share of each completed transaction instead of charging rent in advance.

### Q: Why did thirty percent become the standard rate?
Thirty percent became the standard through a mixture of inheritance, imitation, and bargaining power. Console licensing and boxed software retail had accustomed publishers to surrendering large shares for manufacture, distribution, and shelf access long before digital storefronts existed. When Apple launched the App Store in 2008 with a seventy thirty split in favour of the developer, the figure felt familiar to the industry and generous beside older retail economics. Google adopted the same headline split for Android distribution, and Steam and the console stores held comparable lines, so repetition across every major channel turned one company choice into an industry convention. Once buyers and developers treated the figure as normal, any single store had little reason to undercut it without a strategic need for supply.

### Q: What does a developer keep from a ten dollar sale?
At the standard thirty percent rate, a developer keeps seven dollars from a ten dollar sale before the developer own costs and any applicable taxes on the developer side are considered. At a fifteen percent rate, the same sale returns eight dollars and fifty cents. The arithmetic scales directly, so a two dollar purchase returns one dollar and forty cents at the standard rate, while a one hundred dollar purchase returns seventy dollars. What the developer keeps is not profit, because development, servers, support, and marketing still come out of the retained share. The figure that matters for planning is therefore the net proceeds line in the store sales report, which shows the deduction applied transaction by transaction and the total actually remitted for the period.

### Q: What does the commission share actually pay for?
The share pays for a bundle of functions delivered as one service. Distribution covers hosting the software file, delivering it quickly in the buyer country, and managing updates across many device models. Payment handling covers authorisation, fraud screening, refunds, and subscription billing. Review covers examination of submitted software for malicious behaviour, deceptive practices, and rule compliance. Storefront placement covers search, categories, charts, recommendations, and editorial featuring, which together decide whether buyers encounter the product at all. Developer tooling, analytics, crash reporting, beta testing channels, and customer purchase records also draw on the same revenue. No store publishes a binding breakdown assigning a fixed portion of the percentage to each function, so the bundle is priced as a whole rather than itemised.

### Q: How does the small business rate work?
The small business rate halves the commission for developers whose proceeds stay below a defined threshold. Apple introduced its App Store Small Business Program with a fifteen percent rate for developers earning under one million dollars in proceeds during a calendar year, with eligibility assessed against the prior year and adjusted if earnings cross the threshold. Google applied a fifteen percent rate to the first one million dollars of earnings each year for developers enrolled in its corresponding programme. The design targets the complaint that a uniform rate burdens small sellers most heavily, while preserving the standard rate on the largest earners who generate most store revenue. A developer who grows past the threshold returns to the standard rate under the programme rules for that store.

### Q: How do subscriptions change the commission rate?
Subscriptions change the rate because a long retained subscriber is worth more to both parties than a first month buyer. Apple and Google both reduce the share on a subscription to fifteen percent after a subscriber completes twelve months of paid service, rewarding the developer for retention while keeping the subscriber inside store billing. Google also applies fifteen percent to many subscription products from the start under its revised terms for enrolled developers. The reduction recognises that acquisition value, the main service a store provides, is delivered once at the beginning of a subscription, while later renewals depend mainly on the quality of the product itself. Billing, retry handling for failed payments, grace periods, and cancellation management remain with the store even after the rate falls.

### Q: Do physical goods and real world services pay the commission?
Physical goods and real world services generally sit outside the commission because store rules draw a line between content consumed inside the app and goods or services used in the physical world. A retailer selling clothing, a restaurant selling a meal, or a ride service selling transport collects payment through its own arrangements or through store approved methods for such categories without the digital goods share applying to the underlying sale. The distinction rests on fulfilment. The store delivers a digital item instantly and completely, while a physical purchase involves manufacture, shipping, and returns that the store never touches. Boundary disputes arise with hybrid products, such as digital access bundled with a physical item, and each store publishes category rules that developers must follow when classifying a purchase.

### Q: Do free apps pay a commission?
A free app pays no commission on its download because the commission is calculated as a share of a purchase price, and a price of zero yields a share of zero. Free apps still operate under the developer agreement, still pass review, and still pay the annual developer programme fee where a store charges one. Money enters the picture when a free app sells digital content, features, or subscriptions inside the app, because those transactions attract the standard share unless a reduced rate applies. Advertising supported apps occupy a different position, since advertising revenue collected outside the store billing system is not a store transaction. The free model therefore uses store distribution, review, and delivery without a per download toll, which explains why stores treat in app purchases as the point where the toll attaches.

### Q: How does Steam compare with the mobile stores?
Steam compares closely on the headline figure and differs on structure at high revenue. Valve charges thirty percent on initial sales, then reduces the share to twenty five percent once a product passes ten million dollars in revenue, and to twenty percent past fifty million dollars. Mobile stores historically applied a flat rate regardless of scale, with reductions tied to subscriptions and small business status rather than to product revenue. Steam also operates on open personal computers where rival stores and direct sale are technically possible, while mobile stores benefit from tighter integration with the device operating system. Steam bundles community forums, modification support, cloud saves, and a powerful review culture into its offering, so its share purchases a feature set that extends well beyond delivery and billing alone.

### Q: How do console stores compare with mobile stores?
Console stores operated by Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo have used thirty percent as the standard share for digital game sales, matching the mobile headline figure. The surrounding arrangement differs in important ways. Console platforms are closed systems with no rival storefront permitted on the hardware, and developers also face certification requirements, licensing terms, and development hardware costs that have no direct mobile equivalent. A console maker invests in hardware design, controllers, and platform software, and may sell the machine itself at a thin margin, recovering value through software sales. Mobile stores sit on general purpose devices bought for communication and many other tasks. The identical percentage therefore rests on different foundations, and a comparison that stops at the number misses the certification burden on one side and the device subsidy argument on the other.

### Q: What does steering mean in the app store debate?
Steering means a developer directing customers toward a purchasing option outside the app, such as a website where the same content costs less or carries different terms. Anti-steering rules barred developers from including links, buttons, or even written information telling customers that an outside option existed. The practice became a central legal issue because it separated the commission from customer knowledge. A developer could accept the store terms yet still resent being forbidden to mention an alternative that the developer operated lawfully elsewhere. Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers struck down the Apple anti-steering provision under California law in 2021, and the Digital Markets Act in Europe required gatekeepers to permit steering. The word now labels the wider fight over whether a store may control communication inside an app it distributes.

### Q: What did the Epic Games case against Apple decide?
The Epic Games case against Apple ended with the 2021 judgment of Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in the Northern District of California. The court defined the relevant market as digital mobile game transactions and held that Apple was not a monopolist under federal antitrust law, so most claims by Epic Games failed, including the demand to force rival stores onto the platform. Epic Games also failed in its claim that the commission structure itself violated federal law. One claim succeeded under California unfair competition law, where the court found the anti-steering provision unlawful and ordered Apple to let developers communicate outside purchasing options and link toward them. Fortnite remained absent from the store for an extended period because Epic Games had breached the developer agreement, a point the court addressed separately from the competition findings.

### Q: What did the Epic Games case against Google decide?
The Epic Games case against Google reached a jury in the Northern District of California in 2023, and the jury returned a verdict for Epic Games on its claims. The jury found that Google Play and the Google Play billing system constituted markets in which Google held monopoly power, and that Google maintained that power unlawfully through agreements with device makers, arrangements that discouraged rival stores, and requirements tying distribution to the store billing system. Evidence concerning payments to developers and manufacturers, including the programme known as Project Hug, featured prominently in the trial. The verdict differed from the Apple outcome because the underlying operating system allowed alternative distribution in principle, which made the contractual barriers look like deliberate suppression of an existing possibility rather than the design of a closed system.

### Q: What did the Digital Markets Act change for app stores?
The Digital Markets Act, adopted by the European Union in 2022, imposed advance obligations on firms designated as gatekeepers, including Apple and Alphabet. For app stores, the statute required allowance of third party stores and sideloading, permission for developers to steer customers toward outside offers, and access to alternative payment processing inside apps. The change in method mattered as much as the list of duties. Instead of a developer proving monopoly harm in court over several years, obligations attached automatically to designation, and argument moved to compliance design. Apple introduced alternative business terms for Europe with a reduced commission option and a core technology fee, which in turn drew proceedings about whether the new structure honoured the statute. Other gatekeeper duties covered interoperability, data use, and self preferencing across designated services.

### Q: Can a developer sell on the web instead of in a store?
A developer can sell digital content through a website in many circumstances, and large services have done so for years by selling subscriptions and credits on their own pages for use inside their apps. The constraints come from store rules rather than from technology. Rules on steering historically limited what an app could say about web purchasing, and platform terms in some regions still govern links, buttons, and external payment flows, with the position differing between the United States, Europe, and other markets after litigation and regulation. Web selling also transfers work to the developer, including checkout construction, tax handling, fraud screening, and customer support for billing. The option is real, and its practical value depends on how many customers will leave the app, complete a web purchase, and return, a friction cost each developer must measure against the share saved.

### Q: How do refunds affect the commission?
A refund reverses the transaction on which the commission was calculated, so the store share attached to that sale is returned as part of the reversal rather than kept by the store. The developer proceeds for the sale disappear along with the purchase price, and settlement reports adjust accordingly in the period when the refund is processed. Chargebacks initiated through a card issuer follow a similar reversal path, though they may carry additional handling consequences under store policies. Subscription refunds and partial refunds for unused periods are handled under each store published policy, which also decides whether the customer or the developer initiates the request. Repeated refund abuse can trigger store enforcement against a customer account. For planning purposes, a developer treats refunds as negative sales that reduce both gross revenue and the commission total in the same proportion.

### Q: How do taxes and value added tax interact with the share?
Taxes sit alongside the commission rather than inside it, and the interaction depends on who acts as seller of record. In many regions, the store acts as merchant of record for app sales, collecting value added tax or sales tax from the buyer at the local rate, remitting it to the tax authority, and calculating the commission on the price under the store published method for that region. Where the developer is the seller of record in a direct web sale, the developer carries the duty to register, collect, and remit across every jurisdiction where customers live, a burden that grows quickly for digital products sold worldwide. Price displays also differ by country, with some markets showing tax inclusive prices and others adding tax at checkout, so identical headline prices can produce different net proceeds after tax and commission are both applied.

### Q: Why did rival stores converge on similar commission rates?
Convergence came from shared history, shared costs, and shared bargaining positions rather than from a single decision. Console licensing and physical retail had normalised large distribution shares before digital stores existed, so thirty percent entered the market as a familiar figure. Each new storefront that adopted the familiar figure avoided explaining a novel one, and each store with a committed audience had little incentive to discount access that developers needed. Costs for payment handling, delivery networks, review, and support pushed in the same direction without fully determining the level. Where a store lacked an audience, as the Epic Games Store demonstrated with its twelve percent offer from 2018, a lower rate served as a recruitment tool rather than a new standard. Rates converged because the structural position of audience holders converged, and they diverged exactly where that position was weak.

### Q: What does a payment processor alone charge compared with a store?
A payment processor alone charges for one component of the bundle, and its price is built mainly on card network interchange, the fee that flows to the card issuing bank, plus scheme fees and the processor own margin. For ordinary card transactions, the combined result in many developed markets falls in a low single digit percentage range of the transaction value, sometimes with a fixed amount per transaction added, though the exact figure varies by card type, country, transaction size, and risk profile. That range buys authorisation, settlement, and basic fraud tooling. It buys no storefront, no review system, no audience, no update delivery, and no subscription discovery. Comparing that range with a thirty percent store share therefore measures the price of the remaining bundle components, including access to the installed base, rather than proving that the entire difference lacks any function behind it.

### Q: How can a reader tell what share applies to a given sale?
A reader can tell by checking four facts about the sale. The store involved sets the base rate, so the developer agreement and rate schedule for that storefront supply the starting figure. The product type matters next, because subscriptions may qualify for a reduced share after a retention period or from the outset under specific programme terms. The developer status matters as well, since small business programmes lower the rate below a proceeds threshold, and platform specific tiers, such as the Steam revenue bands, lower the share after defined revenue levels. The region matters last, because alternative terms adopted under European regulation and similar regimes can change the applicable structure. Store sales reports and developer programme pages publish the governing schedule for each case, and the settlement statement for a completed sale records the deduction actually applied.
