This page tracks every layoff round at PepsiCo from 2022 onward in one place, and it is updated each time the company runs another one. It records what each round cut, where, and under what stated reasoning, alongside the standing machinery that governs how PepsiCo separations work: how notice is given, how pay in lieu of notice is used, what the WARN Act filings show, how union contracts and logistics handovers shape outcomes, and how the company's long-running productivity plan pays for all of it. Event coverage ages quickly; the record below is built to stay accurate as the history grows.

![PepsiCo Layoffs: Every Round, Severance and What Changed - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-55.webp)

The cumulative picture is eleven tracked rounds in four years. The largest single action is the organisational round announced in November 2026, which eliminated about 1,000 roles across the North American food and beverage business. Around it sits a longer sequence of site actions: the closure of the Quaker plant in Danville, Illinois in 2024, production shutdowns at four bottling plants the same year, a run of Frito-Lay plant closures through 2025 in New York, California and Florida, and a 2026 series of bottling and warehouse actions in California, South Carolina, Maryland and Tennessee. Summed across the eleven rounds, the tracked total is a reported range of roughly 3,900 to 4,700 roles. The width of that range is itself part of the record: PepsiCo publishes exact headcounts in its WARN filings and almost never anywhere else, so this tracker labels every figure with the strength of its source, from state filings and company statements at the top to reported figures and ranges below them.

A note on how this page is kept. The ledger begins at 1 January 2022 by design: PepsiCo's earlier reductions under its 2019 productivity plan are part of the company's history and are summarised in the snapshot, but they predate the documentary standard the ledger applies. A round enters the ledger only when a company announcement or a WARN filing establishes it, or when two independent outlets report the same reduction of at least 100 roles with a consistent scope. Actions documented by WARN filings but falling below that threshold are named in the snapshot rather than silently dropped, and rounds that are known but cannot be verified to the standard are named there as reported-but-unverified. When a new round qualifies, it is added at the top of the ledger, the cumulative table is recomputed, and the date at the head of this page is updated; nothing already in the ledger is removed, and a corrected figure is amended in place with the correction visible in the round's own section.

## Company Snapshot

PepsiCo is one of the largest food and beverage companies in the world, formed in 1965 by the merger of the Pepsi-Cola Company and Frito-Lay, and headquartered in Purchase, New York. Its portfolio spans soft drinks (Pepsi, Mountain Dew, Gatorade), salty snacks (Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos, Tostitos), and foods (Quaker), sold in more than 200 countries and territories. Net revenue was $91.85 billion in fiscal 2024, as reported in the company's annual report. The business is organised, for the period this tracker covers, around PepsiCo Foods North America — the Frito-Lay and Quaker businesses — and PepsiCo Beverages North America, alongside international divisions in Latin America, Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and Asia Pacific. The company operated approximately 300 manufacturing plants as of its 2024 reporting, and its defining operational feature is direct-store delivery: a vast company-run network of plants, warehouses, trucks and route salespeople that manufactures products and also stocks them on store shelves. That structure explains the geography of every round in this ledger, because it gives PepsiCo hundreds of individual sites at which a reduction can occur, most of them small enough to pass unnoticed by national coverage and visible mainly through statutory filings.

The workforce trajectory frames the period. PepsiCo reported approximately 309,000 employees at the end of 2021, approximately 315,000 at the end of 2022, approximately 318,000 at the end of 2023, and approximately 319,000 at the end of 2024, including about 134,000 in the United States. At the end of 2025 the annual report recorded approximately 306,000 employees worldwide, including about 125,000 in the United States — a net decline of about 13,000 in a single year. Net decline is not the same number as roles eliminated, because the company hires continuously across its network; but the 2025 figure is the first in the period to move sharply downward, and it coincides with the heaviest year of site closures in the ledger. The filings confirm the direction and the scale, and they give this tracker its anchor points for the share-of-workforce figures used below: measured against a global workforce of more than 300,000, even the largest round in this ledger touches well under one percent of the company's people, which is precisely why PepsiCo's reductions so often escape the attention that a comparable percentage at a smaller company would draw.

The structural pressure behind the rounds is the performance of the North American business. Through the pandemic years PepsiCo grew by raising prices, and for a time consumers paid them; from 2023 onward, volumes told a different story, as shoppers traded down, bought less, or switched to cheaper alternatives, and as weight-loss drugs of the GLP-1 class began to change snacking habits at the margin. North America beverage volumes fell through 2024 — down 3 percent in both the second and third quarters of that year — and were still falling in the third quarter of 2026, when beverage volume declined 2 percent and food volume was flat. The company's own leadership said the quiet part aloud: on the October 2026 earnings call, chairman and chief executive Ramon Laguarta told analysts the company did not feel good about its beverage business and was putting urgency into improving soft drinks performance, and weeks earlier the company had cut its full-year 2026 earnings outlook, citing North American weakness. The cuts in this ledger are the cost side of that story: a company whose largest market stopped growing in volume, converting payroll and plant capacity into margin defence and into funding for price cuts, marketing and automation.

Standing behind every round is a single financial mechanism, and the snapshot records it because it predicts where the documentary trail will appear. In 2019 PepsiCo announced a multi-year productivity plan built on simplifying and automating processes, re-engineering its go-to-market and information systems, and optimising its manufacturing and supply chain footprint. In 2024 the company expanded the plan and extended it through the end of 2030. As set out in its quarterly filing for the second quarter of 2025, the plan is expected to incur pre-tax charges of approximately $6.15 billion, including about $5.1 billion of cash expenditures; roughly 55 percent of the charges are severance and other employee-related costs, about 10 percent are non-cash asset impairments resulting from plant closures, and the remaining 35 percent are other implementation costs. By June 14, 2025, the company had incurred $3.05 billion of charges under the plan, of which $1.56 billion was severance and other employee costs. The plan's expected charges are allocated across the whole company — about 30 percent to Europe, the Middle East and Africa, 25 percent to PepsiCo Beverages North America, 15 percent each to PepsiCo Foods North America and corporate functions — which is the filing-grade evidence that the restructuring is global and continuous, even though the rounds that can be verified individually, and therefore the rounds in this ledger, are concentrated in the United States, where WARN filings make them visible.

The activist campaign of 2025 sharpened the pressure into a public programme. In September 2025, Elliott Investment Management disclosed a stake of roughly $4 billion and pressed the company on what it characterised as self-inflicted underperformance: a bloated product range, lagging margins, and a North American beverage business losing ground. In December 2025 the two sides reached an agreement under which PepsiCo committed to eliminating roughly 20 percent of its United States product range, lowering prices — suggested price cuts of up to 15 percent on core snack brands followed in February 2026 — targeting 100 basis points of core operating margin improvement over three years, and conducting a review of its entire North America supply chain, while a full refranchising of the North American bottling system was taken off the table. The supply chain review hangs over the later entries in this ledger: the warehouse handovers and bottling closures of 2026 were executed while it was running, and the November 2026 organisational round was announced in the season it was due to report. The tracker records the agreement as context, not as a cause it can prove for any individual round; what the filings establish is that the rounds and the agreement draw on the same stated logic of simplification and cost removal.

The company's own reporting structure maps the same division the rounds follow. Through the period, PepsiCo reported its North American operations as two segments — PepsiCo Foods North America, the snacks business built around Frito-Lay and Quaker, and PepsiCo Beverages North America, the drinks business — alongside its international divisions. The ledger's site rounds divide along exactly that seam: the foods segment contributes Danville, Orlando, Liberty and the Rancho Cucamonga campus; the beverages segment contributes the four bottling plants of 2024, Detroit's partial shutdown, Cheverly and Knoxville; and the handovers and the November 2026 round belong to the logistics and corporate layers that serve both. The December 2025 creation of a single PepsiCo North America leadership over the two segments is therefore more than an organisational chart entry: it merged the two chains of command that had run the ledger's rounds separately, and the first organisational round announced after the merger was, correspondingly, announced for the combined business rather than for either segment.

Leadership and organisation form the last piece of standing context. PepsiCo spent the period integrating its North American foods and beverages businesses under single leadership: a combined PepsiCo North America organisation was led by Steven Williams before Ram Krishnan, previously the head of the United States beverages business, became chief executive of PepsiCo North America effective December 28, 2025, with an explicit mandate to accelerate the integration of the foods and beverages operations in the United States and Canada. It was Krishnan who signed the November 2026 memo that opens the ledger. The integration matters to this tracker because it changed what a PepsiCo round looks like: the December 2022 round was announced separately inside the beverages and snacks headquarters, while the November 2026 round was announced once, for the combined food and beverage business, in the language of a single organisation. The site closures, by contrast, kept their older form throughout — decided plant by plant, announced locally, and documented in state filings.

PepsiCo has reduced its workforce before 2022, and those episodes belong to the snapshot rather than the ledger. The 2019 productivity plan ran through the pandemic years with its own sequence of footprint and organisational actions, charged through the same mechanism described above; the plan's existence since 2019 is why the company's restructuring charges appear in its filings every year of the period, in good years and bad alike. The December 2022 headquarters round also had a prelude that belongs to the record: before the involuntary cuts were announced, the snacks business had already reduced positions through a voluntary retirement programme, a sequencing — voluntary exits first, involuntary cuts after — that recurs in American corporate practice and that this tracker records separately wherever it appears, because voluntary separations are not layoffs and are never summed into a round's figure.

Four actions in the period are documented by WARN filings but fall below this tracker's 100-role threshold for a ledger round, and they are named here so that the record is complete rather than selective. In Muncie, Indiana, a WARN letter dated October 30, 2023 recorded PepsiCo discontinuing its management and operation of the Pepsi Beverages facility there effective December 31, 2023; the site employed about 101 people, and the filing's exhibit listed 45 named separations, many of them Teamsters Local 135 members whose contract afforded bidding rights. In Detroit, Michigan, a WARN letter dated July 21, 2025 discontinued production, maintenance and transport operations at the Mack Avenue beverage plant effective September 27, 2025, eliminating about 83 roles while the site's warehouse, fleet, delivery, sales and field-service teams continued. In Aberdeen, Maryland, a Frito-Lay notice recorded in the state's 2025 WARN log closed a warehouse effective July 12, 2025, eliminating 56 jobs. And in Raleigh, North Carolina, a WARN letter dated July 6, 2026 eliminated 65 of 68 positions at a Frito-Lay distribution center effective September 6, 2026, as warehouse management passed to an outside logistics provider and the site stayed open; reporting on that action described pay and benefits continuation tied to years of service. Each of these actions is real, filed, and finished; each is excluded from the ledger only by the size threshold, and each will be promoted into the ledger if it is ever consolidated with related actions into a single announced round.

Three reported items are recorded here as reported-but-unverified, because they are known to this tracker but do not meet its evidence standard for a ledger entry. First, when the December 2022 headquarters round was reported, a low-quality aggregator claimed the cuts would reach 4,000 to 8,000 jobs in January 2023; no reputable outlet, filing or WARN record has ever corroborated a figure of that order, the company published no total, and the claim is not carried in the ledger. Second, when Frito-Lay's Rancho Cucamonga manufacturing plant shut in June 2025, some commentary put the positions eliminated at up to 480; the California filing records 432, and no filing supports the higher figure, so the ledger keeps 432 and records the claim here. Third, commentary in late 2026 anticipated further North America reductions flowing from the supply chain review agreed with Elliott; anticipation is not an announcement, no company statement or filing establishes a further round beyond those in the ledger, and nothing is carried for it. Each of these items will move into the ledger if filings or corroborated reporting establish it, and the distinction between the two treatments — documented and reported — is the tracker's core discipline.

The company's reported headcount gives the period its frame, and its limits. PepsiCo employed approximately 309,000 people at the end of 2021, about 315,000 at the end of 2022, about 318,000 at the end of 2023, about 319,000 — including some 134,000 in the United States — at the end of 2024, and about 306,000, including some 125,000 in the United States, at the end of 2025. Three features of that trajectory matter for reading the ledger. First, the rounds of 2022 through 2024 were executed while total employment was still rising: the programme reallocated and removed roles inside a growing company, which is why no single round of those years is visible in the annual totals. Second, the fall of roughly 13,000 in 2025 is the first year in the period in which the annual figure moved the way the rounds would suggest, and even then the verified United States rounds of 2025 explain only a small part of it — the balance is hiring patterns, attrition, international actions and programme effects the filings do not itemise. Third, the United States share of employment fell by roughly 9,000 in the same year, consistent with a programme whose verifiable rounds are American but whose arithmetic is plainly larger than the American record. The tracker reports the trajectory as context and draws no per-round conclusions from it.

One more piece of standing context explains the shape of nearly every round that follows: a PepsiCo site usually houses two workforces under one roof. The first makes or stores product — production lines, warehouses, maintenance. The second sells and delivers it, under the direct-store-delivery system in which PepsiCo's own people stock retailer shelves and manage the customer relationship in the store. The rounds in this ledger fall, with near-perfect consistency, on the first workforce and not the second: production ended at Cincinnati, Harrisburg and Atlanta while those sites' sales and delivery teams kept working; Cheverly lost manufacturing and warehousing while its sales and delivery functions continued; Detroit lost production, maintenance and transport while its warehouse, fleet, delivery and sales teams stayed. The company is not leaving the markets where it closes plants. It is re-sourcing the supply behind an unchanged storefront, treating the industrial side of each site as capacity the network can replace and the commercial side as presence it cannot. A reader who holds that division in mind can predict, from any future PepsiCo site announcement, which jobs it will name.

Two features of the table deserve a reader's attention before the detail. The first is how small every share figure is: PepsiCo's global workforce never fell below roughly 306,000 in the period, so no single round in this ledger reaches even half of one percent of it, and the cumulative tracked total across four years is a little over one percent. The reductions are nevertheless locally decisive — a plant closure removes an entire site's workforce in one action — and the table's company-wide percentages should be read alongside the round sections, where the same numbers appear as whole plants, whole warehouses and whole headquarters floors. The second feature is the change in the kind of action over time. The 2022 round was organisational and corporate. The 2024 and 2025 rounds were manufacturing closures, executed through WARN filings. ## November 2026: The North America Simplification Round

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Monday, November 9, 2026, Ram Krishnan, chief executive of PepsiCo North America, wrote to employees across the North American food and beverage business announcing that the company would eliminate about 1,000 roles, with the changes to be made over the coming week. The Wall Street Journal reported the memo the same day, and Bloomberg, CNBC and Reuters reported it independently within the same news cycle. Unlike the site closures that fill most of this ledger, the round was announced by internal letter rather than by press release or filing, and PepsiCo issued no public statement beyond what the memo contained. The separations were scheduled to begin within days of the announcement rather than at the end of a notice period, which places this round at the fast end of the company's practice as documented below.

**Roles cut.** About 1,000 roles across North America, in the United States and Canada, spanning the food and beverage operations. The figure is the company's own as reported from the memo — the Journal's reporting of approximately 1,000 was consistent across Bloomberg, CNBC and Reuters — and it is carried here as approximate because PepsiCo has not published an exact count and the memo, as reported, did not break the total down. No roles were described as merely left unfilled in the reporting of this round; the memo described positions being eliminated, and this tracker records no separate unfilled figure for it.

**Share of workforce.** Against approximately 306,000 employees worldwide in the company's 2025 annual report, about 1,000 roles is roughly 0.3 percent of the global workforce. Against the approximately 125,000 employees in the United States recorded in the same report, it is under one percent of the domestic workforce — and the round also touched Canada, so the United States share is smaller still. The round's significance is not its percentage but its location: it fell on the organisational core of the company's largest and most troubled market, eleven months into the integration of that market's foods and beverages businesses under one chief executive.

**Divisions and geographies.** The memo, as reported, named no divisions, functions or locations. Coverage consistently described the scope as the North American food and beverage organisation as a whole — the combined business Krishnan had led since December 28, 2025 — and the company declined to identify which parts of the organisation would lose positions. That breadth is itself a datum: where the site rounds in this ledger can be located to a street address, this round was deliberately announced at the level of the whole regional organisation, and the specific incidence of the cuts will be knowable, if at all, only through later filings and reporting.

**The company's stated framing.** Krishnan's memo, as quoted across the reporting, said that too much of how the company works remains complex and fragmented, and that the ambition was to build a stronger, more future-fit business that delivers industry-leading growth. The changes were described as simplifying structures, clarifying accountability and making decisions faster, so that teams could move faster, execute better, and invest behind the opportunities that would drive growth. The framing is organisational rather than financial: the memo did not present the round as a response to a single quarter, and it named no savings target. It arrived, however, one month after the company cut its full-year 2026 earnings outlook in its third-quarter release — in which North America beverage volume fell 2 percent and food volume was flat — and after chief executive Ramon Laguarta had told analysts that further structural cost actions were being identified for the coming months. The tracker records both facts: the memo's own framing, and the results context in which it was sent.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filings were reported alongside the memo coverage. That absence is consistent with the round's structure: the federal WARN Act's thresholds are counted site by site, and a reduction dispersed across a continental organisation can eliminate 1,000 roles without any single location crossing the plant-closing or mass-layoff triggers that would require a filing. State mini-WARN laws in jurisdictions such as California, New York and Illinois have their own thresholds, and any filings made under them would surface in state records after the fact; none had been identified in the reporting this section rests on. The contrast with the rest of the ledger is instructive — PepsiCo's site actions generate a paper trail in advance, while its organisational rounds are documented mainly by the internal communications that announce them.

**Severance terms.** Terms were not disclosed in the memo as reported, and PepsiCo has published no severance schedule for this round. The standing section below sets out what the documented record across earlier rounds establishes about the company's separation practice; nothing more specific can be stated for this round without dropping below this tracker's evidence standard.

**Sources.** This section rests on the Wall Street Journal's November 9, 2026 report of the Krishnan memo; independent reporting the same day by Bloomberg and Bloomberg Law, CNBC, and Reuters; and follow-on coverage by Fast Company, Fox Business and Transport Topics, together with the company's third-quarter 2026 release as reported in October 2026 coverage.

**The integration dimension.** This round is the first in the ledger announced for the combined North America organisation, and its form follows from the December 2025 leadership change that created that organisation. In December 2022, the comparable round had been run separately inside the beverages headquarters in Purchase and the snacks headquarters in Chicago and Plano, under two divisional structures. Four years later, with foods and beverages under a single regional chief executive whose mandate was integration, the announcement was single, regional and framed in the vocabulary of organisational design — complexity, fragmentation, accountability, speed — rather than in the vocabulary of either division's market. Readers comparing the two rounds should note what stayed the same: the stated reason is a near-relative of the 2022 memo's promise to simplify the organisation, and the figure, about 1,000, is of the same order as the upper end of the range reported in 2022. What changed is the machinery around it: an activist agreement, a supply chain review, and a combined organisation to simplify.

**The reinvestment dimension.** The November 2026 memo is also the round in which the destination of the savings was stated most concretely, because by 2026 the company's bargain with its shareholders had become explicit: costs removed from the organisation were to fund the price investment and marketing needed to revive volume. The sequence is documented across the year. In February 2026, reporting described suggested price cuts of up to 15 percent on core snack brands following the Elliott agreement; in October 2026 the company cut its earnings outlook while identifying further structural cost actions; in November the memo presented the cuts as funding a stronger, more future-fit business delivering industry-leading growth. The tracker records the linkage because the company and its agreement partner stated it, and because it distinguishes this round from a pure margin defence: the 2022 cuts were justified by efficiency in a growing business, while the 2026 cuts were justified, in part, as the funding source for giving revenue back at the shelf. Whether the exchange worked is a question the volume figures of later quarters will answer; the ledger's business is to record that the exchange was the stated design.

**The disclosure dimension.** The round also illustrates how organisational reductions at PepsiCo become public, and why their record differs from the site rounds'. There was no filing: a reduction of about 1,000 roles spread across a continental organisation touches no single site at WARN thresholds, so the statutory paper trail that anchors the rest of this ledger does not exist for it. What exists is the memo, seen by the Wall Street Journal and reported on November 9, 2026, with Bloomberg, CNBC and Reuters carrying the same account the same day — four independent newsrooms, one document, and a company figure of about 1,000 that none of them could decompose further because the company did not. The tracker can therefore state the round's scale, sponsor, language and week of execution, and cannot state its distribution across functions, its split between the Purchase, Chicago and Plano offices and the wider organisation, or its severance terms. That asymmetry — the largest round in the ledger is also the least decomposable — is a property of the disclosure regime, recorded here so that the round's thinner section is read as a limit of the evidence rather than of the event.

## October 2026: The Knoxville Bottling Plant Closure

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Monday, October 19, 2026, PepsiCo Beverages U.S. announced the immediate closure of its bottling plant at 2708 Federal Road in Knoxville, Tennessee, and filed notice the same day with the Tennessee Department of Labor and Workforce Development, where it was recorded as WARN notice 202600012. The plant closed at once; the separations are formally effective January 1, 2027, and the company committed to continue pay and benefits through that date. The sequence — operations ending immediately, employment ending ten weeks later — is the paid-notice pattern that recurs throughout this ledger when PepsiCo closes a site without an advance warning period.

**Roles cut.** The WARN filing lists 112 workers: manufacturing, maintenance and warehouse roles at the plant. No roles were reported as left unfilled rather than cut; the filing records a permanent closure of the site.

**Share of workforce.** One hundred and twelve roles is under 0.1 percent of the company's approximately 306,000 global employees as of its 2025 annual report. Measured against the site itself, it is the entire plant workforce; measured against the company's East Tennessee presence, it is partial, since PepsiCo continues other operations in the region, including facilities in Johnson City and Chattanooga and its Knoxville sales and delivery organisation.

**Divisions and geographies.** The affected operation is a single PepsiCo Beverages plant in south Knoxville, producing a range of the company's beverages. The company's statement confirmed that its sales and delivery staff in the Knoxville area were not affected and that it would work to identify opportunities for affected employees at nearby facilities. The plant's workforce was not unionised, according to the state filing record.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo described the closure in a brief statement as a difficult decision, taken to close the plant immediately. It offered no detailed rationale for the site itself. The timing supplied the context: the announcement came days after the company's third-quarter 2026 earnings, in which North America beverage volume fell 2 percent, and after chief executive Ramon Laguarta had told analysts on the earnings call that the company did not feel good about its beverage business and was putting urgency and focus into improving soft drinks performance. The tracker attributes the round to that stated corporate context, not to any site-specific explanation, because none was published.

**WARN Act posture.** The Tennessee filing was made on the day of the announcement and recorded an effective date of January 1, 2027 — seventy-four days later. The structure satisfies the federal 60-day notice period through continued employment on paper: the plant stopped operating on October 19, but the employment relationship, with pay and benefits, runs to January 1. This is the compliant version of the pattern that, in its compressed form at Chicago in 2024 and Orlando in 2025, drew union allegations and legal scrutiny; here, with the notice period fully funded and the filing made on time, no dispute was reported.

**Severance terms.** Beyond the paid notice period through January 1, 2027 and transition assistance, no severance terms were reported for this round, and the company published none. Affected employees were told they would receive information about other openings, and the company said it was actively working to place employees at nearby facilities.

**Sources.** This section rests on Tennessee WARN notice 202600012 as reported by the Knoxville News Sentinel; PepsiCo's statement as carried by Food Dive on October 20, 2026; and reporting by TheStreet and Inc. on the closure and its earnings context.

**The bottling pattern.** Knoxville was the sixth bottling or beverage production site in this ledger to lose its production function, after the four plants of October 2024 and Cheverly a month earlier, and the sequence has a consistent anatomy: production and warehouse roles end, sales and delivery continue, and the market keeps being served from elsewhere in the network. The company's beverage system was built, over decades, as a dense lattice of local bottling plants feeding local direct-store delivery; the ledger's beverage rounds are that lattice being thinned, plant by plant, as volume falls and as production concentrates in larger facilities. Knoxville's distinguishing feature is only its paperwork: it is the cleanest WARN execution in the ledger, filed on the day, funded to the day, and undisputed.

**The age dimension.** The bottling plants in this ledger share a profile that the individual rounds can obscure: they are old. Chicago's plant had operated for more than sixty years when it closed; Cheverly's for more than sixty; Knoxville's closure drew no dispute partly because the plant's role in the network had visibly narrowed to serving its immediate region from an ageing site. PepsiCo operated roughly 300 manufacturing plants worldwide in the period's reporting, and the beverage subset in the United States was built for a carbonated-soft-drink market far larger, per capita, than the one that now exists. The closures are therefore not best understood as a series of local failures. They are the managed contraction of a production lattice designed for a different demand era, executed at the pace the volume figures dictate and documented, site by site, in the WARN record this ledger consolidates. Knoxville's 112 workers are the human content of one lattice point being switched off; the pattern they belong to is the story.

## September 2026: The Cheverly Operations Shutdown

**Announcement and effective dates.** On September 14, 2026, PepsiCo ended manufacturing and warehouse operations at its Pepsi Bottling Group facility at 2611 Pepsi Place in Cheverly, Maryland — a site that had operated for more than sixty years — and notified the Town of Cheverly the same day the action took effect. A Maryland WARN notice was filed dated September 14, 2026, with separations effective November 13, 2026. Sales and delivery operations at the site continue without interruption, according to the company.

**Roles cut.** The sources for this round disagree, and the ledger records the disagreement rather than resolving it by preference. The Town of Cheverly's official statement, issued on the day and based on PepsiCo's notification to the town, records that the facility employed 143 people and that 98 of them were expected to be affected. The Washington Post's reporting on the closure likewise described 98 of the site's 143 workers losing their positions. Several WARN trackers and trade reports, reading the Maryland filing, instead list 143 as the number affected, with 98 of the affected described as members of Teamsters Local 639. The two readings cannot both be right: either 98 people were cut from a workforce of 143, or 143 were cut of whom 98 were union members. This tracker carries the round as 98–143, names both readings here, and will amend the figure in place if the filing itself or further reporting settles it. The affected functions are not in dispute: fleet, transport, manufacturing and warehouse operations, together with other salaried employees at the site.

**Share of workforce.** At either end of the range, the round is under 0.1 percent of the company's global workforce of approximately 306,000. At the site, it removed either roughly two-thirds of the workforce or all of it — the disagreement above is therefore material to the local record even though it does not move the company-wide figure, and it is a worked example of why this tracker names its sources for every number it carries.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single PepsiCo Beverages bottling facility in Prince George's County, Maryland, in the Washington suburbs. Production and warehouse work ended; the sales and delivery organisation that serves the area from the site continues. The town's statement recorded that local officials were notified on the day the reduction took effect and moved immediately to connect affected workers with assistance.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo did not publish a detailed statement. A company spokesperson, in the coverage of the closure, pointed to changing consumer demand, technology and the company's operating network as the context for the decision. The town's statement recorded the company's position that sales and delivery would continue without disruption. Beyond that, the rationale on record is the corporate one running through the 2026 rounds: a beverage network being re-shaped as volumes fall.

**WARN Act posture.** The Maryland filing is dated the day operations ended, with separations effective November 13, 2026 — sixty days later — and affected employees continued to receive pay and health care through November 13. The structure is the paid-notice pattern: the statutory window is funded rather than worked. The town's same-day notification drew public criticism from local officials, who said they learned of the reduction when it took effect; no legal finding about the notice was reported in the coverage this section rests on. Teamsters Local 639, which represents workers at the site, organised assistance for its members in the weeks after the announcement, including a job fair reported in late October 2026.

**Severance terms.** No severance schedule was published for this round. The documented terms are the pay and health care continuation through November 13, 2026 described above; anything beyond that was not disclosed in a source meeting this tracker's standard.

**Sources.** This section rests on the Town of Cheverly's official statement of September 14, 2026; the Maryland WARN notice as reported by Food Dive and WARN trackers; the Washington Post's October 18, 2026 reporting on the closure and its effect on the town; and coverage by WJLA and NBC Washington, including the Teamsters Local 639 job fair.

**The community dimension.** Cheverly is the round in this ledger where the local record is richest, because the plant was a small town's anchor employer and the town government documented its own notification in public. The Washington Post's account, published a month after the shutdown, described a community that had relied on the plant for generations of employment and learned of its production shutdown on the day it happened. The tracker records that dimension without embellishment: a sixty-year-old plant's production function can be ended by a decision communicated the same morning, and the documentary trace it leaves — a state filing, a town statement, a union job fair — is thinner than the local fact. It is the clearest illustration in the ledger of why this page exists: without consolidation, the Cheverly round would be remembered, if at all, as a one-day local news story, when it is in fact one entry in a four-year, eleven-round pattern.

**The response dimension.** Cheverly also produced the ledger's fullest example of organised local response. Teamsters Local 639, which represented the plant's unionised workers, publicly mourned the closure and organised support for its members, including a job fair reported by local television in October 2026 — a concrete instance of the union layer functioning as an employment service when the statutory machinery had run its course. County and town officials, for their part, put their own notification on the public record on the day itself, which is why this round's numbers can be stated with unusual precision about who was employed and who was expected to be affected. The contrast with quieter rounds is instructive: where a local institution documents a closure, the tracker can be exact; where the only documents are a state log line and a company sentence, the record is thinner. Cheverly's documentation is not a credit to the closure's execution — the town learned of it the day it happened — but to the town's and the union's insistence on recording it.

## August 2026: The Columbia Warehouse Handover

**Announcement and effective dates.** PepsiCo Beverages Sales LLC filed its WARN notice with the State of South Carolina on August 18, 2026, recording permanent layoffs effective October 18, 2026 at its facility at 6925 North Main Street in Columbia. The notice period ran the full statutory sixty days: the filing preceded the separations by two months, and the warehouse kept operating under PepsiCo management in the interval.

**Roles cut.** The South Carolina filing lists 105 workers. The majority — 57, according to the reporting of the notice — were warehouse workers, forklift operators and checkers, with the remainder in associated roles at the facility. The filing records permanent layoffs; no roles were described as left unfilled.

**Share of workforce.** One hundred and five roles is under 0.1 percent of the company's global workforce. The round's interest is not its size but its mechanism, set out below: it is one of the clearest examples in the ledger of a job loss in which nothing closed.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single PepsiCo Beverages warehouse in Columbia, South Carolina. The facility itself remains open, and its other operations continue without interruption; what ended was PepsiCo's direct management of the warehouse function at the site. Sales, delivery and the site's remaining operations were not part of the action.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo Beverages U.S. said, in a statement carried by TheStreet, that it was shifting how warehouse logistics are managed at the Columbia facility in order to continue to support its customers and consumers in the area. The framing is operational continuity: the work continues, under different management. The company did not present the action as a closure, and the filing supports that description — the site was not closed, and the layoff type recorded by the state is a permanent layoff rather than a closure.

**WARN Act posture.** The filing is a model of the advance-notice pattern: notice filed August 18, 2026, separations effective October 18, 2026, exactly the sixty-day window the federal Act contemplates, given while the operation continued. No dispute about notice was reported. The contrast with the same-day filings elsewhere in this ledger is the point: where the company chooses to run a handover on a schedule, the WARN machinery works as designed, and where it chooses immediacy, it substitutes pay for time.

**Severance terms.** Local reporting recorded that affected employees would receive pay and benefits continuation and assistance in applying for positions with the incoming logistics provider. No severance formula was published. The offer of application assistance with the successor employer is a documented feature of the handover mechanism in this ledger — present here and in Raleigh — and its value depends on hiring outcomes that the public record does not track; the tracker notes the offer, and its limits, without estimating either.

**Sources.** This section rests on the South Carolina Department of Employment and Workforce's official WARN report for 2026, which records the filing, the address, the date and the count; TheStreet's reporting of the notice and the company's statement; and WIS News 10's August 20, 2026 account of the terms offered.

**The handover mechanism.** Columbia belongs to a family of actions that the 2026 entries made visible: the transfer of a working operation to an outside logistics provider, in which the building stays open, the work stays in place, and the employment ends. The same mechanism appears at Raleigh, North Carolina — below this tracker's size threshold and recorded in the snapshot — and, in an earlier corporate form, at Muncie, Indiana in 2023, where PepsiCo discontinued its management and operation of a facility it ran. For the affected workforce the distinction between closure and handover is real but narrow: some employees may be rehired by the incoming provider, on terms the record does not establish, while others are simply separated. For the company's network, the handover converts a fixed cost into a purchased service without surrendering the site's position in the delivery geography. The tracker treats handovers as layoff rounds when they meet the evidence and size standards, because the separations are permanent and filed as such, whatever happens to the building.

## February 2026: The Rancho Cucamonga Distribution Closure

**Announcement and effective dates.** Frito-Lay filed its WARN notice with the State of California on February 10, 2026, recording the closure of the distribution and warehouse operation at 9535 Archibald Avenue in Rancho Cucamonga, effective June 6, 2026. The company confirmed the closure and the count in response to Los Angeles Times reporting in February 2026. The notice period ran to just under four months — the longest advance warning in this ledger.

**Roles cut.** The Los Angeles Times reported 247 layoffs, on the company's confirmation; some state trackers list 248 for the same filing. The ledger carries 247–248 and names both. The affected roles are the campus's distribution, warehouse and transportation workforce — the units that had kept the site alive after its manufacturing operation ended the previous June.

**Share of workforce.** At either figure, about 0.1 percent of the company's global workforce of approximately 306,000. The locally meaningful denominator is the campus itself: across its two stages, in June 2025 and June 2026, the Rancho Cucamonga site lost 679 to 680 roles in twelve months, the largest single-site cumulative total in this ledger.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single Frito-Lay campus in San Bernardino County, California. The closure ended PepsiCo's presence at a site it had operated since 1970. The company said the distribution work would move to a new distribution center in the local community, so the function remained in the region under a different roof; the sales and delivery geography the campus served was not withdrawn.

**The company's stated framing.** Frito-Lay said the shift to a new local distribution center would better serve its customers. It presented the closure as a relocation of the distribution function rather than a withdrawal from the market, and confirmed the affected employees would receive pay and benefits continuation and career support.

**WARN Act posture.** Filed February 10, 2026 for a June 6, 2026 closure, the notice satisfied the federal requirement and California's mini-WARN law with months to spare. Set beside the same campus's manufacturing shutdown eight months earlier — where the state confirmed no advance filing had been made when employees were told — the distribution closure shows the company executing the same kind of action two ways on the same site in consecutive years: once immediately, once by the book. No notice dispute was reported for this round.

**Severance terms.** The company stated that affected employees would be given pay and benefits continuation as well as career support. No severance formula was published, and no employee-reported terms meeting this tracker's standard emerged for this round; the section records the continuation commitment and nothing further.

**Sources.** This section rests on the California WARN filing of February 10, 2026 as indexed in state filing records; the Los Angeles Times' reporting of the closure and the company's confirmation, carried by Industrial Equipment News and the Pasadena Star-News; and Inland Empire business coverage of the June 6, 2026 completion.

**The completion dimension.** A two-stage site exit has a documentary rhythm of its own, and Rancho Cucamonga is the ledger's only complete example of it. The first stage, the manufacturing shutdown, was executed in a day and generated its record after the fact, in a filing the state confirmed had not been made in advance. The second stage, this distribution closure, was filed four months ahead, with the workforce's end date known from the start and the company able to point to a receiving facility in the same community. Read together, the two stages describe the same company executing the second half of an exit it had already decided with markedly better paperwork than the first: the decision was constant, the notice practice changed. The tracker draws no conclusion about motive from the contrast, but records it because it is the strongest internal evidence in the ledger that the notice pattern at any given PepsiCo site is a choice rather than a constraint — the same company, on the same campus, eleven months apart, produced both the weakest and the strongest notice practice in the record. For the campus's combined workforce of 679 to 680 across the two stages, the practical difference was the difference between learning of a closure on the day and planning around one for a season.

**The destination dimension.** Unusually for a closure in this ledger, the Rancho Cucamonga distribution work had a stated destination: a new distribution center in the local community, which the company said the move would allow it to better serve customers. The record does not state how many of the 247 to 248 affected employees, if any, were offered roles at the new facility, and the tracker does not assume transfers it cannot document — a WARN closure of this kind records separations from the closing site, and any rehiring at the receiving site belongs to a different record that was not published. The distinction matters for how the round is counted and how it was lived. In the ledger's arithmetic it is a full 247 to 248 separations; in the community's experience it may have been, for some workers, a change of building rather than a loss of work. Both statements are carried here because both are true to the evidence available, and the gap between them is one of the standing limitations of filing-based tracking that the snapshot acknowledges.

**The two-stage site.** Rancho Cucamonga is the ledger's clearest case of a staged exit. In June 2025 the company ended manufacturing at the campus and kept its logistics functions; in February 2026 it announced the end of those functions too. Each stage was filed and reported separately, and a reader of either story alone would have missed the whole: a 56-year-old site, once the largest kind of employer in its city, wound down in two moves a year apart. The tracker keeps the two stages as separate rounds because they were separate announcements with separate filings and separate workforces, but it records their sum — 679 to 680 roles — wherever the campus is discussed, because the sum is the site's true weight in the company's history.

## November 2025: The Orlando Plant Closures

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Tuesday, November 4, 2025, PepsiCo Foods U.S. announced the shutdown of its Frito-Lay plant in Orlando, Florida, and filed two WARN letters with the Florida Department of Commerce the same day. The first recorded the termination of 454 employees at the manufacturing plant and onsite warehouse at 2800 Silver Star Road, effective that day: the plant shut on the day it was announced. The second recorded 46 employees at an offsite warehouse at 2000 Parks Oaks Avenue, to be terminated when that facility closed on May 9, 2026. The round therefore has two effective dates under one announcement, and this section treats it as one round because the company announced it as one action in one pair of filings.

**Roles cut.** Five hundred roles in total: 454 at the plant and 46 at the warehouse. The plant roles included packaging machine operators, maintenance mechanics, technicians and general laborers. The WARN letters recorded no bumping rights for the affected employees — no contractual mechanism by which senior workers could displace junior ones elsewhere — and the workforce was not unionised.

**Share of workforce.** Five hundred roles is about 0.2 percent of the company's global workforce, measured against the approximately 306,000 employees of the 2025 annual report. At the site it was total: the Silver Star Road plant had operated since 1965, and its closure ended six decades of production in a single morning.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single Frito-Lay manufacturing campus in Orlando, Florida, producing Lay's, Doritos and Cheetos, plus its support warehouse. The action belonged to PepsiCo Foods North America. Coverage at the time placed it in the context of softening snack demand — Frito-Lay's North America volumes were falling as consumers resisted years of price increases — and the company had sold land near the plant earlier in 2025, a detail the Orlando Sentinel's coverage noted.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo called the shutdown a difficult decision, driven by business needs, and committed to treating every impacted employee with care, providing transition assistance, career support, and pay and benefits during the transition. The statement named no site-specific cause beyond business needs; the demand context comes from the company's results reporting, not from the closure statement, and this tracker attributes it accordingly.

**WARN Act posture.** The federal WARN Act requires sixty days' advance notice of a plant closing; Frito-Lay's letters were filed on the day the plant closed. The company's mechanism, recorded in the WARN letter as quoted in Orlando coverage, was to pay the 454 plant employees sixty days' pay in lieu of the notice period — converting the notice obligation into cash. A class-action law firm, Strauss Borrelli, said in November 2025 that it was investigating whether the federal requirement had been met; that is recorded here as the firm's stated position, not as a finding, and no adjudicated outcome is part of the record this section rests on. The warehouse's 46 employees, by contrast, received the full six months between filing and closure as working notice.

**Severance terms.** The documented terms are the sixty days' pay in lieu of notice for the plant workforce, plus the transition assistance, career support, and pay and benefits continuation the company's statement promised. No severance formula beyond the notice substitution was published.

**Sources.** This section rests on the two Florida WARN filings of November 4, 2025 as recorded by the Florida Department of Commerce; Food Dive's November 7, 2025 report of the closures and the company's statement; News 6 WKMG's November 4, 2025 coverage; and the Orlando Sentinel's and Orlando Weekly's accounts of the WARN letters' contents.

**The site dimension.** The Orlando plant had made snacks since 1965 — Lay's, Doritos and Cheetos among them — which made it, at sixty years, one of the oldest operating plants in the ledger and a fixture of its industrial neighbourhood for three generations of workers. Its closure also carried the ledger's only open legal question of its kind: in November 2025, a plaintiffs' firm announced it was investigating whether the federal notice requirement had been met, on the basis that the WARN letters were filed the same day the 454 plant employees were terminated. No outcome of that investigation is recorded in the sources this section rests on, and the tracker states the position exactly as it stands: the question was raised publicly by an interested firm, the company paid sixty days in lieu of notice, and whether the same-day filing satisfied the Act was not adjudicated in any record available to this tracker. The round is therefore carried on its filed numbers, with its legal question open — a status the ledger will amend if a filing, settlement or judgement closes it.

**The abruptness question.** Orlando is the round that most sharply poses the question this ledger keeps meeting: what does a company owe a workforce in time? The Danville workforce, in 2024, had been kept on payroll for six months between the production pause and the closure. The Liberty workforce had three months' filed notice. The Orlando plant workforce had a morning. The money was made roughly whole — sixty days' pay is the statutory measure — but the sequence matters beyond the money, because notice is also the period in which a worker searches from employment rather than from unemployment. The tracker does not editorialize the choice; it records the variation, because the variation is the fact about how this company closes plants: the notice pattern is decided case by case, and the filings show no single standard governing it.

## June 2025: The Rancho Cucamonga Manufacturing Shutdown

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Monday, June 9, 2025, employees at the Frito-Lay manufacturing plant at 9535 Archibald Avenue in Rancho Cucamonga, California were notified that production at the plant had ended. The California filing record for the action lists 432 workers with a state-posted effective date of June 11, 2025. The shutdown was immediate: the line that had made Cheetos, Doritos, Tostitos and Funyuns at the site for fifty-five years stopped on the day the workforce was told.

**Roles cut.** The filing record establishes 432 manufacturing roles. Some commentary put the positions eliminated as high as 480; no filing supports a figure above 432, and the higher claim is recorded in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified rather than carried here. The campus's warehouse, distribution and transportation units were not part of this action and continued operating — their turn came in the February 2026 round — so the 432 are the plant's production workforce only.

**Share of workforce.** Four hundred and thirty-two roles is about 0.1 percent of the company's global workforce, measured against the approximately 319,000 employees of the 2024 annual report. At the site, it was the end of manufacturing after fifty-five years; the plant, opened in 1970, is credited in the company's own lore as the birthplace of Flamin' Hot Cheetos in the early 1990s, a detail the local coverage of the closure returned to repeatedly because it captures what a legacy site means beyond its headcount.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single Frito-Lay plant in San Bernardino County, California, part of PepsiCo Foods North America. Production of its brands was absorbed elsewhere in the company's snack network; the company continued to operate more than thirty United States Frito-Lay plants after the closure. No other site was named in the action.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo issued no detailed public rationale for ending production at the site. Its statement to local television confirmed the shape of the campus after the action — manufacturing ended, the hub's warehouse, distribution and transportation portions still operational — without offering a site-specific reason. The context on record is the company's snack demand picture in 2025: volumes under pressure across the North America foods business as consumers pushed back on prices. The tracker attributes the round to that documented context and to no more specific claim, because the company made none.

**WARN Act posture.** This round carries the ledger's sharpest notice dispute. The California Employment Development Department confirmed to the Pasadena Star-News that Frito-Lay had not filed a Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification with the state when employees were told they were losing their jobs; the filing recording the 432 workers was posted afterward, with its June 11 effective date. California's mini-WARN law requires sixty days' notice of a layoff of fifty or more at a covered establishment, and the absence of an advance filing drew legal scrutiny of the kind that follows same-day closures; the record this section rests on establishes the missing advance filing as a matter of the state's own confirmation, and no adjudicated outcome is recorded here. Set against the Liberty filing four months earlier — three months of warning, given in full — Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing shutdown is the opposite pole of the company's notice practice.

**Severance terms.** PepsiCo published no severance terms for this round. Employees said in social media posts reported by the Pasadena Star-News that they were given ten weeks' pay as severance; that figure is recorded here as an employee report, not a company-confirmed term, and it is the only severance datum the round produced. The company's statement confirmed pay and benefits support for impacted employees in general terms, as carried in local coverage.

**Sources.** This section rests on the California filing record for the action as indexed from state data; the Pasadena Star-News' reporting of the shutdown, including the Employment Development Department's confirmation regarding the missing advance filing and the employee-reported severance; and KTLA coverage carried in the same reporting, together with the Inland Empire business press.

**The legacy-site dimension.** Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing plant was not a marginal facility in the company's story about itself: it was the site associated with the creation of one of its most famous products, operating since 1970, in a city where it had been an anchor employer for two generations. Its closure in a single day, without advance filing, is the fullest expression in this ledger of the immediacy pattern — and the reaction it drew, including a city-level response that later recruited a replacement bottling plant to the site, is part of the round's documented aftermath. The tracker records the sequence because it demonstrates the asymmetry the ledger exists to correct: the company's network absorbed the production without public difficulty, while the site's workforce and city absorbed the closure all at once.

## February 2025: The Liberty PopCorners Plant Closure

**Announcement and effective dates.** Frito-Lay filed its WARN notice with the New York State Department of Labor on February 18, 2025, recording the closure of its manufacturing plant in Liberty, New York, and the layoff of the plant's entire workforce. Layoffs under the notice ran from May 21 through June 6, 2025, when the plant closed. The filing gave the workforce roughly three months between notice and first separations — the fullest advance-notice execution in this ledger, and the reference case for how the company's closures look when run entirely by the book.

**Roles cut.** The WARN notice lists all 287 workers at the plant. The affected employees were not unionised, according to the filing, and the filing cited economic reasons for the closure. No roles were recorded as unfilled rather than cut; the action was a whole-site closure.

**Share of workforce.** Two hundred and eighty-seven roles is about 0.1 percent of the company's global workforce, measured against the approximately 319,000 employees of the 2024 annual report. In Liberty — a town of roughly ten thousand people in Sullivan County — the plant was one of the largest employers, and the closure's local weight is correspondingly out of proportion to its company-wide percentage, a pattern this ledger meets at almost every site it records.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single Frito-Lay plant in the Catskills region of New York, producing PopCorners corn snacks. PepsiCo had acquired the plant only in 2019, as part of its purchase of BFY Brands, the snack company that created PopCorners; the site itself had operated since 1997 under its original owner, Ideal Snacks. The closure therefore ended a PepsiCo ownership of six years over a site with nearly three decades of operating history — the shortest ownership-to-closure interval in this ledger.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo Foods U.S. said the plant had played a vital role in producing the PopCorners brand, but that the pace of growth for the product line, paired with the broader industry pace of growth, had made it difficult to sustain the site's long-term viability. The company said it had notified its workforce and was working closely with employees and local community officials to provide a supportive transition. The framing is unusually specific by this ledger's standards: a product-line growth judgement, named as such, rather than a general appeal to business needs.

**WARN Act posture.** The February 18 filing for separations beginning May 21 satisfied the federal sixty-day requirement with a month to spare, and no notice dispute was reported. The Liberty execution matters to this tracker precisely because it is unremarkable as compliance: it demonstrates that the same company, in the same programme, in the same year, gave one workforce three months of filed warning and another — at Rancho Cucamonga, four months later — none at all. The difference is a choice made site by site, and the ledger records it as such.

**Severance terms.** No severance formula was published for this round. The documented terms are the notice itself and the transition support the company described in its statement; nothing further meeting this tracker's evidence standard was reported.

**Sources.** This section rests on the New York WARN notice filed February 18, 2025; Food Dive's February 26, 2025 report of the closure and the company's statement; the Albany Times Union's reporting of the filing as carried by trade coverage; and Sullivan County local reporting on the plant's history and role.

**The acquisition dimension.** Liberty closes an arc that begins with an acquisition. PepsiCo bought BFY Brands in 2019 to own PopCorners, a growth brand in the better-for-you snack segment it was then assembling; six years later it closed the brand's founding plant because the product line's growth no longer justified the site. The brand itself continued — production moved elsewhere in the network — which is the recurring structure of these rounds: the product survives, the plant does not. For the tracker, Liberty is the cleanest small example of the portfolio logic that the snapshot describes at company scale, in which sites are held to a growth standard and closed when they fall short of it, whatever their local history.

**The county dimension.** Liberty's closure landed in a county where a single plant's payroll carries unusual weight. Sullivan County's economy rests on healthcare, hospitality and a small manufacturing base, and the Frito-Lay plant — operating since 1997, under three owners — was among its largest private employers; local reporting on the closure treated it accordingly, as a county event rather than a company item. The three months of filed notice the workforce received gave the county's response time to organise, and the closure proceeded without the disputes that mark the ledger's immediate-pattern rounds. The tracker notes the combination because it is the record's best evidence that the notice pattern changes the local experience of a closure, not only its legal exposure: the same company that told Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing workforce on the day gave Liberty's workforce a season, and the local record of the two rounds reads entirely differently as a result.

## October 2024: The Four Bottling Plant Closures

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Monday, October 28, 2024, PepsiCo announced the closure of production at four United States bottling plants — in Cincinnati, Ohio; Chicago, Illinois; Harrisburg, Pennsylvania; and Atlanta, Georgia — in a single action reported the same day by the Associated Press. The Chicago plant closed entirely and immediately: its workforce learned of the closure on arrival that morning. At the other three sites, production ended while sales, delivery and warehouse functions continued. The separations were structured around a sixty-day paid period from the announcement.

**Roles cut.** The company gave the figures site by site: 136 workers in Cincinnati, 131 in Chicago, 127 in Harrisburg, and fewer than 50 in Atlanta — 394 documented at the three fully counted sites, plus the Atlanta figure, which the ledger carries as a range of 394–443 in total. Contemporary reporting summarised the round as nearly 400 jobs. The Chicago count of 131 comes from the Illinois state filing; early coverage, drawing on the union's account of a plant employing about 150, sometimes used the higher site-employment figure, and the tracker follows the filing.

**Share of workforce.** At either end of the range, about 0.1 percent of the company's global workforce, measured against the approximately 319,000 employees of the 2024 annual report. The round was nevertheless the largest single announced action in the ledger to that date, and the first to span four states in one announcement.

**Divisions and geographies.** Four PepsiCo Beverages production sites across the Midwest, Mid-Atlantic and Southeast. Only Chicago's plant — the more than sixty-year-old facility on 51st Street in the Back of the Yards neighbourhood — closed in full. In Cincinnati, Harrisburg and Atlanta, the sites' sales, delivery and warehouse operations continued, so the action ended those plants' manufacturing function rather than the company's presence in those markets. The distinction matters to the local record: a production closure removes the site's industrial workforce while leaving its distribution footprint standing.

**The company's stated framing.** PepsiCo said the decision to close the Chicago plant was a difficult one, and described the building as a facility with physical limitations; the four-site action was presented as part of streamlining its operations. The results context was on the record: North America beverage sales volumes had fallen 3 percent in both the second and third quarters of 2024, and the company's third-quarter net income had fallen 5 percent to $2.9 billion. The tracker attributes the round to that documented context of declining beverage volume and network streamlining.

**WARN Act posture.** Chicago produced the ledger's most contested notice record. Teamsters Local 727, which represented the plant's workers, said it was informed of the closure early on October 28 in an email from PepsiCo attorneys, and that workers were told the site had shut when they reported for work; the union alleged that the closure violated the federal WARN Act's sixty-day notice requirement and the collective bargaining agreement — which, the union noted, had been negotiated that summer without any mention of a closure — and it filed suit on November 4, 2024 over the closure of the warehouse operation. PepsiCo said its plans met applicable legal requirements and that it would actively work with union leadership on the details of the closure. Those positions are recorded here as the parties' stated positions; the tracker takes no side and reports no adjudicated outcome. The company's mitigation was financial: all affected employees across the four sites were to be paid and receive benefits for sixty days, most of them not required to work during that period — pay in lieu of the notice that was not given in time.

**Severance terms.** The documented terms are the sixty days of pay and benefits continuation described above. No severance formula beyond the paid notice period was published for this round.

**Sources.** This section rests on the Associated Press' October 28, 2024 reporting of the announcement and the site figures; the Illinois state filing recording 131 Chicago separations; Manufacturing Dive's November 2024 account of the closure and the parties' positions; and Teamsters Local 727's public statements, including its December 2024 release on the dispute.

**The template round.** In retrospect, October 2024 is the round that set the template the beverage entries in this ledger have followed since: production ended at ageing plants, distribution and sales preserved, sixty days of pay substituted for sixty days of warning, and the whole action defended as streamlining in a falling-volume business. Cheverly and Knoxville in 2026 are this round executed one site at a time, with cleaner paperwork. The Chicago dispute also fixed the union dimension of the pattern: where a site is organised, the closure lands inside a collective bargaining relationship with its own notice and bargaining obligations, and the contest is fought in that arena as much as under the WARN Act. Both features — the paid-notice substitution and the union contest — recur often enough in the ledger to be treated in the standing section as machinery rather than incident.

**The simultaneity dimension.** The four-site structure of the October 2024 announcement is itself evidence about how the beverage network is managed. Closing production at four plants in four states on one morning is not four local decisions surfacing at once; it is a single network plan, in which the volume of each closed plant had already been assigned to surviving facilities before the announcement was made. That is why the company's framing could be so brief — streamlining, and one building's physical limitations — and why the local records, rich as some of them are, contain no hint of a site-by-site contest over which plants would survive. The plan's unit of decision was the network, and the sites experienced it as weather. Later beverage rounds in the ledger were announced singly, but nothing in their execution suggests a different unit of decision: Cheverly and Knoxville read as instalments of the same network logic, announced one address at a time. The tracker flags the point because it governs how future rounds should be read — a single-site beverage closure at PepsiCo is evidence about the network plan it belongs to, not only about the site it names.

## April 2024: The Danville Quaker Plant Closure

**Announcement and effective dates.** On Wednesday, April 3, 2024, the Quaker Oats Company confirmed the permanent closure of its plant on Voorhees Street in Danville, Illinois, in a statement issued after employees were told in a series of meetings that day. An Illinois WARN notice recorded the layoffs, and the plant closed permanently on June 8, 2024. The workforce had not produced since December 2023, when production was paused following the recall described below; employees remained on payroll through the June closure, so the separation, when it came, ended roughly six months of paid idleness rather than a working job — a sequence unique in this ledger.

**Roles cut.** The WARN notice and the company's letter to employees record more than 510 employees laid off; the ledger carries 510. The roles were the full workforce of a plant that made Quaker Chewy granola bars, ready-to-eat cereals and related products. No part of the workforce was retained at the site, which closed in full.

**Share of workforce.** Five hundred and ten roles is about 0.2 percent of the company's global workforce, measured against the approximately 318,000 employees of the 2023 annual report. In Danville, a city where the plant had been one of the largest employers for fifty-five years, the closure was a civic event: the mayor, the county's economic development leadership, the community college and the workforce agency issued a joint statement the day it was announced, pledging coordinated support for the affected families.

**Divisions and geographies.** A single Quaker plant in Vermilion County, Illinois, part of Quaker Foods North America. The plant had operated since 1969. Its production was permanently shifted to other Quaker facilities in the company's network; the brand's products continued without interruption, which is the structural point the company's statement emphasised.

**The company's stated framing.** Quaker's statement tied the closure directly to the December 2023 recall, in which more than sixty products made at the plant were recalled for potential Salmonella contamination, with the recall expanded twice in January 2024. Following the recall, the company said, production was paused; after a detailed review it determined that meeting its future manufacturing needs would require an extended closure for enhancements and modernisation, and that production would instead need to shift permanently to other facilities in order to continue timely delivery of its products. The statement said the decision was not made lightly and recognised its impact on employees, their families and the Danville community. The financial context was in the company's own reporting: Quaker Foods North America's operating profit fell 19 percent in 2023 to $492 million, with the recall among the causes. The tracker records the closure as the recall's direct corporate consequence, as the company itself framed it.

**WARN Act posture.** The Illinois WARN notice was filed with the closure announcement in April 2024 for a June 8 closure, and the workforce's paid status from the December pause through the closure date meant the notice period was exceeded in substance: employees had been neither working nor unpaid since December, and their formal notice in April preceded the June separation by two months. No notice dispute was reported. Danville is the ledger's example of a closure whose timeline was dictated by an operational crisis rather than by a notice strategy, and its paperwork is correspondingly clean.

**Severance terms.** The documented terms are the continuation of pay from the December 2023 production pause through the June 8, 2024 closure, and the company's commitment, stated to employees and local officials, to work with the workforce and community on transition support, including individual meetings about opportunities elsewhere in the company. No severance formula beyond that was published.

**Sources.** This section rests on the Illinois WARN notice as reported by Manufacturing Dive on April 19, 2024; Quaker's closure statement as carried in full by WCIA; Illinois Public Media's account of the announcement and the community response; and the City of Danville's joint statement of April 3, 2024.

**The recall dimension.** Danville is the only round in this ledger caused by a product safety failure, and it deserves separate statement because its logic differs from every other entry. The other closures in the ledger are network decisions: a site becomes surplus to a shrinking or reorganising system. Danville's plant became unusable for its purpose the moment its products were recalled; the question the company faced was whether to invest in modernising a paused, contamination-associated facility or to move its production to plants without that history, and it chose the network over the site. The workforce consequence — 510 jobs in a small city — followed from a quality failure, not from demand. The tracker records the round among the layoff rounds because that is what it was for the people in it, while noting that its stated cause places it apart from the productivity programme that frames the rest of the ledger: no efficiency argument was made for Danville, and none was needed.

**The paid-pause dimension.** Danville's workforce experienced the closure in a form no other round in the ledger reproduces: six months of paid non-work between the last production day and the separation date. The arrangement functioned, in substance, as the longest notice period in the record — longer than any WARN filing's window — and as a severance equivalent paid in salary rather than in a lump sum, though the company described it in neither of those terms. It also complicates any simple ranking of the company's rounds by generosity: the Danville employees had the most time and the most income continuity of any workforce in the ledger, and they had it because the plant's crisis, not a policy, dictated the timeline. The tracker records the sequence in the round's own section rather than converting it into a severance figure, because the payments were wages for an employment that continued on paper until June 8, 2024 — a distinction that mattered to benefits, tenure and the employees' own status during the pause, and one the ledger preserves rather than flattening.

## December 2022: The Headquarters Simplification Round

**Announcement and effective dates.** In the first week of December 2022, PepsiCo told employees in a memo that it would eliminate hundreds of corporate roles at its North American headquarters operations. The Wall Street Journal reported the memo on December 5, 2022, having viewed it, and trade coverage followed on December 6. The cuts were executed from December 2022. This is the earliest round in the ledger, and the only one besides November 2026 that was organisational rather than site-based.

**Roles cut.** The reporting said only hundreds, and PepsiCo never published a total. This tracker carries the figure as the range the reported word supports — 200 to 900 — labelled as a reported range wherever it appears, including the cumulative table, and it flags the range as the softest figure in the ledger. A claim carried by a low-quality aggregator at the time, that the round would reach 4,000 to 8,000 jobs in January 2023, is not corroborated by any reputable outlet, filing or WARN record and is recorded in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified; it is not part of this figure. Separately, and never summed into it: the snacks business had already reduced positions through a voluntary retirement programme before this round, a fact the Journal's reporting noted in explaining the round's incidence. The number of voluntary separations was not reported, and voluntary exits are recorded here as context, not as layoffs.

**Share of workforce.** At the reported range, the round represents roughly 0.1 to 0.3 percent of the company's global workforce, measured against approximately 309,000 employees at the end of 2021 and 315,000 at the end of 2022. Its weight was concentrated rather than broad: the affected population was the headquarters staff of two divisions, not the company's front line.

**Divisions and geographies.** The cuts fell on the North America beverage business headquartered in Purchase, New York, and on the North America snacks and packaged-foods business headquartered in Chicago, Illinois and Plano, Texas. Reporting consistently described the beverage side as taking the heavier share, precisely because the snacks side had already been reduced through the voluntary programme. No plant, warehouse or front-line roles were part of this round as reported; it was a headquarters action in three office locations.

**The company's stated framing.** The memo told employees the cuts were intended to simplify the organization so the company could operate more efficiently. The results context ran the other way from later rounds: PepsiCo had raised its full-year outlook in October 2022 after a strong third quarter built on price increases, and its chief financial officer, Hugh Johnston, had publicly committed to continued cost discipline in the same period. The round is therefore the ledger's clearest case of a reduction made from a position of reported commercial strength — a margin action, not a rescue — and it set the pattern the November 2026 memo would repeat almost verbatim four years later, down to the language of simplification.

**WARN Act posture.** No WARN filings accompanied this round in the reporting. Headquarters reductions spread across three office locations in three states characteristically fall below the single-site thresholds that trigger federal filings, and state mini-WARN thresholds in New York and Illinois were not reported as triggered. The round's documentary record is the memo and the reporting of it — the thinnest paper trail of any entry in the ledger, and the reason its figure remains a range.

**Severance terms.** No severance terms for this round were published or reported to this tracker's standard. The voluntary retirement programme that preceded it in the snacks business was, by definition, offered on voluntary terms that were not made public. The section records the absence rather than filling it: for the ledger's first round, the separation machinery is the least documented, and later rounds' filings are what allow the standing section below to describe the company's practice at all.

**Sources.** This section rests on the Wall Street Journal's December 2022 reporting of the company memo, as carried and corroborated by Food Navigator on December 6, 2022; Just Food's December 6, 2022 account; TheStreet's December 6, 2022 report; and FoodBev Media's December 7, 2022 summary.

**The bookend dimension.** The ledger begins and ends, in its current form, with the same sentence said two ways. In December 2022 the company simplified its organisation to operate more efficiently; in November 2026 it simplified its structures to clarify accountability and decide faster. Between the two memos lie nine site rounds, thousands of filed separations, a productivity plan extended to 2030, an activist agreement, and the integration of the two headquarters that the first memo had addressed separately. The tracker keeps the 2022 round at the foot of the ledger not as a footnote but as the baseline: it is the round against which the scale, method and language of every later round can be measured, and its documentary thinness — one memo, no filings, no total — is itself the measure of how much more visible the company's reductions became once they moved from offices to plants.

**The margin dimension.** The 2022 round also deserves its place as the ledger's clearest statement of the company's margin doctrine. PepsiCo's growth that year was built on price: revenue rose strongly while volumes were roughly flat, and the October 2022 outlook raise told investors the model was working. In the same season, the company's chief financial officer said publicly that it would keep cutting costs where necessary to protect its margin goals — and in December, the headquarters cuts followed. The sequence matters because it removes any ambiguity about the round's function: it was not a response to weak trading, a failed product or a troubled division, but the cost half of a price-led strategy, executed while the strategy was succeeding. Every later round in the ledger can be read against that baseline. The plant closures of 2024 and 2025 added a volume problem the 2022 round did not have; the 2026 rounds added an activist agreement it did not have. The doctrine underneath — that the cost base is continuously adjustable to defend the margin — was present from the first entry, stated by the company's own finance chief in the same quarter the first memo was sent.

## How Cuts Work at PepsiCo

**The standing programme.** Almost every round in this ledger was executed inside a single, long-running financial structure: the productivity plan PepsiCo announced in 2019 and has extended twice since. The plan's stated purposes, in the company's own filing language, are to simplify, harmonise and automate processes, to re-engineer its go-to-market and information systems, and to simplify the organisation and optimise its manufacturing and supply chain footprint. In 2024 the company expanded the plan and extended it through the end of 2030, and its quarterly filing for the second quarter of 2025 put the expected pre-tax charges at approximately $6.15 billion, of which about $5.1 billion is cash. The expected composition is the anatomy of a layoff programme stated in accounting form: approximately 55 percent severance and other employee-related costs, approximately 10 percent non-cash asset impairments resulting from plant closures, and approximately 35 percent other implementation costs. By June 14, 2025, $3.05 billion of charges had been incurred under the plan, including $1.56 billion of severance and other employee costs. The practical meaning for anyone reading this tracker is that PepsiCo's reductions are not episodic reactions to individual quarters; they are instalments of a funded, disclosed, multi-year programme whose remaining envelope, at any given filing, is a matter of public arithmetic.

**Two forms of round.** The ledger contains two distinct mechanisms, and nearly everything about how a PepsiCo reduction feels to the people inside it depends on which form it takes. The first is the site action: a plant, warehouse or bottling facility is closed, partially closed, or handed to another operator, and the workforce impact is concentrated at one address. Site actions are documented in WARN filings, announced locally, and executed on a site timetable. The second is the organisational round: a headquarters or regional-office reduction announced by internal memo, dispersed across locations, and documented mainly by the reporting of the memo itself. The December 2022 and November 2026 rounds are the ledger's organisational entries; the other nine are site actions. The two forms differ in visibility (filings versus memos), in precision (filed counts versus reported approximations), in notice practice (statutory windows versus internal timetables), and in contest (union contracts and state agencies versus almost no external forum at all). A reader who understands which form a round takes can predict most of its documentary shape before reading its section.

**How notice works.** The federal WARN Act requires employers with 100 or more employees to give sixty calendar days' written notice before a plant closing affecting fifty or more employees at a single site, or a mass layoff meeting its thresholds, and several states in this ledger — California, New York, Illinois among them — operate mini-WARN laws with their own triggers. PepsiCo's documented practice divides into two patterns. In the advance-notice pattern, the filing precedes the separations by the full window or more: Liberty was filed about three months before its first separations, Rancho Cucamonga's distribution closure about four months before, Columbia and Knoxville at or inside the statutory window with employment formally continuing to the effective date. In the immediate pattern, the announcement and the operational end arrive together: Orlando's plant shut the day its letters were filed, Chicago's workforce learned of the closure on arrival, Rancho Cucamonga's production ended the day its workforce was told, and Cheverly's town government was notified the day the reduction took effect. The law's sixty days are honoured in both patterns — in the first as time, in the second as money, through the pay-in-lieu mechanism described next. What varies is not the company's acknowledgement of the obligation but the form in which the affected workforce receives it.

**The state layer.** Federal WARN is only the floor of the notice regime, and the ledger's geography runs through states that build above it. California's mini-WARN law reaches layoffs of fifty or more at a covered establishment within thirty days and dispenses with some of the federal thresholds, which is why the Rancho Cucamonga actions were judged against it and why the state's confirmation about the missing 2025 filing mattered. New York's law likewise applies its own triggers and a longer state notice expectation, against which Liberty's three-month filing was comfortably compliant. Illinois operates its own WARN statute alongside the federal one, and the Danville and Chicago filings were made into that dual system. Maryland, Florida, Tennessee and South Carolina, by contrast, appear in this ledger chiefly as administrators of the federal filing — their state logs and reports are the record the tracker cites, and the operative obligations were the federal sixty days. The practical consequence for a multi-state employer is that a single network plan must be executed under several notice regimes at once; the practical consequence for this tracker is that the quality of the public record varies by state, richest where a state publishes letters or detailed logs, thinnest where it publishes only a count.

**Pay in lieu of notice.** The recurring financial instrument of the immediate pattern is sixty days of pay and benefits continuation, granted without a requirement to work. It appears, in documented form, at Chicago and the other three bottling sites in October 2024, where all affected employees were to be paid for sixty days though most would not work them; at Orlando, where the WARN letter recorded sixty days' pay in lieu of the notice period for the 454 plant employees; at Cheverly, where pay and health care ran through November 13, 2026, sixty days after the September notice; and at Knoxville, where pay and benefits ran through January 1, 2027 after an October closure. The mechanism converts a timing obligation into a cash one. It also has a documented edge: in the contested cases — Chicago, where the union alleged the closure itself breached the Act and the contract, and Rancho Cucamonga, where the state confirmed no advance filing had been made — the existence of paid continuation did not settle the legal argument, because the union and regulator positions concerned the notice and bargaining obligations themselves, not only their cash value. The tracker records both the payments and the disputes, since both are part of how the mechanism actually operates.

**Severance: the documented record.** PepsiCo does not publish a company-wide severance formula, and no filing in the record states one. What the ledger establishes is a set of documented instances. At Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing shutdown, employees reported ten weeks' pay as severance — an employee-reported figure, labelled as such in its round section. At Raleigh, reporting described pay and benefits continuation tied to years of service, the only tenure-linked formula documented in the period. At Danville, the operative term was not severance at all but six months of continued payroll between the production pause and the closure. Everywhere else, the documented package is the paid notice period plus transition assistance and career support of the kind the company's closure statements routinely promise. The aggregate picture exists only at programme level: the productivity plan's expected charges allocate roughly 55 percent of $6.15 billion — on the order of $3.4 billion across the plan's life — to severance and other employee-related costs, a figure that includes voluntary separation arrangements and spans the whole world, and therefore cannot be divided into a per-person or per-round entitlement. Any source that states a single standard PepsiCo severance formula is going beyond the public record, and this tracker does not.

**The union machinery.** A large share of PepsiCo's bottling and plant workforce is unionised, principally by the Teamsters, and the collective bargaining layer changes how a site action proceeds. The WARN letters themselves record the machinery: at Muncie, the filing noted that the Teamsters Local 135 contract afforded impacted workers bidding rights — the contractual right to claim other open positions under the agreement's rules — and at Detroit, about eleven of the affected employees were represented by Teamsters Local 337 and Operating Engineers 324. Where no such rights exist, the filings say so: Orlando's WARN letter recorded that its workforce had no bumping rights and no union representation. The contested cases arise in the union layer as much as under the statute. Chicago's Local 727 alleged breaches of both the WARN Act and a contract negotiated months before the closure, sued over the warehouse operation's closure, and later rejected the company's request that information about the closure be shared only under non-disclosure agreements. None of that contest prevented the closure; all of it shaped the terms, the timeline and the public record. At non-union sites — Liberty, Knoxville, Orlando — no comparable forum existed, and the rounds proceeded on the company's stated terms alone. Whether a PepsiCo site is organised is therefore one of the strongest predictors of how its closure will be documented and disputed.

**The handover mechanism.** The 2026 entries added a third form to the company's repertoire, distinct from both closure and organisational rounds: the logistics handover, in which management of a working warehouse passes to an outside logistics provider. At Columbia, South Carolina, the facility stayed open and fully operational while 105 PepsiCo warehouse jobs ended; at Raleigh, North Carolina, 65 of 68 positions at a distribution center were eliminated as its management changed hands; at Muncie in 2023, the same structure appeared in corporate form, as PepsiCo discontinued its management and operation of a facility it ran for the beverage system. The company's consistent offer in handovers is assistance applying for positions with the incoming provider, plus pay and benefits continuation. The separations are permanent and are filed as permanent layoffs; whether any individual continues in the same building under a new employer, and on what terms, is not something the public record tracks, and this tracker does not estimate it. The mechanism's significance is structural: it lets the company convert a fixed labour cost into a purchased service while keeping the site's position in its delivery network, and it is the form most likely to be invisible to anyone counting plant closures alone — one reason this ledger counts filed separations rather than shuttered buildings.

The handover also has a distinctive evidence profile, which is why two of its three documented instances sit in the snapshot rather than the ledger. A closure announces itself: the plant gates lock, the local paper writes the story, the brand's production visibly moves. A handover announces almost nothing — the same trucks leave the same dock under new management — and surfaces only in the WARN log, where a reader must already know what a line like "management of warehouse operations transferred" signifies. Muncie in 2023 and Raleigh in 2026 were each documented well enough to name, with dates, counts and contract details, but each fell below the size threshold; Columbia in 2026, at 105, is the first handover large enough to enter the ledger on its own. The tracker treats the three as one family in the standing sections precisely because the ledger's threshold would otherwise scatter them, and a reader counting only ledger rounds would underestimate how much of the company's 2026 adjustment took this quiet form.

**Why sales and delivery survive.** Read across the site rounds, one line repeats: production and warehouse roles end; sales and delivery continue. The pattern is the fingerprint of PepsiCo's direct-store-delivery system, in which the company's own people stock shelves and manage the customer relationship in store. That front end is the company's competitive asset — the reason retailers give its brands their shelf position — and it is labour the company does not cut even when it closes the plant that supplied the route. Production, by contrast, is networked: a plant's output can be absorbed by other plants, as Danville's was, and a bottling plant's volume can be served from neighbouring facilities, as Knoxville's was expected to be. The result is a consistent asymmetry in the ledger: the industrial workforce of a site is treated as adjustable capacity, while the commercial workforce attached to the same site is treated as infrastructure. Readers assessing a future PepsiCo site announcement can apply the pattern directly — the question is never whether the market will still be served, but which of the site's functions the company counts as capacity and which it counts as presence.

**Benefits, pensions and the paper trail.** The WARN letters in the record are consistent on one point: retirement and group insurance benefits are provided in accordance with the terms of the applicable employee benefit plans, a formulation that appears in PepsiCo's filings from Muncie onward and reflects the legal separation between a layoff decision and the benefit plans that survive it. Accrued pension and retirement benefits are plan obligations, not severance, and are not forfeited by a layoff; what ends with employment is future accrual and, after any continuation period, employer-subsidised active coverage. The productivity plan's charges include pension-related effects — the company's non-GAAP reporting notes that settlement and curtailment effects can be triggered by restructuring actions under the plan — which is the filing-level trace of large separations moving through the retirement system. Equity plays a smaller role at PepsiCo than at the technology companies this programme also tracks: the workforce in these rounds is predominantly hourly and front-line, and no round in the ledger produced reporting of stock-treatment disputes. The paper trail that matters at PepsiCo is statutory and contractual — WARN letters, state logs, union correspondence — and it is unusually complete for site actions precisely because the law requires it to exist before, or at worst on, the day.

**The geography of the record.** Every round in this ledger is in the United States, and that is an artefact of evidence, not a map of the programme. The productivity plan's expected charges allocate the largest single share — about 30 percent — to Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and a further quarter to PepsiCo Beverages North America; the restructuring is global by the company's own arithmetic. What differs by country is the documentary regime. United States site actions generate WARN filings with dates, addresses and counts, which is why the ledger can be built at all. Reductions in most other jurisdictions surface, if at all, as segment-level charges in quarterly filings, without round-level counts. This tracker therefore describes the United States record in detail and the international programme only at the level the filings support, and it flags the imbalance rather than correcting it by inference: the absence of an international round from the ledger means no international round met the evidence standard, not that none occurred.

**How a round surfaces.** The order in which PepsiCo's reductions become knowable is itself a standing fact. For site actions, the first public document is usually the WARN filing or the local reporting of it — often before any company statement exists, and occasionally, as at Rancho Cucamonga, after the action has already been executed. For organisational rounds, the first document is the internal memo, which reaches the public through the financial press within hours, with the company's confirmation limited to the memo's own text. The financial filings come last and say the least about individual rounds, while saying the most about the programme: the productivity plan note in each quarterly filing is where the cumulative cost of all rounds, announced and unannounced, is totalled. A reader who wants early warning of a PepsiCo round should therefore watch state WARN logs and the trade press that monitors them; a reader who wants the programme's true scale should read the restructuring note in the quarterly filing. This tracker is built to do both, in that order.

**What the remaining envelope does and does not say.** As of the second-quarter 2025 filing, roughly half of the plan's expected $6.15 billion of charges had been incurred, with the plan running through 2030; the company's later filings continued the same structure. Arithmetic of that kind establishes that funded restructuring capacity remains — it does not establish that any particular site or organisation will be cut, and this tracker does not publish speculation about unannounced rounds. What can be said on the record is narrower and more useful: every round in this ledger was charged, or is chargeable, to a programme whose existence and scale the company discloses in advance; the December 2025 agreement with Elliott added public commitments — product-range reduction, a supply chain review, a margin target — whose implementation the 2026 rounds accompanied; and the integration of the North America organisation under a single chief executive created the structure within which the largest organisational round in the ledger was announced. When the next round qualifies, it will be added at the top of the ledger, the table will be recomputed, and the standing sections will be amended only if the round changes the machinery they describe.

**How to read a PepsiCo WARN letter.** The filings that anchor this ledger follow a standard anatomy, and knowing it makes the record easier to audit. A PepsiCo WARN letter typically identifies the employer entity and site address, states the action — closure, layoff, or discontinuation of operations — gives the expected separation dates or the window in which they fall, and records the number of affected employees, sometimes with an exhibit listing positions or, as at Muncie, named separations. It states whether the action is permanent, whether bumping rights exist under any applicable contract, which union, if any, represents the workforce and which union official has been notified, and it closes with the standard assurance that retirement and group insurance benefits are provided in accordance with the applicable plans, over the signature of a human resources officer. Each of those fields does work in this tracker: permanence separates rounds from temporary layoffs, the bumping-rights line maps the union layer, the union-notification line starts the dispute record where one exists, and the count in the letter outranks every other figure for the round. Where a state's log rather than the letter itself is the surviving record — Maryland's log for Aberdeen, South Carolina's official report for Columbia — the tracker says so, because a log line is one step further from the company's own hand than its letter.

**The record's silences.** A standing section should also state what the machinery never produces. PepsiCo does not disclose, for any round in this ledger, the selection criteria applied to individuals inside an affected site; it does not publish the severance schedule its human resources teams apply, beyond the instances employees or filings have made visible; it does not state how many separated employees were rehired elsewhere in the company, though its closure statements routinely promise consideration for other roles; and it does not reconcile the rounds it announces with the headcount in its annual reports, which move for many reasons besides layoffs — hiring, attrition, acquisitions, divestitures and the productivity programme all at once. The global headcount fell by roughly 13,000 between the end of 2024 and the end of 2025 while the verified United States rounds of those two years account for a small fraction of that movement, and the tracker draws no line between the two figures because the filings do not draw one. These silences are not evidence of concealment; they are the ordinary boundaries of the disclosure regime the company operates in. But they define what a tracker like this one can and cannot claim, and they are recorded here so that the ledger's precision about what is documented is never mistaken for completeness about what occurred.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many people has PepsiCo laid off in total since 2022?

Across the eleven rounds this tracker has verified to its evidence standard, the total is a reported range of roughly 3,900 to 4,700 roles. The range exists because three rounds rest on sources that disagree or on reported approximations: the December 2022 headquarters round was reported only as hundreds, the September 2026 Cheverly round's sources give 98 and 143, and the October 2024 bottling round includes an Atlanta figure reported only as fewer than 50. Every other round carries a filed or company-stated count. Four further WARN-documented actions below the tracker's 100-role threshold — Muncie, Detroit, Aberdeen and Raleigh — account for roughly another 250 separations and are named in the snapshot rather than summed here.

### Q: Why does PepsiCo cut jobs when it is profitable?

The rounds are a margin and network programme, not a response to losses. PepsiCo remained profitable throughout the period; what deteriorated was volume growth in North America, as consumers resisted years of price increases and beverage demand softened. The company's stated logic, in its filings and memos, is simplification and productivity: removing organisational complexity, closing surplus production capacity, and redirecting the savings into price investment, marketing and automation. The December 2022 round is the clearest evidence that profitability is not the trigger — it was announced weeks after the company raised its full-year outlook on a strong quarter.

### Q: What is PepsiCo's 2019 Productivity Plan?

It is the multi-year restructuring programme, announced in 2019 and expanded and extended in 2024 through the end of 2030, inside which most of the rounds in this tracker were executed. PepsiCo's filings put its expected pre-tax charges at approximately $6.15 billion, including about $5.1 billion in cash, with roughly 55 percent allocated to severance and other employee-related costs, about 10 percent to asset impairments from plant closures, and the rest to other implementation costs. By June 14, 2025, the company had incurred $3.05 billion of charges under the plan. The plan's disclosure in every quarterly filing is the most reliable running total of the company's restructuring activity. Its expected charges are allocated across the whole company — about 30 percent to Europe, the Middle East and Africa, 25 percent to PepsiCo Beverages North America, 15 percent each to PepsiCo Foods North America and corporate functions, with the balance across Latin America, Asia Pacific and the franchise bottling group — which is the filing-grade evidence that the programme is global even though the individually verifiable rounds are American.

### Q: What counts as a layoff round in this tracker?

A round enters the ledger when the company announces a workforce reduction, when a WARN Act or state mini-WARN filing by the company is on record, or when two independent outlets report the same reduction of at least 100 roles with a consistent scope. Actions documented by filings but below the 100-role threshold are named in the snapshot instead of the ledger. Reported claims that meet none of these tests — such as the claim that the December 2022 round would reach 4,000 to 8,000 jobs — are recorded as reported-but-unverified and are never included in the totals.

### Q: How much notice does PepsiCo give before a layoff takes effect?

The documented record shows two patterns rather than one standard. In the advance-notice pattern, WARN filings precede separations by the full statutory window or more: about three months at Liberty, about four months for the Rancho Cucamonga distribution closure, and sixty days at Columbia and Knoxville. In the immediate pattern, operations end on the day of the announcement — at Orlando, Chicago and Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing plant — and the company substitutes sixty days of pay and benefits for the sixty days of warning. Which pattern a workforce receives is decided site by site, and the filings show no single rule governing the choice. The same campus proves the point: Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing workforce was told on the day in June 2025, while its distribution workforce received about four months' filed notice for the closure completed in June 2026.

### Q: What severance does PepsiCo offer?

PepsiCo does not publish a company-wide severance formula, and this tracker reports only documented instances. Employees at Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing plant reported ten weeks' pay as severance. At Raleigh, reporting described pay and benefits continuation tied to years of service. At Danville, employees were kept on payroll for about six months between the production pause and the closure. The most common documented package across the ledger is the paid notice period — typically sixty days of pay and benefits — plus transition assistance and career support. Anything presented as a single standard PepsiCo formula goes beyond the public record.

### Q: Does the WARN Act apply to PepsiCo layoffs?

Yes, where its thresholds are met: the federal Act requires sixty days' written notice for plant closings affecting fifty or more employees at a single site and for qualifying mass layoffs, and states including California, New York and Illinois add their own mini-WARN requirements. Most site rounds in this ledger generated filings. Two notice disputes are documented: in Chicago in 2024, Teamsters Local 727 alleged WARN and contract violations and sued, which the company disputed; in California in 2025, the state confirmed no advance filing had been made when Rancho Cucamonga's manufacturing workforce was told. Organisational rounds dispersed across many sites, such as the November 2026 round, characteristically produce no filings because the thresholds are counted site by site. The Act also carries exceptions and defences that surface in contested cases — unforeseeable business circumstances, and disputes over whether pay in lieu satisfied the obligation — and the two disputes in this ledger were argued in exactly that territory: the Chicago union's position was that the closure breached both the statute and the contract, while the company's was that its plans met applicable legal requirements.

### Q: Why do PepsiCo's plant closures keep sales and delivery jobs?

Because of the company's direct-store-delivery system. PepsiCo's commercial presence in a market — the route salespeople and delivery teams who stock its products on store shelves — is a competitive asset the company protects even when it closes the plant that supplied the route. Production is networked and can be absorbed by other facilities; shelf presence cannot be outsourced as easily. The result, consistent across the ledger, is that closures remove a site's manufacturing and warehouse workforce while its sales and delivery organisation continues, which is why so many of these rounds are partial-site actions rather than full withdrawals from a market.

### Q: What is a logistics handover, and why does it appear in a layoff tracker?

A handover is the transfer of a working operation — typically a warehouse — to an outside logistics provider. The building stays open and the work continues, but the PepsiCo jobs end, and the separations are filed as permanent layoffs. Columbia, South Carolina in 2026 is the ledger's clearest case, with Raleigh, North Carolina and Muncie, Indiana as further documented instances. The tracker counts handovers as rounds when they meet the evidence and size standards, because the job losses are real and filed, even though nothing closed. The company's documented offer in handovers is help applying for roles with the incoming provider, plus pay and benefits continuation.

### Q: What role did the Quaker recall play in PepsiCo's layoffs?

It caused one round directly. In December 2023, more than sixty Quaker products were recalled for potential Salmonella contamination, and production at the Danville, Illinois plant that made them was paused. In April 2024 the company announced the plant would close permanently, eliminating 510 jobs, saying that production would shift to other facilities rather than return to a plant that would have required extended closure and modernisation. Quaker Foods North America's operating profit had fallen 19 percent in 2023, with the recall among the causes. No other round in the ledger is attributed to a product safety event.

### Q: What did Elliott Investment Management have to do with the layoffs?

Elliott disclosed a stake of roughly $4 billion in September 2025 and pressed PepsiCo on underperformance, product-range complexity and margins. The December 2025 agreement between them committed PepsiCo to cutting roughly 20 percent of its United States product range, lowering prices, targeting 100 basis points of margin improvement over three years, and reviewing its North America supply chain — while a full refranchising of the bottling system was taken off the table. The 2026 rounds were executed in the agreement's shadow, and the November 2026 round accompanied the supply chain review's reporting season. The tracker records the agreement as context; the filings do not attribute any individual round to it.

### Q: How is the November 2026 round different from the December 2022 round?

In structure more than in language. Both were organisational rounds announced by internal memo in the vocabulary of simplification, and both were sized at roughly a thousand roles or fewer. The 2022 round ran separately inside two divisional headquarters — beverages in Purchase, snacks in Chicago and Plano — and its total was never published, so it is carried as a reported range. The 2026 round was announced once, for the combined North America food and beverage organisation created in December 2025, with the company's figure of about 1,000 reported consistently across outlets. Between the two, the company's reductions moved from offices to plants and back, accumulating the filed record the 2022 round lacked.

### Q: Has PepsiCo laid off workers outside the United States in this period?

The productivity plan is global — its expected charges allocate the largest share, about 30 percent, to Europe, the Middle East and Africa — so restructuring activity outside the United States is established at programme level by the company's own filings. What the tracker cannot do is verify individual international rounds to its standard: outside the United States there is generally no WARN-equivalent public filing with dates and counts, and international actions surface mainly as segment charges. Every round in the ledger is therefore a United States round, and that reflects the evidence regime, not a finding that reductions happened only there.

### Q: Do laid-off PepsiCo employees get to keep their retirement benefits?

Accrued retirement and group insurance benefits are governed by the terms of the applicable benefit plans, as PepsiCo's WARN letters state in standard language, and accrued pension benefits are not forfeited because of a layoff. What ends with employment is future accrual and, after any continuation period the company grants, active employee coverage on employer terms. The company's filings also note that restructuring actions under the productivity plan can trigger pension settlement and curtailment effects, which is the accounting trace of large separations moving through the retirement system. Individual entitlements depend on the plan documents that apply to each employee.

### Q: What happens to unionised workers when a PepsiCo plant closes?

Unionised sites carry a contractual layer on top of the statutory one. PepsiCo's WARN letters record where collective bargaining agreements afford affected workers bidding rights — the right to claim other open positions under the contract — as at Muncie under the Teamsters Local 135 agreement. Where no union represents the workforce, the filings can record the absence of such rights, as at Orlando, where the WARN letter noted no bumping rights. Union sites also generate the ledger's disputes: Chicago's Local 727 contested the 2024 closure under both the WARN Act and its contract, and Cheverly's Local 639 organised support, including a job fair, for its members in 2026.

### Q: Why are some figures in this tracker ranges instead of exact numbers?

Because the sources disagree or report approximately, and the tracker records the evidence rather than tidying it. Cheverly is carried as 98–143 because the town's statement of the company's notification and the Washington Post give 98 affected of 143 employed, while several WARN trackers read the filing as 143 affected. The October 2024 round is 394–443 because Atlanta's figure was reported only as fewer than 50. The December 2022 round is 200–900 because reporting said only hundreds. Where a WARN filing or company statement gives an exact count, the tracker uses it exactly, and any figure that is later settled is amended in place with the correction noted.

### Q: Which PepsiCo site has lost the most jobs in this period?

The Rancho Cucamonga, California campus, across two rounds in two years. Its manufacturing shutdown in June 2025 eliminated 432 roles, and the closure of its distribution operation, filed in February 2026 and completed that June, eliminated a further 247 to 248 — a combined 679 to 680 roles at a single site that had operated since 1970. The tracker keeps the stages as separate rounds because they were separately announced and filed, but records the combined total wherever the campus is discussed. Orlando's 500 roles in a single announcement is the largest one-action site total in the ledger.

### Q: Are the roles eliminated, or are some just left unfilled?

The figures in this tracker are roles eliminated, as recorded in filings, company statements and reporting. Where a related but distinct mechanism appears, it is recorded separately and never summed in: the clearest case is the voluntary retirement programme in the snacks business that preceded the December 2022 headquarters round, whose separations were voluntary and whose count was never reported. Attrition and hiring freezes undoubtedly shaped the company's net headcount across the period — the annual reports show employment rising to about 319,000 at the end of 2024 before falling to about 306,000 at the end of 2025 — but only documented elimination actions are counted as rounds.

### Q: Will there be more PepsiCo layoff rounds?

The funded capacity for them is a matter of public record: the productivity plan runs through 2030 with roughly half of its expected $6.15 billion of charges incurred by mid-2025, and the December 2025 agreement with Elliott added commitments, including a North America supply chain review, whose implementation the 2026 rounds accompanied. That establishes possibility, not prediction, and this tracker does not speculate about unannounced rounds. Commentary in late 2026 anticipated further reductions from the supply chain review; it is recorded in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified, and a further round will enter the ledger when a company announcement, a WARN filing or corroborated reporting establishes it.

### Q: How is this tracker kept accurate?

Each round is admitted only on a company announcement, a WARN or mini-WARN filing, or two independent outlets reporting the same reduction of at least 100 roles with consistent scope. Figures follow a source hierarchy — filings first, then company statements, then reputable reporting — estimates are labelled as estimates, and disagreements are carried as ranges with the sources named. The cumulative table is recomputed from the round rows each time the ledger changes, corrections are amended in place with the change visible in the round's own section, and claims that fail the evidence standard are named in the snapshot as reported-but-unverified rather than silently omitted.
