The constitutional question at the center of the campaign finance Supreme Court cases asks what limits a legislature may place on the money that funds election speech, and under what justification a court may sustain a limit after a challenger invokes the First Amendment. Money enters an election in distinct forms, and the decisions treat each form according to the relationship between the transfer and the communication the transfer makes possible. A contribution places funds under the control of a candidate, a party, or a committee. An expenditure purchases communication directly. A disclosure rule compels identification rather than prohibition. A public financing program offers a subsidy on stated conditions. The litigation traced in the sections that follow assigns each form a different measure of protection, a different standard of review, and a different fate, and the pattern of outcomes depends less on the amount of money involved than on the classification a court gives the regulated act. Two statutes supply the principal federal texts under construction. The decisions construe [the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971](/2021/09/01/federal-election-campaign-act-1971/) as rebuilt by the amendments of 1974 and by the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, together with state statutes of the same kind that raised parallel questions under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Buckley v. Valeo, decided in 1976, supplied the organizing distinction on which the field rests. The per curiam opinion sustained limits on contributions to candidates because a contribution expresses support in symbolic form while the candidate supplies the substantive message, and because large contributions create a danger of an exchange of money for official action that a legislature may act to prevent. The same opinion struck ceilings on expenditures because a ceiling on spending operates as a ceiling on the quantity of communication itself, limiting the issues discussed, the depth of treatment, and the audience reached. Disclosure and reporting survived under exacting scrutiny, and voluntary presidential public financing survived because a subsidy accepted on stated conditions differs from a prohibition imposed on every speaker. Equality of influence received an express rejection as a justification, a rejection that later majorities repeated whenever a legislature defended a restriction as a means of adjusting the relative strength of voices in public debate. Coordination supplied the hinge between the two categories. Spending arranged with a candidate functioned as a contribution, while spending undertaken independently of a candidate received the protection owed to direct expression. Every later dispute in the sequence presented a contest over classification, over the scope of the corruption interest, or over the fit between a chosen limit and that interest.

The corporate decisions tested whether the identity of a speaker could change the answer that the Buckley framework produced. A referendum decision in 1978 refused to sustain a ban grounded in corporate identity alone, a decision in 1986 protected a class of ideological nonprofit corporations from an expenditure ban, and a decision in 1990 sustained a state treasury ban on a rationale addressed to distortion produced by wealth accumulated through the corporate form. The high point of deference arrived in 2003, when McConnell v. Federal Election Commission sustained the soft money ban and the electioneering communication provisions of the 2002 act against facial attack on an extensive congressional record. The contraction that followed proceeded by stages. An as applied decision in 2007 narrowed the electioneering communication prohibition to advertisements susceptible of no reasonable interpretation apart from an appeal to vote. A decision in 2008 struck asymmetrical limit increases triggered by personal spending, and a decision in 2011 struck public matching funds triggered by the spending of an opponent. Between those decisions, the 2010 judgment in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission overruled the 1990 corporate precedent in relevant part and held that government may not prohibit independent expenditures because of the corporate or union identity of the spender, while sustaining disclaimer and disclosure duties by eight votes to one.

One correction of attribution governs the reading of the modern independent spending landscape, and the article treats that correction as a central claim rather than as a footnote. Two decisions, not one, built the independent spending landscape. Citizens United freed independent spending from a ban grounded in speaker identity, and SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission, a decision of the en banc Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, removed the contribution ceiling for donors who gave to committees that spend only independently. A committee able to accept amounts without a ceiling and to spend those amounts without candidate coordination emerged only from the combination, completed by administrative guidance in 2010. The doctrine ledger placed after the account of McConnell and Wisconsin Right to Life collects the sequence in findable form, giving each decision with its year, the provision at issue, the holding in one sentence, and the direction of the ruling measured by whether the space for regulation expanded or contracted. A reader who follows the ledger against the narrative sections can test every summary against the reasoning that produced the result, and can locate the precise point at which a rationale entered the doctrine, governed for a period, and in several instances left the doctrine through overruling or narrowing construction.

## The Foundation: Buckley v. Valeo Draws the Line

The Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1974 created the first comprehensive federal system for regulating money in elections for federal office, and the system rested on a legislative judgment that large financial contributions and large campaign expenditures both threatened the integrity of the electoral process. The statutory design reflected a decision to regulate money at nearly every point where money entered a campaign. Individual contributions to a candidate were capped at 1,000 dollars per election, and a separate provision imposed an annual aggregate ceiling of 25,000 dollars on the total contributions an individual could make during a calendar year. Candidates faced ceilings on campaign expenditures, with limits that varied by office and, for congressional races, by the voting age population of the state or district. A ceiling of 1,000 dollars applied to independent expenditures, defined in the statute by reference to expenditures made relative to a clearly identified candidate without cooperation or consultation with the candidate or an authorized committee. Limits also restricted the amounts a candidate could contribute or expend from personal funds and from funds of an immediate family. Disclosure and reporting provisions required political committees and candidates to register, to keep records, and to file reports identifying contributors and expenditures above stated thresholds, and the amendments established a system of public financing for presidential elections, including primary matching payments and general election grants funded through the income tax checkoff. A new agency, the Federal Election Commission, received authority to administer and enforce the scheme, and the composition of that agency became a separate constitutional question from the limits on money itself.

Opposition formed immediately upon enactment, and the litigation that reached the Supreme Court carried a breadth that shaped the character of the decision. The plaintiffs included Senator James L. Buckley of New York and former Senator Eugene McCarthy of Minnesota, joined by candidates, contributors, political committees, and organizations drawn from sharply different ideological camps. That coalition mattered because the challenge did not present a single partisan objection to regulation in general. Conservative and liberal plaintiffs joined in attacking provisions that restricted their respective activities, and the alignment demonstrated that the statute burdened speech and association across the political spectrum rather than within one faction alone. Congress had provided for expedited review, and the case moved through the lower courts on an accelerated schedule that reflected both the statutory timetable and the proximity of the 1976 election cycle. The Supreme Court issued a per curiam opinion on January 30, 1976, in Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1 (1976). The opinion proceeded provision by provision, sustained some parts of the scheme, invalidated others, and left Congress with a task of reconstruction during the same election year.

### What did Buckley v. Valeo decide, provision by provision?

Buckley v. Valeo sustained limits on contributions to candidates, disclosure and reporting, and presidential public financing, while striking ceilings on candidate expenditures, overall campaign expenditures, independent expenditures, and candidate personal spending, and invalidating the Federal Election Commission's composition under the Appointments Clause in the January 30, 1976 per curiam decision.

The contribution limits formed the first and most consequential sustained portion of the statute, and the reasoning for sustaining those limits set the terms for every provision that followed. The per curiam opinion treated a contribution to a candidate as a form of political expression and association, because a contribution served as a general expression of support for the candidate and the views the candidate represented, and because the act of contributing enabled like minded persons to pool resources in support of a common enterprise. At the same time, the Court held that a contribution involved a more limited measure of speech by the contributor. The size of a contribution conveyed only a rough indication of the intensity of support, and the message remained largely symbolic. The candidate, rather than the contributor, supplied the substantive communication to voters through speeches, advertising, and campaign organization financed in part by the funds received. For that reason, a ceiling on contributions imposed only a marginal restriction on the contributor's ability to engage in political communication, and the restriction left the contributor free to speak independently, to volunteer personal services, and to associate with others in support of a candidate in every manner apart from the size of a financial transfer. The distinction between a direct restriction on speech and a restriction on the symbolic act that accompanied a transfer of funds supplied the doctrinal basis for applying a less demanding form of review to contribution limits than to direct limits on expenditures.

The governmental interest offered to justify the contribution ceilings was the prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption, and the 1976 decision accepted that interest as sufficiently important to support the specific limits Congress had enacted. The opinion described the danger in concrete transactional terms. A large contribution, given to secure a political quid pro quo from an officeholder or a candidate, represented the central form of corruption the statute could constitutionally target, and public awareness of the opportunity for abuse carried a separate injury because the appearance of corruption undermined confidence in the system of representative government. The Court treated the aggregate ceiling in place in 1976 under the same anticorruption rationale and sustained that ceiling as a means of preventing evasion of the base limits on contributions to any one candidate, since a contributor otherwise could divide large sums among many committees that funneled support to the same candidate and thereby defeat the per election ceiling in practice. A later decision removed the aggregate ceiling, a development addressed in a subsequent portion of the article. The per curiam opinion also considered and rejected a broader justification grounded in equality. The concept that government could restrict the speech of some elements of society in order to enhance the relative voice of others was, in the language of the decision, wholly foreign to the First Amendment, and the opinion refused to permit Congress to equalize relative influence among citizens or groups as a basis for limiting campaign money. That rejection confined the permissible justification for contribution limits to corruption and its appearance, and the confinement shaped the analysis of the expenditure provisions, where the same anticorruption interest failed to carry the same weight.

Candidate expenditure ceilings received the opposite treatment, and the contrast between the two holdings created the central line of the case. The statute imposed ceilings on total expenditures by candidates for nomination and election, and the Court held those ceilings unconstitutional. The reasoning began from the function of money in modern campaign communication. Virtually every means of communicating ideas in a mass society required the expenditure of money, whether for newspaper advertising, broadcast time, printed material, staff, travel, or the rental of halls. A restriction on the amount of money a candidate or campaign could spend on communication therefore imposed a direct and substantial restraint on the quantity of political speech. Unlike a contribution limit, which left the speech itself to the candidate, an expenditure ceiling limited the number of issues discussed, the depth of exploration of those issues, and the size of the audience reached. Because the ceiling operated directly on communication, the per curiam opinion subjected the provision to the strictest level of review and found the anticorruption interest inadequate as applied to a candidate's own campaign spending. Spending by a campaign, coordinated through the candidate's own organization, did not present the same danger of a bargain between contributor and officeholder that justified contribution limits, because the funds expended represented the campaign's own effort to persuade voters rather than a transfer designed to obtain a reciprocal benefit. The equality interest again received rejection, and the decision refused to allow Congress to determine that a candidate had spent enough money and could therefore be barred from additional communication with voters.

Overall campaign expenditure ceilings, including limits placed on expenditures by political committees and other participants in support of candidates, fell under the same reasoning and met the same outcome. The Court treated the operative effect as a ceiling on the total communication a campaign could undertake during an election, and the justices concluded that such a ceiling could not survive exacting review merely because Congress sought to reduce the overall cost of elections or to place campaigns on a more equal financial footing. Reduction of expense, standing alone, did not constitute a constitutional justification for restricting speech, and the opinion emphasized that the First Amendment protected discussion of governmental affairs in a manner that precluded legislative judgments about the proper volume of campaign debate. After January 30, 1976, a candidate who could raise funds lawfully under the contribution limits could spend those funds without a federal ceiling on the total, under a system that regulated the sources and sizes of receipts rather than the volume of disbursements.

The ceiling of 1,000 dollars on independent expenditures received the most emphatic invalidation in the opinion, and the analysis of that provision clarified why coordination became the pivotal fact in campaign finance doctrine. The statute covered expenditures by individuals and groups made relative to a clearly identified candidate, and the provision restricted spending undertaken independently of the candidate rather than in concert with a campaign. The Court held that independent spending posed no corresponding danger of corruption because the absence of prearrangement and coordination undermined the value of the expenditure to the candidate and reduced the danger that the expenditure would become a quid pro quo. An independently financed advertisement might help a candidate, might duplicate a message the campaign preferred to present differently, or might appear at a time and in a form the campaign regarded as unhelpful, and the candidate exercised no control over the content or placement. The opinion also stressed the speech burden. Independent expenditures represented direct expression by the spender, not a symbolic transfer to another speaker, and a ceiling of 1,000 dollars in practical effect prevented most individuals and groups from placing advertisements in mass media or mounting an effective communication campaign of any scale. Advocacy of a political candidate and discussion of public issues stood at the core of First Amendment protection, and the per curiam opinion refused to sustain a provision that placed such a low ceiling on that activity in the absence of a corruption danger comparable to the danger presented by large direct contributions.

Limits on the personal funds of a candidate fell as well, and the holding extended the expenditure reasoning to a setting where the corruption rationale had even less application. The 1974 amendments restricted a candidate's use of personal and immediate family resources in seeking office, and the Court invalidated those restrictions. A candidate's expenditure of personal funds did not create a debt of obligation to an outside contributor, and the anticorruption interest that sustained limits on contributions from other persons therefore offered no support for restricting the candidate's own resources. The opinion also treated the provision as a direct restraint on the candidate's speech, because personal funds financed the candidate's own communication with voters and a ceiling on those funds operated as a ceiling on the quantity of that communication. An additional concern involved relative advantage. A restriction on personal spending burdened a candidate who relied on personal resources while leaving an opponent free to raise and spend funds from other sources under the surviving portions of the statute, and the Court refused to treat equalization of resources between candidates as a legitimate basis for the restraint.

Disclosure and reporting requirements survived, but the survival depended on a narrowing construction that confined the reach of the statute to communications susceptible of clear application. The amendments required registration by political committees, detailed recordkeeping, and periodic reports disclosing the identity of contributors above a threshold and the amounts and purposes of expenditures. The Court sustained those requirements under exacting scrutiny, a standard that required a substantial relation between the governmental interests asserted and the information required to be disclosed. Three interests supported the provisions. Disclosure provided voters with information about the sources of campaign money and the interests to which a candidate might feel a measure of obligation, and that information assisted evaluation of candidates and the messages candidates presented. Disclosure also deterred actual corruption and the appearance of corruption by exposing large contributions and expenditures to public examination, and the exposure reduced the opportunity for arrangements that depended on secrecy. Finally, reporting requirements supplied the data necessary to detect violations of the contribution limits that survived review, and enforcement of those limits depended on records of receipts and disbursements. Against those interests, the opinion weighed the burdens on association and preserved a route for a group to obtain an exemption from disclosure as applied upon a showing of a reasonable probability of harassment.

The narrowing construction for disclosure focused on the phrase that defined covered expenditures by reference to a clearly identified candidate, and the Court limited the relevant provisions to communications containing express advocacy of the election or defeat of such a candidate. Express advocacy in that construction meant explicit words of advocacy, such as language urging a vote for or against a named candidate, rather than discussion of issues that might influence an election without naming electoral action in direct terms. The limitation served administrability and clarity. Speakers, committees, and enforcement officials required a test that could be applied without prolonged inquiry into motive or context, and a construction that covered any communication influencing public debate about issues would have raised serious vagueness concerns and would have swept a large volume of discussion of public affairs into a registration and reporting regime designed for election campaigns. By confining the expenditure disclosure provisions in that manner, the decision preserved the disclosure system for core electoral activity while excluding issue discussion that lacked explicit electoral advocacy. Disclosure survived because the governmental interests met exacting scrutiny, and the express advocacy limitation prevented the sustained provisions from reaching speech beyond the construction the Court adopted.

Presidential public financing also survived, and the holding distinguished between a governmental decision to subsidize speech on stated conditions and a governmental decision to prohibit speech above a ceiling. The amendments offered public funds for presidential primary campaigns on a matching basis and public grants for presidential general election campaigns, financed through the voluntary checkoff on federal income tax returns. The Court sustained the system against challenges grounded in the First Amendment and in the equal protection component applied to federal action. Public financing, in the view taken by the per curiam opinion, facilitated and enlarged public discussion and participation in elections rather than restricting communication, because public money enabled candidates with limited access to private contributions to present their positions to voters. Participation in the system was voluntary. A candidate could decline public funds and raise private contributions under the contribution limits, or accept public funds and accept the spending conditions attached to acceptance. The Court sustained those attached conditions, including expenditure ceilings that applied only as a consequence of voluntary acceptance of a public grant for the general election and corresponding limits for candidates accepting primary matching payments. That distinction reconciled public financing with the invalidation of mandatory expenditure ceilings elsewhere in the same opinion. A ceiling imposed by law on every candidate restricted speech directly, whereas a ceiling accepted as the price of a subsidy represented a condition a candidate could avoid by forgoing the subsidy. The opinion also rejected a discrimination challenge to the eligibility thresholds for public funds, reasoning that Congress could condition funding on demonstrations of public support without funding every candidacy at an equal level.

The composition of the Federal Election Commission produced a separate invalidation grounded not in the First Amendment but in the separation of powers, and the holding demonstrated that the institutional design of enforcement carried constitutional weight independent of the substantive limits. The statute vested the Commission with a broad range of powers, including authority to require reports, to issue advisory opinions, to investigate possible violations, and to file civil enforcement actions. Commissioners were appointed through a divided process that placed a portion of the appointments in the hands of congressional officials, including the President pro tempore of the Senate and the Speaker of the House, subject in part to confirmation procedures. The Court held that officers exercising executive functions of that character had to be appointed in conformity with the Appointments Clause of Article II, which vested appointment of officers of the United States in the President with the advice and consent of the Senate, or, for inferior officers where Congress so provided, in the President alone, the courts, or the heads of departments. Congress and congressional officers could not appoint officials charged with executing the laws, because the legislative branch would thereby control administration assigned by the Constitution to the executive branch. The per curiam opinion therefore held the Commission as constituted incapable of exercising the enforcement powers conferred by the statute, while distinguishing functions of a more investigative or informational character that Congress could assign through other arrangements. The practical effect was a period of suspension for the enforcement provisions dependent on the Commission in its enacted form, and Congress responded in the Federal Election Campaign Act Amendments of 1976 by reconstituting the Commission with appointments made in conformity with the Appointments Clause. The substantive holdings on contributions, expenditures, disclosure, and public financing therefore took effect within a reconstructed administrative structure rather than the structure Congress had designed in 1974.

The doctrinal reasoning that connected the separate holdings rested on a single proposition about the relationship between money and communication, applied with different conclusions depending on the distance between the regulated act and speech by the person regulated. Restricting money that made communication possible restricted the communication itself when the money purchased the communication directly, and expenditure ceilings therefore fell under the strictest review the Court applied to restraints on political speech. A speaker prevented from spending above a fixed amount was prevented from speaking above a corresponding volume, and no independent message by another person cured the restraint. Contributions occupied a different position because the transfer itself expressed support in a symbolic and generalized manner, while the candidate's own speech carried the substantive message onward to voters. That one step of removal did not place contributions outside the First Amendment, and the opinion recognized both an expressive element and an associational element in the act of contributing. The removal did mean, however, that a limit on the size of a contribution regulated the act of support at a point where the impact on public discussion was more indirect, and where the danger of corruption arose directly from the transfer and the obligations a large transfer could create. The balance between those considerations permitted Congress to guard against quid pro quo arrangements through contribution ceilings that would have been impermissible as ceilings on the expenditures financing speech itself.

The consequence for the statute was immediate reconstruction. Congress rewrote the Federal Election Campaign Act around the surviving half of the 1974 design in the amendments of 1976, retaining contribution limits, disclosure and reporting, and presidential public financing, removing the mandatory expenditure ceilings the Court had struck, conforming definitions to the express advocacy construction, and reconstituting the Federal Election Commission in a form consistent with the Appointments Clause. Campaign practice adjusted to the same division. Fundraising under strict per election contribution limits became the central regulated activity for candidates and committees, while spending by campaigns faced no federal ceiling and independent spending by individuals and groups proceeded without the 1,000 dollar cap that Congress had enacted in 1974. The contribution and expenditure distinction drawn in Buckley v. Valeo therefore functioned both as a judgment about one statute and as the organizing principle for a field. Later cases in the sequence traced in the article applied the distinction, narrowed the anticorruption interest, and contested the classification of particular transfers and communications. Each development depended on the foundation laid on January 30, 1976, when the per curiam opinion sustained half of a comprehensive statute, invalidated the other half, and explained the difference in terms of the relationship between a transfer of funds, the speech that funds made possible, and the danger of corruption that a transfer could create.

## Two Levels of Review and One Accepted Interest

The per curiam decision in Buckley v. Valeo, decided on January 30, 1976, did more than uphold portions of the Federal Election Campaign Act and invalidate others. The justices constructed a framework that governed nearly every campaign finance restriction reaching a federal court during the following four and a half decades. That framework rested on a distinction between contributions and expenditures, on two different levels of judicial review attached to that distinction, and on a single governmental interest strong enough to sustain a restriction. A limit classified as a contribution limit entered analysis under a comparatively forgiving standard and stood a realistic chance of survival if a legislature had connected the limit to the prevention of corruption. A limit classified as an expenditure limit entered analysis under the most demanding standard in constitutional law and almost always failed. The early applications, from a California ballot measure case in 1981 through a Missouri contribution case in 2000 and a pair of Colorado party spending decisions in 1996 and 2001, tested each element of that structure and confirmed that the classification of money, rather than the amount, determined the outcome.

### Why do contribution limits survive challenges that expenditure limits fail?

Contribution limits survive because the Court treats giving money as a symbolic act that leaves independent speech open and requires only a closely drawn fit to a sufficiently important interest, while expenditure limits face strict scrutiny as direct restrictions on the quantity of political expression a speaker may produce during campaigns.

The distinction began with the nature of the act each form of regulation burdened. A contribution, in the reasoning adopted in 1976, served as a general expression of support for a candidate and the candidate's positions, but the size of a contribution communicated only the intensity of that support in the roughest terms. The expressive content of a five hundred dollar contribution and a five thousand dollar contribution differed far less than the dollar figures suggested, because the act of giving signaled allegiance rather than articulating an argument. A contribution limit therefore restricted what the majority regarded as a symbolic act at the margin of speech, and the burden fell lightly in one further respect. A person prevented from giving more than a set amount to a candidate remained entirely free to spend unlimited sums speaking independently about the election, to volunteer personal services, and to discuss public issues without restriction. The limit closed one channel of association with a candidate while leaving the field of independent expression open, and that combination shaped the standard of review that followed.

Expenditure limits stood on opposite footing in the same opinion. Spending money to publish an advertisement, print a pamphlet, or broadcast a message determined how much expression reached the public, and a ceiling on spending operated as a direct ceiling on the quantity of political communication. The 1976 decision treated a restriction on spending for communication as functionally equivalent to a restriction on speaking itself, because in a mass society nearly all effective political expression beyond a conversation required the expenditure of funds. An expenditure ceiling did not regulate conduct with an incidental effect on speech. The ceiling regulated the speech, measured in messages disseminated, audience reached, and frequency of repetition. Restrictions of that character attracted strict scrutiny, the standard under which a law survives only if the government demonstrates a compelling interest and a narrowly tailored fit. The expenditure provisions of the 1974 amendments, including ceilings on candidate spending, personal spending by candidates, and independent spending, fell under that burden in Buckley itself.

Contribution limits, by contrast, were measured against a lesser standard that the opinion described in terms of fit and importance rather than compulsion and necessity. A contribution limit survived if the limit was closely drawn to match a sufficiently important interest. Each element of that formulation did work. Sufficiently important demanded more than a legitimate or rational objective, and a legislature could not sustain a limit by pointing to administrative convenience or generalized good government. Closely drawn demanded a genuine connection between the ceiling chosen and the interest asserted, so that a limit set so low that it prevented candidates from mounting effective challenges, or drawn so indiscriminately that it burdened giving far removed from any plausible corruption risk, remained vulnerable. The standard nevertheless stopped well short of strict scrutiny. The government did not have to prove that the chosen ceiling was the least restrictive alternative. The space between closely drawn and narrowly tailored explains much of the field's history between 1976 and 2001, because contribution limits repeatedly survived inside that space while expenditure limits repeatedly failed outside it.

The difference in standards also reflected a difference in corruption risk. Money given directly to a candidate created the danger that animated the statutory scheme, because a candidate who received a large contribution knew the source, understood the favor banked, and held office with the power to repay. Money spent independently, without the candidate's knowledge or control, could influence an election just as powerfully, but the exchange element weakened or disappeared, because an independent spender could not hand anything to the candidate and any gratitude felt afterward lacked the character of a bargain struck in advance.

The single interest the 1976 decision accepted at full strength was the prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption, and the opinion defined the operative corruption in specific terms. The corruption that counted was quid pro quo corruption, meaning the exchange of contributions for official acts. The paradigm case involved a large contribution given or promised in return for a commitment, express or understood, that the officeholder would act in a particular way on a matter within official power. The Federal Election Campaign Act, in the majority's reading, responded to a documented record of precisely such arrangements and of pledges extracted in connection with very large gifts to candidates during the elections preceding the 1974 amendments. A contribution ceiling addressed that danger at the point of exchange. By capping the amount any one source could give, the law limited the size of the favor a candidate could owe and reduced both the temptation to sell an official act and the leverage a donor could acquire through generosity. Other asserted benefits of the statutory scheme, such as slowing the growth of campaign costs or reducing the time candidates spent raising funds, received acknowledgment in the opinion but carried no independent sustaining weight.

The appearance of corruption received treatment nearly equal to actual corruption, and the reasoning turned on public confidence in representative government. The 1976 opinion reasoned that a system in which large contributions appeared to purchase access or action would lose the confidence of citizens who lacked the means to give at that scale, and that the loss of confidence itself injured democratic government whether or not any particular official act had in fact been sold. The justices treated the perception of exchange as a harm to the legitimacy of elections and to the willingness of citizens to participate in a process widely believed to be for sale. That treatment had practical consequences for evidence. A government defending a contribution limit did not have to prove completed bargains involving named officeholders, because a record showing widespread belief in corrupt dealing, documented instances of suspicious giving, and the structural opportunity for exchange could together establish the appearance problem that justified preventive limits, provided the ceiling remained closely drawn to that purpose.

Against that one accepted interest stood a series of interests the justices rejected, and the rejections defined the doctrine as firmly as the acceptance. The most consequential rejection concerned equality. The government in Buckley defended portions of the statute partly on the ground that limiting spending and contributions could equalize the relative ability of citizens and groups to influence elections, giving quieter voices a fairer share of public attention. The majority refused that rationale in terms that later decisions quoted for decades, holding in paraphrase that the concept that government may restrict the speech of some elements of society in order to enhance the relative voice of others is wholly foreign to the First Amendment. The refusal was structural rather than incidental. The First Amendment, in that reasoning, protected a process of public debate in which the government could not adjust the volume of participants to achieve a preferred balance, and a legislature dissatisfied with the loudness of wealthy speakers could not answer by lowering a ceiling over those speakers. Equality of influence might describe an attractive political arrangement, but the interest could not sustain a restriction on expression, and every later attempt to reintroduce equalization under a different label faced the barrier erected in 1976.

A second rejected interest treated the reduction of money in politics as a good in itself. Legislatures periodically defended limits on the ground that campaigns cost too much, that fundraising consumed excessive time, or that the aggregate volume of spending degraded public debate. The framework built in 1976 gave that reasoning no independent purchase. Spending levels, standing alone, established neither corruption nor the appearance of corruption, and a limit aimed merely at shrinking the total quantity of campaign communication amounted to an expenditure restriction in search of a compelling interest it could not supply. Cost concerns could enter the analysis only insofar as the record connected large contributions to the exchange danger. The distinction mattered because it forced litigants and lawmakers to translate every justification into the language of quid pro quo exchange or its appearance, and arguments that could not survive translation, however widely shared as policy preferences, failed as constitutional justifications.

A third rejected interest involved the prevention of distortion said to arise from accumulated wealth deployed in the political arena. That rationale held that wealth aggregated in certain forms could drown out other voices and distort public debate away from any measure of genuine popular support, and the sentence that states its fate in the case law is that the antidistortion rationale was accepted once, in Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce in 1990, and repudiated in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission in 2010, with the full account of both decisions given in the corporate sections of this article. Within the early doctrine covered in this section, the point is narrower. Buckley supplied no basis for treating the distorting effect of wealth as a freestanding justification for limiting expression, and applications of the framework before 1990 proceeded without that rationale, resting outcomes on the presence or absence of a corrupt exchange instead.

Citizens Against Rent Control v. City of Berkeley, decided in 1981 and reported at 454 U.S. 290, supplied the cleanest early demonstration that the corruption interest was the whole engine of the doctrine. Berkeley had adopted an ordinance limiting contributions to committees formed to support or oppose ballot measures to two hundred fifty dollars. Ballot measure committees presented a setting in which the accepted interest could not operate, because a ballot measure is not a candidate. A measure holds no office, performs no official acts, grants no favors, and owes no debts that a contribution could call due. The electorate itself decided the question at the polls, and a contribution to a committee advocating an outcome created no opportunity for the exchange of money for official action that Buckley had identified as the danger justifying limits. The Court struck the ordinance down. Berkeley could argue that large contributions to ballot campaigns gave wealthy interests disproportionate influence over direct legislation, and that argument amounted to the equality and distortion reasoning the framework had already rejected. With corruption absent and only rejected interests remaining, nothing sustained the limit, and the case isolated the corruption interest as the doctrine's whole engine. Contribution limits survived because of the candidate, the office, and the bargain those elements made possible, and where no candidate stood at the center of the transaction, the justification collapsed.

Federal Election Commission v. National Conservative Political Action Committee, decided in 1985 and reported at 470 U.S. 480, applied the expenditure side of the framework to independent spending by political committees. The Presidential Election Campaign Fund Act provided public financing to eligible presidential candidates and, in the provision under challenge, made it a criminal offense for an independent political committee to spend more than one thousand dollars in support of a publicly financed candidate. The National Conservative Political Action Committee planned independent expenditures supporting the reelection of President Ronald Reagan in 1984 and challenged the cap before spending the money. The majority invalidated the provision. Independent expenditures, the opinion reasoned, did not present the danger of corruption that contribution limits addressed, because spending undertaken independently of a candidate, without prearrangement or coordination, could not be understood as a contribution to that candidate or as one side of a bargain. The government argued that large independent spending created at least the appearance of an obligation, since a candidate aware of the support would feel indebted to the spender. The justices rejected that extension. A presumption that independent spending corrupts, absent coordination with the candidate, swept too broadly and rested on speculation about gratitude rather than evidence of exchange, and the appearance rationale could not be stretched to cover spending the candidate neither requested nor controlled without swallowing the distinction Buckley had drawn. Dissenting justices would have credited the appearance concern more generously, but the majority answered that the remedy lay in coordination rules and disclosure requirements rather than in capping independent expression.

The two Colorado decisions translated that pattern into the law of political parties, and the pair must be read together because the second decision explained the limits of the first. In Federal Election Commission v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee, decided in 1996 and reported at 518 U.S. 604, the Colorado Republican Party had purchased radio advertisements attacking a Democratic congressional candidate before the Republican nominee for the seat had been selected, and the Federal Election Commission treated the spending as a coordinated party expenditure subject to statutory limits. The Court held that a political party, like any other speaker, retained the right to make independent expenditures, and that the government could not presume that party spending was coordinated merely because a party existed to support candidates of its own affiliation. The expenditure at issue, made before a nominee existed with whom coordination could plausibly occur, illustrated the point concretely. A party's speech did not lose protection simply because the party's entire purpose involved electing candidates, and the presumption of coordination, applied without evidence of an actual arrangement with a specific candidate, replicated the error rejected in the 1985 political committee case. Colorado I, as the decision came to be known, extended the independence principle into party law.

Federal Election Commission v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee returned to the Court five years afterward and produced the opposite outcome on different facts, in a decision decided in 2001 and reported at 533 U.S. 431, known as Colorado II. The question in the second case involved the constitutionality of limits on party spending coordinated with a candidate, challenged on its face. The majority sustained the limits. Coordinated party spending, the opinion held, functioned as a contribution in substance, because a party working in concert with a candidate spent money in ways the candidate directed, valued, and could treat as part of the campaign's own resources. Unlimited coordinated party spending would also create a conduit around the contribution limits imposed on individual donors. A wealthy supporter prevented from giving more than a set amount directly to a candidate could instead give a far larger sum to the party, with an understanding that the party would spend the money in coordination with that candidate, and the candidate contribution ceiling would become optional for any donor with a cooperative party committee nearby. The majority treated that circumvention danger as a corruption concern within the Buckley framework rather than as a separate regulatory convenience, and the closely drawn standard for contribution limits gave the limits room to survive. Dissenting justices objected that parties occupied a distinctive position in democratic politics, that party spending on behalf of a nominee was core associational activity, and that the majority had allowed a hypothetical evasion risk to justify limiting the speech of the organizations most central to electoral competition. The disagreement revealed the framework operating as designed, with the outcome turning entirely on classification.

Nixon v. Shrink Missouri Government PAC, decided in 2000 and reported at 528 U.S. 377, confirmed that the framework governed the states and clarified the evidence a government needed to defend contribution limits. Missouri voters had adopted contribution limits for state offices in 1994, setting ceilings that varied by office and that a political committee and a candidate challenged as too low and insufficiently supported by evidence of corruption in Missouri politics. The Court sustained the limits. Writing for the majority, Justice David Souter explained that Buckley had not demanded a record of proven quid pro quo bargains before a legislature could act, and that the quantum of evidence needed to support the appearance of corruption varied with the plausibility of the justification and the relative novelty of the problem addressed. The Missouri record contained evidence from the public debate surrounding the referendum, including statements about large contributions, special access, and the buying of influence, together with material drawn from the national experience that the 1976 decision had credited. That record sufficed, and the evidentiary burden on a state defending limits was therefore modest. The opinion also rejected the argument that the ceilings were so low as to fail the closely drawn requirement, noting that the limits resembled amounts sustained in Buckley itself and left candidates able to raise the funds necessary for competitive campaigns. Through the Fourteenth Amendment, the First Amendment framework of Buckley bound state legislatures just as the framework bound Congress. A state defending contribution limits after 2000 did not need trial proof of legislators exchanging votes for contributions, only a record from which the appearance of improper influence could reasonably be inferred, evaluated with the deference the lesser standard implied.

The coordination concept runs through each of these decisions as the hinge on which the doctrine swings. Spending coordinated with a candidate is treated as a contribution, subject to the lesser standard of review and to ceilings justified by the danger of exchange. Spending that is genuinely independent is protected expression, subject to strict scrutiny when limited and sustained by no corruption interest the courts have recognized in that setting. The classification inquiry asks whether the candidate and the spender acted together in producing the communication, through prearrangement, substantial discussion, shared information about campaign plans, or direction and control over content and placement. A spender who learned a campaign's strategy from public sources and independently decided to echo that strategy remained independent in the eyes of the law, while a spender who received nonpublic plans and timed an advertisement to fit those plans crossed into coordination and into the contribution category. Much of the litigation in the field between 1976 and 2001 reduced to that classification, because the classification selected the standard of review, and the standard usually selected the winner.

## The Corporate Line: Bellotti, the Nonprofit Exception, and Austin

The corporate cases did not arise in a statutory vacuum. Federal law had restricted organizational money in elections for decades before the Supreme Court addressed corporate speech directly, and state legislatures had built parallel regimes modeled on the federal example. The Tillman Act of 1907 barred corporations from making direct contributions in federal elections, a prohibition rooted in congressional concern about corporate treasuries entering candidate contests as organized blocs of capital. Four decades later, the Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 extended the federal restriction beyond contributions to independent expenditures by corporations and labor unions alike. The distinction mattered from the outset. A contribution transferred money to a candidate or committee, while an expenditure spent money independently to praise, criticize, or otherwise influence an electoral outcome. By covering both forms, federal law treated the corporate treasury itself as the object of regulation.

Congress paired the prohibitions with a designated alternative channel. Corporations and unions could establish, administer, and solicit contributions for separate segregated funds, commonly described as political action committees, financed through voluntary donations from a restricted class of contributors rather than from general treasury receipts. Treasury wealth, in the legislative account, reflected commercial success, government conferred advantages such as limited liability and perpetual life, and the pooled resources of shareholders and customers with divergent political views. Voluntary political funds, by contrast, aggregated money given for an avowed political purpose, marking a boundary between commercial accumulation and political choice. State statutes adopted the same architecture in varying forms, banning corporate expenditures in candidate elections, referendum campaigns, or both, and offering a segregated fund mechanism as the lawful route for corporate political spending. Those state laws supplied the test cases through which the Court confronted the corporate form.

First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti, decided in 1978, presented the question in the referendum setting. Massachusetts had prohibited corporations from spending money to influence a vote on a ballot question unless the subject materially affected corporate property, business, or assets. Several banks and business corporations wished to spend funds opposing a proposed constitutional amendment that would have authorized a graduated personal income tax. The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court sustained the statute, reasoning in part that corporate speech fell outside full First Amendment protection when the speech concerned matters beyond corporate business interests. The Supreme Court reversed by a five to four margin, in an opinion by Justice Powell, and rejected an approach that turned protection on the identity or legal form of the speaker.

The reasoning in Bellotti proceeded from the speech rather than from the speaker. The opinion treated the relevant inquiry as whether the expression addressed a matter of public concern and contributed to democratic deliberation, not whether a corporation, association, or natural person had uttered the expression. A referendum, the Court emphasized, submitted a question of governance directly to voters, and information and argument about taxation bore on the core function of self government. Corporate identity alone did not strip protection, because the value of speech in a public debate did not depend on the corporate character of the source. The majority also refused to sustain the ban on grounds offered by Massachusetts that corporate wealth might overwhelm public discussion or that shareholders required protection from management spending. The record, in the majority view, contained insufficient evidence of domination in referendum campaigns, and dissenting shareholders retained remedies under corporate law and through the political process itself.

Limits in Bellotti were as significant as the holding. The case concerned ballot questions rather than candidates, and the Court expressly reserved the candidate context for another occasion. That reservation preserved a doctrinal opening. Referendum speech lacked a candidate who could be corrupted or appear corrupted by large independent spending, and it lacked the party and campaign structures that had supported contribution regulation in earlier decisions. The opinion also acknowledged in passing that the government might possess stronger interests when corporate spending related to candidate elections, where the integrity of the electoral process and the prevention of quid pro quo arrangements presented different concerns. The tension between a speaker neutral principle and a candidate specific reservation defined the field when the nonprofit corporation arrived.

Federal Election Commission v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life, decided in 1986, tested the federal expenditure ban under the Federal Election Campaign Act. The statute, codified in the provision then numbered as section 441b, prohibited corporations from using general treasury funds for independent expenditures in federal candidate elections. Massachusetts Citizens for Life was a nonprofit corporation organized to promote respect for human life from conception, engaged in publishing and advocacy, and funded overwhelmingly by individual donations rather than by business corporations or unions. The organization had published a voting record edition of a newsletter identifying candidates and urging readers to vote pro-life, and the Federal Election Commission treated the publication as an expenditure subject to the ban. The organization challenged the statute as applied, arguing that a small ideological corporation dependent on individual supporters resembled a voluntary political association more than a commercial enterprise.

The Supreme Court agreed in an opinion by Justice Brennan. The Court held that the expenditure ban could not constitutionally apply to Massachusetts Citizens for Life and carved an exception defined by three characteristics drawn from the organization before the Court. The exempt corporation had been formed for the express purpose of promoting political ideas. The exempt corporation engaged in no business activities of the kind that generated commercial wealth. The exempt corporation accepted no funds from business corporations or labor unions, so that treasury money did not enter political spending through an ideological intermediary. Corporations meeting those conditions, the majority reasoned, were associations of individuals united by shared beliefs, and their independent expenditures posed neither the appearance nor the reality of corruption that justified regulation of commercial treasuries. The individuals who funded such an organization had chosen to associate for advocacy, and barring the corporate vehicle would burden the speech of those individuals without advancing the governmental interests behind the statute.

The exception threatened the coherence of the expenditure ban while leaving the ban formally intact. After Massachusetts Citizens for Life, the constitutionality of corporate expenditure regulation depended on a classification among corporations, sorted by purpose, activity, and funding source. A commercial corporation with vast treasury resources remained barred from independent candidate expenditures through general funds. An ideological nonprofit meeting the exception could spend without limit from its treasury, even though the legal form was identical in corporate law terms. The distinction rested on an empirical and moral judgment about the source of wealth and the reason for association, not on the content or effect of the speech itself. The decision also weakened any claim that the corporate form alone justified suppression, because the Court had protected corporate expenditures when the corporation looked sufficiently like a group of citizens. The ban survived, but the rationale for applying the ban to some corporations and not to others demanded a fuller defense than corruption prevention had supplied in the expenditure context.

### Why did the corporate form change the constitutional answer in Austin?

In Austin the corporate form mattered because the majority treated wealth accumulated through state conferred advantages as a distinct regulatory problem. The Court held that such aggregations could distort candidate elections without reflecting public support, and that a segregated fund requirement preserved a channel for corporate speech.

Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, decided in 1990, supplied that fuller defense. Michigan law, through the Michigan Campaign Finance Act, prohibited corporations from using general treasury funds to make independent expenditures in support of or in opposition to candidates for state office, while permitting expenditures from separate segregated funds. The Michigan Chamber of Commerce, a nonprofit corporation with more than eight thousand members, roughly three quarters of which were for-profit corporations, wished to spend general treasury money on a newspaper advertisement supporting a candidate for the Michigan House of Representatives. The Chamber did not qualify for the Massachusetts Citizens for Life exception because corporate members contributed to its treasury. The organization therefore mounted a facial and as applied challenge that forced the Court to decide whether the reasoning of Bellotti extended to candidate elections and whether a state could defend a corporate expenditure ban on grounds broader than quid pro quo corruption.

Justice Marshall wrote for a majority of six. The Court sustained the Michigan prohibition and declined to extend Bellotti to the candidate expenditure setting in the manner the Chamber proposed. The opinion accepted that independent expenditures in candidate elections implicated protected speech and that a ban on treasury spending burdened expression. The decisive move lay in identifying a governmental interest of sufficient weight and a fit between that interest and the corporate restriction. The majority named the interest in antidistortion terms. Michigan could act to prevent the corrosive and distorting effects of immense aggregations of wealth accumulated with the help of the corporate form and bearing little or no correlation to public support for the political ideas of the corporation. Aggregation described scale, corporate assistance described origin, and absence of correlation described a democratic defect.

The antidistortion rationale differed from the anticorruption rationale that had sustained contribution limits and some expenditure related rules in earlier cases. Corruption, in the conventional formulation, centered on exchanges between donors or spenders and officeholders, or on public confidence that such exchanges had not occurred. Antidistortion centered on the structure of public debate itself. A corporation could amass resources through limited liability, perpetual existence, favorable tax treatment, and the ability to accumulate capital from investors who sought economic return rather than political expression. Those resources could then finance candidate support on a scale unrelated to the number of citizens who endorsed the corporate position. In the majority account, a wealthy corporation might command attention in a campaign disproportionate to popular backing, and voters might hear a message amplified by legal advantages that had no bearing on persuasiveness.

The separate segregated fund option answered the claim of total suppression. Michigan had not silenced the Chamber or its members in the majority characterization. The Chamber could establish a segregated fund, solicit voluntary contributions designated for political use, and spend from that fund in candidate elections. The majority treated the fund requirement as a burden on the corporate treasury route, not as an elimination of corporate associated speech. Funds raised specifically for politics would also bear a closer correlation to political support than treasury balances accumulated through commerce, which in turn reduced the distortion the statute targeted. By channeling corporate political spending into voluntary contributions, Michigan preserved participation while disconnecting electoral influence from commercial accumulation.

Justice Scalia filed a dissenting opinion that rejected the antidistortion rationale as a license for government to manage public debate. In that account, the First Amendment forbade the state to decide that a speaker possessed too much influence or that an audience heard a viewpoint with excessive force. Equalization of relative voice, the dissent argued, was not a legitimate governmental purpose but the opposite of free expression, because robust debate permitted popular and unpopular speakers alike to seek persuasion without an official ceiling. The dissent questioned the factual premise as well, noting that corporations varied widely in size and resources, that many possessed modest means, and that wealthy individuals faced no comparable expenditure ban despite an equal capacity to amplify a message. A rule directed at corporate speakers, in the dissenting view, singled out a disfavored class of participants and invited legislatures to suppress associations through which citizens commonly organized economic and political activity. Justice Scalia would have extended Bellotti to candidate elections, treating the identity of the speaker as an impermissible basis for the Michigan ban and the segregated fund alternative as an inadequate substitute for treasury speech chosen by the organization itself.

Justice Kennedy filed a separate dissent, joined by other members of the minority, that pressed related objections through a different emphasis. The Kennedy dissent treated the Michigan statute as a direct restriction on political speech at the center of the First Amendment and argued that the majority had sustained a ban without the compelling justification and narrow tailoring that such a restriction required. The dissent disputed that distortion, as the majority defined the concept, described a cognizable harm distinct from the speech itself, and warned that the rationale would permit regulation whenever officials concluded that a speaker had gained an unfair advantage through lawful success or legal organization. The segregated fund route, in that reading, imposed administrative burdens, solicitation limits, and disclosure structures that made the alternative channel substantially less effective than the prohibited treasury expenditure. The dissenters also drew on Bellotti for a broader proposition that the government could not suppress speech because of disagreement with, or distrust of, a corporate speaker. Stated at the strongest fair version, the minority position held that voters, not legislatures, should discount corporate messages, and that campaign finance law could address bribery and circumvention without empowering the state to recalibrate the persuasive weight of independent speech.

The division in Austin therefore turned on competing accounts of equality and liberty in elections. The majority treated equality as a structural condition that a legislature could protect when a legal form generated influence detached from public support. The dissents treated equality imposed by government as a danger to liberty, because an official judgment about proper influence required an official judgment about speech. The disagreement concerned whether the source of aggregated wealth transformed protected expression into a regulable distortion. For the decade after 1990, the majority answer controlled corporate expenditure law in candidate elections. Legislatures could maintain treasury bans for corporations outside the Massachusetts Citizens for Life exception, provided a segregated fund channel existed.

Federal Election Commission v. Beaumont, decided in 2003, brought the corporate question back to the Court through contributions rather than expenditures. North Carolina Right to Life, a nonprofit advocacy corporation resembling the type protected for independent expenditures in Massachusetts Citizens for Life, challenged the federal ban on direct corporate contributions to candidates and related provisions governing segregated funds. The organization sought to contribute treasury funds to federal candidates. Federal law, continuing the line from the Tillman Act through the Federal Election Campaign Act, prohibited any corporation from making a direct contribution in connection with a federal election, without an exception for ideological nonprofits. The question was whether the reasoning that shielded such a corporation from an expenditure ban also shielded the corporation from a contribution ban. The Court, in an opinion by Justice Souter, answered no and sustained the contribution prohibition as applied.

The reasoning in Beaumont rested on the longstanding distinction between contributions and expenditures and on the deference accorded to contribution limits. A contribution, the Court recalled from earlier precedent, served as a symbolic expression of support and as a general expression of association, but a limit on contribution size imposed only a marginal restriction on speech because the contributor could communicate support in other ways and because the amount contributed communicated limited information beyond support itself. Contribution restrictions were therefore reviewed under a standard less demanding than the strict scrutiny applied to expenditure bans, asking whether the regulation was closely drawn to a sufficiently important governmental interest. Prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption satisfied that interest for corporate contributions, as did concern about circumvention of limits on individual contributions through corporate entities. The opinion traced an unbroken legislative and judicial acceptance of corporate contribution bans reaching back to 1907, and treated that history as evidence that the restriction addressed a recognized danger without suppressing political association. The availability of a political action committee preserved a route for the corporation and its supporters to contribute through voluntary funds, reinforcing the conclusion that the treasury ban burdened only the corporate form of giving.

The pairing of Austin and Beaumont revealed the contribution and expenditure distinction doing decisive work inside the corporate field itself. The same ideological nonprofit corporation could claim a constitutional exemption from an expenditure ban under Massachusetts Citizens for Life, yet possess no exemption from a contribution ban under Beaumont. An organization could spend unlimited treasury sums independently praising a candidate, subject to the contested expenditure line, while a direct treasury gift of modest size to the same candidate remained prohibited. The result appeared paradoxical if influence alone supplied the measure, because independent spending could exceed a contribution in value to a campaign. The doctrine explained the pairing through different governmental interests and different burdens. Contributions presented a risk of exchange and circumvention that justified deference and a categorical corporate rule. Expenditures presented a burden on direct public communication that demanded stronger justification, found for most corporations in Austin through antidistortion and withheld for qualifying nonprofits in Massachusetts Citizens for Life. Corporate status thus operated differently across the two channels, opening one door partway while holding the other closed.

Beaumont also clarified the limits of the nonprofit exception. Massachusetts Citizens for Life had not created a general constitutional status for ideological corporations that displaced campaign finance regulation across the board. The exception had addressed independent expenditures, where the speech burden was direct and the corruption rationale was attenuated for a corporation funded by individuals for advocacy. The exception did not transfer to contributions, where precedent, history, and the segregated fund alternative supported legislative authority even over the same class of organization. The Court in Beaumont declined to revisit Austin and declined to extend the nonprofit reasoning beyond the expenditure setting. That restraint left an uneasy equilibrium. Contribution law rested on deference and a history of sustained bans. Expenditure law for corporations rested on a single rationale announced in 1990, qualified by an exception from 1986, and distinguished from a referendum decision from 1978 that had rejected speaker based suppression in forceful language.

By 2003, the expenditure side of corporate doctrine rested on the antidistortion rationale alone, and the narrowness of that foundation set the terms for the 2010 decision treated later in the article on campaign finance. The Court had never applied antidistortion reasoning outside the corporate setting. No decision had held that the government could limit expenditures by a wealthy individual because personal wealth bore little correlation to public support, and no decision had treated distortion as a general justification for equalizing independent speech in elections. Yet the supporting structure contained visible strain. Bellotti had framed speaker identity as an impermissible ground for suppression in the referendum context. Massachusetts Citizens for Life had protected a class of corporations from the very ban Austin sustained for other corporations. The dissents in Austin had supplied a complete alternative framework, grounded in speaker neutrality and suspicion of equalization, that later litigants could invoke without constructing a new theory. A challenge presenting corporate independent expenditures in candidate elections directly, with a record framed around Bellotti and the Austin dissents, would force a choice between extending a corporate specific exception to equality reasoning and returning to the proposition that the speech, not the speaker, supplied the constitutional measure. That choice awaited resolution after 2003, on a doctrinal foundation narrower than the broader campaign finance edifice surrounding the corporate line.

## McConnell v. FEC: The high-water mark of deference

Congress enacted [the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002](/2021/09/15/bipartisan-campaign-reform-act-2002/), commonly identified with Senators John McCain and Russell Feingold, after years of hearings and investigation into the financing of federal elections during the 1990s. The law attacked two channels that reformers in Congress regarded as central to the system that had developed under the Federal Election Campaign Act. Title I addressed soft money, the unlimited contributions that individuals, corporations, and labor unions had directed to national political parties for purposes described as party building and generic advertising, even though the practical effect of such funds reached federal campaigns directly. Title II, through section 203, addressed electioneering communications financed from corporate and union general treasuries. The definition covered broadcast advertisements that named a federal candidate within thirty days of a primary election or within sixty days of a general election and that reached the relevant electorate.

Opposition formed before the statute took effect. A plaintiff group led by Senator Mitch McConnell, joined by parties, candidates, advocacy organizations, unions, business groups, and civil liberties organizations, filed challenges under the First Amendment. The breadth of the plaintiff group reflected the breadth of the statute. Provisions that restricted party fundraising affected party committees and donors, while provisions that restricted treasury funded broadcasting affected corporations and unions across the ideological spectrum. Because Congress had provided for expedited review, the cases proceeded before a three judge district court and then reached the Supreme Court on direct appeal during 2003. The Court confronted a fresh statute, an enormous legislative record, and challenges that asked for invalidation on the face of the law before enforcement experience had accumulated. Congress had assembled testimony describing how soft money solicitations operated, how access followed large donations, and how closing week advertisements named candidates while avoiding only the words of express advocacy.

### What did McConnell v. FEC sustain, and on what reasoning?

The Court sustained the ban on soft money contributions to national parties and sustained section 203 against facial challenge. The joint opinion treated large soft money donations as a source of undue influence and access, treated electioneering communications as functionally equivalent to express advocacy, and relied on Austin for the treasury restriction.

Justices John Paul Stevens and Sandra Day O'Connor delivered the joint opinion in McConnell v. Federal Election Commission, 540 U.S. 93 (2003), for the principal holdings. The opinion sustained Title I in its core applications. National party committees were barred from soliciting, receiving, directing, or spending soft money, and federal candidates and officeholders faced restrictions on soliciting such funds in defined circumstances. State and local parties faced related restrictions when activity affected federal elections. The reasoning began with classification. The joint opinion treated soft money donations to national parties as contributions for constitutional purposes, notwithstanding the argument that funds designated for nonfederal accounts or generic activity differed from direct contributions to a candidate. Contributions had long received less protection than independent expenditures under the framework of Buckley v. Valeo, because a contribution communicated general support through the act of giving while leaving the candidate or party to determine the message, and because limits on contributions left open alternative avenues of expression through volunteering and through contributions within permitted amounts.

The corruption rationale supplied the decisive step. Earlier doctrine had recognized prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption as interests sufficient to justify contribution limits, and narrower formulations had emphasized exchanges of money for official acts. The joint opinion read the interest more broadly. On the congressional record, large soft money donations created opportunities for undue influence and privileged access that carried the same dangers as more direct exchanges, even when no explicit promise accompanied a donation. An officeholder who solicited a very large donation for a party committee understood the donor's identity and the scale of the favor, and a donor at that scale obtained meetings and opportunities to present policy concerns that other constituents rarely obtained. The joint opinion treated the sale of access, and the public perception that access followed wealth, as forms of corruption within the meaning of the earlier cases rather than as extensions beyond those cases. Deference reinforced the analysis, on the ground that members of Congress possessed direct experience with the pressures of the soft money system and courts lacked comparable competence to second guess predictive judgments about how money influenced politics.

The evidentiary treatment mattered as much as the doctrinal language. The joint opinion declined to require proof of specific trades of donations for votes. Such a requirement, in the reasoning of the opinion, would have confined the corruption interest to conduct already punishable under bribery law and would have left Congress powerless against arrangements that stopped short of explicit agreements. Testimony describing the gratitude of recipients, the expectations of donors, and the fundraising role of officeholders sufficed to support the legislative conclusion that soft money threatened the integrity of federal elections. The appearance standard performed related work, since dependence on six figure and seven figure donations from a narrow class of contributors generated public cynicism that Congress could address without proof in individual transactions. Tailoring followed from the contribution framework, because hard money contributions remained available within limits.

Section 203 presented a different problem because the provision regulated spending on communications rather than contributions to an intermediary. The statute extended to corporations and labor unions a prohibition, long applied to express advocacy, on the use of general treasury funds for electioneering communications as defined by the timing, medium, and candidate reference criteria. Alternative structures softened the prohibition in ways the joint opinion treated as constitutionally significant. A corporation or union remained free to establish a separate segregated fund and finance covered communications through the fund, and the statute contained an exemption for certain nonprofit corporations that met criteria developed in Federal Election Commission v. Massachusetts Citizens for Life. The joint opinion therefore framed section 203 as a regulation of the source of funds for a defined category of broadcasting, not as a complete prohibition on the speech itself.

The functional equivalence holding supplied the bridge between the older express advocacy boundary and the new statutory category. Under doctrine that had developed after Buckley, the distinction between express advocacy and issue discussion had allowed speakers to finance candidate focused advertisements from unregulated funds by omitting words such as vote for or defeat. The joint opinion concluded that the distinction had proved unworkable in practice and that Congress could treat advertisements satisfying the electioneering communication definition as the functional equivalent of express advocacy for regulatory purposes. Evidence showed that strategists designed such advertisements to influence elections and that viewers understood the electoral purpose without explicit directives. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce supplied an additional ground. Austin had sustained a state prohibition on corporate treasury expenditures in candidate elections on the basis of an antidistortion interest, described as preventing the corrosive and distorting effects of immense aggregations of wealth accumulated through the corporate form and bearing little correlation to public support for the political ideas that corporations advanced. The joint opinion invoked that rationale to support section 203, and parallel reasoning addressed labor unions through the accumulated resources of union treasuries. The expenditure holding therefore rested on two foundations, one emphasizing equivalence with express advocacy and circumvention, the other emphasizing the distinctive character of treasury wealth.

Disclosure survived alongside the substantive restrictions. The joint opinion sustained the disclosure provisions attached to electioneering communications, treating disclosure as a measure that informed voters about the sources of campaign related messages, deterred corruption, and aided enforcement of the substantive limits, without imposing the same burdens as a prohibition. Compelled identification of sponsors and major funders was permissible in the electoral context, where interests in an informed electorate had supported disclosure requirements since Buckley, and thresholds confined the obligation to communications with an electoral character as Congress had defined that character.

The dissenting opinions stated the opposing position in several registers that shared a common charge. Justices William Rehnquist, Antonin Scalia, Anthony Kennedy, and Clarence Thomas, in varying combinations across the many opinions filed in the case, treated the statute as a broad suppression of political speech enacted by incumbents with an interest in muting criticism. Justice Kennedy disputed the evidentiary basis for the expanded corruption concept, arguing that the record demonstrated access and influence of a kind common to representative politics rather than corruption that the First Amendment permitted Congress to suppress, and that the majority had accepted anecdote and officeholder self description in place of proof. Justice Scalia characterized the statute as a restriction on the speech of disfavored speakers during the periods when speech about officeholders mattered most, and warned that deference to Congress on questions of political regulation inverted the judicial role where the regulators were also competitors in the elections being regulated. Justice Thomas maintained positions developed in earlier cases that would have subjected contribution and expenditure limits to more demanding review and that rejected much of the Buckley framework. The division reflected a deeper disagreement about institutional trust, between confidence in congressional fact finding about a system that members experienced daily and confidence in courts to guard political speech against legislative self interest.

A self limiting feature qualified the scale of the judgment. The Court had sustained section 203 against facial attack, a posture that required challengers to show that the provision lacked constitutional applications or that valid applications were insufficient under the standards governing facial challenges. Facial validity left as applied challenges open to speakers whose advertisements differed from the campaign advertisements that dominated the congressional record. The joint opinion itself acknowledged that genuine issue advertisements might fall outside the statute's permissible reach, and the opinion noted the possibility that application to a communication serving a legislative purpose unconnected to an election could exceed constitutional bounds. That acknowledgment preserved a path for later litigation without conceding that such applications undermined the statute on its face. The distinction between facial and as applied review thus became the mechanism through which McConnell could remain formally intact while its practical scope changed.

Four years separated the judgment in McConnell from the decision that exercised the opening the 2003 opinion had preserved. Federal Election Commission v. Wisconsin Right to Life, 551 U.S. 449 (2007), arose from advertisements prepared by Wisconsin Right to Life, a nonprofit corporation, during the blackout period preceding a Wisconsin primary in 2004. The advertisements addressed a filibuster in the United States Senate against judicial nominees, urged listeners to contact Senators Russell Feingold and Herb Kohl to oppose the filibuster, and named both senators. Senator Feingold stood for reelection in that cycle, so the advertisements named a federal candidate within the statutory window and satisfied the other elements of an electioneering communication. Wisconsin Right to Life had financed the advertisements from its general treasury and sought a declaration that application of section 203 violated the First Amendment. The organization characterized the advertisements as genuine issue advocacy about a pending legislative controversy, while the Commission characterized the advertisements as electoral advocacy subject to the prohibition sustained in McConnell.

Chief Justice John Roberts delivered the controlling opinion, joined in full by Justice Samuel Alito. The controlling opinion held section 203 unconstitutional as applied to the Wisconsin Right to Life advertisements because the advertisements were not express advocacy or the functional equivalent of express advocacy under a narrowed definition of equivalence. An advertisement is the equivalent of express advocacy only if the advertisement is susceptible of no reasonable interpretation other than as an appeal to vote for or against a specific candidate. An advertisement that could reasonably be understood as taking a position on a legislative issue, urging constituents to contact representatives, or commenting on official conduct fell outside the category that Congress could prohibit from treasury funding, even if the advertisement also carried electoral implications that a court might detect from context. The opinion instructed courts to err on the side of protecting speech in close cases and rejected inquiries into the intent of the speaker or the likely effect on an election as bases for expanding the prohibition. Extrinsic evidence about strategy or audience reception could not convert an advertisement susceptible of an issue based reading into an electoral appeal.

The facts of the filibuster advertisements illustrated the force of the new test. The advertisements named senators, yet the text urged contact with those senators about a vote in the chamber rather than a vote at the polls. A reasonable listener could understand the message as pressure directed at pending Senate action. Under the controlling opinion, that reasonable interpretation ended the inquiry. The existence of an additional possible interpretation, under which the advertisements might damage Senator Feingold's reelection prospects by associating the senator with obstruction of judicial confirmations, did not satisfy the standard. Chilling effects figured prominently in the reasoning. A speaker uncertain whether a court would later characterize an advertisement as the functional equivalent of express advocacy faced a choice between silence and the risk of enforcement, a burden that the controlling opinion treated as intolerable where an alternative reasonable interpretation existed.

Justice Scalia concurred in the judgment in an opinion joined by Justices Kennedy and Thomas, and the concurrence stated a position considerably broader than the controlling analysis. The concurring justices would have overruled the relevant part of McConnell outright and held section 203 unconstitutional insofar as the provision prohibited treasury funded electioneering communications. From that perspective, the as applied route produced an unstable body of law in which speakers gained protection advertisement by advertisement while the prohibition remained available against others. The concurrence rejected the premise that Austin and the functional equivalence reasoning of McConnell could sustain a prohibition keyed to candidate naming within fixed windows before elections. Five justices agreed that Wisconsin Right to Life deserved protection for the advertisements at issue, while only three expressed willingness to discard the McConnell holding on section 203 in full. The Chief Justice and Justice Alito chose narrowing through as applied adjudication over formal overruling, preserving the language of the 2003 judgment while altering outcomes in a central class of cases.

The practical effect followed directly from that alignment. The facial holding of McConnell survived the 2007 decision, and section 203 remained on the statute books as a prohibition that the Commission could enforce against advertisements meeting the narrowed equivalence standard. Applications contracted sharply within four years of the original judgment. Advertisements that named a candidate during a blackout window and that a reasonable audience could interpret as commentary on an issue, an appeal for constituent contact, or criticism of official performance gained constitutional protection for treasury financing, even where the timing coincided with a campaign and even where the speaker harbored electoral hopes. Enforcement consequently shifted from the broad statutory definition toward case specific adjudication under a standard designed to resolve doubt in favor of the speaker. The distance between the statute as sustained in 2003 and the statute as enforceable after 2007 measured the significance of the as applied reservation that the joint opinion had preserved.

Doctrinal tension after Wisconsin Right to Life extended beyond the electioneering communication definition. The controlling opinion rested on construction of the functional equivalent category and on principles governing vagueness and chilling effects, without repudiating the corruption rationale that had sustained Title I, which therefore retained the full force of the 2003 judgment after 2007. Section 203 occupied a weaker position. Its facial validity depended on reasoning that treated a broad statutory class as regulable, while its enforceable scope after 2007 depended on a test that protected much of that same class when a reasonable issue interpretation existed. That coexistence, possible under the distinction between facial and as applied challenges, placed pressure on the premises that had made facial validity plausible.

Austin occupied the most exposed position in the structure by the end of 2007. The antidistortion rationale had supplied an additional ground for sustaining the corporate and union treasury restriction in McConnell, independent of the equivalence analysis, and Austin had stood since 1990 as the principal precedent permitting government to limit corporate political expenditures because of the source of the funds rather than the content of a particular communication. Wisconsin Right to Life did not overrule Austin, and the controlling opinion avoided resting its judgment on a rejection of antidistortion reasoning. Exposure nevertheless followed from the logic of the 2007 decision. A test that protected treasury funded advertisements whenever a reasonable issue interpretation existed left little room for a rationale that would permit prohibition of the same advertisements because corporate wealth distorted debate. The concurring justices made the conflict explicit by urging abandonment of the McConnell section 203 holding, a step that would have required confronting Austin directly. Postponement therefore identified Austin as the load bearing precedent for the expenditure side of the 2003 decision, because the equivalence reasoning had contracted and the remaining justification for a broad treasury prohibition depended on acceptance of antidistortion as a legitimate governmental interest.

By the end of 2007 the doctrine stood at an unstable equilibrium. McConnell remained controlling precedent for the soft money prohibitions of Title I and for the proposition that Congress could legislate against influence and access purchased through large donations to parties. McConnell also remained precedent, in formal terms, for the facial validity of section 203. Wisconsin Right to Life governed the application of section 203 to advertisements susceptible of a reasonable issue based interpretation, and that governance removed a substantial share of real world broadcasting from the prohibition that the 2003 joint opinion had sustained. The reasoning that connected the formal holdings to the narrowed applications depended on distinctions between contributions and expenditures, between facial and as applied challenges, and between express advocacy and other forms of candidate related communication. A later case that questioned Austin, or that revisited the deference owed to congressional findings about corruption, would test the foundations on which the 2003 judgment had rested during the period when campaign finance doctrine deferred most fully to legislative judgment.

## Citizens United: The Ban Falls, Disclosure Stands

Citizens United was a nonprofit corporation organized for the promotion of conservative political views, and the dispute began with a film rather than with a conventional campaign advertisement. The organization produced a documentary that examined the record and public career of Senator Hillary Clinton in sharply critical terms, at a time when Clinton sought the Democratic presidential nomination. Citizens United planned to make the film available through video on demand during the period preceding several primary contests. The organization also prepared television advertisements that promoted the film by identifying its subject, indicating where the production could be seen, and conveying an unfavorable judgment about Clinton. Those plans drew the organization into a statutory regime that Congress had constructed for communications funded from corporate treasuries and broadcast close in time to a federal contest, and the resulting litigation placed two decades of precedent about corporate political spending directly before the Supreme Court.

The statutory obstacle was section 203 of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act. That provision extended the longstanding federal prohibition on corporate treasury spending in connection with federal contests to a defined category called electioneering communications. An electioneering communication was a broadcast, cable, or satellite communication that referred to a clearly identified candidate for federal office, reached a substantial audience within the relevant jurisdiction, and aired within thirty days before a primary or sixty days before a general contest. Corporate treasury funds could not pay for such a communication during those windows, although a corporation could use a separate segregated fund, commonly called a political action committee, financed by contributions solicited from a restricted class. Citizens United conceded that the film and the advertisements referred to Clinton, yet contended that the film did not amount to express advocacy and that section 203 could not constitutionally be applied to a work distributed through video on demand or to the advertisements promoting that work. A three judge district court ruled against the organization, relying substantially on McConnell v. Federal Election Commission, and Citizens United appealed to the Supreme Court.

The manner in which the case was decided altered its reach as decisively as the arguments first presented. The Supreme Court heard argument in March 2009, when the questions centered on narrower grounds, including whether the statute applied to a film offered through video on demand and whether any defect could be confined to nonprofit corporations funded in a particular way. On June 29, 2009, the Court set the case for reargument and directed the parties to address whether Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce and the relevant part of McConnell should be overruled. That order changed a dispute about statutory application into a reconsideration of the authority of government to prohibit corporate independent spending altogether. Reargument took place on September 9, 2009. The Court decided the case on January 21, 2010, as Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, reported at 558 U.S. 310.

### What did Citizens United actually decide, and what did it leave untouched?

Citizens United held that the federal government could not prohibit independent expenditures by corporations and unions for express advocacy or electioneering communications, overruled Austin and part of McConnell, and sustained disclaimer and disclosure duties by eight votes to one, while leaving direct contribution prohibitions, individual limits, coordinated spending rules, and foreign national questions untouched.

Justice Kennedy wrote the opinion for a five justice majority, and the central holding addressed independent expenditures rather than contributions made directly to a candidate or a campaign. The Court held that the First Amendment prohibited the government from suppressing political speech on the basis of the speaker's corporate identity. Under that principle, section 203 could not bar a corporation or a labor union from spending general treasury funds on communications that advocated the election or defeat of a candidate, provided that the spending was independent of the candidate. The holding reached two forms of communication that earlier doctrine and statutory drafting had often treated through separate categories. Express advocacy, meaning a communication that explicitly urged a vote for or against a candidate, received protection, and an electioneering communication that referred to a candidate within the statutory windows received protection as well when financed independently. The distinction between the two kinds of message therefore no longer supplied a basis for imposing the corporate treasury prohibition at issue once the communication fell within the statute only because of its timing, audience, and reference to a candidate.

The majority rested the expenditure holding principally on the limited conception of corruption that it drew from earlier campaign finance doctrine. Prevention of corruption or the appearance of corruption had long been accepted as a governmental interest capable of justifying some regulation of money in politics, but the opinion confined the operative concern to quid pro quo corruption, understood as a direct exchange of an official act for money or another thing of value. Independent expenditures, by definition, were made without coordination or consultation with a candidate or a campaign, and the majority reasoned that the absence of coordination also meant the absence of prearrangement and coordination of a kind that could constitute a quid pro quo. Because the spender and the candidate did not agree upon the communication, the spending did not place the candidate under an obligation created by an exchange, and the opinion treated any resulting political access or influence as a feature of representative politics rather than as corruption within the meaning that could justify suppression. The majority further held that independent spending did not give rise to the appearance of corruption on the reasoning offered, since the electorate could evaluate an independent message on its own terms when the source and the lack of candidate control were understood.

A second rationale for the ban received an extended answer. Austin had sustained a state prohibition on corporate independent expenditures in part through an antidistortion rationale, which described the concern that aggregations of wealth accumulated through the corporate form could distort political debate by giving corporate speakers influence disproportionate to public support for their views. The Citizens United majority rejected that rationale as an impermissible attempt by government to equalize the relative ability of speakers to participate in public discussion. The opinion stated that the First Amendment did not permit restrictions on speech because a speaker had accumulated sufficient resources to communicate effectively, and that government could not decide which voices were too powerful. The majority also treated the distinction between corporations and natural persons as an inadequate foundation for the prohibition, reasoning that a speaker did not lose protection because state law had conferred an organizational form, and that associations of individuals did not surrender speech rights by adopting the corporate structure through which publication, broadcasting, and advocacy were commonly conducted.

The Court next addressed an argument framed as protection for shareholders and, by extension, for persons whose funds a corporation or union might deploy for messages that those persons opposed. The majority rejected the contention that the prohibition was necessary to protect dissenting shareholders from compelled support of speech. The opinion answered that corporate democracy supplied the relevant protection, because shareholders dissatisfied with decisions about political spending could pursue remedies through internal governance and could dispose of their shares if disagreement persisted. A further answer distinguished the political action committee from a justification for banning treasury expenditures. A committee remained available as a separate vehicle, but the majority treated that alternative as a burden rather than as an equivalent channel, because formation and operation of a committee involved distinct solicitation limits, administrative obligations, and reporting duties. The opinion emphasized that restrictions on contributions stood on a different footing from a prohibition on expenditures. A corporation could be required to route a direct contribution through a committee, the reasoning ran, without that condition establishing authority to forbid the corporation from speaking independently with its own funds.

Those conclusions required a direct disposition of precedent. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, decided in 1990, had upheld a Michigan statute that prohibited corporations from using general treasury funds for independent expenditures supporting or opposing candidates, subject to a separate fund option. Citizens United overruled Austin. McConnell v. Federal Election Commission, decided in 2003, had upheld section 203 against a facial challenge, relying in relevant part on the reasoning of Austin. Citizens United overruled the part of McConnell that had sustained section 203 on that basis. The overruling was therefore partial as to McConnell, a decision that had addressed numerous provisions of the same statute. The majority also declined to resolve the controversy through the narrower as applied theories pressed at the first argument, holding that a ruling limited to video on demand distribution or to a particular type of nonprofit corporation would leave the prohibition in place for other speakers presenting the same constitutional question.

Separate from the ban, the Court sustained the disclaimer and disclosure requirements that applied to the film and its advertisements. That part of the decision commanded eight votes, with only Justice Thomas taking the contrary position on the disclosure questions. Sections 201 and 311 of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, as applied in the circumstances before the Court, required certain statements identifying responsibility for a communication and required reports that allowed the Federal Election Commission and the public to identify the spender and, subject to the statutory thresholds and terms, sources of funding connected to electioneering communications. The majority sustained those requirements under exacting scrutiny, a standard that demanded a substantial relation between the requirement and a sufficiently important governmental interest. The interest identified was informational. Disclosure allowed voters to evaluate a message in light of the identity of the speaker and the interests behind the expenditure, allowed shareholders and citizens to assess the conduct of corporations and officials, and supplied data that assisted enforcement of other valid rules. Knowledge of who paid for a communication, in the opinion's account, helped the electorate place the message in context rather than leaving voters to guess at the motives of an unnamed sponsor.

Precision about the disclosure holding matters because the opinion drew a sharp line between informing the electorate and capping or suppressing speech. A disclosure duty imposed no ceiling on the amount a corporation or union could spend, imposed no prohibition on the content of a communication, and did not require government permission before a message could be disseminated. The majority described disclosure as a less restrictive alternative to a ban, since the state could serve the informational interest by identifying the source of a communication instead of preventing the communication from reaching the public. The holding was expressly as applied to the film and to the advertisements in the case, and the Court found that the requirements satisfied the governing scrutiny on that record. Justice Thomas alone would have invalidated the disclosure provisions as applied, on the ground that compelled identification exposed donors and affiliated persons to a risk of retaliation, harassment, and reprisal that burdened anonymous political speech. The majority acknowledged that disclosure could be unconstitutional as applied in a case in which a group demonstrated a reasonable probability that identification would subject contributors to threats or reprisals, but it held that Citizens United had not made the showing necessary to defeat the requirements on the record presented.

The composition of the majority for the disclosure portion altered the alignment that had divided the Court on expenditures. Justices who dissented from the holding that invalidated the expenditure prohibition joined the judgment sustaining disclaimer and disclosure, so that ruling rested on a cross cutting eight justice agreement even as the central speech question produced a five to four division. That structure gave the decision two distinct propositions. The first denied government the power to forbid independent corporate and union expenditures on the grounds advanced. The second affirmed the power of government to require identification and reporting for covered communications under the scrutiny the opinion specified. The Court severed the fate of the ban from the fate of the reporting and disclaimer duties, preserving the latter as a response addressed to information rather than to suppression.

Several bodies of law remained outside the expenditure holding, and the opinion marked those boundaries with care. The federal prohibition on direct contributions by corporations to candidates stood undisturbed. A corporation could not, by virtue of Citizens United, transfer treasury funds directly to a federal candidate, and a labor union remained subject to the corresponding restriction on direct contributions from union funds. Contribution limits applicable to individuals also stood undisturbed, including the premise that a contribution, because it was given to a candidate or campaign and could be coordinated as part of a common enterprise, presented a corruption concern that an independent expenditure did not. The Court offered no holding that equated contributions with expenditures, and the opinion repeatedly used the difference between the two transactions to explain why a prohibition on one could fall while limits on the other remained governed by different precedents and different reasoning. The political action committee consequently retained a central lawful role as the vehicle through which corporations and unions could make permitted contributions, even though the committee could no longer be treated as the exclusive channel for independent speech.

Two further boundaries completed the reservation of questions. Coordinated spending remained regulable as a contribution. When an expenditure was made in coordination with a candidate or a campaign, the law could treat its value as a contribution to that candidate, subject to the limits and prohibitions applicable to contributions, because coordination removed the independence on which the holding depended. The opinion also expressly declined to decide the validity of the federal ban on contributions and expenditures by foreign nationals. The majority noted that the case did not require a ruling on whether government could bar foreign individuals or foreign entities from spending in connection with federal contests, and no part of the expenditure holding resolved that separate question. Those reservations meant that the reasoning about corporate identity could not be read, on the opinion's own terms, as a ruling for foreign speakers, nor could the treatment of independence be read as protection for spending arranged with a candidate. The boundaries were doctrinal rather than incidental, since each reservation tracked a feature, either coordination or foreign status, that the majority's stated rationale had not examined.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote a concurrence, joined by Justice Alito, that addressed stare decisis and the institutional legitimacy of overruling precedent. The concurrence accepted that fidelity to precedent promoted stability and predictability, yet maintained that precedent did not command adherence under the circumstances presented. Austin had been a departure from the principle that government could not restrict speech based on speaker identity, and a prior decision that was itself an anomaly did not acquire additional force merely through repetition. Austin and the relevant part of McConnell also had not generated reliance that counseled retention, because no legislature had structured a permissible system of public discourse in reliance on the authority to ban corporate speech. The concurrence further emphasized that the earlier rules had proved difficult to apply coherently, producing distinctions among corporations, media entities, and types of communication that lacked a stable foundation. On that account, retaining an erroneous departure would damage the Court's legitimacy more than correcting it, particularly when the precedent restricted a fundamental right.

Justice Scalia wrote a separate concurrence, joined by Justice Kennedy and in relevant part by Justice Thomas, that approached corporate speech through the original understanding of the First Amendment. The concurrence rejected the proposition that the amendment's protection had been understood at ratification to exclude corporations or other associations. Its account stressed that speech did not lose constitutional status because the speaker was an artificial entity formed by individuals for a common purpose, and that many influential forms of political expression in the founding era had issued from associations, publishers, and corporate bodies. The concurrence also answered the dissent's reliance on the absence of an early judicial decision invalidating a corporate expenditure ban, treating that silence as a weak basis for inferring a broad original power to suppress speech. In the concurrence's formulation, the burden rested on government to find an original basis for excluding corporate speakers, and no such basis had been shown.

Justice Stevens wrote the principal dissent, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor, and the dissent presented the contrary position across constitutional history, democratic structure, and the factual record. The dissent maintained that corporations were not members of the political community in the same sense as natural persons. A corporation was a creature of state law, endowed with perpetual life, limited liability, and an ability to accumulate capital that had no direct counterpart in the political rights or vulnerability of a human citizen. From that premise, the dissent argued that government could regulate corporate electoral spending without treating the corporation as indistinguishable from an individual voter, and that the majority had erred by moving from the protection of speech to a rule that disabled legislatures from addressing the distinctive features of corporate wealth and organization. The dissent did not deny that corporate communications could contribute information to public debate. Its objection centered on authority and consequence, namely whether the Constitution compelled invalidation of democratically enacted limits that legislatures had adopted to preserve the integrity of representative institutions against a form of spending the dissent regarded as capable of overwhelming candidate centered debate.

The dissent also charged the majority with disregarding a century of regulation and the evidentiary record that Congress had assembled. Federal law had restricted corporate contributions since the Tillman Act of 1907, federal law had restricted corporate and union expenditures since the middle of the twentieth century, and many states had maintained comparable rules, a pattern the dissent read as evidence of a sustained legislative judgment rather than an isolated experiment. McConnell had reviewed an extensive record concerning the influence of corporate and union spending, the role of electioneering communications in candidate contests, and the practical difficulty of distinguishing genuine issue discussion from campaign advocacy by timing and content alone. The dissent argued that the majority displaced that record through abstract reasoning about independence, understated the capacity of large expenditures to secure gratitude, access, and responsiveness from officeholders, and treated a lack of formal coordination as decisive even when a spender and a candidate shared interests and understood the value of the support. In the dissent's strongest terms, the ruling threatened public confidence in government by permitting corporate treasury wealth to dominate the channels through which candidates were presented to voters, while leaving citizens to suspect that official decisions answered to the largest spenders rather than to the electorate.

The dissent further disputed the majority's use of precedent and the breadth of its disposition. It maintained that Austin had correctly recognized a governmental interest in preventing corporate wealth from distorting electoral debate, that McConnell had applied settled principles after full consideration of the statute and its record, and that stare decisis carried particular force because legislatures and regulators had ordered their conduct under those decisions. The dissent also objected that the Court had reached the facial validity of the prohibition after Citizens United had framed a narrower challenge, describing that path as a departure from restraint in constitutional adjudication. On shareholder protection, the dissent answered that corporate democracy supplied an incomplete remedy, because shareholders faced collective action obstacles, limited information, and costs in attempting to change corporate policy, and because union members and investors could not always exit without economic sacrifice. Those objections gave the dissent a complete alternative account, grounded in institutional competence, legislative fact finding, and the distinctive legal status of corporations.

The scope of the majority holding extended well beyond the nonprofit corporation that brought the case. The reasoning was not confined to ideological corporations, to corporations with a limited number of shareholders, or to any category defined by a benign or educational mission. The opinion rested on a principle about speaker identity, namely that the government could not suppress speech because the speaker was a corporation, and that principle reached business corporations and labor unions alike. A for profit corporation with substantial treasury resources stood in the same constitutional position for independent expenditures as the nonprofit organization whose film generated the dispute, and a union spending from its treasury received the same treatment. Any contrary limitation would have required the government to classify corporations by purpose, wealth, tax status, or source of funds, an exercise the majority treated as inconsistent with the identity blind basis of the holding. The decision therefore changed the status of the federal prohibition in general terms, rather than creating an exemption for a sympathetic plaintiff.

The same constitutional logic reached state prohibitions modeled on the federal ban. A state could not retain a rule that prohibited corporations or unions from making independent expenditures in state contests on the rationale rejected in Citizens United, because the First Amendment applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment and the reasoning did not depend on a feature unique to federal law. State disclosure rules, by contrast, fell within the separate analysis that had sustained sections 201 and 311 as applied. After January 21, 2010, the framework thus distinguished among three forms of regulation. A ban on independent expenditures could not stand on the antidistortion rationale or on corporate identity alone. A limit on direct contributions remained governed by contribution doctrine. A disclosure requirement remained permissible when it satisfied exacting scrutiny, subject to an as applied challenge founded on a demonstrated probability of retaliation.

## Two Decisions, Not One: SpeechNow and the Making of the Super PAC

The January 2010 judgment in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission answered a question about spending. The Supreme Court held that the federal ban on corporate and union expenditures from general treasury funds, applied to communications that advocated the election or defeat of a candidate without coordinating with a campaign, violated the First Amendment. The holding concerned the identity of the speaker. A corporation or a labor union, the Court reasoned, did not lose protection for political speech because of an organizational form, and independent spending did not corrupt or create the appearance of corruption in the manner that a large contribution delivered directly to a candidate could. That reasoning removed a barrier at the point of expenditure and left a second barrier untouched.

The remaining barrier stood at the point of contribution. Federal law limited an individual contribution to a political committee to 5,000 dollars per year, and committees that accepted contributions were also subject to broader source and amount restrictions built for organizations connected to candidates. A group of individuals who wished to pool resources, place those pooled funds in a committee, and spend the total entirely on independent expenditures faced a practical difficulty created by that ceiling. Each participant could give only up to the statutory cap, even though the committee itself made no contribution to any candidate, transferred no money to any campaign, and coordinated no message with any candidate. The spending that the committee produced at the end of the chain was, under the Supreme Court premise announced in January, incapable of corrupting an officeholder, because the spending remained independent.

That mismatch rested on a distinction drawn decades earlier in Buckley v. Valeo, where the Supreme Court treated expenditure limits and contribution limits as different kinds of burdens calling for different levels of justification. Expenditure limits restricted the quantity of expression directly and received the most demanding scrutiny. Contribution limits restricted the contributor, left other avenues of expression open, and received a lesser though substantial review tied to the prevention of corruption and its appearance. The distinction survived in Citizens United and supplied both the opening and the limit for the argument that followed. If the anticorruption interest justified contribution limits because a contribution could become a conduit for influence over a candidate, then a contribution destined exclusively for independent activity presented a harder case for the government, because the destination severed the link between the donor and the candidate that made the interest plausible in the first place.

### Did Citizens United create super PACs?

No. Citizens United held that corporations and unions could spend treasury funds independently, but SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission removed the contribution cap for donors to committees that spend only independently, and the Federal Election Commission applied both rulings during 2010 to permit unlimited contributions to independent expenditure only committees.

The case that closed the gap was SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission, 599 F.3d 686, decided on March 26, 2010 by the en banc United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit. En banc meant that the full court heard the matter rather than a three judge panel, a procedure reserved for questions of exceptional importance or for reconsideration of circuit precedent. The judgment was unanimous, and Chief Judge Sentelle wrote the opinion. SpeechNow.org presented itself as an organization that wished to operate as an independent expenditure committee, accepting contributions from individuals and spending the funds on communications unconnected to any candidate campaign. Several individual plaintiffs wished to contribute amounts above the federal ceiling. The challenge therefore arrived in a concrete posture rather than as an abstract request for advice, with named donors, stated amounts, and a defined organizational purpose limiting every expenditure to independent activity.

The court decided the contribution question as an as applied challenge. That posture mattered to the scope of the ruling. The plaintiffs did not ask the court to invalidate contribution limits across federal election law, and the court did not do so. The holding reached contributions to an organization that makes only independent expenditures, on the facts and commitments presented. Within the defined setting, however, the court held the 5,000 dollar ceiling and related limits unconstitutional as applied. The narrow framing explains both the force and the boundary of the decision, and later administrative practice had to translate that bounded holding into registration rules for committees claiming the same posture.

The reasoning followed the Supreme Court premise step by step. Citizens United had held that independent expenditures, including expenditures financed from corporate general treasuries, did not corrupt or create the appearance of corruption. The District of Columbia Circuit treated that proposition as controlling beyond its original setting. If the expenditure at the end of the process carried no corrupting potential, then a contribution made for the sole purpose of funding such an expenditure carried no corrupting potential either. The government could identify no anticorruption interest served by limiting the contribution, because the contribution never reached a candidate, never passed through a campaign account, and never purchased access or favor from an officeholder through the channel that contribution doctrine had long treated as sensitive.

The court also confronted the argument that large contributions to an independent committee could corrupt indirectly, by producing expenditures that a candidate valued and that might generate gratitude or dependence. Citizens United had rejected a comparable argument about independent spending itself, holding that independent expenditures did not lead to corruption and that the electorate could evaluate the sources and messages placed before voters through disclosure. The court of appeals declined to revive through contribution doctrine a theory that the Supreme Court had set aside in expenditure doctrine, because the two inquiries collapsed into one once the destination of the funds was fixed. The opinion therefore rested on doctrinal consistency rather than on a fresh assessment of political practice, and that dependence on the January holding is precisely why the March decision cannot be understood as a freestanding development.

A second strand of the case concerned the organizational burdens that accompanied political committee status. SpeechNow challenged not only the contribution ceiling but also registration, reporting, and disclosure obligations imposed under federal law. The court sustained those obligations. The challenge to reporting failed, and the distinction between the two outcomes carried lasting significance for the committee form that developed afterward. Removal of the amount ceiling did not remove the committee from the regulatory system. A qualifying committee registered with the Federal Election Commission, organized its accounts, filed reports at the intervals the law prescribed, identified contributors in the manner the disclosure rules required, and placed disclaimers on communications where the law called for attribution. The decision separated permissible transparency requirements from impermissible amount limits, treating disclosure as a burden the government could justify on informational grounds even where a ceiling on giving lacked a corruption rationale.

That separation aligned with the treatment of disclosure in Citizens United itself, where the Supreme Court upheld disclaimer and disclosure provisions for electioneering communications by a substantial majority even as the Court invalidated the expenditure ban. Read together, the two decisions produced a recognizable architecture by the middle of 2010. Money could enter an independent expenditure only committee without an individual amount ceiling, money could leave that committee for independent communications without a source ban based on corporate or union identity, and the flow in both directions passed through a registration and reporting structure that placed the committee name, its officers, its receipts, and its disbursements into a public record. The architecture was permissive on amounts and restrictive on anonymity and coordination, and every element of that combination traced to a specific holding rather than to a single sweeping pronouncement.

Doctrine alone did not create a working committee form, because organizers required administrative confirmation of how the Federal Election Commission would register and regulate a committee built on the two holdings. During 2010 the Commission supplied that confirmation through advisory opinions, the mechanism by which the agency answers a specific requester about the application of the law to a specific factual plan. Advisory Opinion 2010-09, issued for the Club for Growth, and Advisory Opinion 2010-11, issued for Commonsense Ten, applied Citizens United and SpeechNow to committees that proposed to solicit and accept unlimited contributions for independent expenditures only.

The terms that the advisory opinions confirmed were specific. A committee registering as an independent expenditure only committee could accept contributions in amounts without the ceiling that SpeechNow had invalidated for that use, from individuals. It could also accept contributions from corporations and labor unions, a point that rested on the Citizens United holding about treasury funds rather than on the text of SpeechNow, which had concerned individuals pooling their own resources. In exchange, the committee accepted the full set of obligations that the court of appeals had left intact. Registration applied. Periodic reporting applied. Disclaimer duties applied. The prohibition on making contributions to federal candidates applied, and the prohibition on coordinating expenditures with a candidate or a campaign applied with equal force. A committee that crossed either line forfeited the premise on which unlimited receipts rested, because a contribution or a coordinated expenditure reestablished the candidate connection that both courts had treated as decisive.

The Supreme Court left the court of appeals ruling in place. In the related Keating proceedings, which carried a parallel challenge to the same contribution restrictions, the Supreme Court denied certiorari during 2010. The District of Columbia Circuit decision remained the governing appellate authority on contributions to independent expenditure only committees, no contrary circuit holding displaced the reasoning, and the Commission continued to administer registration on the foundation that the two advisory opinions had laid. Organizers, treasurers, and counsel therefore built the new committee form on a court of appeals holding plus agency guidance, a foundation one level below a Supreme Court judgment on the contribution question and firm enough in practice because no reviewing court disturbed the arrangement.

Against that record, the central correction of the section can be stated directly. Two decisions, not one, produced the committee form that public discussion calls the super PAC. The Supreme Court holding addressed who may spend, removing a ban that had rested on the corporate or union identity of the spender and on the treasury source of the funds. The court of appeals holding addressed who may give to a spender and in what amount, removing a ceiling that had rested on contribution doctrine. Neither holding, standing alone, yielded the complete form. Citizens United without SpeechNow left corporations and unions free to spend directly from treasuries but left individuals who preferred to act through a shared committee subject to the 5,000 dollar ceiling for gifts to that committee, a constraint that limited pooling and favored direct spenders over associated ones. SpeechNow without Citizens United left individuals free to give without a ceiling to independent committees but left the corporate and union source restrictions on spending in place, narrowing the range of contributors that the Commission later confirmed. The combination, ratified through the 2010 advisory opinions, produced a committee able to receive large amounts from several source categories and to spend those amounts independently.

Precision about the two operations prevents the most common factual error in public discussion of the field, which attributes the whole development to Citizens United alone. The error compresses an expenditure question and a contribution question into a single event and thereby misstates what the Supreme Court decided. The January opinion freed the spending. The March opinion freed the giving that funds the spending at scale. Speaker identity and donor amount were separate legal objects, governed by separate lines of precedent, challenged by separate plaintiffs, and resolved by separate courts at separate levels of the judicial system. A description that credits the Supreme Court with eliminating contribution limits misplaces the second holding by institution as well as by doctrine, assigning to nine justices a judgment that an en banc court of appeals delivered under the compulsion, as that court saw the matter, of the precedent the justices had set.

The distinction also clarifies why the resulting system permits some very large financial commitments while forbidding others of identical size. A donation of a given sum to a candidate campaign remained subject to the per election limits that Buckley had sustained and that later law adjusted, because the funds passed directly to a candidate. A contribution of the same sum to an independent expenditure only committee fell outside an amount ceiling after SpeechNow, because the funds could finance only messages produced without candidate control. The amount did not determine the rule. The channel determined the rule, because doctrine attached corruption risk to proximity between money and candidate. Observers who track only dollar figures miss the legal sorting that the two decisions performed, which was categorical rather than quantitative.

Terminology adds a further layer of imprecision that careful treatment can remove. Super PAC is a nickname that arose in political reporting and commentary during 2010 as shorthand for the new committee form. The phrase appears in no statute as a category of registration, and the Federal Election Commission does not register an organization under that label. The Commission term is independent expenditure only committee, a descriptive phrase that encodes the limitation that makes the unlimited receipts doctrinally coherent. The committee is defined by a negative commitment about every expenditure. Each disbursement for communications must be independent, and the organization makes no contributions to federal candidates at all. The nickname conveys scale and suggests a general upgrade over an ordinary political action committee, yet the legal reality is narrower and more conditional, resting on the surrender of the two activities, candidate contributions and coordinated spending, that connect a conventional committee to a campaign.

Disclosure practices follow from that registration posture. Donors to an independent expenditure only committee are not hidden by the SpeechNow holding, and the committee reports its receipts and disbursements to the Commission, which makes the filings public. A report identifies contributors above the statutory reporting thresholds and itemizes expenditures, vendors, dates, and purposes in the format the agency prescribes. The court of appeals expressly sustained the reporting framework challenged in the case, so an account that treats the committee form as a judicial creation of secrecy inverts the disclosure portion of the judgment. The doctrine permitted unlimited amounts inside a disclosed structure, and the limits of visibility in practice stemmed from surrounding rules rather than from the March holding.

The as applied character of SpeechNow also disciplines any account of corporate and union participation. The plaintiffs who obtained the judgment were an organization and individuals who wished to contribute personal funds above the ceiling. The opinion analyzed contributions by individuals to a group engaged solely in independent expenditures. Corporations and unions entered the completed committee form through a different legal door. Citizens United had held that the government could not suppress political speech on the basis of the speaker corporate identity, and the Commission applied that holding in the 2010 advisory opinions when it confirmed that an independent expenditure only committee could accept corporate and union contributions. Attributing the corporate contribution point to the text of SpeechNow misreads the opinion and obscures the two source structure of the regime, in which the individual contribution rule came from the court of appeals and the corporate and union source rule came from the Supreme Court as administered by the agency.

A related discipline concerns coordination, which functions as the doctrinal hinge of the entire arrangement. Independence in the governing cases was never a casual description of tone or sympathy. A communication coordinated with a candidate, produced at a campaign request or suggestion, or shaped through material involvement or substantial discussion with campaign representatives under the Commission standards, was treated in law as a contribution rather than as an independent expenditure. That treatment preserved a boundary that both decisions assumed. SpeechNow removed a ceiling because the funded activity stayed on the independent side of the line, and the Commission advisory opinions repeated the condition as a term of registration. Public discussion that describes a committee and a candidate as formally separate while describing shared strategy in the same account presents a tension that the doctrine itself recognizes, because the legal category depends on separation in decision making rather than on separation in political outlook or personnel history alone.

The two decision structure finally explains an asymmetry that runs through federal campaign finance doctrine after 2010. Unlimited money could enter the independent channel, through contributions that SpeechNow placed beyond amount limits and through treasury spending that Citizens United placed beyond source bans. The channel that reached candidates directly remained capped, through contribution limits that neither decision invalidated and that SpeechNow expressly left outside the scope of the as applied holding. A candidate received support in the form of independent messages financed at any scale, while a direct contribution to the same candidate campaign remained confined to a fraction of the amounts moving through the neighboring channel. That uneven pattern was not a legislative design enacted as a coherent whole after deliberation about the combined effect. No statute created the independent expenditure only committee as a finished institution. Courts produced the components sequentially, applying the Buckley distinction between contributions and expenditures to successive challenges, and the Commission assembled the components into a registrable form during a single year of advisory opinions. The architecture of the field after 2010 is therefore best read as an accretion of holdings, each bounded by the question presented, whose combined operation exceeded the design of any single opinion.

## The Limits Line: Floors, Triggers, and Aggregate Ceilings

The cases that followed Buckley v. Valeo tested contribution and expenditure doctrine at its edges. Buckley had sustained base contribution limits, struck most expenditure ceilings, upheld disclosure, and accepted voluntary public financing. The later disputes asked different questions. A legislature might set a contribution ceiling so low that the ceiling itself burdened communication, raise limits for one candidate because an opponent had spent personal wealth, release public money because an opponent had spoken, or cap what a donor gave in total across a two year period rather than to any single recipient. The line that emerged between 2006 and 2014 refined Buckley by identifying when a measure framed as a modest adjustment crossed into a restriction on political expression that the interest in preventing corruption or its appearance could not support.

### Can a contribution limit be unconstitutionally low?

Yes. In Randall v. Sorrell in 2006, a plurality of the Supreme Court held that Vermont contribution limits set at two hundred to four hundred dollars per election cycle were so low that they prevented candidates and contributors from engaging in effective political communication and therefore violated the First Amendment.

Randall v. Sorrell, 548 U.S. 230 (2006), arose from Vermont Act 64. Act 64 capped contributions at 400 dollars per two year election cycle for statewide candidates, 300 dollars for state senate candidates, and 200 dollars for state house candidates. The same caps applied to political parties. Act 64 counted certain volunteer expenses against the cap, so activity that in other systems remained outside the contribution definition consumed part of the small allowance the statute permitted. The statute made no adjustment for inflation, so the real value of each ceiling declined with each passing cycle. Act 64 also imposed candidate expenditure limits, restricting the total amount a candidate could spend during the cycle.

A plurality led by Justice Breyer disposed of the expenditure limits under Buckley, which had held that ceilings on candidate spending restrict the quantity of political communication and cannot be justified by an interest in equalizing resources among candidates. The contribution limits required a different analysis, because Buckley had sustained contribution ceilings of 1,000 dollars per election and had reasoned that such limits impose only a marginal restriction on expression while serving the interest in preventing quid pro quo corruption. The plurality in Randall nevertheless held the Vermont contribution limits unconstitutionally low, and the decision marked the first time the Court invalidated a contribution limit on that ground. Judicial deference to legislative choices about contribution levels therefore has a lower boundary, even though Buckley had emphasized legislative discretion to set amounts within a permissible range.

The plurality reached that conclusion through a comparative and functional inquiry rather than a single numerical test. The Vermont limits were substantially below contribution limits the Court had previously sustained, and the amounts applied across an entire cycle rather than per election, so a donor who gave the maximum early could give nothing further in the decisive contest. Binding political parties to the same figure as individuals denied candidates a source of organized support that had historically offset the advantages of incumbency and personal wealth. Counting certain volunteer expenses against the cap burdened grassroots campaigning and deterred the modest participation that low limits were said to encourage. The absence of inflation adjustment meant the burden would grow more severe in later cycles. Behind those factors lay a concern about challengers, who lacked the name recognition, donor networks, and record of incumbents and needed to spend heavily at the outset to become known to voters. The plurality reasoned that the limits threatened to prevent challengers from amassing the resources needed for effective advocacy, a burden disproportionate to any additional anticorruption benefit over higher amounts already sustained.

Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Scalia, concurred in the judgment on broader grounds. In that view, Buckley itself should be abandoned and all limits subjected to strict scrutiny, because the distinction Buckley drew between contributions and expenditures lacked a sound basis in the First Amendment. The concurrence would have resolved Randall without a special inquiry into whether a limit had fallen too low. The dissenters, Justice Stevens among them, would have deferred to the legislature. Vermont legislators and voters, on that account, had weighed the influence of money in a small state with inexpensive media markets and had concluded that modest campaigns remained feasible at the amounts chosen, and federal courts possessed no reliable scale for declaring one amount constitutional and a slightly smaller amount invalid. The holding in Randall establishes a floor without fixing its height. Some limit can be too low, and the inquiry is comparative and functional rather than numerical. Legislatures retained authority to set contribution amounts below federal levels, but that authority operated above an undefined minimum defined by whether candidates could mount effective campaigns under the ceiling chosen.

Davis v. Federal Election Commission, 554 U.S. 724 (2008), presented a different defect. The provision raised limits for one candidate in response to the personal spending of another. Section 319 of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act, known as the Millionaire's Amendment, addressed candidates who financed campaigns substantially from personal funds. When such a candidate spent more than 350,000 dollars of personal funds, the statute raised contribution limits for the opposing candidate, trebling them, and relaxed limits on coordinated party expenditures made on the opponent's behalf. Supporters of the design in Congress had described the mechanism as a response to the advantage Buckley conferred on wealthy candidates, whom no expenditure ceiling could restrain.

Justice Alito's majority opinion held the provision unconstitutional. The reasoning began with the right Buckley had recognized to spend personal funds without ceiling. A candidate who exercised that right under section 319 triggered a legal benefit for the opponent, and the majority described the resulting choice as a burden on the exercise of a constitutional right. The self financing candidate had to choose between unfettered speech and conferring a fundraising advantage on the opponent. Every dollar spent beyond the threshold made the opponent's campaign easier to finance under higher limits unavailable to the candidate who had triggered them. The burden operated through a penalty structure attached to protected activity, and the majority treated that structure as a substantial burden requiring a compelling justification. No sufficient justification existed, because the interest asserted amounted to leveling electoral opportunities. The majority answered that the interest in leveling electoral opportunities is not a legitimate, let alone compelling, justification. Buckley had rejected the premise that government may restrict the speech of some elements of society in order to enhance the relative voice of others, and Davis treated the Millionaire's Amendment as that premise in a new form. Personal spending created no risk that a candidate would exchange official acts for contributions from others, and disclosure addressed transparency without altering the opponent's limits. The asymmetry was the defect. Identical speech triggered unequal legal treatment depending on who funded it. Two candidates could address the same voters in the same election under sharply different contribution rules, and the difference depended entirely on the source of one candidate's resources.

Arizona Free Enterprise Club's Freedom Club PAC v. Bennett, 564 U.S. 721 (2011), extended the reasoning of Davis from asymmetrical limits to triggered public funds. Arizona's Citizens Clean Elections Act granted publicly financed candidates additional matching funds, up to a cap of roughly twice the initial grant, triggered by the spending of a privately financed opponent and by independent expenditures supporting that opponent. When the privately financed candidate spent beyond the initial grant amount, or when independent groups spent in support of that candidate, the publicly financed candidate received further state money.

Chief Justice Roberts's majority opinion held the trigger mechanism unconstitutional. The majority characterized the mechanism as a substantial burden on the speech of privately financed candidates and independent groups, because their spending generated subsidies to an opponent. A privately financed candidate deciding whether to spend beyond the grant level faced the knowledge that additional spending would deliver public money to the rival campaign. An independent group faced the same arithmetic. The majority reasoned that some speakers would spend less, delay spending, or structure activity to avoid the trigger, a hesitation imposed as the direct consequence of engaging in political expression. The majority held that neither the anticorruption interest nor a leveling rationale justified the trigger, because the matching mechanism operated precisely when a privately financed opponent spoke, and its function was to counteract the effect of that speech rather than to free the participating candidate from corrupting obligations. Public financing itself was expressly not called into question. Arizona remained free to fund participating candidates through grants fixed by criteria established in advance. Only financing keyed to an opponent's speech drew invalidation.

Justice Kagan's dissent, stated at full strength, answered that the scheme burdened no one's speech, restricted nothing, and simply funded more speech in response to speech. No Arizona law limited what a privately financed candidate could spend, what an independent group could say, or when either could act. The dissent emphasized that a speaker has no constitutional right to a favorable competitive environment or to the relative advantage that a fixed public grant would otherwise preserve. On the dissenting account, the majority transformed a decision to subsidize one speaker into a burden on another, a transformation that threatened public financing generally, because every public grant alters the competitive position of candidates who decline to participate. The dissent also defended the anticorruption rationale on broader terms, arguing that a system that keeps participating candidates from chasing large contributions serves the interest Buckley recognized even when the timing of additional funds follows opponent spending.

McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission, 572 U.S. 185 (2014), returned to contribution limits directly. Federal law imposed biennial aggregate ceilings on individual giving. For the 2013 to 2014 cycle, the aggregate ceiling stood at 123,200 dollars in total, divided between candidate contributions and party and committee contributions. A donor who respected every base limit, the ceiling on what a donor may give any single candidate or committee, could nevertheless violate federal law by giving the lawful maximum to too many recipients. The plaintiff in McCutcheon wished to contribute to additional candidates in amounts within the per recipient limits and challenged only the aggregate ceilings. Base limits were expressly left intact and were not challenged in the case.

A plurality opinion by Chief Justice Roberts, in a five to four judgment, struck the aggregate ceilings. The plurality reasoned that aggregate ceilings restrict how many candidates and causes a donor may support without adding meaningful protection against quid pro quo corruption beyond what base limits and disclosure already supply. A contribution within the base limit to a given candidate presents whatever risk of an improper exchange the base limit tolerates, and giving the same lawful amount to a sixteenth or seventeenth candidate creates no greater risk of corruption in any of those transactions. The aggregate ceiling therefore restricted expressive and associational activity while serving the recognized interest only marginally. Circumvention occupied much of the dispute. The government argued that without aggregate ceilings, donors could channel large sums toward a particular candidate through transfers among committees and contributions to intermediaries. The plurality answered that circumvention fears are answered by the base limits themselves and by transfer restrictions. Earmarking rules prohibited a donor from directing a contribution through an intermediary to a specific candidate beyond the base limit for that candidate, transfer rules constrained the movement of money within political networks, and disclosure exposed the pattern of a donor's giving. The plurality regarded scenarios in which a donor assembled influence through many individually lawful contributions as speculation about influence and access rather than evidence of quid pro quo exchanges.

Justice Thomas concurred in the judgment on the ground that all contribution limits should face strict scrutiny and Buckley's distinction should be discarded. That position treated the contribution as protected expression entitled to the same demanding review as a direct expenditure. The plurality did not adopt that position, and the judgment depended on the poor fit between aggregate ceilings and the anticorruption interest rather than on a reclassification of contributions generally. Justice Breyer's dissent, stated at full strength, read the corruption interest more broadly to include influence and access, and warned that the plurality's narrow definition dismantles the framework that keeps large money from dominating. The dissent described a system in which a single donor, freed from aggregate limits, could distribute very large total sums among officeholders who understood the source of the support, creating dependence even without an explicit exchange, a channel of influence that a definition confined to quid pro quo exchanges missed.

Continuity with Buckley framed the result. The 1976 aggregate ceiling sustained in Buckley was the ancestor of the ceiling struck in McCutcheon, and the plurality distinguished rather than overruled Buckley on the point. Buckley had considered a 25,000 dollar annual aggregate limit as a minor restraint serving to prevent evasion of the base limits, in a statutory environment with different transfer rules and disclosure practices. The plurality treated that assessment as a brief consideration rather than a holding immune to later examination, and reasoned that intervening changes in regulation had weakened the necessity rationale. The dissenters regarded the treatment as a substantial narrowing of precedent in practical effect. The plurality response pointed to the preservation of Buckley's central structure, in which base limits survived, the expenditure holdings stood untouched, disclosure remained approved, and the anticorruption interest remained the sole recognized basis for contribution regulation.

The broader debate surrounding these cases often compresses the doctrine into a slogan, and fair treatment requires that compression to be addressed directly. The common summary that the doctrine holds that money is speech misstates the reasoning in the opinions. No majority opinion in Buckley, Randall, Davis, Bennett, or McCutcheon held currency itself to be expression or equated a banknote with a pamphlet. The opinions' proposition is narrower. Restricting the resources that communication requires restricts the communication, and a ceiling on spending is a ceiling on the quantity of expression a speaker can produce. Political communication in a mass society requires printing, travel, staff, data, airtime, and digital distribution, each purchased with money. A law that forbids spending beyond a fixed sum does not ban an idea, but after the sum is exhausted the speaker cannot buy the advertisement, rent the hall, or distribute the mailing through which the idea reaches an audience. The doctrine treats that practical foreclosure as a restriction on expression subject to constitutional review, while treating a limit on the size of a contribution to another person's campaign as a restriction one step removed, because the contributor's own communication continues through other channels. That proposition has been applied to individuals, unions, media corporations, and advocacy groups alike, a neutrality across speaker categories that formed part of its defense within the opinions, since a principle protecting only speakers a legislature favored would hand government the power to select voices.

The serious critique, stated at full strength, questions the equivalence itself. Whether amplified reach purchased with wealth is the kind of expression the First Amendment values equally lies at the center of that critique, advanced by dissenting justices in the cases covered in this section and by legislators defending the statutes under review. A pamphlet and a saturation advertising campaign differ not merely in quantity, the critique maintains, but in the relationship between speaker and audience that each creates. Wealth converts into repetition, professional production, and access to channels that crowd out competing messages in a finite attention environment, so treating dollars spent as a neutral measure of expression protects a distribution of voice that tracks a distribution of property. The critique also questions the narrowness of the corruption definition. Influence and access, in that account, are the predictable return on large scale support, shaping which petitions receive a hearing without any exchange that a bribery concept captures. Dependence completes the argument. Candidates who require large sums to remain competitive organize careers around the preferences of those who can supply such sums, and that structural dependence distorts representation even when every transaction complies with base limits and disclosure rules.

The majority answers, presented with equal care in the opinions, rest on distrust of the regulatory alternative and on the availability of narrower tools. Disclosure serves as the less restrictive instrument in that answer. A regime that compels prompt reporting exposes relationships between donors and officeholders to voters, journalists, and opposing campaigns, allowing the political process itself to judge whether access has become obligation. The second answer concerns the danger of government deciding which speakers are too influential. A doctrine that permits restriction once expression reaches an undefined level of effectiveness requires officials, often incumbents with interests in the outcome, to set the point at which a challenger, a union, or an advocacy group has spoken enough. Majorities in Davis and Bennett treated the leveling rationale as that danger in concrete form, because each invalidated measure operated by improving the position of one side in direct response to the efforts of the other. The third answer returns to fit. Measures aimed at an exchange of official acts for contributions, enforced through base limits, bribery law, earmarking prohibitions, and transfer restrictions, address the harm the Constitution permits government to target without suppressing quantities of expression across an entire system. Neither line of argument eliminated the other during the period covered by these decisions, and the section leaves both standing. The dissents treat large scale spending as a condition government may moderate to protect the integrity of elections, while the majorities treat most attempts to moderate that spending as restrictions on expression that the recognized interest cannot justify.

The inventory the cases leave after McCutcheon defines the working system within which legislatures operated at the close of the period. Base contribution limits stand, subject after Randall to a floor against amounts so low that effective advocacy becomes impracticable. Disclosure and disclaimer requirements stand, repeatedly described in the opinions as the preferred instrument against hidden influence. The corporate contribution prohibition stands, untouched by challenges in this sequence that concerned expenditures, aggregates, and triggers rather than direct corporate contributions to candidates. Non triggered public financing stands, preserved expressly in Bennett and consistent with the voluntary system sustained in Buckley, provided grants rest on criteria fixed independently of an opponent's spending. Coordination rules stand, supplying the boundary between contributions subject to base limits and independent expenditures protected from ceilings, and supplying part of the plurality answer in McCutcheon to fears of circumvention. Against that standing inventory, the invalidated inventory is equally specific. Expenditure ceilings do not stand, a position fixed in Buckley and reaffirmed in Randall. Aggregate ceilings do not stand after McCutcheon. Triggered matching funds do not stand after Bennett, even within a public financing system whose basic grants remain permissible. Asymmetrical limit raising does not stand after Davis, and sub floor contribution caps do not stand after Randall. Each invalidated device remains in the case law as an example of a distinct failure, of fit in McCutcheon, of symmetry in Davis, of burden in Bennett, and of proportion in Randall.

## The Judicially Settled Shape of the Field

The decisions leave a field divided by classification rather than by amount. On the contribution side, regulation remains available under the standard Buckley v. Valeo announced, under which a limit survives when a legislature draws the limit closely to the prevention of corruption or the appearance of corruption understood as an exchange of money for official action. Base limits on what a donor may give to a single candidate, party, or committee stand on that foundation, and the corporate prohibition on direct contributions to candidates stands with those base limits. The contribution side carries two boundaries drawn by later litigation. Randall v. Sorrell placed a floor beneath legislative discretion, holding that a ceiling set so low that candidates and contributors cannot engage in effective communication fails the closely drawn standard. McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission removed the aggregate ceiling above the base limits, holding that a restriction on the number of recipients a donor may support adds no meaningful protection against an exchange once each individual contribution respects a lawful base amount. A legislature may therefore regulate the size of a single transfer within limits, between a floor fixed by effectiveness and a structure confined to individual transactions rather than to totals across a cycle.

On the expenditure side, prohibition has closed for independent spending by any speaker. A candidate faces no mandatory ceiling on campaign spending or on personal spending, an individual or group faces no ceiling on spending undertaken independently of a candidate, and a corporation or union faces no ban grounded in organizational identity or in the treasury source of the funds. Measures that burden independent spending indirectly through asymmetry have fared no better than direct ceilings. Davis v. Federal Election Commission struck limit increases that rewarded the opponent of a candidate who spent personal funds, and Arizona Free Enterprise Club's Freedom Club PAC v. Bennett struck matching funds released because an opponent or an independent group had spoken, while leaving public financing granted on criteria fixed in advance untouched. Disclosure and disclaimer stand apart from both sides of the divide as the instrument every majority in the sequence has sustained. Reporting of contributions and expenditures survived in 1976, disclaimer and disclosure for electioneering communications survived by eight votes to one in 2010, and registration and reporting for independent expenditure only committees survived in the court of appeals decision of the same year, in each instance under exacting scrutiny tied to the interest of an informed electorate and subject to relief upon a showing of a reasonable probability of retaliation.

Coordination supplies the classification on which outcomes turn across the whole structure. Spending arranged with a candidate, shaped through substantial discussion with a campaign, or produced under campaign direction counts as a contribution and enters the regulable category, while spending produced without that connection counts as independent expression and enters the protected category. The same communication can therefore change constitutional character according to the relationship between spender and candidate, and litigation in the field repeatedly reduces to evidence about that relationship. The attribution correction stated in the narrative sections deserves restatement in a single sentence. Two decisions, not one, built the independent spending landscape, because Citizens United freed the spending from a ban based on speaker identity and SpeechNow removed the ceiling on giving to committees that spend only independently. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did Buckley v. Valeo decide about campaign finance?

Buckley v. Valeo, decided on January 30, 1976, sustained limits on contributions to candidates, disclosure and reporting requirements, and presidential public financing, while striking ceilings on candidate expenditures, overall campaign expenditures, independent expenditures, and candidate personal spending. The per curiam opinion also invalidated the composition of the Federal Election Commission under the Appointments Clause. The reasoning distinguished a contribution, treated as a symbolic expression of support that creates a danger of an exchange of money for official action, from an expenditure, treated as direct communication whose quantity a ceiling restricts. That distinction between contributions and expenditures organized the holding and supplied the framework that governed later litigation over money in federal and state elections.

### Q: Why do contribution limits survive campaign finance challenges?

Contribution limits survive because the Supreme Court treats a contribution as a symbolic act of support rather than as substantive communication by the contributor. The candidate supplies the message to voters, and a ceiling on the size of a contribution imposes only a marginal restriction on the contributor, who remains free to speak independently, to volunteer services, and to associate with others. Under the standard drawn from Buckley v. Valeo, a contribution limit survives when the limit is closely drawn to a sufficiently important interest. Prevention of corruption and the appearance of corruption, understood as an exchange of contributions for official acts, supplies that interest. Expenditure limits face strict scrutiny instead, because a ceiling on spending restricts the quantity of expression directly.

### Q: What did Citizens United actually hold in campaign finance?

Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, decided on January 21, 2010, held that the federal government could not prohibit corporations and labor unions from spending general treasury funds on independent expenditures for express advocacy or electioneering communications. The majority reasoned that independent spending, made without coordination with a candidate, does not create the exchange of money for official action that defines corruption in the doctrine, and that government may not suppress speech because of the corporate identity of the speaker. The decision overruled Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce and the part of McConnell v. Federal Election Commission that had sustained the electioneering communication prohibition. Disclaimer and disclosure requirements survived by eight votes to one.

### Q: Did Citizens United create super PACs in campaign finance?

Citizens United alone did not create the committee form known as the super PAC. The 2010 Supreme Court decision freed corporations and unions to spend treasury funds on independent communications, but federal law continued to cap contributions to a political committee at 5,000 dollars per year for an individual donor. A committee that pooled large contributions and spent the pooled funds only on independent expenditures required a second ruling on the contribution side of the doctrine. SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission supplied that ruling in March 2010, and Federal Election Commission advisory opinions later in 2010 confirmed the registration of independent expenditure only committees. The completed form therefore rests on two decisions rather than on one judgment.

### Q: What did SpeechNow do to campaign finance?

SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission, 599 F.3d 686, decided on March 26, 2010 by the en banc Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, held federal contribution caps unconstitutional as applied to contributions given to a committee that makes only independent expenditures. The court reasoned that Citizens United had established that independent expenditures do not corrupt or create the appearance of corruption, and that a contribution funding only such expenditures presents no exchange danger that a ceiling could address. The court sustained registration, reporting, and disclosure obligations for such committees. The holding addressed contributions by individuals in an as applied posture and left contribution limits for money given to candidates and other committees intact.

### Q: Was disclosure upheld in campaign finance cases?

Disclosure received sustained support across the sequence of decisions. Buckley v. Valeo upheld registration and reporting requirements under exacting scrutiny, reasoning that disclosure informs voters about the sources of campaign money, deters corruption and the appearance of corruption, and supplies data needed to enforce contribution limits. Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission sustained disclaimer and disclosure provisions for electioneering communications by eight votes to one in 2010, with Justice Thomas alone dissenting on that part of the case. SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission likewise sustained reporting duties for independent expenditure only committees. The doctrine preserves relief from disclosure upon a showing of a reasonable probability that identification would subject contributors to threats, harassment, or reprisals.

### Q: What did McCutcheon change in campaign finance?

McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission, decided in 2014, struck the federal biennial aggregate ceilings that limited the total amount an individual could contribute across all candidates and committees during a two year cycle. The plurality reasoned that a contribution within the base limit for a single recipient presents whatever exchange risk the doctrine tolerates, and that giving lawful amounts to additional recipients creates no greater danger of an exchange of money for official action. Aggregate ceilings therefore restricted expression and association while adding little protection beyond base limits, earmarking rules, transfer restrictions, and disclosure. The decision left base limits on contributions to any single candidate or committee fully intact and undisturbed.

### Q: Can campaign finance limits be too low?

A contribution limit can be set so low that the limit violates the First Amendment. Randall v. Sorrell, decided in 2006, held Vermont contribution limits of 200 to 400 dollars per election cycle unconstitutionally low. A plurality reasoned that the amounts applied across an entire cycle, bound political parties to the same figures as individuals, counted certain volunteer expenses against the cap, lacked adjustment for inflation, and threatened to prevent challengers from amassing the resources required for effective advocacy. The decision established a floor beneath legislative discretion without fixing a numerical minimum. Legislatures retained authority to set contribution amounts, but that authority operates above a level at which candidates can mount competitive campaigns.

### Q: What did Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce hold?

Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce, decided in 1990, sustained a Michigan prohibition on the use of corporate general treasury funds for independent expenditures in candidate elections, while permitting spending from separate segregated funds. The majority identified an antidistortion interest, described as preventing the corrosive and distorting effects of immense aggregations of wealth accumulated with the help of the corporate form and bearing little correlation to public support for the political ideas a corporation advanced. The segregated fund channel answered the claim of complete suppression, because a corporation could solicit voluntary contributions designated for political use. Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission overruled Austin in 2010 and rejected the antidistortion rationale as an impermissible equalization of voices.

### Q: What did McConnell v. FEC decide about the McCain-Feingold law?

McConnell v. Federal Election Commission, decided in 2003, sustained the central provisions of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 against facial challenge. The joint opinion upheld the ban on soft money contributions to national political parties, treating large donations to parties as a source of undue influence, privileged access, and the appearance of corruption on the congressional record. The opinion also sustained section 203, which barred corporations and unions from financing electioneering communications with general treasury funds, treating such communications as the functional equivalent of express advocacy and relying on Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce for the treasury restriction. Disclosure provisions attached to electioneering communications survived as well.

### Q: Why did the Supreme Court order reargument in Citizens United?

The Supreme Court ordered reargument in Citizens United because the questions presented at the first argument concerned narrower matters, including application of the statute to a film distributed through video on demand and to a particular class of nonprofit corporation. On June 29, 2009, the Court set the case for reargument and directed the parties to brief whether Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce and the relevant part of McConnell v. Federal Election Commission should be overruled. That direction converted a dispute about statutory application into a reconsideration of governmental authority to prohibit corporate independent spending altogether. Reargument took place on September 9, 2009, and the Court decided the case on January 21, 2010.

### Q: What did the dissent in Citizens United argue?

Justice Stevens wrote the principal dissent in Citizens United, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, and Sotomayor. The dissent maintained that corporations are creatures of state law endowed with perpetual life, limited liability, and an ability to accumulate capital without a direct counterpart in the rights of natural persons, and that government may regulate corporate electoral spending on that basis. The dissent defended Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce and McConnell v. Federal Election Commission under stare decisis, credited the congressional record on influence and access, and argued that corporate democracy supplied incomplete protection for dissenting shareholders. The dissent also objected that the majority reached facial invalidation after the challenger had framed a narrower as applied case.

### Q: Did Citizens United change the limits on contributions to candidates?

Citizens United did not change the limits on contributions to candidates. The holding addressed independent expenditures, meaning spending on communications made without coordination with a candidate or a campaign. The federal prohibition on direct contributions by corporations to candidates remained undisturbed, and contribution limits applicable to individuals likewise remained governed by the framework of Buckley v. Valeo, under which a limit survives when the limit is closely drawn to the prevention of corruption or the appearance of corruption. The opinion repeatedly relied on the difference between a contribution, which passes funds to a candidate and can form part of an exchange, and an independent expenditure, which finances communication that the candidate neither requested nor controlled.

### Q: What is the difference between a contribution and an independent expenditure in the cases?

A contribution transfers money or something of value to a candidate, a party, or a committee, and the recipient controls the use of the funds. An independent expenditure spends money on a communication that advocates the election or defeat of a candidate without coordination, prearrangement, or consultation with the candidate or the campaign. The distinction controls the standard of review. Contribution limits receive review under a closely drawn standard tied to corruption and its appearance. Limits on independent expenditures receive strict scrutiny and have failed because independence removes the prearrangement that could turn spending into an exchange. Spending coordinated with a candidate loses independent status and counts in law as a contribution.

### Q: How did Arizona Free Enterprise Club v. Bennett change public financing?

Arizona Free Enterprise Club's Freedom Club PAC v. Bennett, decided in 2011, invalidated the trigger mechanism of the Arizona Citizens Clean Elections Act, under which a publicly financed candidate received additional matching funds when a privately financed opponent spent beyond the initial grant amount or when independent groups spent in support of that opponent. The majority held that the mechanism burdened speech because spending by one side generated a subsidy for the other side. The decision expressly left public financing itself intact. A government may fund participating candidates through grants fixed by criteria established in advance, provided the amount of a grant does not rise or fall in response to the speech of an opponent.

### Q: What did Davis v. FEC hold about self-funded candidates?

Davis v. Federal Election Commission, decided in 2008, held the Millionaire's Amendment unconstitutional. Section 319 of the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act raised contribution limits for a candidate whose opponent financed a campaign substantially from personal funds beyond a threshold amount, and relaxed limits on coordinated party expenditures for the opponent. The majority reasoned that a candidate possesses a right to spend personal funds without a ceiling under Buckley v. Valeo, and that a provision penalizing the exercise of that right by conferring a fundraising advantage on an opponent imposes a substantial burden. Leveling electoral opportunities supplied no sufficient justification, because government may not restrict the speech of some participants in order to enhance the relative voice of others.

### Q: What standard of review applies to campaign finance laws?

The standard of review depends on the classification of the regulated act. Contribution limits receive review under a standard that asks whether the limit is closely drawn to a sufficiently important governmental interest, a formulation less demanding than strict scrutiny because a contribution expresses support symbolically while the candidate supplies the substantive message. Expenditure limits receive strict scrutiny, under which government must demonstrate a compelling interest and a narrowly tailored fit, because a ceiling on spending restricts the quantity of communication directly. Disclosure and disclaimer requirements receive exacting scrutiny, which demands a substantial relation between the requirement and a sufficiently important interest such as informing the electorate. Classification therefore determines the test, and the test usually determines the outcome.

### Q: Are corporate contributions to candidates banned after Citizens United?

The federal ban on corporate contributions to candidates survived Citizens United without disturbance. Federal law has prohibited corporations from making direct contributions in connection with federal elections since the Tillman Act of 1907, and Federal Election Commission v. Beaumont sustained that prohibition in 2003 even as applied to a nonprofit advocacy corporation. Citizens United addressed independent expenditures financed from corporate treasuries and expressly distinguished contributions, which pass funds to a candidate and present the exchange concern that justifies regulation. A corporation may contribute through a separate segregated fund financed by voluntary contributions from a restricted class, and a corporation may spend treasury funds independently, but a direct treasury contribution to a federal candidate remains prohibited.

### Q: How did Buckley v. Valeo treat presidential public financing?

Buckley v. Valeo sustained the presidential public financing system created by the 1974 amendments, which offered matching payments for primary campaigns and grants for general election campaigns financed through the income tax checkoff. The per curiam opinion reasoned that public financing facilitates and enlarges public discussion rather than restricting communication, because public funds enable candidates with limited access to private contributions to present positions to voters. Participation was voluntary, and conditions attached to acceptance of funds, including expenditure ceilings that applied only as a consequence of accepting a grant, survived for that reason. A mandatory ceiling imposed on every candidate differed in kind from a ceiling accepted as the price of a subsidy that a candidate could decline.

### Q: What should a student know about campaign finance cases for an exam?

A student preparing on the campaign finance cases benefits from organizing the material around classification. Contribution limits receive deferential review under a closely drawn standard and survive on the anticorruption interest, subject to the floor established in Randall v. Sorrell and the removal of aggregate ceilings in McCutcheon v. Federal Election Commission. Independent expenditure bans receive strict scrutiny and fail, including bans grounded in corporate identity after Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission. SpeechNow.org v. Federal Election Commission completes the account of super PACs on the contribution side. Disclosure survives under exacting scrutiny, coordination converts spending into a contribution, and Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce supplies the overruled rationale that explains the structure of the modern doctrine.
