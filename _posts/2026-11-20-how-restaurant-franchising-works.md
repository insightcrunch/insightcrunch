A restaurant franchise works by licensing a proven brand and operating system to an independent owner who funds, staffs, and runs a single store under rules set by the brand owner in exchange for an initial fee and continuing payments tied to sales. A restaurant franchise is a rental agreement for a business format in which the franchisor collects revenue-linked rent on its brand while the franchisee supplies nearly all of the capital and absorbs nearly all of the operating risk, and the arrangement holds together only while the operator residual margin stays positive. That residual, the amount left after food, labor, rent, royalties, and advertising contributions are paid from gross sales, decides whether a store renews, sells, or closes, regardless of how busy the dining room appears on a Friday evening.

---

## A Brand Rented by the Store

The structure separates ownership into two layers that outsiders often confuse. The corporation at the top owns trademarks, recipes, training systems, and design standards, yet owns few of the kitchens where meals are prepared. The operator at street level owns or leases the equipment, hires the crew, orders the ingredients, and signs the loan documents, yet owns none of the marks on the sign. Each side holds something the other side lacks, and the franchise agreement is the contract that prices that exchange over a term that commonly runs ten to twenty years.

![Franchise restaurant kitchen line and counter during a lunch service - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-38.webp)

A single fried chicken restaurant in a mid-sized American city shows the outline in concrete form. The building sits on a corner lot with a drive-through lane, illuminated menu boards, and seating for sixty guests. Cars arrive steadily at lunch, the fryers run in timed batches, and the register records several thousand dollars in sales on a strong day. Behind that visible activity sits a stack of obligations invisible to customers. The operator paid a fee before the doors opened, borrowed to build the kitchen to a franchisor blueprint, and agreed to remit a percentage of every dollar rung up at the counter, whether the month ends in profit or in loss.

The same outline repeats across burger, pizza, sandwich, coffee, and chicken concepts, with variations in rent and supply arrangements layered on top. What remains constant is the direction of risk. Demand falls first on the store. A road closure, a wage increase, a spike in cooking oil prices, or a new competitor across the street reduces the operator margin before it touches the franchisor royalty stream, because royalties are calculated before store profit is known. The franchisor feels a downturn later and more gently, through slower unit growth, weaker renewal rates, and occasional store closures that shrink the royalty base.

Understanding franchising therefore begins with cash flow rather than with menus. The menu is the public face, the part a customer can judge in minutes. The contract is the private engine, the part that determines who gets paid first, who decides how the kitchen is laid out, who sets the price of a combo meal within legal limits, and who may sell the store when the owner wishes to exit. Read in that order, the system stops looking like a partnership of equals and starts looking like what the thesis describes, a tenancy in a commercial format, with rent collected as a share of revenue and with the tenant funding the property improvements.

That reading also explains why franchising expands so efficiently in strong periods. A brand can add fifty stores without borrowing to build fifty kitchens, because fifty operators supply the capital and recruit the labor. It also explains why disputes cluster around the same points in weak periods. When the residual narrows, every percentage point taken from the top of the sales line feels larger, every required remodel feels heavier, and every rule in the operations manual is weighed against the cash left at month end.

## What a Franchise Actually Sells

A franchise sells permission, and the precision of that word matters. The operator receives a licence to use trademarks, trade dress, logos, uniforms, signage, and product names for a defined term inside a defined place, under conditions the franchisor may enforce through inspection and, in serious cases, termination. The licence is not a transfer of ownership. At the end of the term, or upon a lawful termination, the right to display the marks ends, the signs come down, and the store must be de-identified so that no customer mistakes an independent restaurant for a member of the chain.

Alongside the marks comes a format, a complete method for producing a consistent result in thousands of separate kitchens. The format covers recipes and portion weights, cooking times and holding times, equipment specifications, store layout, opening and closing procedures, cleaning schedules, and service scripts. Ray Kroc described the McDonald system in Grinding It Out, published in 1977, as a discipline in which individual stores followed shared procedures for fries, shakes, and grill work so that a meal in one town matched a meal in another. The format is the real product, because a trademark without a method behind it would promise a consistency the stores could not deliver.

Training and support form the third element of the sale. Most systems run an initial training program for owners and managers, supply an operations manual that may run to hundreds of pages, assign field consultants who visit stores and score them against brand standards, and operate a marketing program funded by contributions from the whole network. The franchisor sells know-how packaged as a system, then charges continuing fees for continued access to that system.

Equally instructive is the list of items that a franchise does not sell. The operator does not buy the business as a going concern with goodwill that belongs to the operator alone, because much of the goodwill attaches to the brand and reverts to the network when the licence ends. The operator does not buy customers, since no franchisor can guarantee traffic, and the disclosure rules forbid earnings promises outside a narrow, regulated channel. In most systems the operator also does not buy real estate from the franchisor, although a significant minority of systems, examined in the section on land and leases, place the franchisor in the landlord position as well.

Nor does the operator buy freedom to adapt at will. A franchisee who wishes to add a local dish, change a supplier, alter opening hours, or redesign the dining room generally needs approval, and many agreements reserve broad franchisor discretion over such changes. That restriction is not an accident of drafting. Uniformity is the asset being rented, and a store that diverges too far weakens the promise that every other store in the network relies upon. The tension between local judgment and network uniformity runs through nearly every dispute in franchising, from menu pricing to remodel timing.

What the operator does acquire, when the system works, is a shortcut. An independent founder must invent a menu, build a reputation from zero, negotiate supply contracts alone, and learn through expensive error. A franchise operator purchases a tested menu, instant recognition, pooled purchasing, and a manual written from the errors of earlier stores. The price of that shortcut is the fee stack and the loss of autonomy. Whether the exchange is fair in a given system depends on arithmetic rather than on slogans, on whether the sales lift from the brand exceeds the total cost of renting it, year after year, store by store.

## The Unit of Sale: One Restaurant, One Territory

The basic unit of sale in restaurant franchising is a single restaurant attached to a defined territory, and nearly every other arrangement is a variation on that foundation. A single-unit franchise grants the right to open one store at one approved site. The operator signs one agreement, pays one initial fee, builds one kitchen, and lives or dies on the performance of that address. A large share of restaurant franchisees in the United States operate in this form, particularly in systems with lower build costs.

Multi-unit arrangements extend the same logic across a map. An area development agreement grants an operator the right, and usually the obligation, to open a stated number of stores inside a stated region on a stated schedule, with a separate franchise agreement signed for each restaurant as it opens. A master franchise goes one step further and allows the holder to sub-franchise to others inside a large territory, sometimes an entire country, collecting a share of fees and royalties in return for recruiting and supporting local operators. Each layer adds a party between the brand owner and the kitchen, and each layer takes a portion of the same sales dollar.

Territory language decides how much protection that dollar receives. A protected or exclusive territory bars the franchisor from opening, or licensing another operator to open, a competing store inside the boundary during the term. An unprotected territory offers no such bar, leaving the operator exposed to a new store nearby, to sales through grocery channels, to delivery platforms, and to nontraditional sites such as airports, stadiums, and travel plazas, unless the agreement expressly covers those channels. Many agreements fall between the poles, granting protection within a radius or a trade area while reserving specified channels and venues to the franchisor.

The definition itself deserves close reading because small differences carry large consequences. A territory may be drawn as a circle of fixed radius around the store, as a set of streets and natural boundaries, as a population count, or as a list of postal codes. A radius drawn on a map ignores highways, rivers, and commuting patterns that shape real trade areas. A population definition can shrink in practical value as a city grows, because the same count covers a smaller geographic area over time. Experienced operators therefore study the map exhibit attached to the agreement with the same care given to the fee schedule.

Site approval sits inside the territory grant as a separate franchisor power. Even an operator holding development rights for a region must usually submit each proposed address for approval against criteria covering traffic counts, visibility, access, parking, proximity to other stores, and demographic fit. Approval protects the network against weak sites that would fail and damage the brand, yet it also gives the franchisor control over the pace and placement of growth.
For the single-store owner, territory is often the most consequential clause after the royalty rate, because a restaurant draws most of its trade from a short drive. A second store placed too close divides the same pool of lunch customers between two kitchens paying two sets of fixed costs. That division, called encroachment when operators challenge it, receives fuller treatment later in the discussion of disputes and litigation. At the grant stage, the essential point is simpler. The operator rents a brand for a place, and the legal description of that place determines how much of the local demand the rent actually buys.

## The Money In: Franchise Fees and Build-Out Costs

Money enters a restaurant franchise long before the first meal is sold, and almost all of it flows from the operator side of the table. The first payment is the initial franchise fee, a lump sum paid at signing or at opening that purchases the licence, the initial training, and the right to use the system for the term. In American restaurant systems the fee commonly falls between 20,000 dollars and 50,000 dollars per store, although smaller concepts charge less and premium brands charge more. The fee is generally non-refundable once training or site work has begun, which means the operator commitment becomes real well before revenue begins.

The larger sum is the build-out, the cost of turning a leased shell or a raw parcel into a restaurant that matches the franchisor prototype. The Franchise Disclosure Document standardises this estimate in Item 7, which lists the estimated initial investment in a prescribed table covering the franchise fee, lease deposits, construction, leasehold improvements, signage, equipment, opening inventory, insurance, training travel, and working capital for the first months. Item 7 gives low and high ranges rather than a single figure, because construction costs differ by region and by whether the operator builds from the ground or converts an existing restaurant space.

Construction and equipment dominate the range in most full-service and quick-service formats alike. A drive-through restaurant built from bare land can require an investment well above 1,000,000 dollars once land preparation, building, kitchen equipment, point-of-sale systems, and exterior signage are counted, while a conversion inside a shopping centre can cost a fraction of that sum. Fryers, ovens, grills, refrigeration, ventilation hoods, and fire suppression systems must meet both brand specifications and local health and building codes, and the operator pays for both sets of requirements. A change in the prototype mid-project, such as a revised counter design or a new cooking platform, also lands on the operator account unless the agreement states otherwise.

Signage deserves separate attention because it illustrates who bears every cost in the system. The operator pays to fabricate, ship, install, illuminate, and maintain signs bearing trademarks the operator does not own. When a system refreshes its logo or trade dress, the operator pays again to replace those signs. The same pattern holds for menu boards, uniforms, packaging, and smallwares stamped with brand marks. Each item is a legitimate cost of presenting a uniform image, and each item is funded at store level, which is why experienced operators read the remodel and reimaging clauses as financial obligations rather than as design notes.

Opening inventory and pre-opening labor complete the initial stack. Freezers and storerooms must be stocked before opening day, crews must be hired and trained while the register records nothing, and managers often train at another store for weeks at operator expense. Many systems also require a grand opening marketing spend, paid by the operator, to launch the store into its trade area. Working capital must then carry the business through the early months while sales build toward mature levels, a period in which a new store may lose money even inside a strong brand.

Financing ties the whole stack together. Operators commonly combine personal savings, commercial bank loans, and loans guaranteed in part by the Small Business Administration, pledging personal assets and signing personal guarantees that survive even if the store fails. The franchisor is rarely the lender and rarely guarantees the debt. That separation is central to the thesis stated at the outset. The brand expands on capital supplied by hundreds of separate borrowers, each bearing a concentrated risk in one or a few addresses, while the franchisor investment in any single store remains modest by comparison.

## The Money Out Every Week: Royalties on Sales, Not Profit

After opening, the direction of payment reverses into a steady weekly rhythm, and the royalty is the largest continuing charge in most systems. A royalty is a percentage of gross sales paid to the franchisor for continued use of the brand and system, commonly between 4 percent and 6 percent for restaurant concepts, with some brands below that band and others above it. Gross sales generally means all revenue from the store, including dine-in, takeaway, drive-through, catering, and delivery sales rung through the store, minus only narrow exclusions such as sales tax collected for a government and, in some agreements, refunds and employee meals.

### How is a royalty payment calculated each week?

A royalty payment is calculated by applying the percentage stated in the franchise agreement to the store gross sales for the period, with gross sales defined by the agreement to include dine-in, takeaway and delivery receipts and to exclude sales tax collected for government and, in many systems, refunded amounts and employee meals.

The verifiability point has a long scholarly history. Francine Lafontaine, in Agency Theory and Franchising, published in the RAND Journal of Economics in 1992, examined why franchisors combine company-owned and franchised stores and why contracts take their observed form, treating franchising as a response to monitoring and incentive problems between a brand owner and distant store operators. A royalty on sales fits that framework neatly. Sales are observable at low cost through electronic point-of-sale reporting, which in modern systems transmits daily totals directly to the franchisor. Profit, by contrast, can be moved by decisions on staffing, portioning, maintenance deferral, related-party rent, and owner salary, none of which a distant franchisor can audit cheaply for thousands of stores.

Incentive alignment follows from the same base. Because the franchisor earns more only when store sales rise, the franchisor gains from advertising, menu development, and product launches that lift the whole network. Because the operator keeps the residual after the royalty, the operator gains from controlling food waste, scheduling labor carefully, and serving guests well enough to bring them back. Each side is paid for a different contribution, the brand for demand creation and the operator for cost discipline, and the sales-based royalty is the hinge between the two.

Collection mechanics make the priority of the claim visible. Many systems collect royalties weekly by electronic funds transfer, with the franchisor authorised to debit the operator account on a set day for the prior week sales. Others collect monthly, yet the principle is identical. The royalty leaves before the operator decides how the remaining cash is divided among suppliers, landlord, lender, and payroll for the next period, although legal payroll and tax obligations carry their own priority. A store can therefore be busy, respected locally, and still fall behind on royalties in a month when food or labor costs spike, because the royalty does not adjust to store costs at all.

That rigidity is the sharpest criticism operators raise against the model, and it deserves a direct statement. A percentage of sales takes the same share in a good month and a bad month, while rent, loan payments, and minimum staffing also remain fixed, so a modest fall in sales can erase the entire residual. Franchisors answer that the brand created a large part of the demand being taxed, that company services continue in bad months, and that a profit-based royalty would invite manipulation and conflict. Both positions describe real features of the same contract, which is why royalty structure remains a permanent subject of negotiation, litigation, and academic study rather than a settled detail.

## The Advertising Fund and Who Controls It

Alongside the royalty, most restaurant systems collect a second percentage of gross sales for advertising, commonly between 2 percent and 5 percent, paid into a brand fund dedicated to marketing the network as a whole. The fund pays for national and regional television, digital campaigns, sponsorships, menu photography, promotional materials, and the agency and production costs behind them. An individual operator cannot direct the fund toward a single store, cannot withhold contributions during a local downturn, and generally cannot demand an accounting tailored to the trade area around one address. The contribution is a condition of membership in the brand.

The logic of pooling is strong on its face. A single restaurant could never buy a national television campaign, create a professionally produced advertisement, or sponsor a major sporting event. Ten thousand restaurants contributing a few percent of sales create a marketing budget that competes with the largest consumer brands in the country. Local marketing cooperatives add a second layer in many systems, grouping operators in a region to buy local media, fund local promotions, and vote on regional spending under rules set by the franchisor or by cooperative bylaws. The cooperative structure gives operators a voice at regional level while the national fund remains under franchisor administration.

Governance is where agreement frays. Franchise agreements typically state that the franchisor administers the brand fund in its discretion, that fund money is not held as a trust for operators, and that the franchisor may spend contributions in markets where company stores or growth priorities sit, even if some contributing stores gain little direct benefit. Operators in slow markets subsidise campaigns aimed at fast-growing regions. Operators near retirement fund brand building whose value accrues to stores they will never own. None of that is hidden, since the arrangement appears in the agreement and in Item 8 and related disclosures, yet disclosure and satisfaction are different things.

Accounting disputes recur across decades and across brands for the same structural reason. Operators ask how much of the fund went to media placement and how much went to franchisor staff salaries, agency retainers, production overhead, and administrative charges. They ask whether company-owned stores contribute on the same terms as franchised stores. They ask why a surplus sits unspent in one year or why a deficit is recovered from later contributions. Litigation and franchisee association campaigns in several major systems have turned on exactly those questions, and some systems have responded with advisory councils, annual fund statements, and independent audits that go beyond the minimum the contract requires.

The deeper issue is control over brand direction. Advertising decides which products are promoted, which price points are advertised, and which occasions the brand claims in the mind of the customer. A promotion built around a discounted sandwich may lift traffic and franchisor royalties while squeezing the operator residual, because the discount comes out of store margin while the royalty is still calculated on the discounted sale. When operators object to a promotion, the objection is therefore rarely about taste in advertising. The objection is about who bears the cost of a decision taken elsewhere, a theme that connects the advertising fund to every other payment stream in the system.

## The Supply Chain as a Second Profit Centre

A restaurant sells food, so control over food supply is control over the largest cost line in the store. Most franchise agreements require the operator to purchase ingredients, packaging, and supplies either from the franchisor, from an affiliate, or from approved suppliers designated by the franchisor, in each case to specifications the franchisor sets. The requirement has a genuine quality rationale. A chain that promises the same taste in every town must control the grind of the beef, the formulation of the sauce, the cut of the potato, and the specification of the bun, and open purchasing would dissolve that consistency within months.

The same requirement also creates a second profit centre that operates beside the royalty stream. A franchisor or its affiliate may manufacture a proprietary sauce, distribute a full line of products to stores, or negotiate national contracts with independent suppliers and receive rebates, allowances, or volume payments based on operator purchases. Distribution adds a markup between the manufacturer price and the delivered price paid by the store, covering warehousing, trucking, and order systems, and in some structures retaining a margin beyond those costs. Item 8 of the disclosure document requires the franchisor to describe restrictions on sources of supply and to disclose payments the franchisor receives from suppliers based on operator purchases, in the categories the rule prescribes.

For the operator, the relevant figure is the delivered cost of goods as a share of sales, often called food cost or cost of goods sold, which in quick-service restaurants commonly runs near 30 percent of sales depending on concept and menu mix. A difference of one percentage point on that line, caused by a distribution markup or by a specification change, can equal a fifth of a typical royalty burden in practical effect, yet supply prices attract less attention at signing than the headline royalty rate. Experienced operators therefore compare total occupancy of the sales dollar, royalties plus advertising plus rent plus delivered supply cost, rather than judging a system by any single percentage.

Purchasing cooperatives are the answer some systems built to that imbalance. In a cooperative model, franchisees collectively own the purchasing and distribution organisation, hire its management, and receive any surplus back as patronage or lower prices, while the franchisor retains specification and approval rights to protect quality. The Subway system built its Independent Purchasing Cooperative in 1974 to negotiate and purchase for operators in the United States, and Yum Brands operators participated in a supply chain cooperative structure through Restaurant Supply Chain Solutions, formed in the late nineteen nineties. Cooperative governance does not remove conflict, since operators and franchisors still argue over specifications and service levels, yet cooperative ownership changes who keeps the distribution margin.

Disputes arise when the supply chain serves two masters at once. Operators allege in some systems that approved-supplier prices exceed open-market prices for equivalent goods, that rebates paid to the franchisor are not shared, or that a specification change raises store costs while lifting franchisor supply income. Franchisors answer that national contracts deliver consistency, food safety, and continuity of supply that spot purchasing cannot match, and that a shortage handled by a dedicated distributor is worth more than a small saving in a calm month. The honest assessment weighs both claims against delivered cost data, service records, and the disclosure in Item 8, because the supply chain is neither a pure service nor a pure tax. The supply chain is a business inside the business, and its ownership decides its character.

## Real Estate: The Landlord Inside the Franchisor

Real estate separates restaurant franchising into two families, and the difference shapes every other term in the agreement. In one family the operator finds a site, negotiates a lease with an independent landlord or buys the land, and deals with the franchisor only as licensor. In the other family the franchisor controls the land and building and leases them to the operator, collecting rent beside royalties and fees. The second family reaches its fullest form in McDonald, where land ownership became the financial foundation of the corporation.

Harry Sonneborn, the first president of McDonald Corporation, built that model in the nineteen fifties by forming the Franchise Realty Corporation to buy or lease land and then sublease the parcels to operators at a markup, with the corporation also taking a share tied to store performance in early arrangements. John F. Love recounts the strategy in McDonald: Behind the Arches, published in 1986, describing how property income gave the young corporation a steadier revenue line than royalties from fifteen-cent hamburgers could provide. Sonneborn summarised the logic in a remark widely attributed to him in that history, that the corporation was in the real estate business rather than the hamburger business, a paraphrase of a position he stated repeatedly to lenders and investors.

The mechanics deserve precision. Where the franchisor buys the land and builds or funds the building, the operator signs a lease, often coterminous with the franchise agreement, and pays base rent plus, in some systems, a percentage element. Where the franchisor ground-leases land from a third-party owner, the operator sublease sits beneath that ground lease, and a default or expiry at the upper level can threaten the store even when the operator has performed well. Rent is generally due regardless of store profit, escalates on a schedule written into the lease, and continues during remodel closures unless the documents provide relief. Control of the site also strengthens the franchisor hand at transfer, renewal, and termination, because loss of the franchise usually means loss of the premises as well.

Rent as an income line behaves differently from a royalty, and the difference explains franchisor preference for the model. A royalty fluctuates with sales week by week. Contract rent arrives in a fixed amount, supports borrowing against a property portfolio, and builds an asset that remains valuable even if a particular operator fails and must be replaced. A failed store under a royalty-only model produces nothing until a new operator opens. A failed store on franchisor land can be re-let to a stronger operator while the land itself appreciates with the surrounding district. The corporation therefore holds both a claim on sales and a claim on place, diversified against each other.

Systems in the other family trade that control for speed and lower capital demands on the franchisor. An operator who leases from a third-party landlord negotiates rent directly, may secure renewal options independent of the franchise term, and may own the building even while renting the brand. The risks shift accordingly. The franchisor cannot guarantee a site at renewal, cannot capture rent, and may watch a prime corner pass to a competitor when a franchise ends. The operator gains a measure of independence and a possible property asset, yet also carries the full burden of site selection and landlord negotiation. Neither family is uniformly better for operators. A franchisor landlord can offer a prime site an individual could never secure, at a rent that still leaves a healthy residual, or can load a marginal site with rent that consumes the residual entirely. The lease, read beside the franchise agreement, tells which outcome a given store faces.

## The Disclosure Document and the Franchise Rule

Franchising in the United States is sold under a disclosure regime rather than under a federal approval regime, and that distinction defines what protection a buyer receives. The Federal Trade Commission issued the Franchise Rule in 1978, requiring franchisors to furnish prospective franchisees with prescribed information before signing an agreement or accepting payment. Amendments adopted in 2007 modernised the rule, harmonised federal requirements with state disclosure formats, and produced the Franchise Disclosure Document, universally shortened to FDD, as the single standard instrument for the offer of a franchise.

### What does a franchise disclosure document have to contain?

A franchise disclosure document has to contain the twenty-three items prescribed by the Franchise Rule, covering the franchisor history, litigation, bankruptcy, initial and continuing fees, estimated initial investment, supply restrictions, territory, trademarks, renewal and termination terms, financial performance representations when made, outlet statistics, and audited franchisor financial statements, together with the agreements and receipts attached as exhibits.

The twenty-three items follow a deliberate order from identity to economics to exit. Early items identify the franchisor and its parents, predecessors, and affiliates, describe the business experience of key executives, and disclose litigation and bankruptcy history. Middle items price the offer, covering initial fees in Item 5, other fees in Item 6, the estimated initial investment in Item 7, supply restrictions and franchisor revenue from required purchases in Item 8, and any financing the franchisor offers in Item 10. Later items address territory in Item 12, trademarks in Item 13, obligations of the operator in Item 17 in a standard summary table, outlet openings and closures in Item 20, and audited financial statements in Item 23. A prospective operator checking documents against that order, perhaps with a [reference tool](https://reportmedic.org) open beside the printed exhibits, can locate any required disclosure within minutes because every franchisor must use the same sequence.

Timing is regulated as strictly as content. A franchisor must furnish the FDD at least fourteen calendar days before the prospective operator signs a binding agreement or pays any money, and the franchise agreement itself must be furnished in final form at least seven calendar days before signing when the rule conditions apply. The waiting period exists so that enthusiasm generated by a sales presentation cools against the litigation history, the fee tables, and the outlet closure statistics in Item 20, and so that advisers have time to read the documents. A sale made inside the waiting period violates the rule even if the buyer later prospers.

The sharpest limit concerns earnings. A franchisor may not state how much a store will earn, orally or in writing, outside Item 19, the financial performance representation item. Item 19 is optional, yet if a franchisor makes a representation there, the representation must have a reasonable basis, must state its basis and assumptions, and must be available for substantiation. If Item 19 states that the franchisor makes no financial performance representation, then sales staff may not lawfully fill the silence with projections at a discovery day or over dinner. Many disputes begin at exactly that gap, between a formal document that promises nothing and an informal conversation remembered as a promise.

Disclosure has clear boundaries that buyers often misunderstand. The Federal Trade Commission does not approve FDDs, does not verify their accuracy as a routine matter, and does not judge whether a franchise is a good investment. Several states add registration or filing requirements and may review documents for completeness, yet even in those states registration is not a merit endorsement. The regime rests on a theory that an informed buyer, given standardised facts and time, can protect private capital better than a licensing board could. Whether that theory holds for a first-time operator facing a document of several hundred pages is a fair subject for argument, and the closing sections return to the quality of decisions made under that regime.

## State Relationship Laws and the Right to Terminate

Disclosure law governs how a franchise is sold. Relationship law governs how a franchise may be ended, transferred, or refused renewal, and in the United States that second body of law sits mainly at state level. A franchise agreement may run for fifteen or twenty years, during which an operator builds a staff, a customer base, and personal guarantees tied to the store. Without statutory limits, a franchisor holding broad contract language could end that investment for minor breaches, refuse renewal to take a successful store into company hands, or block a sale to a qualified buyer. State relationship statutes exist to narrow that discretion.

California supplies the most cited example. The California Franchise Relations Act, enacted in the nineteen seventies and amended repeatedly in later decades, requires good cause for termination, prescribes notice periods and opportunities to cure many breaches, and regulates non-renewal and transfer. Good cause under statutes of that type generally includes material failure to comply with a lawful requirement of the agreement after notice and a chance to cure, while allowing faster action for grave matters such as abandonment, insolvency, conviction affecting the business, or repeated noncompliance. Wisconsin, New Jersey, Illinois, Minnesota, and other states maintain statutes in the same family, with definitions and procedures that differ in detail while sharing the good-cause core.

Termination procedure matters as much as the substantive standard. A typical protective statute requires written notice stating the grounds, a cure period that commonly runs thirty days for curable monetary and operational defaults, and immediate or shortened notice only for defaults that cannot be cured by nature. An operator who pays arrears or corrects an operational deficiency inside the cure window defeats the termination in many statutory schemes, although the agreement and the governing statute decide each case. Franchisors retain strong rights against operators who falsify sales reports, endanger public health, misuse trademarks, or abandon the premises, and relationship laws were drafted to preserve those rights rather than to make termination impossible.

Non-renewal raises a quieter version of the same conflict. At the end of a term, a franchisor may wish to impose a successor agreement with higher royalties, a smaller territory, a remodel obligation, or a general release of claims as a condition of renewal. Statutes in several states require timely notice of intent not to renew, restrict the grounds for refusal, or protect an operator who declines changed terms in specified circumstances. The economic stakes are high because a mature store carries the value the operator spent the term creating, and a refusal or a punitive successor agreement can transfer much of that value to the franchisor without any finding that the operator performed badly.

Transfer consent completes the protective scheme. An operator exit usually proceeds by selling the store and assigning the franchise to a buyer, and agreements condition assignment on franchisor approval of the buyer financial capacity, experience, and training, plus payment of a transfer fee and often a remodel or upgrade commitment by the incoming owner. Relationship statutes limit withholding of consent to reasonable grounds in several jurisdictions and set deadlines for a franchisor response. A consent right exercised reasonably protects guests and neighbouring stores from an unqualified owner. A consent right exercised slowly or arbitrarily can trap a retiring operator and depress the sale price. Courts and arbitrators deciding transfer disputes therefore examine the stated grounds against the buyer record, a factual inquiry that keeps the statutory standard honest in practice.

## What the Franchisee Actually Owns

Ownership in a restaurant franchise divides into layers that look unified from the dining room and separate sharply in the documents. The operator holds a licence to use trademarks, trade dress, recipes, operating systems and signage for a defined term and a defined territory, and that licence remains the property of the franchisor throughout. The operator also holds, in most arrangements, a leasehold interest in the premises rather than title to the land and building, together with title to kitchen equipment, furniture, smallwares and vehicles purchased for the store. Between the licence and the physical assets sits goodwill, the value created by repeat trade, trained staff and local reputation, and goodwill in a franchised store has a peculiar quality because it attaches to a brand the operator does not own and to a location the operator may not control after the term ends.

The distinction matters most at the boundaries of the term. Equipment can be sold, moved or pledged, subject to lender consent and to franchisor standards that limit transfer to unapproved buyers. A leasehold can be assigned only under the conditions written into the lease and the franchise agreement, which commonly require franchisor approval of the assignee, payment of a transfer fee and completion of training by the incoming operator. Goodwill has no independent life once the licence expires, because the signs come down, the proprietary products leave the menu and the telephone numbers, delivery listings and customer accounts tied to the brand revert under the post term covenants. An operator who built a loyal lunch trade over fifteen years owns the memory of that trade in a personal sense, but the legal right to invite that trade back under the same name belongs to the grantor of the licence.

Renewal therefore functions less as an extension and more as a new negotiation conducted under unequal conditions. Franchise agreements commonly condition renewal on signing the form of agreement then in use for new franchisees, which may carry a higher royalty rate, a different territory definition, a remodel obligation and a broader release of claims than the expiring contract. The operator faces that offer with sunk investment in a specific site, a trained workforce whose skills fit one system and a household income dependent on continuity, while the franchisor faces the same site as one unit among hundreds or thousands. State relationship laws in a minority of states add good cause and notice protections around nonrenewal, and the Federal Trade Commission disclosure regime requires the franchisor to describe renewal terms in Item 17 of the Franchise Disclosure Document, but disclosure describes the bargain rather than equalising it.

The asset that cannot be sold freely is consequently the business as a going concern under its brand. A conventional independent restaurant can be sold as a name, a lease, a kitchen and a book of regular customers, with the buyer free to keep the name and continue the trade. A franchised restaurant can be sold only as a transfer of a licence that the franchisor may approve or withhold under stated standards, to a buyer who must qualify financially, complete training and accept the current form of agreement. That approval right depresses the pool of buyers, lengthens sale timelines and gives the franchisor influence over valuation, because a transfer denied is a business whose brand value falls toward the resale value of used equipment. The operator owns real things of real worth, and the worth of the whole remains contingent on permission that the contract reserves to another party.

## The Operator Margin: Where the Residual Goes

The economics of a single franchised restaurant become clearest by following one dollar of sales through the claims that attach to it in sequence. Franchise Disclosure Documents and industry cost studies published by accounting firms serving restaurant systems describe a recurring cost structure for quick service and fast casual formats, stated here as representative ranges rather than as the experience of any single store. Food and paper cost, the ingredients, packaging and disposables consumed in producing the order, commonly absorbs 28 to 32 cents of the dollar. Labour, including wages, payroll taxes, benefits and workers compensation insurance, commonly absorbs 28 to 33 cents, with the share rising where minimum wage schedules or tight local labour markets push hourly rates upward. Together those two prime costs claim well over half of every dollar before any payment reaches the franchisor or the landlord.

The franchisor claims come next and attach to sales rather than to profit. A royalty in the range of 4 to 6 percent of gross sales takes 4 to 6 cents, and a contribution to the brand advertising fund in the range of 3 to 5 percent takes another 3 to 5 cents, so the combined brand charges consume 7 to 11 cents of the dollar in a typical structure. Occupancy follows, covering rent, common area charges, property taxes passed through under the lease and insurance on the premises, commonly 6 to 10 cents depending on whether the site sits on a high value corner or in a secondary market. Other operating costs fill the remainder of the middle of the statement: utilities, repairs and maintenance, supplies, local marketing beyond the fund, accounting and legal services, bank and card processing fees, waste removal and miscellaneous expenses together commonly absorb 10 to 14 cents.

What remains after those claims is the operator residual, and arithmetic shows how thin the residual runs even in a competently managed store. At the favourable end of every range, with food and paper at 28 cents, labour at 28 cents, combined brand charges at 7 cents, occupancy at 6 cents and other operating costs at 10 cents, total claims reach 79 cents and the residual before debt service, income taxes and owner compensation reaches 21 cents. At the unfavourable end, with food and paper at 32 cents, labour at 33 cents, brand charges at 11 cents, occupancy at 10 cents and other operating costs at 14 cents, total claims reach 100 cents and the residual disappears entirely. Most stores operate between those poles, and industry cost studies consistently place store level margins for franchised restaurants in a band roughly between 10 and 18 percent of sales before debt service, a figure that must then cover loan principal and interest, any salary the owner draws for working in the store and a return on the capital invested in the build out.

The walk through the dollar explains why small movements in any single line carry outsized consequences. A two point rise in food cost, caused by commodity prices or waste, removes two cents from a residual that may have been only twelve cents, a reduction of roughly one sixth of store level profit. A royalty calculated on gross sales does not participate in that pain, because the royalty line holds at its percentage of sales even as the residual compresses. Debt service then claims a fixed sum from a shrunken remainder. The residual claimant position, familiar from corporate finance, describes the franchisee precisely: every other party to the store, supplier, employee, landlord, franchisor and lender, holds a claim senior in practice to the operator, and the operator receives what is left after each senior claim has been satisfied at its contracted rate.

## Fixed Promises Against Variable Takings

The central mechanical tension in a franchised restaurant lies in the mismatch between claims that vary with sales and promises that do not. Royalties and advertising contributions vary in amount while remaining fixed in rate, rising in dollars when sales rise and falling in dollars when sales fall, although the rate never adjusts to the profitability of the underlying sales. Rent under many leases behaves in a hybrid fashion, with a fixed base rent that holds regardless of trade and, in some leases, a percentage rent component above a sales threshold. Debt service does not vary at all within the loan term, because principal and interest follow an amortisation schedule set at origination. Labour sits between the categories, variable in principle through scheduling and fixed in practice through minimum staffing requirements, opening and closing duties and wage floors that no schedule can go below.

Operating leverage names the consequence of that mix. A store carries a block of costs that must be paid to open the doors each day, including base rent, minimum crew, insurance, loan payments and salaried management, and each additional dollar of sales beyond the break even point contributes its margin after food, paper and brand charges to covering that block and then to profit. The same structure reverses on the way down. When sales fall by 10 percent, food and paper costs fall roughly in proportion, and royalty and advertising dollars fall in proportion, but rent, debt service and the labour floor fall hardly at all. A 10 percent sales decline can therefore remove 30, 40 or even 50 percent of the residual, depending on how large the fixed block stands relative to store level profit, and a somewhat larger decline eliminates the residual entirely while every senior claim continues to be presented for payment.

Seasonality and local shocks test the structure repeatedly within a single year. A store near offices loses weekend trade, a store near schools loses summer trade, a road project diverts drive through traffic for months and a competitor opening across the street divides a trade area that was sized for one unit. None of those events renegotiates the lease, the loan or the minimum crew. The franchisor shares the downturn only to the extent of receiving royalty dollars on a smaller sales base, and the franchisor portfolio diversifies that exposure across many trade areas at once, while the single store operator holds the exposure concentrated in one trade area and one format. Multi unit operators diversify within their own portfolios, which forms part of the explanation for consolidation addressed in a later section, but diversification softens rather than removes the leverage.

The leverage also explains the intensity of operator attention to sales building activities that might appear disproportionate to an outside observer. A modest sales gain, held against a fixed cost block, flows to the residual at a high incremental rate, because the only deductions from the added dollar are food, paper, hourly labour for the added volume and the brand charges of 7 to 11 cents combined. An operator who adds five dollars of sales for every hundred already recorded may add far more than five percent to store level profit. The same arithmetic makes operators resistant to discount promotions directed from the centre, since a promotion that raises unit volume while lowering average check can raise food and labour consumption faster than it raises sales dollars, leaving the franchisor royalty whole on the recorded sales and the operator residual smaller than before the promotion began.

## Remodel Cycles and Capital Calls

A franchised restaurant ages on two clocks at once. The physical clock runs through fryer life, roof condition, seating wear and the slow deterioration of finishes under heat, grease and cleaning chemicals. The brand clock runs through design revisions issued by the franchisor, under which colours, signage, menu boards, seating plans, kitchen layouts and customer facing technology are declared outdated on a schedule set for the whole system. Franchise agreements translate the brand clock into a legal obligation, requiring the operator to remodel, refresh or rebuild the store at stated intervals, commonly at the midpoint of the term and again at renewal, and to adopt system changes such as new point of sale systems, kitchen equipment packages and digital ordering infrastructure within stated compliance windows.

The amounts involved rival a substantial fraction of the original build out. Industry cost studies and the investment ranges disclosed in Item 7 of Franchise Disclosure Documents place a mid term remodel for a quick service restaurant commonly in a band from tens of thousands of dollars for a cosmetic refresh to several hundred thousand dollars for a full image conversion involving exterior work, drive through reconfiguration and kitchen replacement. Point of sale replacement adds hardware, software licensing, installation and retraining costs, and the replacement cycle for that technology runs shorter than the life of the building, so an operator may fund two or three generations of point of sale equipment within a single franchise term. Each expenditure arrives as a capital call in substance, a demand for new investment in an existing store, distinguished from the initial investment by the fact that the operator cannot decline and remain in the system.

Timing gives the capital call its particular weight. The franchisor calendar that sets a remodel deadline reflects brand strategy, competitive positioning and the design cycle of the system as a whole, not the cash position, loan balance or family circumstances of any individual operator. A remodel may fall due in a year when sales have softened, when the operator has already funded a roof replacement or when credit conditions have tightened, and the agreement provides limited relief for any of those conditions. Financing a remodel also differs from financing a new store, because the remodel adds no new revenue unit and lenders evaluate the loan against the cash flow of the same store whose residual must also absorb the disruption of construction, partial closure and retraining. Sales commonly dip during the work itself, so the operator funds the investment from a temporarily reduced income stream.

Defenders of the remodel system point to a genuine collective problem that the mandate solves. A customer judges the brand by the last store visited, and a minority of ageing, shabby units imposes a reputation cost on every other operator in the system, so voluntary upgrading would chronically undersupply the shared asset. Periodic mandatory reinvestment functions as the enforcement mechanism for a standard that benefits the network as a whole, and franchisors cite system sales comparisons between remodelled and unremodelled cohorts in disclosure and recruitment materials in support of the return on the required investment. The argument carries force at the level of the system and still leaves the distributional question untouched, because the benefit of a refreshed brand spreads across the network and to the franchisor royalty stream, while the cost, the debt and the construction risk concentrate on the operator of the single store being rebuilt.

## Encroachment: When the Brand Sells Against Its Own Stores

Growth creates the most persistent internal conflict in a franchise system because the franchisor and the established operator measure growth from opposite sides. A new unit in an adjacent territory raises system sales, extends brand presence and adds a royalty paying store to the franchisor ledger, while the same unit may divide the trade of an existing store whose customers live between the two locations. Franchise agreements historically granted narrow territorial protections or none at all beyond the four walls of the store, and the Franchise Disclosure Document regime requires the franchisor to state the territorial terms in Item 12, including reservations of rights to sell through other channels. Disclosure settles what the contract says and leaves open the economic question of how much trade a new channel or unit actually takes.

### What does encroachment mean in franchising?
Encroachment means a franchisor placing a new unit, company store or alternative sales channel close enough to an existing franchised store to divert part of that store trade, reducing the established operator sales and residual while increasing total system revenue and the franchisor royalty income drawn from the wider network.

The channels of encroachment have multiplied beyond the neighbouring unit. The same brand may appear in airports, stadiums, university food courts, hospital cafeterias and inside big box stores under licences that the franchisor classifies as non traditional venues outside any protected territory. Branded products reach grocery shelves as frozen, packaged or bottled goods bearing the restaurant name, and delivery platforms extend the effective trade area of every store until neighbouring territories overlap in the application itself. Each channel looks incremental from the centre and cumulative from the store, because the operator who paid for an exclusive local presence discovers the brand selling against that presence in forms the original agreement reserved to the franchisor.

Empirical work has tried to measure the trade off rather than merely assert it. Arturs Kalnins, in research published in the early two thousands on franchising and local market conditions, examined how ownership structure and entry patterns in hotel and restaurant markets affected incumbent performance, and Kalnins and colleagues in related work on multi unit and plural form systems documented that franchisor decisions about company owned versus franchised entry shape the competitive environment incumbents face. The consistent finding across that literature is that entry effects fall unevenly: system level gains coexist with incumbent level losses, and the distribution of those losses depends on distance, market density and the share of company owned units nearby. The research reframes encroachment from a complaint about fairness into a measurable transfer, in which part of the new unit revenue represents trade moved from an existing operator rather than trade created.

Legal remedies remain narrow. Courts generally enforce the territorial language the parties signed, implied covenant claims succeed only in limited circumstances where a franchisor action destroys the expected benefit of the contract, and a small number of state statutes restrict specific forms of encroachment or require impact consideration. The practical protections operators rely on are therefore negotiated rather than granted: defined protected areas, rights of first refusal on nearby units, impact policies adopted voluntarily by franchisors and franchisee associations that bargain collectively over placement rules. None of those protections changes the underlying incentive, under which a franchisor compensated as a percentage of system sales gains from total volume even when a portion of that volume has been transferred from one operator to another at the first operator expense.

## Multi-Unit Operators and the Area Developer Layer

The single store owner operator standing at the counter describes the founding image of restaurant franchising and a shrinking share of its ownership. Consolidation has moved large portions of mature systems into the hands of multi unit operators holding ten, fifty or several hundred stores, often across multiple brands, with professional management layers between ownership and the individual restaurant. District managers supervise groups of stores, regional directors supervise districts, and specialists in human resources, accounting, maintenance and local marketing serve the portfolio from a central office. The shift changes the character of the franchisee from a tradesperson with a licence into a regional business that resembles, in organisational form, the franchisor itself, while remaining a licensee in law.

Area development agreements formalise one route to that scale. Under such an agreement, a developer commits to open a stated number of units in a defined territory on a stated schedule, paying an area fee for the exclusive development rights and a reduced or standard initial fee for each unit as it opens. The franchisor gains a committed development pipeline and a single counterpart responsible for an entire market, and the developer gains territorial control that answers part of the encroachment concern, since no other franchisee enters the area while the schedule is met. Failure to meet the schedule, however, can forfeit exclusivity or the agreement itself, so the developer carries an obligation to keep building through soft markets, funding units on the contract calendar in a pattern that mirrors the remodel obligation at the level of the whole territory.

Subfranchising adds a further layer where permitted, under which a master franchisee for a region may grant subfranchises to operators of individual stores, collecting a share of their fees and royalties and assuming part of the training and supervision role. The structure appears most often in international expansion, where local knowledge, language and capital argue for an intermediary, and it inserts another residual claimant and another supervising party between the brand owner and the person running the store. Disclosure law treats subfranchisor offers as franchise offers in their own right, and the layered structure multiplies the questions of responsibility when standards slip or a market fails.

Financing follows the consolidation. Banks with dedicated franchise lending groups and non bank lenders that specialise in franchise portfolios underwrite the cash flow of whole portfolios rather than single stores, and business development companies among those lenders supply [lending structures](/2026/11/12/how-business-development-companies-work/) suited to middle market operators whose borrowing needs exceed local bank appetites and fall below public debt market scale. Portfolio lending changes the risk picture in both directions. A lender diversified across forty stores tolerates the failure of two, and an operator with forty stores negotiates fees, remodel timing and territory from a position no single store owner can match. The same scale raises the stakes of a system wide downturn, because portfolio debt rests on one brand concept in many cases, and a concept decline reaches every store and the single loan structure resting on all of them at once. Scale also changes succession and sale, because a portfolio can be sold as a platform with management in place, attracting buyers and valuations that a collection of separately held single stores could never command in any market.

## The Franchisor Side of the Ledger

The franchisor ledger reads as the mirror image of the operator ledger, and the mirror reverses the risk. Royalty revenue arrives as a percentage of franchisee sales without a corresponding food cost, labour cost or occupancy cost on the franchisor books, so royalty streams carry gross margins far above those available to any party that actually operates restaurants. Advertising fund contributions pass through as restricted funds for brand marketing rather than as franchisor revenue in substance, although the administration of the fund supports the brand asset the franchisor owns. Initial fees arrive in lump sums at signing and opening. Supply chain margins, rent from subleased or franchisor held properties and interest on financing provided to franchisees add further lines, each drawn from the same store sales that fund the operator residual last.

Company owned stores occupy a surprisingly small place in the mature version of that ledger. The annual reports of Yum! Brands and of McDonald's, filed year after year with segment disclosures that separate company restaurant results from franchised results, show the structural pattern in audited form: franchised units constitute the large majority of total units in both systems, company restaurant margins run far below the effective margin on franchised revenue, and franchised revenues contribute a share of operating profit out of proportion to their share of total revenue. McDonald's reporting adds a further instructive line, because rent revenue from franchisees operating on company controlled land and buildings stands as one of the largest and most stable components of franchised revenue, confirming the real estate layer as a profit centre in its own right rather than as a service to operators.

Refranchising waves expose the preference embedded in those numbers. In a refranchising programme, a franchisor sells company owned stores to franchisees, booking a gain on the sale, converting a low margin operating unit into a royalty and rent paying unit and transferring the labour, food cost and capital expenditure burden to the buyer. Systems across the industry have run such programmes in successive waves, each announced as a move toward a more capital light model, and the phrase capital light deserves attention because it states the thesis from the franchisor side: the same sales, the same brand presence and a fraction of the capital employed and risk carried. The buyer in a refranchising sale pays for the privilege of assuming the residual position, and the price the buyer will pay depends entirely on whether the projected residual, after royalties, rent and remodel obligations, clears the buyer cost of capital.

None of that makes the franchisor position costless. Brand management, menu development, training systems, quality assurance, supply chain coordination and legal protection of the trademarks require sustained spending, and a system wide failure of consumer demand reaches the franchisor through falling royalty dollars, closed units and a damaged brand that cannot be licensed at any price. The asymmetry lies in sequence and diversification rather than in immunity. The franchisor feels a downturn as a percentage decline across thousands of stores and several revenue lines, while the operator feels the same downturn as a threat to the survival of the only store, or the only few stores, from which a household and a loan are supported.

## Why Brands Franchise Instead of Owning Stores

The question of why a successful restaurant concept sells franchises instead of opening company stores has a capital answer and an incentives answer, and the scholarly literature treats the two as complements. The capital answer begins with rationing. A proven concept faces demand for new units faster than retained earnings and prudent borrowing can fund them, particularly in the growth phase before the brand commands public market access. Franchising recruits the savings, home equity and borrowing capacity of hundreds of individual operators as the expansion fund of the system, allowing unit growth at a pace the franchisor balance sheet alone could not sustain, while the franchisor concentrates its own capital on brand, system and supply chain assets that serve every unit at once.

The incentives answer addresses what happens inside a store run by a salaried manager. Paul Rubin, in The Theory of the Firm and the Structure of the Franchise Contract, published in 1978, argued that franchising solves a monitoring problem: an employed manager whose pay does not depend on store profit has reason to shirk, to overstaff for personal ease, to let quality slip where customers cannot easily detect it and to consume the perquisites of the position, and supervising managers across dispersed locations costs the firm heavily in travel, inspection and information systems. An owner operator whose household income equals the store residual needs no supervisor to care about waste, staffing or cleanliness, because every dollar of slack leaves the operator own pocket. Francine Lafontaine, in a 1992 empirical study of franchise contracting, tested the predictions of that agency framework against contract data and found the pattern of fees, royalties and company ownership shares consistent with franchising as an efficient allocation of capital and incentives between the parties.

The two answers together explain the plural form observed in mature systems, in which a franchisor retains a minority of company stores alongside the franchised majority. Company stores serve as training grounds, test kitchens for menu and equipment changes and benchmarks against which franchisee performance can be judged, and their presence gives the franchisor direct knowledge of operating costs when contract terms are set. A franchisor that owned nothing would lose that information, and a franchisor that owned everything would lose the capital and incentive advantages that made expansion possible. The observed minority share, visible in the segment reporting of the largest systems, reflects that balance struck at different points by different brands.

Risk allocation completes the explanation and returns to the governing thesis. Local demand risk, labour market risk, commodity risk and site risk sit with the party closest to the local information, which agency theory counts as efficient, and the same placement means the franchisor holds a diversified claim on system sales while each operator holds a concentrated claim on one residual. Efficiency and asymmetry describe the same contract from different ends. The arrangement persists not because either party misunderstands it, since disclosure documents lay the terms out in standardised detail, but because both parties receive something they value: the franchisor receives capital, motivated local management and a revenue linked return on the brand, and the operator receives a tested format, a recognised name and a residual that, in a well chosen territory with disciplined costs, rewards the investment and the labour poured into it.

## Sewing Machines, Soda Fountains and the First Formats

Restaurant franchising feels like a twentieth century invention tied to highways and automobiles, and the form itself is older than the restaurant industry that adopted it. The pattern of a central firm granting local operators the right to sell a branded product under controlled methods, in exchange for fees tied to that privilege, was worked out in the nineteenth century by manufacturers whose products required demonstration, service and local trust. Isaac Singer and the Singer Sewing Machine company built the clearest early version. Sewing machines were expensive, unfamiliar and prone to need adjustment and repair, so Singer licensed local agents who sold on installment plans, taught buyers to use the machines and serviced them afterward, under brand standards and territorial arrangements directed from the centre. The licence solved distribution, financing and instruction in one structure, and it taught manufacturers that a network of motivated local sellers with capital at stake could cover a continent faster than a salaried sales force.

Soft drink bottling supplied the second great template at the close of the nineteenth century. In 1899, Benjamin Thomas and Joseph Whitehead obtained from the Coca-Cola Company the rights to bottle Coca-Cola for most of the United States, under contracts that fixed the price of syrup and granted bottling territories that became enormously valuable as demand grew. The bottling contract separated the brand and concentrate business, which required little capital per unit of volume, from the bottling and distribution business, which required plants, wagons, bottles and local sales effort funded by the bottlers. That separation, brand owner at the centre collecting on the essential input while independent local firms supplied the heavy capital and absorbed local operating risk, prefigures the restaurant franchise structure with unusual precision, including the disputes over pricing and territory that followed as the value of the local rights became clear.

Automobile dealerships carried the form into the twentieth century and added the exclusive territory, the facility standard and the manufacturer right to approve transfers that restaurant agreements later reproduced. Manufacturers needed local showrooms, service departments and inventory financing in every substantial town, and dealers supplied land, buildings and working capital in exchange for the right to sell a branded line within an assigned market. General service stations, hardware chains organised as retailer cooperatives and early lodging and food chains adapted pieces of the same pattern, and by the time Ray Kroc encountered the McDonald brothers restaurant in San Bernardino in the nineteen fifties, every component of the modern franchise already existed in some industry: the licensed trademark, the standardised operating method, the territorial grant, the fee on sales, the inspection regime and the local operator capital.

Restaurants adopted the form late and then adopted it completely, because food service magnified both sides of the bargain. A restaurant requires fresh inputs daily, labour scheduled in hours, quality judged at the moment of consumption and a location whose value depends on traffic patterns within a few streets, so local ownership solved monitoring and adaptation problems that defeated distant management. At the same time the product could be standardised to an unusual degree, with portion, recipe, equipment and layout specified centrally, which made the format replicable by operators with no prior trade beyond capital and diligence. The sewing machine agents, the bottlers of 1899 and the automobile dealers had each demonstrated one part of that combination. The restaurant industry joined the parts into the dominant ownership structure of an entire sector, and the structure retains the marks of its origins in every clause that reserves the brand to the centre and assigns the capital, the labour and the residual to the local operator.

## Kroc, the McDonald Brothers and the System That Scaled

The encounter that reorganised American restaurant franchising took place in 1954 in San Bernardino, California, where Richard and Maurice McDonald operated a single drive-in restaurant that had been rebuilt around a narrow menu and an assembly method they called the Speedee Service System. Ray Kroc, a salesman for Multimixer milkshake machines, visited the location because the brothers had ordered eight of his machines, a volume that implied a throughput no ordinary drive-in achieved. What Kroc observed was a kitchen stripped of wasted motion. Burgers, fries and shakes moved along a line in which each worker performed a small set of repeated tasks, food was prepared in anticipation of demand rather than after an order arrived, and the menu was held deliberately short so that speed and uniformity could be maintained across a lunch rush. The product being sold to the customer was a meal, but the product being demonstrated to a prospective operator was consistency itself, a meal that tasted the same on the hundredth serving as on the first.

Kroc became the brothers franchising agent and in 1955 opened his own restaurant in Des Plaines, Illinois, under an agreement that gave him a modest share of the franchise fee and royalty stream while the brothers retained control of the system and its standards. The arrangement exposed the central tension that runs through the whole history of the format. The brothers prized control and were cautious about expansion that might dilute quality, while Kroc prized growth and understood that a system earns its largest returns when the format is replicated widely and the real estate beneath the restaurants is held by the franchising company. His associate Harry Sonneborn articulated the real estate insight that transformed the economics, namely that the corporation could lease or own the land and buildings and sublease them to operators, capturing a property margin in addition to the royalty on sales. That single move shifted McDonald from a seller of licences toward a landlord with a restaurant system attached, and it aligned the franchisor revenue with the best locations rather than with operator profit.

The partnership ended in 1961 when Kroc bought out the brothers for 2.7 million dollars, a price that also required separate handling of their original San Bernardino restaurant, which they kept and renamed. The buyout matters for structure rather than for drama. Control of the trademark, the operating standards and the territory decisions passed to the party committed to expansion, and the system that scaled was the one in which those rights were consolidated. Under Kroc the company standardised everything that could be standardised, from cooking times and portion sizes to the design of the buildings and the training of operators at Hamburger University, founded the same year as the buyout. Training converted tacit kitchen practice into a transferable manual, and the manual converted a local success into a replicable asset. A franchise, in the mature McDonald form, was therefore not permission to use a name. It was a complete operating system rented to an operator who supplied the labour, the management attention and much of the capital, while the corporation held the brand, the standards and, in the decisive cases, the land. That division, settled in the years between 1954 and 1961, is the template against which later systems were measured, copied and contested. It also explains why operator selection mattered so heavily in the mature system, since a format that could be taught still depended on an owner willing to enforce its standards through every shift, every delivery and every closing procedure, year after year, without supervision from headquarters.

## The Growth Era and the Refranchising Machine

The decades after the Second World War supplied every condition the format required at once. Suburbanisation placed families along new highways and in new subdivisions far from established downtown restaurants, automobile ownership made the drive-in and the roadside unit natural destinations, and a growing teenage and family market rewarded low prices, speed and predictability over variety. Franchisors expanded because franchising solved their capital problem. Each new unit was financed largely by its operator, so a chain could add territory at a pace its own balance sheet could never have funded, while the royalty stream grew with system sales rather than with company investment. By the time the largest burger, chicken and pizza systems matured, the pattern had hardened into a recognisable life cycle in which a company proves a format in stores it owns, sells licences to expand, and then, in maturity, moves in the opposite direction by selling company stores to operators in the process the industry calls refranchising.

The table below settles the question of who pays, who gains and who carries the exposure at each stage of a mature franchise system.

| Stage | Who supplies the capital | Who captures the return | Where the risk sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand and format development | The franchisor in its test stores | The franchisor through later fees | The franchisor before licensing begins |
| Site selection and build-out | The franchisee with borrowed funds | The operator if the site trades well | The franchisee and its lender |
| Supply chain and distribution | Approved suppliers and the franchisor network | Suppliers and the franchisor through margins | The franchisee through input prices |
| Daily operations | The franchisee through wages and working capital | The operator after royalties and rent | The franchisee in each trading period |
| Marketing and advertising fund | Franchisees through a percentage of sales | The system as shared brand exposure | Franchisees collectively if spending misfires |
| Real estate and lease | The franchisor or a third party landlord | The property holder through rent | The franchisee under the lease obligation |

Refranchising carries the logic one step further. When a franchisor sells a company owned restaurant to an operator, it converts a volatile store level profit, exposed to wages, food costs and local demand, into a steadier royalty calculated on the same store sales. The buyer supplies the purchase price and assumes the operating exposure, while the seller keeps the brand income and sheds the cost risk. Repeated across hundreds of units, the shift produces a franchisor whose earnings depend on system sales in the aggregate rather than on the margin of any single kitchen. The movement resembles [a corporate separation](/2026/11/06/how-corporate-spin-offs-work/) in its effect, because an integrated operator is divided into a light holding layer that keeps the durable claim and a working layer that carries the costs, although refranchising reaches that division by licence and sale of individual stores rather than by distributing shares. Some large systems moved toward near total franchising, operating only a small fraction of their units directly and holding the rest as royalty bearing licences. The risk does not disappear in that movement. It relocates, downward to operators and outward to their lenders and landlords, while the franchisor retains the claim that is calculated first, on gross sales, before the operator residual is known.

### Do franchised restaurants fail less often than independent ones?

The evidence is contested and depends heavily on how failure is counted. Timothy Bates, in survival analysis published in 1995, found young franchised outlets discontinued at rates equal to or higher than comparable independents, while industry figures using different denominators report lower franchise closure rates.

The disagreement in that short answer is the subject of the section that follows, because the growth era generated a durable piece of sales language, the claim that franchising is a safer route into business ownership than starting alone. That claim sold licences for decades. Its persistence is itself a structural fact, because a system that recruits operators benefits from a belief in superior survival, and the franchisor controls much of the data from which the comparison is built, including the outlet counts, the transfer records and the definition of a closure. Separating the genuine support a good system provides from that statistical reputation requires the survival evidence to be read on its own terms.

## Failure and the Survival Studies

The most cited challenge to the safety claim came from Timothy Bates, an economist whose survival analysis of franchise and independent small business startups, published in the Journal of Small Business Management in 1995 and extended in related work, followed cohorts of new firms and measured how long they survived and why they exited. Bates drew on United States Census Bureau data covering firms that entered business in the mid nineteen eighties, comparing franchised outlets with independent startups while controlling for characteristics such as owner education, capitalisation and sector. His central finding ran against the sales language of the industry. In his samples, young franchised outlets were discontinued at rates equal to or higher than comparable independents, and franchise ownership did not deliver the survival advantage that promotional material implied. Bates also reported that franchised firms tended to start with more capital and still failed to convert that advantage into longer life, a result that directed attention toward the fixed claims on a franchise unit, its royalties, fees and required purchases, as burdens a young business carries from its first day.

The methodological fight that followed was largely a fight over denominators. A survival rate can be made to look strong or weak depending on whether the base includes every outlet that ever opened, only outlets open at the start of a period, or only systems that report complete data. Industry studies associated with the International Franchise Association and with research firms working from franchisor supplied data tended to count in ways that favoured longevity, in some cases surveying franchisors about outlets over a long window and treating a unit that changed hands as a continuing success. Critics answered that a transfer is not the same event as survival by the original owner. When an operator sells a struggling unit to a new buyer, the location may remain open and the system may record no closure, yet the first owner has exited, often with a loss that the headline closure rate never registers. The distinction between closure and transfer is therefore not a technicality. It is the seam where operator failure can be hidden inside system continuity, and any honest reading of outlet data has to hold the two categories apart.

Disclosure law gives the reader a partial instrument for that work. Item 20 of the Franchise Disclosure Document requires franchisors to publish outlet tables covering openings, closings, transfers, terminations and units sold but not yet open, broken down over three years and separated between franchised and company owned locations. Those tables show movement inside a system with a precision that marketing claims lack, and a reader can compute churn by adding terminations, non renewals, reacquisitions and closures against the base of outlets. What Item 20 does not show is equally important. The tables record the fate of locations, not the fate of the people who owned them, so they cannot reveal whether a transfer rescued an owner or merely passed a weak unit to a successor. They also say nothing about profitability, debt service or the price an exiting operator received. A system can post a low closure rate and still generate steady operator losses through transfers, and a system can post visible closures while its surviving operators earn well. Bates kept the argument at the level of the firm and its owner, and that choice is why his work remains the reference point. Measured by locations, franchising can look stable. Measured by the survival and returns of the individuals who bought in, his samples found young franchised outlets discontinued at rates equal to or higher than comparable independents, and the burden of proof shifted permanently onto anyone claiming the opposite.

## When a Whole Network Fails Together

Individual failure is absorbed quietly by a large system, a unit at a time, through transfer or closure. Network failure is a different event, and it arrives when a single operator has grown so large that its distress becomes the system distress across an entire region. Multi unit operators hold dozens or hundreds of restaurants under related entities, often financed by a single credit facility and bound together by cross default clauses in their franchise and loan documents. A cross default clause provides that a default under one agreement, one lease or one loan can be treated as a default under the others, so a cash shortfall that begins in a weak market does not stay contained there. The lender can accelerate obligations across the portfolio, the franchisor can assert termination rights across the portfolio, and the operator loses the ability to protect its strong units by surrendering only its weak ones. Scale, which in good trading looks like efficiency, becomes in poor trading a mechanism for transmitting failure from the worst restaurant to every restaurant under the same signature.

Bankruptcy reorganises those claims without erasing them. When a large operator files under Chapter 11 of the United States Bankruptcy Code, the automatic stay halts most collection and termination efforts while the debtor attempts to restructure, and the estate gains the power to assume or reject unexpired leases, subject to statutory limits and court approval. Lease rejection is the sharp edge of the process for a restaurant portfolio, because a debtor can shed the locations whose rent exceeds their trading value and keep the sites worth retaining, converting a fixed property burden into a smaller restructured one while landlords are left with capped rejection claims. The franchisor faces a parallel frustration. Its termination notice, issued for unpaid royalties or standards breaches before the filing, meets the automatic stay, and its ability to reclaim territory or enforce cure amounts runs through the bankruptcy court rather than through the contract alone. That meeting between a private termination right and a federal stay explains why franchisors negotiate inside operator bankruptcies instead of simply ending the agreements, a position very different from the [liquidation process](/2026/11/16/how-chapter-7-liquidation-works/) in which a trustee sells the assets and the business ends rather than being restructured.

The resolution of a large operator collapse usually takes the form of portfolio sales, market by market. Rather than selling hundreds of restaurants to a single buyer, the estate, the lender and the franchisor divide the estate into regional packages that existing operators or new entrants can finance and staff, with the franchisor approving each buyer as a franchisee and often contributing revised terms, remodel timelines or temporary royalty relief to make the packages saleable. Some units close in the division, particularly sites that were marginal before the collapse and survive in a portfolio only because shared overhead disguised their weakness. Others transfer at prices that reflect distressed cash flow rather than replacement cost. Through the whole proceeding the brand usually survives the operator, and the reason is structural rather than sentimental. The trademark, the recipes, the supply contracts and the consumer habit belong to the system, not to the failed company, and the restaurants themselves retain value to a successor operator precisely because the sign above the door still draws trade. The failed operator equity is wiped out, its lenders recover in part, its landlords lose the rejected sites, and the franchisor emerges with the same brand collecting royalties from new owners. A network failure therefore tests the thesis in its purest form. The party that supplied the capital absorbs the loss, the parties holding claims on sales negotiate their recoveries, and the owner of the format, which risked the least capital in the units, keeps the asset that made the units worth buying.

## The Complication: The Case That the Model Is Fair

The strongest defence of franchising does not deny the division of capital and risk described in the earlier sections. It argues that the division is efficient, that both parties understand it in advance, and that a contract freely priced between informed adults needs no further justification. Paul Rubin, writing in the Journal of Law and Economics in 1978, supplied the foundational version of that argument. Rubin treated franchising as a solution to two practical problems, the difficulty of raising capital for expansion and the difficulty of monitoring managers in distant outlets. A salaried manager in a company store has weak incentives to control costs or protect quality, because the profit belongs to someone else, while an owner operator keeps the residual after expenses and therefore watches the till, the waste bin and the dining room with an owner attention no supervisor can replicate. On the Rubin account, the franchise fee and royalty are the price of that incentive structure, and the arrangement persists because it lowers the total cost of running a dispersed network below the cost of the company owned alternative.

Francine Lafontaine deepened the defence with empirical work that reframed the royalty itself. In her 1992 study in the RAND Journal of Economics, Lafontaine examined the mix of fixed fees and sales royalties across franchise systems and interpreted that mix through agency theory, in which both parties have effort to supply and both need incentives to supply it. The franchisor must keep investing in the brand, in product development and in system support, and a royalty on sales gives the franchisor a continuing stake in outlet performance that a single upfront fee would not. The franchisee must supply daily effort, and a lower fixed fee with a sales based royalty leaves the operator with a larger share of each additional dollar earned through that effort. Seen through the Lafontaine lens, the royalty is a success fee both sides priced in advance, a sharing rule that rises when sales rise and falls when sales fall, rather than a fixed rent extracted regardless of results.

The owner operator point deserves its full weight. A restaurant is an unforgiving business of perishable stock and thin margins, and an owner on the premises during the Friday rush changes outcomes that no manual can govern. Franchising harnesses that presence, and the operator receives in exchange a tested menu, a recognised name, collective advertising and a supply chain whose purchasing power a single independent could never command. For an entrant without a proven concept, the purchase is rational even at a substantial royalty, because the alternative is a different and riskier restaurant, unknown to customers, negotiating alone with suppliers and lenders.

The thesis survives that case, but it narrows under it, and the narrowing should be stated precisely. Efficiency explains why the form exists, why it outcompeted company ownership across whole categories and why intelligent operators keep choosing it. Efficiency does not distribute the loss when a system sales fall, and the contract allocates that loss downward by design. A royalty calculated on gross sales declines in proportion to sales, yet it never reaches zero while the doors are open, and it is paid before wages arrears, supplier arrears and operator drawings are resolved in the cash order of a failing unit. Rent behaves the same way, and required purchases hold their margin inside the input price. The franchisor therefore shares the downturn in a limited and senior form, while the operator absorbs it in an unlimited and residual form, down to the loss of the invested capital and the personal guarantees behind it. Both statements can be true at once. The model can be an efficient way to organise restaurants and a contract that places the deepest loss on the party with the least diversification, holding one restaurant or one small portfolio against a franchisor holding claims on thousands. Fairness, on the strongest opposing case, means the bargain was understood and priced. The thesis answers that a priced bargain can still be an asymmetric one, and that the asymmetry, not the existence of the bargain, is the fact a prospective operator most needs to see clearly.

## Reading a Disclosure Document Like an Analyst

The Franchise Disclosure Document is the one place where the claims of a sales process meet a regulated format, and reading it as an analyst means treating it less as a brochure and more as a set of partial ledgers, each item answering some questions completely and others not at all. Item 7 estimates the initial investment, breaking the total into the franchise fee, build out, equipment, signage, opening inventory, deposits and additional funds for the first months of trading. The range in Item 7 sets the scale of the capital at risk and exposes how much of that capital flows to the franchisor and its approved suppliers before the first sale, against how much remains as working capital under operator control. A wide range signals that site conditions drive the true cost, while additional funds stated as a thin figure against a heavy build out cost signals an assumption of rapid break even that the rest of the document should be made to justify.

Item 19 is the most debated page in the document, because it governs what a franchisor may say about money.

### What is Item 19 in a franchise disclosure document?

Item 19 governs financial performance representations in the Franchise Disclosure Document. A franchisor may include historical or projected figures for sales, income or profit only under stated conditions, with a reasonable basis disclosed, or may state that no representation is made.

The absence permitted by Item 19 is itself information. A system that publishes detailed unit economics, with the subset of outlets covered and the period stated, exposes itself to comparison and to challenge, while a system that declines to make a representation leaves the prospective operator to reconstruct earnings from royalty mathematics, from Item 20 churn and from conversations with existing owners listed in the document exhibits. Item 20 supplies the movement record described in the failure discussion, openings, terminations, transfers, non renewals, reacquisitions and closures across three years, and the analyst reading treats transfers with the same suspicion as closures, because both represent an original owner leaving. Unit level churn, computed year by year and compared between franchised and company owned columns, reveals whether growth comes from new demand or from recycling existing territory through successive owners. A system can grow its outlet count while churning its operators, and only the Item 20 tables make that pattern visible.

The method, stated whole, is triangulation. Item 7 fixes the capital exposed, Item 19 either supplies or withholds the earnings claim, Item 20 shows how long owners and locations persist, Item 3 records the litigation and bankruptcy history, and Item 8 discloses the restrictions and possible markups inside the supply chain. Together the items answer the questions the document is built to answer, namely what the format costs, what the franchisor asserts, how stable the network has been and where the franchisor earns besides the royalty. The questions the document structurally cannot answer matter as much. It cannot state the profit of a particular site on a particular street, cannot price the value of an owner labour across a decade of evenings and weekends, and cannot predict whether a territory will be encroached, a menu will age or a brand will hold its cultural position. Those unknowns remain with the operator after every page has been read, which is why the document functions as an instrument for narrowing risk rather than removing it, and why the most careful reading ends at the same place the contract begins, with residual uncertainty assigned to the buyer.

## How Outsiders Judge a Franchise System

Insiders argue about fairness and effort, but the outsiders who finance, house and purchase franchise systems judge them by a colder set of signals, read from the same documents and from payment behaviour. A lender begins with royalty coverage, the relationship between a unit or a portfolio operating cash flow and the fixed claims that stand ahead of debt service, including royalty, rent and advertising contributions. Strong coverage means the operator residual can absorb a sales decline and still meet the loan payment, while thin coverage means the lender is effectively relying on the brand strength to keep sales from ever testing the structure. Lenders also price the transferability of the collateral, because a restaurant full of specialised equipment has a low resale value outside its system, whereas a unit inside a recognised brand can be sold to an approved successor operator, and that exit route is part of what makes franchise lending possible at rates an unknown independent cannot obtain.

A landlord reads the system differently again. The question for a property owner is covenant strength over the length of a lease, and a national brand with a stable outlet record offers a different covenant from a local name, even when the legal tenant is a small franchisee company. Landlords examine closure and transfer rates for the territory, the remodel obligations that might strain the tenant midway through the term, and whether the franchisor itself will stand behind the site through a guarantee, a direct lease or a right to step in and reassign the unit after an operator failure. The table of capital and risk set out in the growth discussion is, for a landlord, a practical map, because it shows which party can be pursued when trading falters and which party merely loses its opportunity. Concentration cuts both ways in that judgement. A centre anchored by units of one large multi unit operator gains a single sophisticated tenant and loses diversification, so the failure of that one company can darken several storefronts in a single proceeding.

An acquirer buying a franchisor reads the deepest layer of all. The value of a franchise company lies in the durability of its royalty stream, so diligence concentrates on the same signals with a longer horizon, the litigation history in Item 3 as a record of how the system handles conflict with its own operators, the rate of terminations and non renewals as a predictor of future legal cost, the dependence of system sales on a handful of large operators whose departure or collapse would move the total, and the age and remodel status of the estate, since a system approaching a costly refresh cycle carries a hidden claim on operator capital that will test every relationship in the network. Where the signals align, clean churn, moderate litigation, diversified operators and royalties covered comfortably by unit cash flow, the stream is priced as an annuity. Where they diverge, the acquirer discounts for the day the residual margin at unit level turns negative across enough outlets to convert private operator losses into public system decline.

## Reform Proposals and What Each Would Change

Criticism of the franchise bargain has produced a standing programme of legal reform, and each proposal targets a specific clause or practice rather than the format itself. Good cause termination statutes, enacted in a number of American states and supported over many years by franchisee associations including the American Association of Franchisees and Dealers, would restrict a franchisor ability to terminate or refuse renewal except for defined cause, with notice and an opportunity to cure. Supporters argue that an operator who has invested in a location should not lose the business over a minor or pretextual breach after building local goodwill that the brand then reassigns. The International Franchise Association, representing franchisors and many franchisees, has opposed broad versions of such statutes on the ground that weak termination rights trap a system with operators who damage the brand for every other owner in the network, and that parties should be held to the contract they signed.

Encroachment protection follows a parallel line. Franchisee advocates propose limits, compensation duties or impact studies before a franchisor may place a new unit, a company store or an alternative channel close enough to divert sales from an existing operator. The franchisor answer, stated by the International Franchise Association in policy debates, is that consumer demand and delivery channels change shape continuously, that rigid territories freeze a system while competitors move freely, and that the disclosure document already reveals whether a territory is exclusive, so the protection should be bought in negotiation rather than imposed by statute. Advertising fund accounting proposals address a quieter grievance, the suspicion that contributions collected as a percentage of sales for brand marketing are absorbed into general corporate purposes. Reformers seek segregated accounts, annual audited statements to contributors and limits on administrative charges against the fund. Franchisors answer that brand building cannot be separated cleanly from corporate activity, that rigid accounting raises costs without raising the quality of campaigns, and that existing fraud and contract law already reaches genuine diversion.

The most consequential proposals concern financial performance disclosure. Franchisee associations and some state regulators have pressed for mandatory, standardised disclosure of unit level economics, replacing the optional structure of Item 19 with required figures on sales distribution and, in stronger versions, on costs and margins. Such a mandate would change the recruiting balance of the whole industry, because systems with weak operator economics could no longer recruit on brand strength alone while declining to state a representation. Opponents, including the International Franchise Association, argue that unit results vary so widely by site, operator and market that mandated averages would mislead more than silence does, and that compiling audited figures imposes costs that fall hardest on young systems. Each proposal, fairly weighed, trades one error for another. Stronger operator protection reduces opportunistic termination and silent encroachment, and raises the risk that a system cannot discipline failure or adapt its map. Stronger disclosure reduces uninformed entry, and raises the risk that a single published figure is treated as a promise. The debate persists because the underlying division persists, and every reform is, at bottom, an argument about where the next dollar of loss should land.

## The Structure That Remains

Strip the brands, the menus and the personalities away and a simple machine remains, elegant in design and exact in its allocation of consequences. One party owns a name, a method and a set of standards, and licenses that package to another party who builds the restaurant, hires the staff, signs the lease and borrows the money. Payment flows upward as a percentage of everything the restaurant sells, calculated before the operator knows whether the period produced a profit, while the costs that decide profit, food, labour, rent and debt, sit below that line with the operator. The machine endures because it genuinely works when sales are strong. The brand draws the customer, the method disciplines the kitchen, the collective fund buys recognition no single shop could afford, and the residual left after every senior claim can reward an able operator handsomely across a portfolio of units. The same machine shows its character when sales soften, because the claims do not soften in the same order or to the same depth, and the residual, which is the operator share, is the figure that absorbs the difference until it disappears.

The habit worth keeping from the whole examination is a single exercise in attention. Follow one dollar of sales through the claims on it, from the counter where it arrives, through the royalty taken as a fraction of the gross, the advertising contribution taken the same way, the food and packaging bought inside the approved chain, the wages for the shift that produced it, the rent for the ground beneath it and the debt service on the building around it, until the small remainder that belongs to the operator comes into view. That remainder is the truth of the arrangement, the margin on which families build equity or lose it, the figure whose sign decides whether renewals are signed gladly or territories are surrendered, and the number no brand reputation can substitute for. A royalty statement can look modest as a percentage and still consume the whole remainder in a weak quarter, while a strong quarter can make the same percentage feel invisible, which is why the structure is easiest to misjudge in good trading and impossible to misread in poor trading. Systems rise when that remainder stays healthy across hundreds of kitchens at once and decline, slowly and then visibly, when it does not.

Nothing in that structure requires villains, and nothing in it is repealed by admiration for the founders who built it or the operators who prosper inside it. A rental agreement for a business format can be a fair price for a proven method and still place the deepest risk on the tenant of the format, and both facts travel together through every disclosure document, every bankruptcy schedule and every refranchising announcement the industry produces. The reader who carries the single exercise away, tracing the dollar and watching where it thins, holds the key that unlocks each of those documents in turn, a habit as useful in a set of [companion notes](https://vaultbook.net) as in the pages of a disclosure file itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is a restaurant franchise in plain terms?
A restaurant franchise is a business arrangement in which an independent operator, called a franchisee, pays for the right to open and run a restaurant under an established brand owned by another company, called a franchisor. The franchisee supplies the capital, hires the staff, and manages daily operations, while the franchisor supplies the name, the menu system, the operating standards, and the training that make one location resemble another. The relationship is governed by a written agreement that sets fees, territory, quality rules, and the length of the arrangement. The customer sees a single brand, but behind the counter two separate businesses share the revenue, the risk, and the responsibility for keeping the promise the sign makes.

### Q: How does a franchisor make money from franchised restaurants?
A franchisor earns income from franchised restaurants through several recurring streams rather than through the sale of food itself. The largest stream is usually the royalty, a percentage of each restaurant's gross sales paid weekly or monthly for the continued use of the brand and system. A second stream comes from the initial franchise fee paid when each new restaurant opens. A third comes from contributions to a shared advertising fund, although those contributions are held for marketing rather than treated as profit. Some franchisors also earn rent by owning land or buildings leased to franchisees, and some earn margins on equipment, technology services, or approved supplies sold into the system. Each stream ties payment to system size rather than to any single restaurant's profit.

### Q: What does the initial franchise fee pay for?
The initial franchise fee is a one-time payment made when a franchise agreement is signed, and it buys entry into the system rather than the restaurant itself. It covers the franchisor's cost of recruiting and evaluating the operator, granting the license to use the trademarks, and providing the opening package of training, manuals, site selection guidance, and launch support. It also compensates the franchisor for the value of a proven format, since the operator receives a tested menu, kitchen layout, and operating procedures instead of developing them from scratch. The fee does not cover construction, equipment, inventory, or working capital, which the franchisee finances separately through other sources. Training often continues after opening through field visits and refresher sessions for managers and crew.

### Q: Why do franchisors charge royalties on sales rather than profit?
Franchisors charge royalties on gross sales because sales are visible, verifiable, and difficult to dispute, while profit depends on how each operator records costs. Two restaurants with identical sales can report very different profits depending on rent, wages, waste, and accounting choices, so a profit based royalty would invite constant disagreement and auditing. A sales based royalty also aligns the arrangement with the franchisor's contribution, which is the brand and system that generate customer traffic rather than the operator's cost control. Point of sale systems report sales automatically, making collection simple and predictable. The trade-off is that the royalty is owed in weak months as well as strong ones, regardless of the restaurant's own margin.

### Q: What is an advertising fund in franchising?
An advertising fund is a pooled account into which every franchisee, and usually every company owned restaurant, pays a set percentage of sales for shared marketing. The franchisor administers the fund and spends it on campaigns that no single operator could finance alone, such as national television, sponsorships, menu photography, and the maintenance of the brand's digital ordering platforms. The money is restricted in purpose: it belongs to the system rather than to the franchisor's general revenue, and agreements typically require reporting on how it was spent. Pooling solves a collective problem, because a brand's reputation is shared, and one operator's silence would weaken the recognition that every other operator depends upon for traffic. Fund accounting therefore receives close attention during disputes between operators and brand owners.

### Q: Who owns the restaurant building and land in a franchise?
Ownership of the building and land varies by system, and the franchise agreement and any separate lease define the arrangement. In many cases the franchisee leases land from a third party landlord, builds or fits out the restaurant at personal expense, and owns the equipment and furnishings inside. In other systems the franchisor, or a related property company, buys the land, constructs the building, and leases the whole site to the franchisee, collecting rent alongside royalties. A third pattern has the franchisee owning both land and building outright. Each pattern distributes risk differently: franchisor ownership gives the brand control over the site after an operator leaves, while franchisee ownership gives the operator an asset independent of the brand.

### Q: What is a franchise disclosure document used for?
A franchise disclosure document is a standardized legal document that a franchisor must give a prospective franchisee before any agreement is signed or any payment is taken. It presents, in a prescribed format, the franchisor's history, litigation record, fees, estimated initial investment, territory terms, obligations of both parties, financial statements, and a list of existing and former franchisees. Its purpose is comparability and cooling off: a candidate can place two systems side by side, read the same categories of information in the same order, and contact current operators before committing capital. The document discloses rather than approves, since regulators require its delivery without endorsing the investment, and its length reflects how much of the relationship is fixed before negotiation begins.

### Q: How long does a franchise agreement usually last?
A franchise agreement usually runs for a fixed term long enough for the operator to recover the cost of building and equipping the restaurant, commonly ten, fifteen, or twenty years depending on the brand and the size of the investment. Fast food systems with heavy construction costs tend toward longer terms, while smaller formats with lower build costs often use shorter ones. During the term, the agreement governs fees, standards, territory, and transfer rules, and it can usually be ended early only for defined breaches after notice and a chance to cure. When the term expires, the rights granted under it expire as well, unless a renewal right in the original agreement is exercised under its stated conditions.

### Q: Can a franchisor stop a franchisee from renewing an agreement?
A franchisor can refuse renewal when the franchisee fails to meet the conditions that the original agreement attaches to renewal, and those conditions are disclosed before the first term begins. Typical conditions include being in good standing, settling all amounts owed, signing the renewal agreement in its then standard form, paying a renewal fee, and remodeling the restaurant to current brand standards. State laws in some jurisdictions add further limits, requiring good cause or advance notice before a franchisor declines to renew. The practical effect is that renewal is a right with conditions rather than a guarantee: an operator who has met the standards and accepts the updated terms generally renews, while persistent quality failures give the franchisor grounds to end the relationship.

### Q: What happens to a franchise when the franchisee sells the restaurant?
When a franchisee sells a restaurant, the franchise itself does not pass automatically with the building and equipment, because the agreement is a personal grant to an approved operator. The sale is structured as a transfer, which normally requires the franchisor's written consent, payment of a transfer fee, and evidence that the buyer meets the system's financial and operational qualifications and completes the required training. The buyer usually signs either the remaining term of the existing agreement or a new agreement, and the seller may be required to settle outstanding obligations and, in some systems, to remodel or bring the restaurant to standard before the handover. This approval step protects the brand from an unqualified operator acquiring its sign by purchase alone.

### Q: Why do franchisors require approved suppliers?
Franchisors require approved suppliers so that the food, packaging, and equipment in every restaurant meet the same specifications, which is the practical basis of a brand's consistency. A specified ingredient behaves predictably in cooking, carries traceable sourcing for food safety recalls, and arrives in volumes that support negotiated pricing across the whole system. Approval also lets the franchisor test items against its kitchen equipment and hold times before they reach customers. The arrangement has a debated side, since a captive supply chain can limit an operator's ability to buy more cheaply elsewhere, and some franchisors receive payments from approved vendors. The stated mechanism, however, is uniformity: a customer ordering the same item in different cities expects the same product, and uncontrolled sourcing breaks that expectation.

### Q: What is the difference between a franchisee and a licensee?
A franchisee operates under a continuing relationship in which the franchisor licenses a trademark, provides a complete business format, and exercises significant control over how the business runs, in exchange for fees tied to that system. A licensee, by contrast, receives permission to use a narrower right, such as a name, a recipe, a character, or a piece of technology, with far less control over daily operations and usually a simpler fee structure. The distinction carries legal weight: franchise relationships trigger disclosure requirements and, in some jurisdictions, protections around termination and renewal that ordinary licenses do not. In restaurants, the practical test is depth of control: the more the brand dictates menus, suppliers, training, inspections, and operating hours, the more the arrangement resembles franchising rather than licensing.

### Q: How is franchising different from a chain owning its stores?
In a company owned chain, one corporation owns the restaurants, employs the staff, keeps the full sales revenue, and absorbs the full cost and risk of every location. In franchising, independent operators own or lease their individual restaurants, employ their own staff, keep the revenue after royalties and other fees, and carry the local investment risk themselves. The franchisor expands using other operators' capital, which allows faster growth with less corporate borrowing, while the chain model gives the corporation direct control and the entire margin. Many large brands mix both models, operating flagship or training restaurants directly while franchising the majority, and the balance between the two shapes how much of the system's profit and risk sits with the brand owner.

### Q: What does a franchise territory protect against?
A franchise territory is a defined geographic area, drawn by radius, map boundary, or population, within which the franchisor promises limits on competition from its own brand. Protection typically means the franchisor will not open another restaurant, or grant another franchise, inside that area during the term, so the operator's local sales are not divided by a second unit of the same brand nearby. The protection has boundaries of its own: many agreements reserve the franchisor's right to sell through other channels inside the territory, such as airports, stadiums, grocery products, or delivery platforms, and some grants are non-exclusive from the start. Territory therefore protects against direct same brand encroachment, not against rival brands, changing traffic patterns, or channels the agreement expressly reserves.

### Q: Why do franchise agreements require remodels?
Franchise agreements require remodels at set intervals because a restaurant brand is a physical promise, and worn dining rooms, dated signage, and old kitchen equipment gradually contradict it. Scheduled refurbishment, often required every five to ten years or at renewal, updates the restaurant to the system's current design, technology, and accessibility standards, covering items such as ordering kiosks, drive-through layouts, seating, and exterior branding. Remodels also protect neighboring operators, since one neglected location shapes how customers judge the whole brand. The cost falls on the franchisee, which makes remodel clauses a frequent source of tension: the franchisor gains a refreshed system without spending its own capital, while the operator must judge the remaining term against the size of the required investment.

### Q: What is a master franchise agreement?
A master franchise agreement grants one party, the master franchisee, the rights to develop and sub-franchise a brand within a large territory, often an entire country or region. The master franchisee recruits local operators, collects their fees and royalties, provides their training and support, and adapts the system to local laws, tastes, and supply conditions, remitting an agreed share of the income to the original franchisor. The structure lets a brand enter a distant market through a partner who understands that market and finances the local infrastructure, rather than building a foreign subsidiary. It also adds a layer between brand and restaurant: quality control, communication, and fee flows all pass through the master franchisee, whose competence therefore shapes the brand's reputation across the whole territory.

### Q: How do franchisors choose franchisees?
Franchisors choose franchisees by screening for capital, experience, and temperament against criteria set out in their disclosure and recruitment materials. Financial screening examines net worth and liquid assets, because an undercapitalized operator can fail even with strong sales once construction overruns or a slow opening stretch arrives. Operational screening looks for restaurant or business management experience, although many systems train first time operators and instead weight character references, credit history, and performance during discovery and interview stages. Multi-unit candidates face additional review of their existing operations. The franchisor's interest is protective as much as selective: each operator carries the brand's sign in a local market, and a failure closes a location, harms nearby operators, and weakens the record shown to future candidates.

### Q: What is refranchising and why do companies do it?
Refranchising is the sale of company owned restaurants to franchisees, converting stores the corporation operated itself into independently operated franchised units. Companies do it to raise capital from the sale proceeds, reduce operating costs and debt, and shift the daily risk of wages, rent, and local trading conditions onto operators, while retaining royalty and rent income from the same restaurants. The strategy also changes the franchisor's earnings profile toward steadier fee income and away from the volatility of food and labor costs. The reverse process, sometimes called reacquisition, occurs when a brand buys franchised restaurants back. Refranchising does not change what customers see, since the sign, menu, and standards remain, but it changes who employs the staff and who absorbs a location's losses.

### Q: Who pays restaurant staff in a franchised outlet?
In a franchised outlet, the franchisee is the employer of the restaurant staff and pays their wages, payroll taxes, and benefits from the restaurant's own revenue. The franchisee recruits, hires, schedules, trains to the brand's standards, and disciplines or dismisses employees, and the franchisee's own business entity appears on the employment paperwork. The franchisor sets the service standards the staff must deliver and may provide training materials and systems, but it does not issue paychecks to another operator's crew. This separation matters in disputes over wages or working conditions, where the legal question often becomes how much control the franchisor exercised in practice. For the customer the distinction is invisible; for the worker, the employer is the local operator behind the counter, not the corporation behind the brand.

### Q: Why do some franchised restaurants close while the brand keeps growing?
Individual franchised restaurants close for local reasons even while the brand grows overall, because system growth measures the net of openings and closures across hundreds or thousands of separate businesses. A single location can fail through a poor site, road changes that divert traffic, rent increases, an undercapitalized operator, or weak local management, none of which reflects demand for the brand in other markets. Openings in stronger territories can outnumber such closures for years, so the total count rises while particular addresses disappear. Franchising makes this pattern more visible, since each restaurant is its own business with its own finances rather than a branch cross-subsidized by a corporation. A closure therefore records the failure of one local enterprise operating under the brand, not necessarily the failure of the brand itself.
