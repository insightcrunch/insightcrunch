On 19 March 1950, a presidential notification in New Delhi appointed Sukumar Sen, the Chief Secretary of West Bengal, as the first Chief Election Commissioner of India, and with that single sheet of paper the office of the sukumar sen first election commissioner acquired a holder before it had acquired a staff, a budget line, a building of its own, or a single precedent anywhere in the world for the task it existed to perform. Sen had roughly eighteen months to design a general election for about 173 million electors, most of whom could not read the name of any candidate, in a country whose administrative map had been torn and redrawn by Partition less than three years earlier. This article defends one namable claim about what he did in those eighteen months: Sen's 1951 to 1952 architecture was institutionally improvised under acute resource constraints, and the founding template that improvisation produced established the institutional pattern through which every later Indian election has been run. The claim is architectural rather than biographical. It directs attention away from the man as hero and toward four decisions, on polling stations, on the symbol ballot, on the electoral roll, and on the phased calendar, each of which can be read in the Commission's own statistical record of the first general election.

## The Appointment of 19 March 1950 and the Empty Office

The Election Commission of India came into legal existence on 25 January 1950, one day before the Constitution of the Republic entered into force, because Article 324 of that Constitution vested the superintendence, direction and control of elections to Parliament and to the state legislatures in a commission whose chief was to be appointed by the President. The constitutional text, whose drafting history is traced in the account of [the founding-period long-arc context](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), gave the office formidable powers and almost no machinery. It named a function, protected the chief's tenure against easy removal, and stopped there. Everything else, from the paper of the ballot to the steel of the ballot box, had to be invented, borrowed or commandeered by whoever held the post.

The man President Rajendra Prasad appointed by the notification of 19 March 1950 was fifty two years old and carried an unusual combination of equipment for the job. Sukumar Sen had been born on 2 January 1898, had read mathematics at Presidency College in Calcutta and at University College in London, taking a gold medal in the subject, and had entered the Indian Civil Service in 1922. Mathematics mattered to the appointment more than any speech about democracy did. The first election was, at its core, an arithmetic and logistics problem of a size no administration on earth had attempted: count a population, convert it into constituencies, register every adult, print and move hundreds of millions of pieces of paper, and count what came back. A commissioner who thought in numbers, and who had spent twenty eight years learning how district administration actually behaved under strain, was a rational choice for a problem of that shape.

Sen also brought the hardest schooling the Bengal cadre could offer. He had served as a district officer and as a judge in Bengal districts, and from 1947 to 1950 he had been Chief Secretary of West Bengal, the senior administrative post of a province absorbing the shock of Partition: refugee columns crossing from East Bengal, a capital city crowded with the displaced, a provincial secretariat rebuilding itself after the departure of Muslim and British officers. The correspondence of those years, preserved in the National Archives of India and in the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, shows a chief secretary negotiating with Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and with Jawaharlal Nehru over the administrative consequences of a border drawn through his province. When Sen moved to Delhi in March 1950, he therefore understood, from direct and recent experience, exactly how thin the state's capacity was, how slowly its paper moved, and how much of any grand design would have to be executed by district officers who already had ten other duties. The improvisation that followed was not innocence. It was the work of an administrator who knew precisely what the machine could not do, and designed around the gaps.

The office he inherited can be measured by its absences. The Commission in 1950 was a one member body, and it remained one throughout Sen's tenure, which closed on 19 December 1958. Its central secretariat in Delhi was a handful of officers. It had no field formation of its own in any district of India, no warehouses, no printing presses, no transport fleet, and no cadre. Article 324(6) supplied the substitute, in a sentence that became the load bearing wall of the entire founding architecture: the President or the Governor of a state was required, when so requested by the Commission, to make available such staff as might be necessary for the discharge of its functions. Sen's whole design can be read as an elaboration of that sentence. Where a richer or older institution might have built, he borrowed, and the borrowing was written into the constitutional text before he arrived, waiting for an administrator willing to treat deputation not as a makeshift embarrassment but as the architecture itself.

### How much time did Sukumar Sen have to prepare the election?

Sen had roughly eighteen months between his appointment on 19 March 1950 and the opening of polling on 25 October 1951, and the interval was shorter than the calendar suggests, because the Representation of the People Act governing the conduct of elections received assent in July 1951, three months before the first ballots were cast in the Himalayan foothills.

The eighteen months were not empty, and the roll making had in fact begun before Sen's appointment, a point to which this article returns when it reaches the electoral roll. What Sen had to do inside the interval was to convert a constitutional promise into an operating system: settle the form of the ballot, settle the constituency map within the limits the law allowed, persuade fourteen state governments and the administrations of the Part B and Part C states to lend their district staffs, arrange the manufacture of millions of ballot boxes, print the rolls and the ballot papers, instruct parties on symbols, and fix a calendar that a single borrowed machine could actually execute across a subcontinent of deserts, deltas, jungles and high snow passes. Every one of those tasks had a deadline set not by administrative convenience but by weather, by harvest, and by the constitutional need to seat an elected Parliament in place of the provisional one. The founding election was designed against a clock, and the clock was one of the resource constraints that shaped its architecture as surely as money did.

## The Scale of the Task in the Commission's Own Numbers

The Commission's Statistical Report on the first general election, the document Sen's organisation published after the count, records the dimensions of what was attempted, and its figures remain the spine of every serious account. The report counted 173,212,343 registered electors, excluding Jammu and Kashmir, drawn from a population of 361,088,090 recorded by the census of 1951. For the House of the People, 489 seats were filled from 401 constituencies, because the map still carried multi member seats: 314 single member constituencies, 86 double member constituencies and one three member constituency, the double and triple seats existing so that reserved representation for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes could be delivered inside joint electorates rather than through the separate electorates the republic had rejected. Alongside the parliamentary contest ran the elections to the state legislative assemblies, 3,283 seats in all, so that the average elector who reached a polling station in the winter of 1951 to 1952 was voting in two elections on the same visit, under two rolls entries, for two legislatures.

For the 489 parliamentary seats, 1,874 candidates contested, put forward by 53 political parties and by the large independent contingent that founding elections always produce. The polling apparatus that served them comprised 196,084 polling stations, of which 27,527 were reserved for women voters, a figure that quietly records how seriously the Commission took the problem of bringing women, many of them observing purdah, into a public act of citizenship. The votes were cast into more than two million steel ballot boxes. The rolls alone consumed the labour of about 16,500 clerks engaged for six months to type and collate them, and some 380,000 reams of paper went into their printing. Sen's report and the Commission's narrative Report on the First General Elections in India, published in 1955, present these numbers without astonishment, in the flat tone of an audit. Read together, they describe the largest administrative operation the Indian state had ever mounted, larger than any census, conducted by an institution that owned almost none of the instruments it used.

One further number disciplines every romantic reading of the founding. Of the 173 million registered electors, roughly 85 percent could not read. The census of 1951 put literacy at under one fifth of the population, and the electorate, being adult and disproportionately rural, was less literate still. Sen's electors could not, in their overwhelming majority, read a candidate's name, a party's name, or the instructions printed at the head of a ballot paper. Any architecture that assumed a reading voter would have disenfranchised the republic at its birth. The founding design therefore had to route choice through the eye and the hand rather than through the letter, and that single demographic fact explains more of the 1951 to 1952 architecture, its symbols, its boxes, its screened compartments and its peculiar paper, than any theory of democracy the founders debated in New Delhi.

## The Four-Choice Founding Map

The argument of this article can be stated as a map. Sen's administration made four architectural choices, each answering a named resource constraint, and each surviving its maker by decades. The map is set out in the table below, which this article calls the Four-Choice Founding Map, so that the reading can be cited and tested against the record rather than admired as a tribute.

| Architectural choice | The decision Sen's Commission made | Resource constraint it answered | Post-1952 continuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polling stations by deputation | About 196,000 polling stations staffed by roughly 750,000 officers lent from state services | No dedicated electoral cadre or field staff existed | Deputation of state and central staff remained the operating core of Indian elections |
| Symbol ballot with separate boxes | Each candidate given a distinct symbol and a separate ballot box bearing that symbol | About 85 percent of electors could not read a name | Symbols became permanent; the separate box yielded to the marked ballot and later to the electronic machine |
| Rolls built by state machinery | Electoral rolls prepared constituency by constituency by state officials under Commission supervision | No central registration machinery; local knowledge sat with district staffs | State chief electoral officers and district machinery still build and revise the rolls |
| Phased calendar | Polling spread from 25 October 1951 to 21 February 1952 in successive phases | One borrowed staff pool and one stock of boxes had to serve the whole country | Multi phase polling remained the standard Indian method for large elections |

A map of this kind is also a warning against the two readings this article argues against. Against the heroic reading, the map insists that the founding achievement was a set of decisions, taken under pressure and open to criticism, not the emanation of one man's virtue; other commissioners, facing the same constraints, might have decided several of them differently, and the article will note where contemporaries argued exactly that. Against the merely descriptive reading, which retells the election as a sequence of colourful events, the map insists that the events had a structure, that the structure was chosen, and that the choices, once made, hardened into the template that the later institution inherited. The Election Commission that later generations would watch expand, quarrel and assert itself was built on Sen's floor plan. Tracing that inheritance is the business of the sections that follow, beginning with the choice that made all the others possible: the decision to run a national election with other people's staff.

## Choice One: A Polling Station in Every Reachable Place, Staffed by Borrowed Hands

The first choice concerned the polling station itself: how many there should be, where they should stand, and whose hands would run them. Sen's Commission decided that the vote would travel to the voter. Polling stations were fixed at about 196,084 in number, a density that worked out at roughly one station for every 880 electors, and the instruction to the district officers who sited them was to keep the voter's journey short, inside the village or the neighbourhood wherever the map allowed. The decision looks generous until its administrative meaning is grasped. Every additional station multiplied the demand for presiding officers, polling officers, ballot boxes, seals, paper and police, in an election already short of all six. Sen accepted the multiplication deliberately. A founding election that asked an elector, and above all a woman elector or an elderly elector, to walk miles to a strange building would have taxed exactly the citizens whose participation the universal franchise existed to secure. Proximity was the architecture's first principle, and it was bought with staff the Commission did not possess.

The staffing solution was deputation on a scale that only the constitutional text made thinkable. The Commission's record puts the polling personnel drawn from the existing services at roughly three quarters of a million people, presiding officers, polling officers, clerks and orderlies seconded from state government offices, schools, courts and local bodies, with teachers forming a large share of the presiding cadre in many districts. These were not election professionals. They were the ordinary district state, revenue clerks and schoolmasters and sub inspectors, pulled from their desks for training and for polling duty, and returned to those desks when the count ended. The Commission supervised them through returning officers who were themselves the district magistrates and deputy commissioners of the existing administration, wearing a second hat issued by Sen's office. In the vocabulary of the founding map, the Election Commission in 1951 was less an organisation than a command layer laid over the whole district administration of India, activated for one season.

The consequences of the deputation choice ran in two directions, and an honest account has to record both. In its favour, deputation made the election affordable and administratively literate. The borrowed officers knew their villages, their languages and their registers; they knew which hamlet belonged to which revenue circle, which caste cluster lived across the river, which landlord's word carried weight and which polling site would flood if the winter rain came. No training programme devised in Delhi in eighteen months could have manufactured that knowledge. Against it, deputation embedded a dependence that the institution never afterwards escaped. The Commission's authority over its borrowed army was real but rented, resting on the cooperation of state governments whose own parties were contestants in the election being run. Sen managed the tension by routing every requisition through the constitutional duty in Article 324(6) and by keeping the Commission's own demands narrow, precise and early. The pattern he set, a small central institution commanding a vast borrowed apparatus whose loyalty it must continually earn, is precisely the pattern whose strains the pillar's opening study examines under [the institution-under-stress framework](/2020/06/03/election-commission-institution-under-stress/). The stress was not a later corruption of the founding design. It was present in the design from the first day, the price of the only staffing model the republic could afford.

### How was the secrecy of the vote protected without a marked ballot?

Secrecy was protected by choreography rather than by printing: the elector received a blank ballot paper, passed alone behind a screened compartment, and dropped the paper into the closed box of the chosen candidate, so that no mark, fold or sign on any paper could afterwards connect a vote to the hand that cast it.

The polling station the voter entered was a spare theatre, and its spareness was designed. Sen's instructions standardised the interior: a table for the presiding officer, the electoral roll marked copy on which each voter's name was struck off as identified, a screened compartment built from bamboo, cloth or furniture, and inside or beside that compartment the row of candidate boxes. The 27,527 stations reserved for women applied the same floor plan with women staff where the administration could supply them, answering the objection, heard in district after district, that a woman observing purdah could not vote before male strangers. Where even that arrangement failed custom, the architecture bent rather than the principle: the franchise was universal, but its staging respected the thresholds voters themselves would cross. Students of the founding settlement will recognise the same negotiating instinct that marked the Constituent Assembly's handling of personal law and language, compromises taken so that a universal rule could operate inside a society that had not been consulted about its details.

## Choice Two: The Symbol, the Blank Paper and the Separate Box

The second choice is the one by which the founding election is remembered, and it deserves its fame, because it solved the republic's hardest problem with a piece of inspired simplicity. Faced with an electorate of whom about 85 percent could not read, Sen's Commission declined to treat reading as the gateway to choice. Instead it rebuilt the act of voting around recognition. Every candidate in every constituency received a symbol. Candidates of the recognised parties carried the party's symbol: the pair of bullocks under a yoke for the Congress, the banyan tree for the Socialist Party, the hut for the Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party, the earthen lamp for the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded in October 1951 and fighting its first election within weeks of its birth under the conditions traced in [the Jana Sangh's long first phase](/2019/09/24/bharatiya-jana-sangh-phase-1951-1977/). Independents, who contested in their thousands, received individual symbols from the free list the Commission maintained. The allotment was settled at a conference with the parties in July 1951, and the symbols were then printed, painted and paraded through the constituencies until the association between mark and candidate settled into the memory of voters who had never held a ballot.

The ballot itself inverted the practice of literate democracies. In 1951 to 1952 there was no printed ballot paper bearing the names of the candidates for the elector to mark. The elector was handed a blank ballot paper, identical for every candidate, and the choice was expressed by the destination of the paper rather than by any mark upon it. Inside the polling station stood a separate steel ballot box for each candidate, the candidate's name and symbol displayed upon it, the boxes distinguished in many districts by colour as well. The voter carried the blank paper behind the screen and dropped it into one box. An elector who could not read a single letter could still count, could still recognise a bullock or a lamp, and could still keep the choice secret, since the paper in the hand looked exactly like every other paper until the moment it vanished into the chosen box. Illiteracy, which the sceptics of universal franchise had treated as a disqualification of the Indian voter, was answered not by tutoring the voter to meet the ballot but by redesigning the ballot to meet the voter.

### How many ballot boxes did one polling station have to hold?

A station had to hold one closed steel box for every candidate in the parliamentary constituency and a parallel set for the assembly constituency, which in a crowded seat meant twenty or more boxes ranked behind the screen, each labelled with a candidate's name and symbol and each sealed at the close.

Count the boxes across the whole election and the manufacturing problem comes into focus: more than two million steel boxes had to be made, distributed, guarded, transported to the count and accounted for, in a country whose steel industry was small and whose railways were still absorbing the dislocations of Partition. The boxes were fabricated in workshops across the provinces to Commission specifications, a decentralised production run that was itself an improvisation, Sen's office setting the standard and the districts finding the metal. The design had a further quiet virtue. Because each candidate had a separate box, the count could proceed box by box, and the contents of a box were an unambiguous physical fact: papers in the bullock box were votes for the bullock's candidate, requiring no interpretation of marks, no adjudication of intentions, no disputed crosses. The architecture traded elegance for legibility. It made the vote readable to a counting staff as unlettered, in large part, as the electorate itself, and it made fraud by reinterpretation nearly impossible at the point where founding elections most often rot, the counting table.

The symbol half of the choice outlived the box half by generations. Separate boxes gave way, from the third general election onward, to a single ballot paper printed with names and symbols and marked by the voter, and the marked ballot in turn gave way, in a transition completed in the following century and examined later in this pillar, to the electronic voting machine, a later dated development this article notes only as the founding design's eventual successor. The symbol, though, never left. Sen's insight that a picture could carry a political identity across every language barrier of the subcontinent became a permanent feature of Indian elections, structuring party brands, splitting disputes and courtroom arguments for decades. Parties would fight for their symbols in the Commission's hearing rooms with a ferocity that puzzled outside observers, never puzzling anyone who understood 1951: the symbol was not decoration on the choice. For most voters, for most of the founding era, the symbol was the choice, the candidate's name rendered in the only script the whole electorate could read.

## Choice Three: The Roll, Built by the States Before the Law Existed

The third choice was the quietest and in some ways the boldest: the electoral roll, the list that decides who exists as a voter, would be prepared not by the Commission's own machinery, which did not exist, but by the governments of the states, working constituency by constituency under the Commission's supervision. Like the polling stations, the roll was built by borrowed hands. Unlike the polling stations, the roll was also built ahead of the law. The preparation of draft rolls on the basis of universal adult franchise had begun in 1947, inside the secretariat of the Constituent Assembly, before the Constitution had settled the franchise, before the Election Commission had been created, and long before the Representation of the People Act of 1950 supplied the statutory frame. Officials worked on the working assumption, encouraged at the summit of the Assembly, that every adult would vote, and set enumerators walking from house to house in provinces still reeling from Partition to write down the names of a future electorate that no law had yet authorised them to count.

Ornit Shani's How India Became Democratic, published in 2017, reconstructed that prehistory from the archives and changed the scholarly understanding of the founding. Where an older account treated the franchise as a gift handed down by the Constitution in January 1950, Shani showed the register preceding the gift: the bureaucratic act of listing names, pursued through 1947, 1948 and 1949, itself manufactured the political fact of the universal franchise, because a state that has written 173 million names on a roll has made a promise that its constitution makers could no longer dilute. Sen inherited that promise as a working document. His contribution was to discipline it into an electoral instrument: draft rolls published constituency by constituency, claims and objections invited, names checked against the upheaval of Partition, and the whole apparatus of revision run through the same district officers who would later conduct the poll. The state coordination pattern was thereby fixed at the deepest level of the system. The Commission would direct, standardise and audit; the states would enumerate, revise and maintain. Indian electoral administration remains, in its skeleton, exactly that federation of effort, and the skeleton was assembled before the body of the law existed to describe it.

The roll also carried the founding's most delicate administrative judgment, the treatment of the Partition refugees. Millions of displaced persons had crossed into India from the territories that became Pakistan, and millions of Muslims had crossed the other way, and a roll compiled by house to house enumeration in 1948 or 1949 risked two opposite injustices: striking off the departed as though departure settled citizenship, and omitting the arrived as though arrival were too recent to count. The Commission's working rules, hammered out in the correspondence between Sen's office, the Home Ministry under Patel and the state governments, admitted refugees to the roll on declaration and residence evidence that district officers could realistically verify, and set the machinery to revising rolls in the refugee townships and camps rather than waiting for the displaced to find the state. The decision was administrative in form and constitutional in effect. It declared that the franchise followed the citizen into exile and back, and it was taken by officials with clipboards, not by judges with doctrines.

## The Women Who Would Not Give Their Names

No episode of the roll making tests the founding architecture like the affair of the women's names, and none shows Sen's administrative character more plainly. As the draft rolls came in from the provinces, the Commission found that very large numbers of women had been entered not under their own names but by relation, as the wife of a named man or the daughter of a named man, in keeping with customs that kept a respectable woman's name inside the household. The returns, as Ramachandra Guha recounts in India After Gandhi, published in 2007, ran to some twenty eight lakh women recorded in that relational form, concentrated in the northern provinces. An electoral roll, though, is a list of persons, and a relational entry is not a person the law can summon, identify at a polling station, or protect against impersonation. Sen ruled that women would be registered under their own names or not at all, and the relational entries were struck from the rolls, with revision offered to every woman willing to give the name she was called by.

The ruling was attacked in its day as an official's pedantry defeating the very universality the election existed to prove, and the objection deserves to be stated at its strongest, as this article's charter requires. Critics argued that a Commission which struck millions of women from the first roll for obeying the customs of their households had made the franchise conditional on a cultural reform it had no mandate to impose, and that the women concerned, overwhelmingly rural, poor and without schooling, paid the price of a principle framed in Delhi. Sen's answer, visible in the Commission's report and in the administrative record, was equally structural: a vote entered in another person's name could be cast by another person's hand, and a roll that listed wives as appendages would have delivered their ballots, in practice, to the heads of their households, manufacturing a proxy electorate inside a universal one. Between a smaller roll of real persons and a larger roll of ventriloquised names, he chose the persons. The choice cost the first election electors it could ill spare, and it bought the roll an integrity that every later revision inherited: on an Indian electoral roll, a woman is a voter in her own name, and the founding commissioner is the reason the rule was set before any exception could harden into custom.

The episode also belongs to the roll's larger meaning within [the post-1951 first general election context](/2019/01/30/first-general-election-1951-1952-india/), which this pillar's companion study of the election as a political event sets out in full. Where that study reads 1951 to 1952 as a verdict and a founding ritual, the roll episode reads it as an act of social definition, the state deciding, name by name, who would count as a political individual. Sen's Commission made that decision 173 million times, under time pressure, with borrowed staff, and the pattern of its answers, universal in form, individual in entry, federal in execution, is the founding template in its most concentrated form.

## Choice Four: A Calendar Stretched Across Four Months

The fourth choice was the calendar. Sen's Commission spread the first general election across the period from 25 October 1951 to 21 February 1952, polling in successive phases, region by region and state by state, rather than attempting a single national polling day that the republic's resources could not have survived. The phasing is sometimes described as a concession to India's size, and size was part of it, but the sharper logic was recycling. The founding election possessed one pool of trained polling personnel in each state, one stock of ballot boxes, one supervisory cadre of returning officers, and one thin layer of police. A phased calendar let that single stock serve constituency after constituency in turn: boxes counted, emptied, resealed and moved; presiding officers rested, retrained and redeployed; the Commission's small central staff able to watch one region's polling, learn its failures, and correct the instructions before the next region voted. The calendar converted scarcity into sequence. What a wealthy election authority buys in parallel capacity, Sen's Commission obtained in series, by patience.

The sequence itself was dictated by the land. Polling opened on 25 October 1951 in the Chini tehsil of Himachal Pradesh, in the high Himalaya, because the snows that would close those valleys by February were already gathering, and the Commission chose to take the mountains first while their paths were open. The great body of the country voted in the winter weeks of January and February 1952, after the kharif harvest was in and before the heat of the Indian summer made outdoor queues and long counts a cruelty, a window fixed by agriculture and climate rather than by politics. Travancore-Cochin in the far south, where an early monsoon discipline governed the year, voted on its own schedule inside the window. Assam's riverine constituencies, the desert districts of Rajasthan, the forest tracts of Madhya Pradesh and Orissa each posed their own problems of access, answered locally within the phased frame. The calendar that resulted was not a timetable imposed on a passive map. It was the map's own logic, read by district officers and assembled into a national order of battle.

### Why did the Commission refuse to wait for a new census?

The Commission refused to wait because delay would have postponed the republic's first elected Parliament by years while a fresh enumeration was taken and a delimitation built upon it, so Sen's map rested on the census of 1941, accepting its distortions as the price of holding the founding vote inside the constitutional moment.

The decision carried costs that the statistical report does not hide. Constituencies drawn on a census taken a decade earlier, before Partition had moved millions and before the wartime and postwar growth of the cities, varied wildly in size, and the representation of the displaced and the urban went underweighted in the founding House. The Commission accepted the distortion openly, as the alternative was worse: a provisional Parliament, unelected, governing on while the perfect map was prepared. The founding generation's instinct, here as in [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) that the election created, was to seat the imperfect institution and let it correct itself in office. The first Delimitation Commission followed in 1952, under its own Act, and the map was redrawn on the census of 1951 for the second general election, which is where the founding template's habit of self correction, provisional first and regularised second, can be seen operating as method rather than accident.

Phasing also bought the founding election its insurance against catastrophe. A single day poll concentrates every risk, of weather, of disorder, of administrative breakdown, into one throw. Sen's calendar dispersed the risk across four months and a subcontinent, so that a flood in Assam or a disturbance in a Bombay constituency threatened a phase, not the founding. The method outlasted its inventor's constraints. Indian general elections remain phased affairs long after the republic acquired the staff and the machines to poll faster, because the habit formed under scarcity proved to have independent virtues: security forces can be moved, observers can be spread, and a problem discovered in one phase can still be repaired before the next. What began as the arithmetic of poverty settled into the doctrine of prudence, which is the biography of several of Sen's improvisations in miniature.

## Paper, Steel and Transport: The Material Substrate of the Founding

Beneath the four choices lay a material problem that no constitutional text could solve: the founding election had to be manufactured. Its physical inventory, as the Commission's reports record it, included ballot papers in the hundreds of millions, more than two million steel ballot boxes, seals and sealing wax, indelible ink in its early form, screened compartments built of bamboo and canvas at 196,084 stations, printed rolls for every constituency, and the transport to move all of it across a railway network still reeling from Partition and along roads that, in large parts of the country, were tracks. The printing alone was an industrial mobilisation. The rolls had consumed some 380,000 reams of paper before a single ballot was printed, produced by government presses working to constituency deadlines, with the proof correction cycle run by the same borrowed clerks who had typed the lists. Ballot papers for the parliamentary and assembly elections together ran into the hundreds of millions, printed under security conditions at presses whose managers had never handled work of that sensitivity.

The logistics were solved the way the staffing was solved, by conscription of the existing state. Railway wagons moved boxes and paper on priority indents. District treasuries became strong rooms. Police lines became stores. In the Himalayan districts the boxes travelled by mule and by porter's back; in the Sundarbans and the Assam valley they travelled by boat; in the Rajasthan desert, by camel cart. Sen's central office concerned itself with standards, deadlines and accounts, and left the last fifty miles to the district officer's local knowledge, the same division of labour that governed the rolls. The founding election's supply chain was the Indian district administration itself, temporarily reimagined as a distribution network for democracy, and the reimagining worked because the network already reached every tehsil in the country. No purpose built electoral infrastructure could have been created in eighteen months. The improvisation consisted in seeing that the infrastructure already existed, wearing other uniforms, and that Article 324(6) supplied the legal key to turn it.

There was scepticism to answer, at home and abroad, and the reports answer it with accounts rather than rhetoric. Detractors in the foreign press had treated the Indian experiment as a gamble, in one famous contemporary phrase the biggest gamble in history, on the capacity of a poor and unlettered electorate to perform the central act of democracy. Sen's reply, built into the Commission's narrative report, was to publish everything: the numbers polled and rejected, the stations at which polling failed and was repeated, the petitions filed and their disposal, the cost accounts. The founding institution established, at its birth, the habit of the public statistical record, the practice by which every later Indian election would be rendered auditable by any citizen willing to read a table. That habit is part of the template too, and not the least part: an election authority that publishes its own failures purchases, with candour, the authority to be believed about its successes.

## The Political Weather the Architecture Had to Survive

An election machine is never tested by its drawings. It is tested by the politics poured through it, and the politics of 1951 to 1952 were as demanding as the logistics. The founding election was held in a country four years from a Partition that had killed, by the most careful estimates, several hundred thousand people and displaced some fourteen million, with communal trust at its lowest ebb in living memory and the state's monopoly of force still being rebuilt in the border districts. The architecture had to be visibly neutral between communities at the level of the polling station and the roll, because a single region's conviction that the registers or the boxes had been arranged against it could have set the founding alight. Joint electorates, the constitutional settlement whose genealogy runs through [the Poona Pact's rejection of separate electorates](/2020/02/19/poona-pact-ambedkar-gandhi-1932/), placed every community's candidates before every community's voters in the reserved seats, and Sen's machinery had to make that settlement work in villages where its meaning was still contested. The founding poll passed without a communal conflagration at the stations, a negative achievement that belongs on the credit side of the deputation model: the officers running the stations were known locally, and their knownness, whatever its risks, denied rumour its favourite fuel of strangerhood.

The second weather system was political dominance. The Indian National Congress entered the founding election as the party of the freedom movement, governing at the centre and in nearly every province, supplying the ministers to whom the borrowed polling staff ordinarily reported. An architecture run by government servants for an election the government's party expected to win invited an obvious suspicion, and the suspicion was voiced by the opposition of the day, Socialists, Communists and the Jana Sangh alike. Sen's defence was procedural and total: uniform instructions issued in writing, parties consulted collectively on symbols and arrangements, counting conducted before candidates' agents, results published station by station in the statistical record. The verdict, when it came, gave the Congress 364 of the 489 seats in the House of the People on about 45 percent of the vote, a landslide of seats on a plurality of ballots that the opposition accepted as honestly counted even while contesting its fairness as a translation of votes into power. That acceptance, by parties with every incentive to cry fraud, was the founding architecture's true certificate. Rajendra Prasad's provisional Parliament gave way to an elected House in the spring of 1952, and the institution that had counted its way there settled into permanence, its methods already hardening into the expectations that [the post-1956 federal-architecture context](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/) would soon test when the map of the states themselves was redrawn beneath it.

## The Count, the Petitions and the Verdict

Counting under the separate box system had a physical directness that later methods traded away. At the close of each phase, the sealed boxes were carried to the counting centres under guard, opened in the presence of the candidates and their agents, and their contents tallied box by box: the papers in a candidate's box were that candidate's votes, needing no reading, no marking judgment, no scrutiny of intention. Invalid voting, the bane of marked ballot systems, approached zero by design, because a blank paper in a box could not be spoiled by an illiterate hand. What the system could not prevent, it disclosed: empty boxes and stuffed boxes were physical facts visible to every agent in the hall. The first results were declared in the opening days of February 1952, the parliamentary count completing as the last phases polled, and by the time polling closed on 21 February the shape of the founding verdict was known across the country. About 45.7 percent of the registered electors had voted in the parliamentary election, a turnout the Commission reported without apology, noting the millions of names the rolls still carried for the dead and the departed whom revision had not caught, which inflated the denominator against which participation was measured.

The result rewarded the architecture's maker with the one prize an election commissioner can accept, the losers' consent. The Congress took 364 seats; the Communist Party of India, contesting openly for the first time after years underground and in revolt in Telangana, took 16 seats and the largest opposition vote share after the Socialists, whose 12 seats understated their second place in ballots cast. The Jana Sangh won 3 seats, the Hindu Mahasabha and the Ram Rajya Parishad a handful between them, and independents, the founding election's great reservoir, took 37. B. R. Ambedkar, the Law Minister who had piloted the Constitution whose electoral clauses are part of [the drafting settlement examined in this series](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/), lost his Bombay North contest to an obscure Congress opponent, a result he disputed in an election petition and never forgave, and the system's willingness to record the defeat of the Constitution's own draftsman, and to entertain his petition through the tribunal process the Representation of the People Act of 1951 provided, did more for the institution's credibility than any victory of the famous could have done. Disputes from the founding election went to election tribunals as the Act directed, argued on evidence, decided on the record, and the habit of channelling post election grievance into a forensic process rather than into the street was set at the first opportunity. The verdict's political meaning, the consolidation of what Rajni Kothari would later theorise as [the Congress system of one party dominance](/2019/11/18/kothari-congress-system-post-independence/), belongs to the political reading of the election. Its administrative meaning is the one this article has pursued: the machine worked, everywhere, at the first attempt, and everyone could see that it had.

## A Note on the Founding Numbers, and Why Accounts Differ

Because this article rests its argument on the Commission's own figures, it owes the reader a short account of how those figures behave in the literature, where the founding numbers are repeated with variations that can look like contradictions. The register of 173,212,343 electors is the statistical report's count for the territories that polled, excluding Jammu and Kashmir, and it is the figure from which the founding's scale should always be argued. The candidate count requires more care. Nomination papers filed for the House of the People ran higher than the field that finally contested, because withdrawals and rejections thinned the lists between nomination and poll; the report's contested figure, the one this article uses, is 1,874 candidates for 489 seats, while accounts that print 1,949 are counting candidatures at an earlier stage of the process. Neither figure is an error, but they are answers to different questions, and the failure to distinguish them has inflated many summaries of the founding field. The assembly contests, sixteen thousand candidates and more for 3,283 seats, are usually dropped from popular accounts altogether, which halves the founding's true size in the reader's imagination and erases the simultaneity that made the operation so demanding.

The turnout figure demands the same discipline. The 45.7 percent of the parliamentary poll is computed against a register that the Commission itself acknowledged to be swollen with the dead, the departed and the duplicated, names that revision had not caught in a population moving as India's moved between 1947 and 1951. Measured against the living electorate the enumerators could actually have reached, participation stood higher, by how much the record cannot precisely say. Honest use of the founding numbers therefore runs in both directions at once: the register's defects forbid triumphalism about turnout, and the same defects forbid the sceptic's use of the raw percentage as proof of popular indifference. What the numbers establish beyond dispute is the order of magnitude of the thing attempted and completed, an electorate larger than every electorate in the world combined at that date, a field of candidates in the tens of thousands across the two tiers, a polling apparatus of nearly two hundred thousand stations, and a count concluded, published and accepted within weeks of the final phase. Sen's statisticians built the record to be argued with. Arguing with it precisely, rather than decoratively, is the least a rereading of the founding can do.

## The Colonial Inheritance Objection, Stated Fairly

The strongest counter to the improvised founding architecture reading must be faced in its strongest form, because the reading claims novelty and the record contains inheritance. Elections in India did not begin in 1951. A limited franchise had operated since the reforms of 1909 and 1919, and the Government of India Act of 1935 had staged provincial elections in 1937 and 1946 on an electorate of some thirty million, with returning officers, printed rolls, ballot boxes and symbols for unlettered voters all present in embryo. The district officer corps that Sen borrowed was itself a colonial formation, trained in the Raj's routines of enumeration and control, and the Representation of the People Acts were drafted with the 1935 Act's electoral chapters open on the table. On this evidence, the objection runs, Sen improvised far less than the founding myth suggests. He inherited a going concern, an electoral administrative tradition forty years deep, and scaled it. The improvisation reading, the objection concludes, overstates the novelty of 1950 to 1952 in order to manufacture a founder.

The objection is right about the pedigree and wrong about the conclusion, and the difference lies in the word scale, which conceals a change of kind. The largest electorate the colonial machinery had ever registered was about thirty million, chosen by property and tax qualifications that excluded the poor, most women and nearly all the countryside's labouring population. Sen's roll held 173 million, every adult, including precisely the citizens the colonial franchise had been designed to keep out. A machine for registering the propertied fifth of a population is not a smaller version of a machine for registering everyone; it is a different machine, because the marginal elector, the landless labourer, the woman in purdah, the refugee in the camp, is the one the procedures must be invented to reach. The symbols of 1937 served voters choosing among a restricted field under a restricted franchise; the symbol system of 1951 had to carry the whole burden of choice for an electorate five times larger and almost wholly unlettered, with parties, independents and double member seats multiplying the marks to be learned. Above all, the colonial elections were instruments of a state answerable elsewhere, run to advise and to contain. Sen's election was run to constitute the state itself, and to bind its makers to the count. The inheritance supplied the bricks. The improvisation was the building, and its plan, borrowed staff, symbol ballot, state built rolls, phased calendar, was drawn in eighteen months by an office of a handful of men. Where the objection does land, this article concedes it: Sen's genius was adaptive rather than inventive, a genius for operationalising inherited patterns at unprecedented scale, and the founding template is none the worse for that. Templates are not poems. Their virtue is that they work and can be repeated.

## Three Readings of the Founding, and a Judgment

The scholarly argument over the founding election runs among three readings, and the article's claim can be fixed by placing it against the other two. The heroic founding reading, carried most widely by Ramachandra Guha's India After Gandhi of 2007, presents the election as a founding miracle of democratic faith, an act of collective self creation by a poor and divided nation, with Sen as its quiet protagonist, the unsung hero of Guha's telling. The descriptive historical reading, embodied in the Commission's own reports and in the standard reference accounts, stays with the events: the dates, the seats, the parties, the incidents of a vast and colourful campaign, recording the founding as chronicle. Both readings are honourable, and both leave the same thing unexamined, the institutional content of what was decided. The heroic reading dissolves decisions into virtue. The descriptive reading dissolves them into sequence. Neither can explain why Indian elections in the following decades kept Sen's shape, why the deputation, the symbol, the state built roll and the phased calendar persisted through changes of party, technology and law, because neither treats the founding as an architectural act whose products could be inherited.

David Gilmartin's essay of 2009, One Day's Sultan, on T. N. Seshan and Indian democracy, supplies the missing term from the other end of the institution's history. Writing about the commissioner who, four decades after Sen, turned the office into a theatre of assertion, Gilmartin located the Election Commission's authority not in its constitutional text alone but in the accumulated public memory of elections honestly run, a reservoir of trust on which a later commissioner could draw. The reservoir had to be filled before it could be drawn on, and it was filled in 1951 to 1952, by the founding architecture's visible, published, losers' consent earning performance. Shani's archival work completes the triangulation from below: the franchise was made by the clerks before it was proclaimed by the Constitution, so that the founding election appears, in her account, as the ratification of a citizenship already administratively constructed. Between Guha's faith, Shani's clerks and Gilmartin's reservoir, the improvised architecture reading takes its stand. The decisions of 1950 to 1951 are the mechanism connecting all three: they are how faith was converted into procedure, how the clerks' lists became a poll, and how the reservoir of trust was first filled. That is the adjudication this article defends, and it is why the four choice map, rather than the portrait of a hero or the chronicle of a season, is the form in which the founding repays study.

## The Template After Its Maker

Sen remained in office until 19 December 1958, long enough to run the second general election of 1957 through the template the first had set, and the 1957 poll reads in the record like a proof of the founding design: the same deputation of state staff, the same symbol ballot, the same phased method compressed by experience, the rolls revised rather than rebuilt, the whole operation cheaper per elector and calmer in execution. An improvisation that cannot be repeated is an accident. The 1957 election demonstrated that Sen's had become a system, operable by hands other than its designer's, which is the definition of an institutional template. He also carried the design abroad. In 1953, between his two Indian elections, Sen chaired the international commission that conducted the first general election in Sudan, in November and December of that year, an appointment that recorded the standing the founding poll had won in the world's estimation, and on his return the republic gave him the Padma Bhushan in 1954. After retirement he became the first vice chancellor of the University of Burdwan in 1960, a mathematician returning to a university, and he died on 13 May 1963, having watched the third general election run on his floor plan in 1962.

The correspondence through which the template was negotiated, Sen's letters with Nehru and with Patel in the founding years, survives in the National Archives and in the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, and it remains under cited by the election's historians relative to its importance, full of the unglamorous decisions, deputation quotas, roll deadlines, the refugee entries, on which the founding actually turned. Papers of that sensitivity teach a lesson about custody that reaches beyond the archive reading room; the same discipline of keeping a fragile record intact and attributable is what leads [journalists to use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) whose safety depends on a document's provenance surviving scrutiny. The founding record survived because its keepers treated paper as evidence. The template they documented had a longer life than any of them. Deputation still staffs Indian polling stations. Symbols still carry choice to the voter. State machinery still builds the roll under the Commission's direction. Phases still order the calendar of the largest elections on earth. The marked ballot and then the electronic machine replaced the separate box, the one founding device that scarcity had shaped most tightly, and even that succession honoured the original logic, expressing choice by a single unambiguous physical act that a counting officer could not reinterpret. Sen's architecture was improvised under acute resource constraints in 1950 and 1951, and the republic has been renovating the building ever since without ever moving out of it.

## Two Elections on One Visit: The Simultaneous Design

Inside the four choices sat a fifth decision, less often named, that doubled the founding election's difficulty and halved its cost: the parliamentary and state assembly elections were held together, at the same polling stations, on the same days, before the same borrowed staffs. The Constitution had left the provisional state legislatures, like the provisional Parliament, to be replaced by elected bodies, and Sen's Commission chose to replace them in a single operation rather than in two campaigns separated by a year. A voter arriving at a founding polling station therefore performed the founding act twice, receiving a blank ballot paper for the House of the People and another for the state assembly, in many provinces distinguished by colour or size, and distributing the two papers among two sets of candidate boxes ranked in the screened compartment. The arithmetic of the design is visible in the statistical report's twin columns: 489 parliamentary seats and 3,283 assembly seats filled in one mobilisation, 1,874 parliamentary candidates and more than sixteen thousand assembly candidates standing, every one of them requiring a symbol, a box, a place in the count.

Simultaneity strained every joint of the borrowed machine. Returning officers for parliamentary constituencies supervised clusters of assembly segments that did not always share their boundaries. A presiding officer might conduct two polls whose rolls differed at the edges, whose candidates numbered in the dozens, and whose boxes filled the screened compartment like a hardware stall. Counting doubled too, and with it the scope for fatigue and error in halls where the tallying ran for days. Sen accepted the strain for reasons the founding map makes intelligible. Two separate elections would have required the same deputation, the same boxes, the same transport and the same security twice within a year, from states whose treasuries were as empty as the centre's, and would have asked the same unlettered electorate to learn the strange ritual twice before it had been learned once. One mobilisation, however overloaded, was cheaper than two, and it carried a constitutional elegance the founders valued: the republic's legislatures, union and state together, would draw their authority from a single founding act of the whole people, on the same days, under the same rules. The simultaneity of 1951 to 1952 became another inherited habit, broken in later decades as assemblies fell out of cycle, and its founding form remains the benchmark against which every later proposal to resynchronise the calendar is measured.

The double member constituencies belonged to the same family of overloaded ingenuity, and they deserve a paragraph of their own because they show the founding architecture solving a representational problem with machinery rather than with doctrine. The constitutional settlement required seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes inside joint electorates, so that a reserved member would be chosen by all the voters of a territory and not by a separate communal roll. Where a constituency's reserved population justified it, the map therefore created double member seats: two members returned from one constituency, one of the seats reserved, every elector casting two votes, with the reserved seat going to the leading candidate of the reserved category in the combined count. Eighty six such seats, and one three member seat, padded the House of the People from 401 constituencies to 489 members. The device was cumbersome, its counting intricate, its tickets confusing to parties and voters alike, and the republic abolished it by statute in 1961, converting the map to single member seats throughout. Its founding presence still instructs. Sen's Commission would rather complicate the count than compromise the joint electorate, would rather ask its borrowed clerks for arithmetic than ask the republic for separate rolls. The hierarchy of values is the founding settlement's own, executed in steel boxes and tally sheets: universality first, integration second, convenience last.

## Teaching an Electorate to Vote

No founding task was stranger than the one the reports describe in their driest chapters: the electorate had to be taught what an election was. Most of Sen's 173 million electors had never voted. Many had never seen a printed form, a sealed box, or an official queue, and the concepts beneath the ritual, that a mark of preference given in secret could dismiss a government, that the district officer's masters could be changed by the district's labourers, were not self evident propositions in the India of 1951. The Commission's answer was a publicity campaign conducted through every channel the young state possessed. All India Radio broadcast explanations of the poll in the regional languages. Documentary films on the mechanics of voting toured the districts with the cinema vans of the Films Division. Posters in pictures rather than words showed the voter the box, the screen, the symbol. District officers staged demonstrations at markets and fairs, setting up dummy polling stations where villagers could rehearse the walk behind the screen with dummy papers and dummy boxes, until the choreography of secrecy lost its mystery.

The political parties became the campaign's unpaid faculty, because teaching the voter the symbol was teaching the voter the party. Congress workers paraded the pair of bullocks through the villages on carts and banners. The Socialists carried the banyan tree, the Jana Sangh its earthen lamp, the Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party its hut, in processions that were at once propaganda and instruction, fixing in the public memory the association on which the whole symbol ballot depended. The Commission watched this partisan tutoring with official neutrality and private relief: every procession that taught a village its symbols reduced the burden on the presiding officers and the risk of confusion at the boxes. There is a lesson in the arrangement that later election management has never unlearned. The state's machinery can build the polling station, but the meaning of the act performed inside it is built by the society around it, by parties, newspapers, teachers and rumour, and the founding Commission prospered by treating that social labour as part of its architecture rather than as noise around it.

Sceptics abroad predicted farce, a subcontinent of unlettered voters stampeding, selling or spoiling a ritual they could not understand. The statistical report is the standing refutation, and Sen's organisation took care to make it one, recording the errors as faithfully as the successes: the handful of stations where polling broke down and was held again, the ballot boxes tampered with or lost in transit, the complaints investigated district by district. The rejected and disputed votes of a marked ballot system barely existed under the blank paper design, and the proportion of the poll that failed for the voter's incomprehension was small enough to be tabulated without embarrassment. What the founding electorate did with its first vote, in the event, was the thing the sceptics had rated impossible: it queued, it chose in secret, it accepted the count, and it sent home, among others, the most famous Law Minister in Asia. The temple of the founding myth was built afterwards, in speeches. The founding fact was administrative, a taught ritual performed correctly 105 million times by citizens performing it for the first time.

## The View from the District: A Polling Day in the Founding Winter

The founding architecture, described from Delhi, is a pattern of decisions. Described from a district, it is a fortnight of exhaustion carried out by men and women whose names the record mostly omits, and the article's claim about improvisation can be tested at that level as surely as at the level of principle. Consider the presiding officer of an ordinary rural station in the winter phase of January 1952, a schoolmaster in the commonest case, summoned weeks earlier by the returning officer's order, trained in a session at the tehsil headquarters where the returning officer or his assistant walked the borrowed staff through the manual Sen's Commission had issued: the arrangement of the station, the order of the boxes, the striking of the roll, the sealing of the boxes at the close, the packets and accounts to be made up before any officer slept. The training was brief because the interval was brief, and its brevity was one of the founding's calculated risks, accepted on the reasoning that a simple procedure taught shallowly to 750,000 people would outperform a perfect procedure taught deeply to a tenth as many.

His polling day began before dawn, with the boxes. Each candidate's box had to be shown empty to the polling agents present, closed, and sealed with the station's seal in their sight, a ceremony of emptiness that the manual treated with liturgical care, because the whole credibility of the separate box system rested on the proposition that nothing entered a box except through the voter's hand in the screened compartment. The papers were counted and recorded. The roll was opened. Then the queue, which in the founding winter formed early and behaved, by the testimony collected in the Commission's report and in the district accounts folded into it, with a patience that visiting journalists recorded with something like disbelief. Voters who had walked from outlying hamlets waited their turn to be identified, often by the village officer who knew their faces, to have the name struck off the marked roll, to receive the blank paper and to disappear behind the screen. The presiding officer's art lay in the junctions: keeping the identification honest without making it humiliating, keeping the compartment private without letting it become a booth for consultation, keeping the agents' challenges inside the rules without letting a challenge become an obstruction.

The close of poll reversed the morning's ceremony under the same witnesses. Boxes were sealed again, this time full, their seals and paper covers signed across by the presiding officer and such agents as chose to add their marks, and the sealed boxes travelled, by cart, by boat, by lorry, by head load, to the strong rooms of the treasury or the sub divisional office, there to wait under guard for the count. The accounts travelled with them: the tally of papers issued and unused, the marked roll, the diary of the day's incidents in which the presiding officer recorded every challenge, every spoiled arrangement, every tendered vote and refusal. Multiplied by 196,084, this is the founding election as it was actually experienced by the state that ran it: not a single national event but a subcontinent of small ceremonies of emptiness and fullness, each performed before witnesses, each leaving a paper trail that a tribunal could later audit. The architecture's genius is visible nowhere more clearly than in this multiplication. Sen's office had designed a procedure robust enough to survive being executed, for the first time, by three quarters of a million amateurs, in languages the designers did not speak, under conditions they could not inspect. Procedures that require excellence fail at scale. The founding procedure required only fidelity, and fidelity could be taught in an afternoon.

The district view also corrects a temptation of the heroic reading, which reaches instinctively for the commissioner's desk. Sen designed the founding election, but he did not conduct it in any sense the voter would have recognised. The election was conducted by the schoolmaster behind the table, by the clerk striking the roll, by the constable at the door, by the boatman who carried the boxes across the Brahmaputra channels and the porter who carried them down from Chini. What Sen supplied that no one else could have supplied was not presence but system: the manual, the calendar, the symbol list, the standardised station, the discipline of the published account. Institutional history written honestly distributes its credit in that proportion, a thin line of design at the top, a continent of execution beneath. The founding map in this article honours the proportion by treating the deputation not as the background of Sen's achievement but as its substance. The borrowed army was the architecture, and its schoolmasters were, for one winter, the republic's returning body.

## The Correspondence: Sen, Patel and Nehru Build the Machine by Letter

The founding architecture was negotiated before it was executed, and the negotiation survives in the correspondence between Sen's office, the Prime Minister and the Home Minister through 1950 and 1951, preserved at the National Archives of India and in the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library. Historians of the election have cited these files less than their importance warrants, preferring the cleaner record of the published reports, yet the letters are where the template's hardest joints were cut. Three questions dominated the exchange. When should the founding poll be held, given that the rolls, the constituency orders and the conduct law were all unfinished? Who would pay for an election conducted by the centre's commission through the states' staffs? And how far could the Commission's requisitions reach into administrations answerable to elected provincial ministries that had their own programmes, their own scarcities and, in some provinces, their own doubts about the timetable of the government in Delhi?

The timetable question was the most political of the three. The provisional Parliament, unelected, was governing a republic whose Constitution promised it an elected successor, and every month of delay compounded the anomaly. Nehru pressed for the earliest practicable poll. The administrative record shows Sen resisting speed where speed threatened the roll, and conceding speed where the risk was tolerable: the decision to delimit on the census of 1941, the decision to poll the Himalayan districts in October ahead of the main season, the decision to run the parliamentary and assembly elections together, each bears the mark of a bargain between a Prime Minister's constitutional impatience and a commissioner's administrative caution. Patel, whose Home Ministry held the levers of the district administrations the Commission needed to borrow, supplied the enforcement that made the bargains real, instructing the provinces that the Commission's requisitions under Article 324(6) were constitutional obligations and not requests to be weighed against local convenience. Sen had served his apprenticeship for this triangle as Bengal's chief secretary, corresponding with both men over the refugee emergency, and the founding correspondence has the ease of an established working relationship: terse, factual, and almost wholly free of the rhetoric of democratic destiny that fills the public speeches of the same months. The founding was argued in public as faith. It was transacted in private as logistics.

The money question reveals the same texture. The founding election's accounts, as the Commission's report renders them, came to a sum in the region of ten crore rupees for the parliamentary and assembly polls together, a charge divided between the centre and the states under arrangements the correspondence had to settle item by item, since no precedent existed for an election that the centre designed, the states staffed and both treasuries funded. Sen's letters on cost have a schoolmaster's severity: estimates demanded early, indents for steel and paper justified against norms, provincial extravagance queried line by line. The severity was itself architectural. A commission that intended to borrow the state's staff every five years for the rest of the republic's life had to establish, at the first asking, that its borrowings would be honestly accounted, or no finance department in India would open its strong rooms at the second. The published accounts of 1955, audited and itemised down to the level of the reams and the boxes, were the completion of a promise made in the correspondence of 1950. Institutional trust, in the founding as in the counting hall, was built out of paper that other people could check.

Read as a whole, the correspondence refines the article's central claim in one respect and confirms it in every other. It refines it by showing that the improvisation was not solitary. The founding template had three authors at its summit, a commissioner who designed, a Prime Minister who pressed and a Home Minister who enforced, and beneath them a fourth authorship that no signature represents, the state chief secretaries and district officers who returned Sen's drafts with the margins full of what would not work in their districts. It confirms the claim because everything the letters negotiate is a resource constraint and its workaround: no staff, so deputation, negotiated province by province; no time, so a phased calendar and a borrowed census; no money, so simultaneity and audited economy; no precedent, so a manual written from first principles and corrected by the field. The published reports present the founding architecture as a finished object. The correspondence shows it being improvised, in the strict sense of the word, composed in real time out of whatever the republic had to hand. That is why the files matter, and why their relative neglect by the election's historians has left the heroic reading in possession of a field the documents would have made more interesting.

## Eyes on the Founding: The World Watches the Count

The founding election was performed before an audience whose scepticism formed part of the test. Foreign newspapers and more than a few foreign officials had treated the Indian franchise as an act of romantic folly, and their correspondents arrived in the winter of 1951 to 1952 expecting the farce they had predicted: queues dissolving into riots, boxes stuffed in the villages, an electorate bewildered into abstention. What they reported home, in dispatch after dispatch preserved in the press record of the season, was the opposite spectacle, orderliness so complete that it bored them. The phrases that travelled the world from that winter, the biggest gamble in history redeemed, the largest electorate in human history conducted to the polls without disorder, entered the founding's mythology and have trailed it since. Sen's Commission neither courted nor managed this coverage in any modern sense. It did something more durable. It made the election legible to inspection: accredited observers and journalists could watch stations open, watch boxes shown empty and sealed, watch counting proceed before the agents, and read, in due course, the published tables against which every anecdote could be checked.

The domestic audience mattered more, and the Indian press of the founding winter, English language and vernacular together, covered the poll as the republic's first great collective self examination, criticising the Commission freely where criticism was due: the names struck from the rolls, the stations where arrangements broke down, the districts where officials were accused of leaning toward the ruling party. The Commission's posture toward that scrutiny completes the portrait of its maker's institutional intelligence. Sen answered criticism with information rather than with dignity. Complaints were investigated through the returning officers and their outcomes folded into the record. The report of 1955 reads, in its complaint chapters, like a gazetteer of everything that went wrong anywhere in India during the founding poll, published by the very office responsible for the poll. A younger institution in a younger republic might have treated the founding as a pageant to be protected from blemish. Sen treated it as an audit to be survived in public, and the distinction shaped the Election Commission's relationship with the Indian press for good: adversarial in season, documentary in substance, founded on the principle that an election authority's best defence is its own tables.

The watching world drew one further conclusion that the founding generation had not anticipated and that the correspondence files make poignant. Within two years of the count, the Indian method had become an export. Sen's summons to Sudan in 1953 was the first instance of a traffic that would run for decades, Indian election administrators advising, observing and staffing polls across the decolonising world, carrying the founding template of borrowed staffs, symbol ballots and published accounts into countries whose constraints resembled those of India in 1950. The architecture designed for a republic too poor to build its own electoral service turned out to be precisely the architecture that other poor republics could adopt, because its genius lay in what it did not require: no permanent electoral bureaucracy, no literate electorate, no machinery beyond paper, steel and the existing district state. Improvisation under constraint, it transpired, travels better than affluence. The founding election's longest shadow may be the one it cast outside India's borders, in polling stations from Khartoum onward where the Commission's borrowed methods were borrowed again.

## The Commissioner Answers for His Machine

Sen's founding election ended, as he intended all Indian elections to end, in print. The Commission's Report on the First General Elections in India, published in two volumes in 1955 alongside the statistical report, is the founding institution's account of itself, and it remains the primary source against which every later reading, this article's included, must be checked. The report does what the heroic reading never does: it argues. It defends the deputation of state staff against the advocates of a dedicated electoral service, on the ground that a permanent army of election officials would stand idle between polls and rot in its idleness. It defends the symbol and the separate box against the advocates of the printed ballot, on the ground that a ballot the voter cannot read is a fraud upon the voter however elegant its typography. It defends the phased calendar and the simultaneous conduct of the two elections as economy measures that a poor republic had no right to refuse. Reading the report at this distance, what impresses is not its modesty, which is genuine, but its completeness as an institutional brief: Sen wrote the manual of the machine while the machine's paint was still wet, and he wrote it for critics he expected to outlive him.

The accountability ran in the other direction as well. The Commission of the founding years was a one man body, and its chief answered for the founding poll before the elected representatives the poll had seated, presenting the Commission's work and its accounts to Parliament and defending its conduct in the forum his own machinery had created. The position had a circularity that Sen handled with the driest of his virtues, submission to the record: the commissioner who had counted the members' votes stood in his turn to be questioned by the members, on figures he had himself published, in volumes any member could buy. Within that circle the founding template acquired its final component, the convention that the Election Commission explains itself in public, in numbers, after every election, whether the explanation flatters it or not. Later commissions would hold press conferences, later commissioners would become public personalities of a kind Sen, who gave the republic an institution and declined to give it a celebrity, would have found unrecognisable. The convention underneath the publicity is his: account first, in print, in full.

Criticism of the founding conduct there was, and the report's value lies in its refusal to step around it. Opposition parties charged, in particular districts, that government servants on deputation had leaned toward the governing party that employed them, the standing temptation of the borrowed machine. Women struck from the rolls for the names ruling protested a universality that had excluded them at the first asking, and their exclusion stood in the returns as a measurable fact, some twenty eight lakh names by the count Guha's history preserves. The turnout figure itself, 45.7 percent of the registered roll, was thrown at the Commission as evidence that the founding had failed to move the founding electorate, to which the honest answer, given in the record, was that the roll was swollen with the dead, the migrated and the duplicated, so that the living electorate had voted in proportions the raw figure understated. Sen's machinery had generated every one of these numbers, including the unflattering ones, and published them in tables that its critics used as ammunition. An institution willing to arm its critics has understood something about authority in a democracy that the founding generation grasped early: credibility is a stock, accumulated by disclosure, spent by assertion. The Election Commission entered Indian public life wealthy in that stock, and the wealth was Sen's doing as surely as the ballot boxes were.

There the founding properly ends, not with the verdict of February 1952 but with the settlement of accounts that followed it, the template complete and available for inspection: a small constitutional commission commanding a borrowed administrative army under Article 324(6); a roll built by the states and owned by no party; a symbol carrying choice across illiteracy; a phased calendar recycling scarce capacity; a simultaneous double election; and a published record closing the loop of accountability. Every element answered a resource constraint of 1950. Every element, the separate box alone excepted, and even it surrendered only to its own logic improved, survived into the practice of the mature republic. That is the improvised founding architecture, and that is why Sukumar Sen, who built no monument and sought none, is best honoured by reading his election as he built it: as a design.

## What the Founding Left Unfinished

An adjudication that ends in admiration has not finished its work, and the founding architecture had defects that its maker's own reports place on the record. The roll, for all its 173 million names, was a first draft of the citizenry rather than a portrait of it. Compiled by enumeration across years of upheaval, it carried the dead who had not been reported dead, the migrated who appeared in two districts or in none, transliterations of the same name spelled three ways by three enumerators, and whole categories of the elusive, pavement dwellers, nomadic groups, the floating population of the great cities, whom house to house listing was structurally bound to miss. The turnout figure of 45.7 percent, computed against that swollen denominator, understated the participation of the living and findable, but the understatement cuts both ways as evidence: a register that cannot say precisely who its electors are has solved the problem of inclusion only in gross. Sen's Commission knew it, and said so, and treated the first roll as a base to be revised annually rather than as a finished scripture, which is why the revision machinery, summary and intensive, claims and objections, became a permanent organ of the template rather than a founding expedient.

The names ruling left its own unfinished business. The women struck from the draft rolls for relational entries could re enter at revision by giving their own names, and many did across the 1950s, as the practice of the roll taught the society what the rule required. Yet the founding cost stands in the record and should stand in any honest summary: the first election's universality was qualified, at its first exercise, by an exclusion that fell entirely on women, imposed for a principle, the integrity of the individual entry, that the excluded women had no part in framing. Later scholarship has generally sided with Sen's reasoning while noting the price, and this article's adjudication follows that divided verdict rather than flattening it. The founding template contained, from its first winter, a tension between the universality it proclaimed and the administrative conditions it attached, and the tension did not retire with its author. It reappears wherever a registration rule prices a citizen out of the roll for want of a document, a fixed address or a legible name, and the founding precedent supplies both sides of that later argument: Sen's insistence that the roll list persons, and the critics' insistence that persons not be sacrificed to the list.

Two further incompletions were structural, and both were repaired by the template's own habit of self correction. The double member constituency, the founding device for reserved representation inside joint electorates, multiplied counting labour and confused the ticket wherever it operated, and Parliament abolished it in 1961, moving to single member constituencies with reservation attached, a simpler machinery for the same constitutional end. The map's dependence on the census of 1941, accepted in 1951 as the price of immediacy, was retired at the first opportunity by delimitation on the census of 1951. In each case the sequence repeats the founding rhythm: an improvised device, adopted under constraint and defended in print, then regularised by statute once the emergency of the founding had passed. The pattern is worth naming because it distinguishes Sen's improvisation from mere makeshift. A makeshift is abandoned when the crisis ends. Sen's devices were either retained, because they worked, or replaced by their own improved descendants, because the institution he founded had learned from operating them what their permanent form should be.

The deepest unfinished business was the one the founding could least afford to examine: the borrowed army's loyalty. Deputation staffed the founding election with the servants of governments whose parties were contesting it, and the arrangement worked in 1951 to 1952 for reasons that were partly circumstantial, a governing party confident of victory, an opposition watching for scandal, a commissioner of granite impartiality, a political culture still fluent in the self denial of the freedom movement. None of those conditions was guaranteed to recur, and the later history of the institution, traced in this pillar, is in large part the history of the Commission struggling to keep command of an apparatus it borrows, against governments tempted to treat the returning officer's second hat as a party favour. The founding template bequeathed the dependence along with the method for surviving it: written instructions, published records, party consultation, counting before agents, and a commissioner willing to be audited. Sen's architecture did not solve the problem of the borrowed state. It built the only platform from which the problem could be fought, election after election, which is perhaps the most that founding architecture, in any republic, has ever honestly done.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Sukumar Sen?

Sukumar Sen, born on 2 January 1898, was a West Bengal cadre officer of the Indian Civil Service who became the first Chief Election Commissioner of India. Educated at Presidency College in Calcutta and at University College in London, where he took a gold medal in mathematics, he entered the civil service in 1922, served as a district officer and judge in Bengal, and was Chief Secretary of West Bengal from 1947 to 1950, administering a province in the shock of Partition. Appointed by presidential notification dated 19 March 1950, he designed and conducted the first general election of 1951 to 1952 and the second of 1957, held office until 19 December 1958, received the Padma Bhushan in 1954, and became the first vice chancellor of the University of Burdwan in 1960.

### Q: When was the first Indian general election?

The first Indian general election was held in phases from 25 October 1951 to 21 February 1952, which is why the founding poll carries both years in its name. Polling opened in the Chini tehsil of Himachal Pradesh, where the Himalayan snows threatened to close the valleys, and the great body of the country voted in January and February 1952, between the harvest and the summer heat. The election filled 489 seats in the House of the People and 3,283 seats in the state legislative assemblies at the same time, on a register of 173,212,343 electors, and its results were declared progressively through February 1952 as the counting of the last phases completed.

### Q: How was the 1951-1952 election conducted?

The election was conducted by a one member Election Commission under Sukumar Sen, commanding staff borrowed from the state governments under Article 324(6) of the Constitution. About 196,084 polling stations were staffed by roughly 750,000 officers on deputation, presiding officers and polling officers drawn from government offices, schools and courts. Electoral rolls prepared by state machinery constituency by constituency supplied the register of 173 million electors. Each elector received a blank ballot paper and dropped it behind a screen into the separate steel box of the chosen candidate, identified by a symbol rather than a printed name. Polling proceeded in phases from 25 October 1951 to 21 February 1952, and counting was conducted box by box before the candidates' agents.

### Q: What were the polling stations like?

A founding polling station was a spare, standardised space, usually a schoolroom, a panchayat building or a tent, arranged to a pattern the Commission prescribed. The presiding officer sat with the marked copy of the electoral roll, striking off each voter's name as identity was established. Behind a screened compartment of bamboo, cloth or furniture stood the row of steel ballot boxes, one for every candidate in the parliamentary constituency and a parallel set for the assembly seat, each box displaying its candidate's name and symbol. Of the 196,084 stations, 27,527 were reserved for women voters, with women staff where the administration could supply them, so that women observing purdah could vote inside a threshold their households accepted.

### Q: How did illiterate voters cast ballots?

Illiterate voters cast ballots by recognition instead of reading, because about 85 percent of the founding electorate could not read a candidate's name. Each candidate was identified by a symbol, a pair of bullocks, a banyan tree, a hut, an earthen lamp or an individual mark for independents, displayed on posters, in processions and at the polling station. The voter received a blank ballot paper, identical for every candidate, walked behind a screened compartment, and dropped the paper into the steel box bearing the chosen symbol. Since the paper carried no mark, the choice stayed secret, and since the vote was expressed by destination rather than by writing, no ballot could be spoiled by an unlettered hand.

### Q: What were the symbols used?

The symbols of the founding election were allotted by the Election Commission at a conference with the parties in July 1951, party symbols for recognised parties and individual symbols from a free list for independents. The Congress fought on a pair of bullocks under a yoke, the emblem carried through its campaign processions across the country. The Socialist Party took the banyan tree, the Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party the hut, and the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, founded in October 1951 weeks before polling, the earthen lamp. Symbols were painted on walls, printed on posters and displayed on the ballot boxes themselves, so that a voter who could not read could still find the mark the campaign had taught, and the symbol system became a permanent feature of Indian elections.

### Q: How long did the first election take?

The first election took just under four months of polling, from 25 October 1951 to 21 February 1952, spread across successive phases rather than held on a single day. The phased calendar was an architectural choice forced by scarcity: one pool of trained polling staff, one stock of more than two million ballot boxes and one thin supervisory cadre had to serve the whole country in turn, counted, resealed and moved from region to region. Preparation consumed far longer than polling. Sukumar Sen had roughly eighteen months from his appointment in March 1950, and the electoral rolls had been in preparation since 1947, so the founding vote was the product of nearly five years of administrative work.

### Q: What was the Election Commission's own staff strength when Sukumar Sen took charge in 1950?

The Commission Sen took charge of in March 1950 was a shell: a one member body, a small secretariat in Delhi of a handful of officers, and no field staff, warehouses, presses or transport of its own anywhere in India. Its constitutional power was large and its payroll tiny. The gap was closed by Article 324(6), which obliged the President and state governors to make staff available to the Commission on request, turning the entire district administration of India into its seasonal workforce. Sen's founding architecture accepted that dependence as permanent design rather than temporary embarrassment, and the Commission he left in 1958 was still a small headquarters commanding a vast borrowed apparatus each election season.

### Q: How were Partition refugees entered on the electoral rolls for the first election?

Partition refugees were entered on the rolls by the same house to house enumeration as other citizens, supplemented by revision drives directed at the refugee townships, camps and colonies where the displaced had gathered. The Commission's working rules, settled in correspondence with the Home Ministry and the state governments, admitted a refugee on declaration and on such residence evidence as district officers could realistically verify, rather than demanding documents that flight had destroyed. The rolls also had to absorb the opposite movement, deleting the departed without presuming that every Muslim who had crossed to Pakistan had surrendered citizenship. The administrative effect was constitutional in weight: the franchise was made to follow the citizen through displacement, and millions of uprooted voters cast founding ballots in places they had reached as strangers.

### Q: How were election symbols allotted to parties and independent candidates in 1951?

Symbols were allotted centrally by the Election Commission after consultation with the parties at a conference in July 1951, five months after Sukumar Sen had settled the symbol principle and three months before polling opened. Recognised parties received a single symbol for use across the country, so that the Congress bullocks or the Socialist banyan tree meant the same thing in every constituency. Independent candidates, who contested in their thousands, chose or were assigned individual symbols from a free list maintained by the Commission, with the returning officer ensuring that no two candidates in one constituency carried marks a voter could confuse. The allotment was then published, painted and paraded through the campaign, because a symbol unknown to the electorate on polling day was a candidate disenfranchised by obscurity.

### Q: What arrangements were made for women voters inside polling stations in 1951?

The Commission reserved 27,527 of its 196,084 polling stations exclusively for women, staffing them with women officers where the district administration could supply them, and applied screened arrangements elsewhere so that a woman observing purdah could establish her identity and cast her ballot without appearing before male strangers. The arrangements sat alongside a harder rule at the registration stage: women were entered on the roll under their own names, and entries recording a woman merely as a man's wife or daughter were struck off, which removed some twenty eight lakh relational entries from the draft rolls. Together the two policies stated the founding position in full: the woman voter would be accommodated in her customs at the station, and recognised as an individual citizen on the roll.

### Q: How many polling officials were drawn from state services to run the first election?

The Commission's record puts the polling personnel drawn from the existing services at roughly 750,000 people for the founding election, presiding officers, polling officers, clerks and orderlies seconded from state government offices, schools, courts and local bodies, with teachers forming a large part of the presiding cadre in many districts. Returning officers were the district magistrates and deputy commissioners themselves, wearing a Commission hat over their ordinary authority. A further 16,500 clerks had earlier been engaged for six months simply to type and collate the electoral rolls. None of this army belonged to the Election Commission. It was borrowed under the constitutional duty in Article 324(6), trained briefly, deployed for the season, and returned to its desks after the count.

### Q: What did Sukumar Sen do after the first general election ended?

After the first general election Sen spent his remaining six years in office converting an improvisation into a system. His Commission published its Report on the First General Elections in India in two volumes in 1955, with the statistical report, defending the founding choices on the record and codifying them as practice. In 1957 he conducted the second general election through the same template, deputation, symbols, state built rolls and phases, at lower cost and with the polish of experience. He retired on 19 December 1958, having already carried the founding design abroad as chairman of the commission for Sudan's first election in 1953, and in 1960 he became the first vice chancellor of the University of Burdwan, the institution building mathematician ending where he began, in a university.

### Q: How did Sukumar Sen come to conduct Sudan's first election in 1953?

Sen came to Sudan because the founding Indian election had made his Commission the world's standing proof that a universal franchise poll could be run in a poor, largely illiterate and newly independent country, and Sudan, approaching independence from the Anglo Egyptian condominium, faced a recognisably similar task. In 1953 he was appointed chairman of the international electoral commission charged with Sudan's first general election, and he conducted that poll in November and December 1953, between his two Indian general elections, adapting the methods of roll, symbol and phased polling to a very different society. The appointment was an early export of the founding template, and its success was marked in Khartoum itself, where a street was named for the commissioner from Bengal.

### Q: What did the Commission publish about the first election once it was over?

The Commission published a full public account of itself: the Report on the First General Elections in India, issued in two volumes in 1955, together with the Statistical Report on the first general election. The narrative report explained and defended the founding decisions, on deputation, on the symbol and the separate box, on phasing and simultaneity, and recorded failures alongside successes, from stations where polling had to be repeated to boxes lost in transit. The statistical report tabulated the poll constituency by constituency: electors, candidates, votes, turnout, the figures from which every later account of the founding election is built. The habit of publishing a complete, auditable record after an election became a permanent part of the template Sen left the institution.

### Q: How did Ornit Shani reinterpret the making of India's first electoral rolls?

In How India Became Democratic, published in 2017, Ornit Shani reinterpreted the rolls as the true founding act of Indian democracy, preceding the Constitution that supposedly created the franchise. Working from the archives, she showed that officials of the Constituent Assembly secretariat began preparing electoral rolls on the basis of universal adult franchise in 1947, before the Assembly had finished debating the franchise and before the Election Commission existed, enumerating Indians as voters while their constitutional status was still undecided. On her reading, the bureaucratic list manufactured the political fact: a state that had written down an electorate of every adult could no longer retreat to a restricted franchise. Sukumar Sen inherited that list and disciplined it into the register of 173 million from which the founding election was run.

### Q: How does Ramachandra Guha frame the first general election in India After Gandhi?

In India After Gandhi, published in 2007, Ramachandra Guha frames the first general election as a founding act of democratic faith, an unprecedented wager by a poor, divided and largely illiterate nation on its own political maturity, and he presents Sukumar Sen as one of the unsung heroes of the republic for designing and delivering it. Guha's chapters dwell on the scale and the improbability: 173 million electors, symbols for voters who could not read, a Congress victory accepted by its opponents as honestly counted. The framing is heroic and commemorative, recovering the election from neglect. The architectural reading developed in this article differs only in emphasis, treating the same events as a set of institutional decisions whose consequences, rather than whose heroism, explain their permanence.

### Q: How did the founding template operate in the second general election of 1957?

The second general election of 1957 operated as the founding template's proof of repeatability. Sukumar Sen, still in office, ran the poll through the same four choices: polling staff deputed from the state services, the symbol ballot with separate candidate boxes, rolls revised by state machinery rather than rebuilt, and a phased calendar, tightened by experience into a shorter season. The map beneath the election had changed, redrawn on the census of 1951 by the first Delimitation Commission and recast by the reorganisation of the states in 1956, yet the machinery ran without reinvention, at a lower cost per elector. A design that a second team of borrowed hands could operate was no longer one man's improvisation. It had become the institution's standard method.

### Q: How were election disputes from the first general election settled?

Election disputes from the founding poll were settled forensically, through election petitions filed after the declaration of results under the Representation of the People Act of 1951 and heard by election tribunals rather than in the street or the legislature. A defeated candidate could challenge a return on grounds the Act specified, corrupt practice, wrongful acceptance or rejection of votes, breach of the rules, and the tribunal inquired on evidence, with power to void the election and order a fresh poll. The most famous petitioner of the founding was B. R. Ambedkar, who contested his Bombay North defeat through the tribunal process. The channel mattered as much as any single outcome: grievance against the founding count was routed into a published legal record from the very first election.

### Q: Did Jammu and Kashmir vote in the first general election?

Jammu and Kashmir did not poll for the House of the People in the first general election, which is why the Commission's statistical report records its 173,212,343 electors as excluding that state. The state's relationship with the Union was still governed by its accession settlement and its own constituent process, and its representatives in the first Lok Sabha were nominated rather than elected, a position that continued until direct elections to its parliamentary seats were first held in 1967. The exception marked the limit of the founding architecture's reach: Sen's machine ran where the constitutional settlement of 1950 ran, and the unfinished business of the accession left one large territory to be connected to the electoral template a decade and a half later.
