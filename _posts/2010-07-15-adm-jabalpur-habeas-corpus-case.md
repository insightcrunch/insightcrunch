ADM Jabalpur is remembered as a verdict about everything: the Emergency, the courage of one judge, the failure of four others, and the fragility of liberty under pressure. That memory is not wrong, but it is imprecise, and the imprecision matters because the case decided far less than the memory suggests and, in deciding that little, managed to do extraordinary damage. Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla, decided on 28 April 1976 by a Constitution Bench of five judges of the Supreme Court of India, answered one framed question about who could approach a court, and on what grounds, while a Presidential Order suspending the enforcement of three fundamental rights remained in force. The Court was not asked whether the Emergency was justified. It was not asked whether the detentions that filled the prisons of the country were wise, or proportionate, or even honestly ordered in the individual cases that came before the High Courts. It was asked whether a person detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act could go to a High Court under Article 226 and say that the order against him was not an order under that Act at all, or was passed for reasons the Act did not permit, and whether the High Court could listen. Four judges said the High Court could not listen. One judge said it must. Everything else that the case has come to stand for flows from that single fork in the reasoning.

This article works through the case the way a judicial interpretation study should: the provision and the ambiguity inside it first, then the route by which the question travelled from detention orders in district towns to a Constitution Bench in Delhi, then the leading decision analysed properly, judge by judge, with the facts, the framed question, the holding, the reasoning, and the limits of what was decided. It then turns to the dissent that has outlived the judgment, to the strongest honest version of the majority's position, because a criticism that cannot state its opponent's argument fairly has understood nothing, and finally to the long undoing: the stages by which a decision that was politically discredited within two years of its delivery waited forty-one years for a court to overrule it in terms. That interval is the article's central claim, and it deserves a name. Call it the forty-one-year overrule. Between discredit and overruling, the decision stood on the record as technically good law that no advocate would cite and no bench would follow, and the history of Indian liberty in those four decades is partly the history of institutions working around a precedent they had not yet formally buried.

## The Precise Question and Why Its Narrowness Matters

The narrowness of the question in ADM Jabalpur is not a technicality to be noted and passed over. It is the first finding any honest account must record, because it disciplines everything that follows. The Supreme Court did not hold that the detentions were lawful. It did not hold that the detention orders complied with the Maintenance of Internal Security Act. It held that nobody had the standing to ask. A locus standi ruling is a ruling about the doorway of the court, not about the merits of what lies behind the door, and the majority's achievement, if the word can be borne, was to convert a merits question that several High Courts had been answering in favour of detenus into a doorway question that admitted only one answer. Once the case is understood in those terms, the shape of the tragedy changes. The detenus did not lose an argument about their detention orders. They lost the entitlement to make the argument at all, and their relatives, who had filed the petitions on their behalf, lost it with them.

### Why does the precise framing of the question in ADM Jabalpur matter?

Because the framing decided the case before the merits were reached. The petitions were framed to test the detention orders against the detaining statute, not to enforce Article 21, and the majority's decisive move was to reframe them as Article 21 enforcement in substance and shut the doorway on that ground alone.

Stated exactly, the question was whether, while the Presidential Order of 27 June 1975 operated, a detenu had any locus standi to move a High Court under Article 226 for habeas corpus on the ground that his detention did not comply with the detaining statute, or was illegal, or was mala fide. Four judges answered no. Khanna J, dissenting alone, answered yes, because the State holds no power to deprive a person of life or liberty without authority of law, whatever the Order suspended.

That formulation repays slow reading. The petitions did not invite the High Courts to enforce Article 21 in the teeth of the Presidential Order. Counsel for the detenus framed a different and deliberately smaller claim: that a detention order which did not comply with the statute, or which was mala fide, or which rested on extraneous considerations, was not an exercise of the statutory power at all, and that testing an order against the statute that purported to authorise it was an ordinary judicial function that survived the suspension of fundamental rights enforcement. A court entertaining such a petition would not be enforcing the right to life and personal liberty against the State. It would be asking the detaining authority the most basic question of administrative law: show the law that lets you do this, and show that you stayed inside it. The majority's answer closed even that avenue, because, in its reasoning, a petition whose real object was release from detention was in substance an attempt to enforce Article 21, whatever label counsel attached to it, and the Presidential Order had suspended precisely that enforcement. Substance prevailed over form, the form being the petitioners' careful drafting.

The consequences of the narrowness run in both directions, and both must be held together. On one side, the decision's authority was always confined to the situation the Order created: a subsisting proclamation of Emergency and a subsisting suspension of the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22. Nothing in the majority opinions purported to govern ordinary times, and the case was never, even at its lowest reputation, a ruling that Indian courts lack the power to examine detention in general. On the other side, within its confined field the ruling was total. It did not say that some petitions were badly framed, or that some grounds were unavailable, or that the detenus had chosen the wrong forum. It said that no person had locus standi, on any of the pleaded grounds, including the ground that the order was no order under the Act at all. A defective order and a perfect order stood in exactly the same position, which was outside the reach of judicial examination. Critics who describe the judgment as the Court licensing unlawful detention are, on this single point, describing its operative effect accurately, whatever refinements the opinions contain.

There is a further discipline the narrow question imposes on the reader, and it is one the popular account habitually drops. Because the case turned on the Presidential Order, the real legal battleground was the meaning of Article 359 and of the Order issued under it, and the real philosophical battleground was a question the Order could not answer by itself: whether Article 21 is the sole source of the right not to be deprived of life or liberty without authority of law. If Article 21 created that right, then suspending its enforcement removed the only ground on which a court could act. If the right existed independently, in the structure of a legal order that knows no power without law, then the Order suspended one remedial channel and left the underlying illegality of unauthorised detention intact and testable. The majority chose the first premise. Khanna J chose the second. The case is, at bottom, a contest between those two premises, and every later development in Indian public law on this point, from Maneka Gandhi to Puttaswamy, can be read as the slow vindication of the second.

## The Constitutional Machinery: Articles 352, 358 and 359

No reading of ADM Jabalpur survives contact with the text unless the emergency provisions are set out plainly, because the majority's argument was, in form, a textual argument built from those provisions, and it cannot be answered by anyone who has not read them. Three articles do the structural work. Article 352 empowers the President to proclaim an Emergency where the security of India is threatened by war, external aggression or, in the language that stood in 1975, internal disturbance. Article 358 provides that while a proclamation operates, the freedoms in Article 19 stand suspended by force of the proclamation itself, without any further order. Article 359 is different in kind, and the difference is the hinge of the case. It empowers the President, while a proclamation operates, to declare by order that the right to move any court for the enforcement of such of the fundamental rights as are mentioned in the order, and all pending proceedings for their enforcement, shall remain suspended for the period of the proclamation or a shorter stated period. The article suspends a remedy. It does not repeal a right, it does not amend the text of Part III, and it does not declare that the conduct the right forbids has become lawful. Those distinctions, elementary on the page, became the ground on which the whole case was fought.

### How does Article 359 differ from Article 358?

Article 358 operates automatically: while a proclamation of Emergency runs, the freedoms in Article 19 stand suspended by force of the proclamation itself, with no further instrument needed. Article 359 works differently. It requires a Presidential Order, names the rights whose enforcement is suspended, and suspends the right to move a court for their enforcement, leaving the rights themselves untouched.

Khanna J built his dissent on exactly this gap, arguing that a State act without legal authority stayed unlawful even when one remedial door was shut. The majority treated the gap as immaterial, because, in its view, the right to life and liberty had no existence outside Article 21 in the first place.

The sequence of instruments in 1975 followed that machinery step by step. A proclamation of Emergency on the ground of external aggression, issued in December 1971, remained in force. On 25 June 1975 a second proclamation issued, on the ground of internal disturbance. On 27 June 1975 the President issued the order under Article 359(1) declaring that the right of any person, including a foreigner, to move any court for the enforcement of the rights conferred by Articles 14, 21 and 22, and all proceedings pending in any court for the enforcement of those rights, should remain suspended while both proclamations remained in force. Article 14, equality before the law; Article 21, protection of life and personal liberty; Article 22, protection against arrest and detention in certain cases, the article that carries the safeguards for preventive detention, the right to be told the grounds of detention and to make a representation against the order. The selection was not random. Between them, Articles 14, 21 and 22 were the provisions a detenu would invoke, and the Order was drafted to meet the litigation that its authors knew would come. The detentions themselves proceeded under an ordinary statute, the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, a preventive detention law whose place in the wider architecture of such laws is traced in the series study of [preventive detention law in India](/2010/06/01/preventive-detention-law-india-guide/), and it is that ordinariness that gave the detenus their argument: the statute was in force, its conditions were on its face, and an order that breached those conditions was, on ordinary principles, no order at all.

One more feature of the machinery must be fixed before the narrative begins, because later sections return to it repeatedly. Article 359, as it stood in 1975, contained no exception for any right. The President could name Article 21 in an order, and did. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act later wrote the exception into the article, providing that Articles 20 and 21 cannot be named in such an order at all, but that protection did not exist when the case was argued, and the majority cannot be criticised for failing to apply a text that had not been written. The criticism that survives scrutiny is different in kind, and this article will state it in its own place: that the text the majority had left interpretive room which it declined to use, and that declining was a choice, not a compulsion. Honesty about what the 1975 text permitted is the price of a durable verdict on what the judges did with it.

## Article 22 in the Background: The Safeguards the Petitions Could Not Reach

Article 22 is the provision that explains why preventive detention occupies its own constitutional compartment, and its structure illuminates the petitions' predicament from a second angle. The article opens with the ordinary criminal process safeguards: a person arrested must be told the grounds of arrest, may consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of choice, and must be produced before a magistrate within twenty-four hours. It then does the thing no comparable democratic constitution of its generation did, and expressly withdraws those safeguards from two classes of person, enemy aliens and persons detained under a law providing for preventive detention. In their place, for the preventive detenu, the article erects its own thinner machinery: detention beyond three months requires the report of an advisory board that there is sufficient cause; the grounds of detention must be communicated to the detenu as soon as may be; and the detenu must be afforded the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the order, subject to the authority's power to withhold facts whose disclosure it considers against the public interest. A constitution that had simply trusted the detaining power would have stopped after the withdrawal. The Indian text instead built the substitute machinery, and the substitute machinery is the proof, internal to Part III itself, that preventive detention was designed as a supervised power, supervised by boards, by disclosure, by representation, and, behind all of those, by courts testing whether the supervision's conditions had been met.

The relevance to ADM Jabalpur is direct. When the Presidential Order of 27 June 1975 named Article 22 among the articles whose enforcement stood suspended, it removed the detenu's ability to enforce even the substitute machinery through a court: the communication of grounds, the representation, the advisory board's discipline all continued to exist as statutory and constitutional text, but the forum that policed them was closed for the same reason and by the same instrument as the forum that policed the statute. The petitions that reached the High Courts were therefore not attempts to smuggle back the withdrawn arrest safeguards of the article's opening clauses, which no one supposed a preventive detenu possessed. They were attempts to keep the substitute machinery honest, by the only route the Constitution had ever provided for keeping any executive machinery honest, an application to a court. The majority's answer closed that route too, and it is worth recording, against the judgment, that nothing in Article 22's own text invited the closure. The article that tolerates preventive detention surrounds it with conditions; a reading of the emergency provisions that left the conditions examinable would have honoured both texts at once, the tolerance and the conditions together. The reading the Court adopted honoured the tolerance and suspended the conditions' enforcement, and then, by the locus standi holding, suspended the examination of the detaining statute into the bargain. Three concentric protections, statute, article and court, were thus neutralised by a single sentence of the operative order, and the detenu was left with the advisory board, a body whose proceedings he could not test and whose materials, under Section 16A of the detaining Act, he might never see. A full statement of the case's human meaning needs no rhetoric beyond that architecture.

## The Law Before the Emergency: The Line the Case Broke

The detenus who went to the High Courts in 1975 were not inventing their argument for the occasion. They were standing on a line of authority that the Supreme Court itself had built, in the only previous Indian emergency, and the strength of that line is the strongest single proof that the result in ADM Jabalpur was chosen rather than compelled. During the emergency proclaimed for the war with China in 1962, detenus held under the Defence of India Act and its rules had faced a Presidential Order under Article 359 suspending the enforcement of Articles 21 and 22, in material respects the same instrument as the Order of 1975. In Makhan Singh v State of Punjab, decided in 1964, a Constitution Bench of seven judges, speaking through Chief Justice Gajendragadkar, had worked through precisely the structure the 1975 petitions presented. The Court held that the suspension barred a detenu from enforcing the fundamental rights named in the order, but that it did not bar him from showing that his detention was not in compliance with the statute under which it was purportedly made, or that it was mala fide, or that it rested on considerations outside the Act. Those pleas, the Court reasoned, did not enforce a fundamental right. They enforced the statute against the authority that invoked it, and a legal system in which the executive could shelter behind its own non-compliance would be one in which the statute book bound everyone except the government.

Makhan Singh was not an obscure ruling. It was the governing authority on the exact point, delivered by a larger bench than the one that sat in ADM Jabalpur, and the High Courts that rejected the Union's preliminary objection in 1975 were, in substance, applying it. The Union's task in the appeals was therefore heavier than the majority opinions sometimes suggest: it had to persuade a five-judge bench to displace the framework of a seven-judge bench without the discourtesy, or the candour, of overruling it outright. The majority managed the difficulty by recasting the Makhan Singh pleas. Where the earlier bench had seen a distinction between enforcing a statute and enforcing a fundamental right, the 1976 majority saw a single substance wearing two labels: every ground in the petitions, compliance, legality, bona fides, was in truth a device to protect personal liberty, and the protection of personal liberty was Article 21 enforcement by another name. The move was presented as an application of the doctrine that courts look to substance over form. In truth it was the decision, because a court that had kept the Makhan Singh distinction in place would have been obliged, on that authority alone, to dismiss the preliminary objection and let the High Courts proceed. Later commentary has treated this recasting as the analytical centre of the case, and rightly: the forty-one-year overrule begins here, in the quiet substitution of one characterisation for another, performed on a precedent the majority did not formally disturb.

Behind Makhan Singh lay the older and less comfortable precedent that both sides had to handle, A. K. Gopalan v State of Madras, decided in 1950, in which a majority of the Supreme Court had read the fundamental rights as a set of separate codes, each article a self-contained silo, and had refused to test a preventive detention law against the freedoms of Article 19 because Article 22 occupied the field. The silo method of Gopalan was the intellectual ancestor of the sole-repository premise of 1976: if each right lives only inside its own article, then suspending an article's enforcement empties the room it furnished. Khanna J's dissent can be read, on one level, as a refusal to extend Gopalan's method into the emergency provisions, and Maneka Gandhi's demolition of the silo method two years later as the same refusal performed by a full bench in a passport case. The line the case broke was thus double. It broke the operative rule of Makhan Singh, which had kept a statutory door open to detenus in an emergency. And it carried forward, one final time, the method of Gopalan, at the very moment when that method's intellectual foundations had already begun to collapse under it. A judgment that breaks the governing precedent on its point while extending a decaying method on its premise is a judgment building on two fault lines at once, and the aftershocks duly arrived from both directions.

## Detention Without a Door: MISA, Section 16A and the Road to the Appeals

The Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971, MISA in the universal shorthand, was the statute under which the Emergency detentions were overwhelmingly made. It authorised preventive detention on grounds connected with the security of India, the security of the State, the maintenance of public order, and the maintenance of supplies and services essential to the community, and it had been on the statute book for four years before the Emergency, as an ordinary, if severe, law of the kind Indian legislatures had enacted in an unbroken line from the first years of the Republic. The Emergency transformed its operation without, at first, transforming its text. Mass arrests of opposition leaders, trade unionists, journalists and activists followed the proclamation of 25 June 1975, and the detaining authorities issued orders under MISA in numbers that no peacetime administration of the Act had approached. The full statutory story of how the Act was tightened as the Emergency deepened is told in the companion study of [MISA and the Emergency detentions](/2010/07/01/misa-1971-emergency-detention/), and only the features that bear on the litigation belong here.

The feature that bears most heavily is Section 16A, inserted into MISA by amendment during the Emergency. In its operative effect, Section 16A permitted the detaining authority to withhold the grounds of detention from the detenu where disclosure was considered against the public interest, and barred disclosure of those grounds and of the detenu's representation in court proceedings; a related provision declared, in substance, that a detention order was not to be deemed invalid by reason of defects of the kind that ordinarily vitiate administrative action. The combined effect was to strip the detenu of the two things a challenge requires: knowledge of the case against him, and a court willing to look at it. Even so, the first wave of habeas corpus petitions was filed, in High Courts across the country, by spouses, parents and colleagues of the detained, and the petitions were framed with the Presidential Order squarely in view. They did not ask for Article 21. They asked for the statute: whether the order was under MISA, whether it complied with MISA, whether it was mala fide, whether it rested on extraneous considerations. The framing was a deliberate piece of advocacy, designed to walk through the one door the Order had not expressly shut.

### What did the High Courts decide before the Supreme Court stepped in?

Most of the High Courts that heard the preliminary objection rejected it, holding that a detenu remained entitled to show his order was not under or in compliance with the detaining statute, or was mala fide, and that a petition framed on those grounds was maintainable.

That position, taken by court after court in the middle of a proclaimed Emergency, is the omitted fact of the case: the Supreme Court was not ratifying a judicial consensus. It was reversing one.

The appeals that became ADM Jabalpur were the Union's and the detaining authorities' response to those rulings. The case name itself preserves the route: an Additional District Magistrate of Jabalpur, a detaining authority in Madhya Pradesh, stood as appellant against Shivkant Shukla, the respondent whose relative's detention had been questioned before the Madhya Pradesh High Court, and the Jabalpur appeal travelled with companion appeals from other States to be heard together by a Constitution Bench. The preliminary objection the Union pressed was the whole case in miniature. In substance, it said, every one of these petitions seeks the enforcement of the right to personal liberty; that right is conferred by Article 21; the enforcement of Article 21 stands suspended; therefore no person has locus standi to maintain such a petition, on any ground, for the duration of the Order. Notice the elegance and the danger of the argument in a single breath. It required the Court to look through the pleaded grounds to the remedy sought, and having seen release at the end of the petition, to treat every step toward it as enforcement of the suspended right. If the Court accepted that method, no drafting could save a detenu, because every successful challenge to detention ends in release. The method, more than any single sentence in the opinions, is the decision.

Arguments were heard over an extended period in the winter of 1975 and 1976, with Niren De, the Attorney General, leading for the Union, and a succession of senior counsel for the detenus across the clubbed appeals. The detenus' side did not lack for authority or ingenuity. It had the High Courts' reasoning. It had the distinction between a right and its enforcement, written into the very words of Article 359. It had the older and deeper proposition that executive action requires legal authority, a proposition no emergency provision had purported to repeal. And it had, in the courtroom, the one exchange that has outlasted every written page of the case, to which a later section of this article is devoted. What it did not have was a majority. On 28 April 1976 the Constitution Bench delivered its answer, four opinions concurring in the result for the Union, and one dissent. The operative order was stated in a single sentence of formidable completeness: in view of the Presidential Order of 27 June 1975, no person had any locus standi to move any writ petition under Article 226 before a High Court for habeas corpus, or any other writ, order or direction, to challenge the legality of an order of detention on the ground that the order was not under or in compliance with the Act, or was illegal, or was vitiated by mala fides, factual or legal, or was based on extraneous considerations. Every ground the advocates had devised was named, and every one was foreclosed.

## The Majority: Article 21 as the Sole Repository

Four judges wrote separately and arrived together. Chief Justice A. N. Ray, Justice M. H. Beg, Justice Y. V. Chandrachud and Justice P. N. Bhagwati each delivered an opinion concurring in the operative order, and the differences among their opinions matter, because the majority was not a monolith and its several strands had different afterlives. The strand that did the decisive work, and that later law had to kill most thoroughly, was the premise about sources. Article 21, in the majority's treatment, was the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty against the State. The right was a creation of the Constitution, a gift of the constitutional text to the citizen, and where the Constitution's own emergency machinery suspended the enforcement of that text, there was nothing behind the text to appeal to. A court could not enforce a right that had no existence outside the provision whose enforcement stood suspended. From that premise, the conclusion followed with mechanical neatness: a habeas corpus petition, whatever its pleaded grounds, was an attempt to protect life and liberty; life and liberty were protected, if at all, by Article 21; Article 21 could not be enforced; the petition could not be entertained. The premise did the work. Once it was granted, advocacy was decoration.

Chief Justice Ray's opinion carried the premise in its purest form, joined to a conception of liberty as a creature of positive law: freedom, on this view, is what the legal order grants, defined and bounded by the instruments that grant it, and a court mourning the absence of a remedy the Constitution had withdrawn was mistaking its own preferences for law. Justice Beg's opinion added a note that has drawn particular criticism across the decades, a confidence in the detaining executive that sat oddly beside the record of the detentions: his opinion expressed trust that the authorities exercising the detention power would act with care and responsibility toward the detenus in their charge, a judicial benediction on the very machinery whose orders the petitions sought to test. Justice Chandrachud's opinion is the most intellectually exposed of the four, because it confronted the hardship directly and conceded, in substance, that the conclusion was severe, while insisting that severity was what the constitutional text, honestly read, required of a judge who had sworn to uphold the Constitution as it stood and not as he wished it to be. Justice Bhagwati's opinion worked through the structure of the emergency provisions and the reach of the Presidential Order with the closest attention to the scheme of Article 359, concluding that the Order's bar on moving any court for the enforcement of Article 21 necessarily covered petitions whose substance was the protection of personal liberty. Each route arrived at the same door, and the door stayed shut.

### Why did four senior judges accept that even a mala fide detention could not be questioned?

Because of where they located the right. If Article 21 is the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty, then a petition against a mala fide detention is still, in substance, a petition to enforce Article 21, whose enforcement the Presidential Order had suspended.

On the majority's premise, the pleaded grounds, non-compliance, illegality, mala fides, were labels on the same forbidden application. The premise was chosen first, and the foreclosure of every ground followed from it as a matter of logic.

Two further elements of the majority position need to be stated accurately, because the case against the judgment is stronger when its target is the real one. The first is the treatment of precedent and principle from before the Constitution. The detenus' argument drew strength from the proposition, familiar in the common law tradition, that the executive must point to legal authority for every act that invades liberty, and that a court may test whether the authority covers the act. The majority did not deny that such testing is the ordinary business of courts in ordinary times. It held that the field was occupied: the Constitution had spoken about what happens to rights enforcement during an Emergency, and its speaking displaced the older understandings for the duration. The second element is the refusal to separate the remedy from the right in the way the petitions proposed. A habeas corpus petition that succeeds ends in release; release is the enforcement of personal liberty; therefore, the majority reasoned, the distinction between testing the detention order and enforcing Article 21 was a distinction without a difference, a pleader's device to obtain by indirection what the Order forbade directly. There is a real jurisprudential instinct inside that move, the instinct that courts should not allow form to defeat a constitutional provision's evident purpose. The tragedy of the case is that the instinct was deployed in one direction only. The Court looked through the petitioners' form to their substance with great sophistication, and declined to look through the Presidential Order's form, a suspension of enforcement, to the substance of what remained: a detaining power still bounded by its own statute, and a statute whose bounds the Order had never purported to erase.

What the majority did not do is as much a part of the holding as what it did. It did not hold that the detention orders were valid. It did not examine the grounds of any individual detention, did not find that MISA had been complied with in the cases before the High Courts, and did not decide that the detaining authorities had acted in good faith. The operative order is scrupulously jurisdictional: no locus standi, no maintainable petition, no examination. That scruple is why the later overruling could be so clean, and also why the judgment's practical effect was so bleak. A court that had examined the detentions and upheld them would have left a record that later courts could distinguish case by case. A court that refused examination left nothing to distinguish, only a closed door and the reasoning that had closed it, and the reasoning, the sole-repository premise, was the part that had to be dismantled root and branch before the law could breathe again.

## Anatomy of the Operative Order

The four opinions run to great length, but the law the case made is contained in the operative order, and a close reading of that single sentence is the best protection against the loose summaries that have accumulated around the case. Its first element is the subject: no person. Not no detenu, not no petitioner who fails to plead a particular ground, but no person at all, a formulation that swept in the wives, parents and colleagues who had in fact filed the petitions, and whose standing to complain of the detention of another had never been doubted in habeas corpus practice, where the writ's whole history is a history of applications made by those outside the prison on behalf of those inside it. Its second element is the forum and the instrument: any writ petition under Article 226 before a High Court, for habeas corpus or any other writ, order or direction. The enumeration was designed to prevent the escape routes that administrative lawyers keep in reserve, the substitution of certiorari for habeas corpus, of a declaration for a direction, of one constitutional label for another. Its third element is the ground, and it is here that the sentence does its most consequential work: to challenge the legality of an order of detention on the ground that the order is not under or in compliance with the Act, or is illegal, or is vitiated by mala fides, factual or legal, or is based on extraneous considerations. Every member of the standard family of challenges to detention is named. A challenge to the authority's satisfaction, to its materials, to its purposes, to its procedure, each finds its phrase in the sentence, and each is foreclosed by it. A lawyer reading the order in 1976 with a client's instructions in hand would have searched it, ground by ground, for the plea it had omitted, and would have closed the file. There was none.

The sentence's silences are as instructive as its contents. It does not say that the detention orders were valid, and the distinction between a holding that no one may challenge an order and a holding that the order is good is the distinction the whole subsequent history exploits. It does not address the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 32 in terms, because the appeals before the Court arose from the High Courts under Article 226, but no reader in 1976 could have supposed that a petition to the Supreme Court itself, founded on the enforcement of the same named rights, would have fared better under the same Presidential Order, whose bar ran to moving any court for that enforcement. And it does not purport to construe the Maintenance of Internal Security Act at all. The statute whose compliance could not be pleaded survived the case uninterpreted, in force, its conditions intact on its face, binding in terms on the authorities who administered it and unenforceable in practice at the instance of the persons it imprisoned. That configuration, a statute simultaneously binding and unexaminable, is the precise legal artefact the Emergency produced and the precise artefact the dissent refused to accept as law. When the formal overruling came forty-one years later, it was this sentence, and the premise packed into it, that the nine judges discharged. Everything else in the four opinions is reasoning toward the sentence or away from it, and the student who masters the sentence, its subject, its forum, its grounds and its silences, has mastered the case in the only form in which it ever governed a single day's detention.

## Reading the Four Opinions Separately

Because the four majority opinions concur only in their conclusion, a serious study of the case disaggregates them, and the exercise repays the effort because each strand found a different posterity. Chief Justice Ray's opinion is the most thoroughly positivist document in the case. Its animating conviction is that rights are artefacts of the constitutional order that confers them, that liberty in a modern state is a regulated condition defined by law and not a natural condition restrained by law, and that a judge who searches behind the text for an unenacted liberty is legislating. On that conviction, the emergency provisions presented no difficulty at all: the Constitution had provided for its own partial suspension, the suspension had been effected in due form, and the Court's duty ended with the forms. The opinion also contains the strand of the majority reasoning most often forgotten, a warning against supposing that judicial power expands as crisis deepens, and a reminder that the Court's authority in normal times depends on its fidelity in abnormal ones. A reader who finds that warning attractive in the abstract, as many do, has located the genuine difficulty of the case, which is that the positivist strand was not foolishness. It was a coherent theory of adjudication, held by a Chief Justice of long experience, and it failed not because it was incoherent but because it answered the question of the rights' source with a completeness the Constitution's own structure, a text that elsewhere speaks the language of inherent dignity and of justice, liberty and equality as founding commitments, does not obviously support.

Justice Beg's opinion followed the conclusion by a warmer road, and the warmth is its lasting problem. Where Ray CJ argued from the nature of positive law, Beg J argued in part from the presumed benevolence of the power being challenged, expressing the expectation that the authorities administering the detention law would treat the detenus in their custody with the care and concern that the situation demanded, and treating the spectre of abuse as an uncharitable hypothesis. The passage has been quoted against the judgment ever since, and it deserves the quotation, not because judicial trust in administration is always misplaced, but because the petitions before the Court were, in form, requests to verify that very care against the statute, and a judge who declines verification on the ground that the verified party can be trusted has answered a question of law with a sentiment. The sequel compounded the damage. Within nine months of the judgment, Beg J was appointed Chief Justice over the head of the dissenting judge, and whatever the appointment's stated grounds, the sequence fixed his opinion in the public memory as the strand of the majority that history read least charitably, the strand in which doctrine and disposition could no longer be separated.

Justice Chandrachud's opinion is the one a thoughtful defender of the majority would choose to stand on, and the one its critics find hardest to dismiss. It does not pretend the conclusion is comfortable. It acknowledges the rigour of a position under which a person detained in defiance of the statute has no forum, and it rests on a single austere proposition: that a judge's oath runs to the Constitution as it is, that the emergency provisions are as much a part of that Constitution as the rights they suspend, and that to strain the text toward mercy in a crisis is to arrogate a constituent power the Court does not possess. The opinion's austerity is also its exposure. The petitions had not asked the Court to strain any text; they had asked it to apply the distinction Article 359 itself enacted, between a right and the right to move a court for its enforcement, and to honour the ordinary rule that executive action needs statutory authority. Declining that invitation required a premise the text had not supplied, and austerity about texts sits awkwardly beside generosity in supplying premises. Chandrachud J's later life gave the strand its coda: seven years and more as Chief Justice, the longest tenure in the office, spent presiding over the very expansion of Article 21 that his 1976 opinion would have made unnecessary, and, as later sections record, a public expression of regret for the part he had played. The arc from the austere opinion to the regret is the nearest thing the case offers to a controlled experiment in judicial psychology: the same judge, the same text, and a different conclusion about what the oath required, reached when the pressure that had framed the question was gone.

Justice Bhagwati's opinion, the most craftsmanlike of the four in its handling of the Article 359 scheme, rewards attention for a reason its author could not have intended: it is the opinion of the judge who would do more than any other to build the jurisprudence that replaced the world of 1976. Within five years of the judgment, Bhagwati J was a central architect of public interest litigation, relaxing the rules of locus standi that ADM Jabalpur had applied at their strictest, admitting letters and postcards as writ petitions, and treating access to the courts for the powerless as the judiciary's defining modern mission. The distance between the judge who held that no person had locus standi to question a detention and the judge who held that almost any person had locus standi to question almost anything is the distance the Indian judiciary travelled in a decade, and it was travelled, in significant part, as a penance. His concurrence also carried a biographical complication that later accounts have documented and that a fair history must state plainly: during the Emergency he had written to the Prime Minister in praise of her leadership, a letter his critics have never allowed the record to forget. The letter proves nothing about the reasoning of his opinion, which stands or falls on its treatment of Article 359. It belongs in the record because the case was decided inside a political atmosphere, and pretending that the atmosphere stopped at the courtroom door helps no reader understand how four able judges arrived where they did.

## The Exchange That Defined the Case

Judgments are remembered by their holdings, but cases are sometimes remembered by a single passage of argument, and ADM Jabalpur is remembered by an exchange between the dissenting judge and the Attorney General. During the hearing, Khanna J pressed Niren De on the logic of the Union's submission. Article 21 speaks of life as well as liberty. If the Union was right that no court could be moved for the enforcement of Article 21 in any form while the Order operated, what followed for life itself? The reported accounts of the hearing agree on the substance of what passed, though they differ in small points of phrasing, and the substance is this: Khanna J asked whether, if a police officer took the life of a detenu, from personal enmity or for reasons wholly unconnected with any lawful purpose, the victim's side would have any remedy in court for the duration of the Emergency. The Attorney General, following his instructions and the logic of his argument where it led, answered that it would not; consistently with the submission, there would be no judicial remedy while the suspension lasted. The exchange has been retold in memoirs, in obituaries, in judgments of later benches, and in the arguments of counsel in liberty cases ever since, because it performed in thirty seconds what volumes of commentary could not: it showed the price of the sole-repository premise, stated by the government's own law officer, without embarrassment and without escape.

Precision about this passage matters, and the embellishments that gather around it should be resisted. The Attorney General was not confessing a personal appetite for lawless killing. He was doing the advocate's duty of following a submission to its consequence, and there are accounts that he later described the consequence he had stated as an unfortunate one, the position of his instructions rather than of his conscience. The exchange indicts the argument, not the advocate's character, and its force lies exactly there: a proposition about locus standi, framed in the dry vocabulary of maintainability, turned out on inspection to be a proposition about whether a wrongful killing by an officer of the State could be brought before any court at all. Khanna J's question was the whole dissent in embryo. If the consequence shocked the conscience, then somewhere in the chain of reasoning a premise was wrong, and the dissent locates the error in the first link: the claim that the right to life and liberty lives only inside Article 21, so that suspending the article's enforcement leaves the citizen with nothing and the State with everything.

The majority opinions, delivered weeks later, did not flinch from that consequence, because on their premises it was not a consequence to be avoided but a corollary to be accepted. That acceptance is the hinge on which the case's reputation turns. Courts are sometimes compelled to unwelcome conclusions by texts they did not write, and a judge who bends a text to escape an unwelcome conclusion trades one constitutional vice for another. But the detenus had placed before the Court a reading of the same text on which the consequence did not follow: enforcement of specified rights was suspended, the statute that authorised detention was not suspended, and testing an order against its statute was not enforcement of a fundamental right. The reading was available, it was supported by the reasoning of most of the High Courts that had considered the objection, and it required no violence to any word of Article 359. The four judges who declined it were not driven to their conclusion by the text alone, and the candour of the Attorney General's answer is what makes that plain: he understood, and said, exactly what the majority's premises entailed, and the majority adopted the premises anyway.

## The Dissent: Khanna J and the Law Above Article 21

Justice H. R. Khanna's dissent is the most consequential separate opinion in the history of the Supreme Court of India, and its argument can be stated in a sequence of propositions, each of which later law has had to reckon with. The first is that Article 21 does not create the right to life and personal liberty; it recognises and protects a right that is antecedent to the Constitution, a basic value of any legal order that claims to be governed by law. The second follows: because the right is not the Constitution's gift, the suspension of one article's enforcement does not extinguish it, and the State's duty to act only under authority of law survives the Presidential Order untouched, since no provision of the Constitution or of the Order purported to authorise action without law. The third is the remedial consequence: a detenu who says his detention is without authority of law, or outside the statute invoked to support it, or mala fide, does not seek to enforce a suspended fundamental right; he seeks the ordinary judicial determination that the executive show its authority, and a High Court entertaining that plea under Article 226 does its constitutional duty rather than defying the Order. The fourth proposition is about the rule of law itself: that the rule of law is not a favour the Constitution grants in fair weather, and that even in the absence of Article 21, a legal system in which life and liberty could be taken without authority of law and without remedy would have ceased, in that respect, to be a system of law at all.

The authorities and the method deserve attention, because the hagiographic version of the dissent treats it as an act of pure moral intuition, and that undersells its craftsmanship and misleads students about how dissents persuade. Khanna J reasoned from the structure of the Constitution, from the distinction Article 359 itself draws between rights and their enforcement, from the history of the writ of habeas corpus as a remedy directed at the custodian's authority rather than at any catalogue of rights, and from the position in other constitutional systems where liberty had been tested by emergency, including the wartime decisions of the English courts, whose majority deference he examined and whose famous dissent, that of Lord Atkin in Liversidge v Anderson, stood as the model of what a judge in his position might do and of the vindication that might, in time, arrive. He was not unaware of the parallel; no judge writing that dissent in 1976 could have been. He is reported, in the recollections of his family, to have said on the night before the judgment that he had prepared his opinion and that it would cost him the Chief Justiceship of India. The foreknowledge strips the act of any plea of surprise and converts it into what it was: a considered payment, made in advance, at a known price.

### What method did Khanna J use to build his dissent?

Khanna J reasoned from the text of Article 359, from the precedent in Makhan Singh, which had preserved the statutory pleas in the previous emergency, and from the structure of a legal order in which executive action requires authority of law. He closed by invoking the account of a dissent as an appeal to a future day.

On that method the conclusion followed directly: a detenu could still ask a High Court to test whether his detention order complied with the detaining statute or was mala fide, because that plea enforces the statute and the rule of law, not a suspended right.

The closing movement of the dissent has been quoted so often that its argument is sometimes lost inside its music. Khanna J ended by invoking, as other accounts of the dissent record, the celebrated description of a dissent as an appeal to the brooding spirit of the law and to the intelligence of a future day, words written by Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes of the United States about the function of dissenting opinions. The invocation was a precise piece of self-knowledge. A dissent cannot release a single detenu. Its author knows that the order of the Court will run against everything he has written, that the prisons will stay full, and that the only tribunal available to him is the future: later benches, later Parliaments, later readers. To write a dissent in that knowledge, and to write it well enough that the future accepts the appeal, is the rarest achievement in appellate judging, and it is why the dissent, and not any of the four concurring opinions, became the living law's account of the case long before the formal overruling arrived. The future day Hughes described took forty-one years to sit, but it sat, and when it sat it read the dissent back into the law almost entire.

One caution belongs in any study of the dissent, and the stance of this article requires it. The dissent's vindication does not make every sentence of it unanswerable, and treating it as scripture repeats, in mirror image, the error of treating the majority as beneath argument. Khanna J's premise, that the right to life and liberty subsists independently of the constitutional text, carries commitments about the sources of law that a positivist reader may resist, and the boundary he proposed, between enforcing a suspended right and testing statutory authority, is a boundary that administrators of the emergency provisions would have litigated at every step had it prevailed. Those are arguments worth having, and later sections return to them. But they are arguments about the price of the dissent's route, and the comparison that matters is with the price of the route taken: a closed door, an Attorney General's concession about a hypothetical killing, and a precedent that stood unreversed for four decades. Measured against its alternative rather than against an ideal, the dissent's stature is not diminished. It is explained.

## The Dissent's Method: Precedent, Comparison and the English Mirror

The dissent's method deserves the same disaggregated reading as the majority's, because its persuasion across forty years was not an accident of sentiment. Its first move was fidelity to the governing precedent. Makhan Singh had held, for a bench of seven, that a detenu could challenge his detention as non-compliant with the statute or mala fide notwithstanding an Article 359 order, and Khanna J's position required no innovation beyond taking that holding seriously: the petitions of 1975 raised the pleas Makhan Singh had preserved, and the burden of displacing them lay on the Union. A dissent that asks the court merely to follow its own larger bench occupies ground that is much harder to abandon than ground staked out by moral appeal, and when Puttaswamy eventually overruled the majority, the restoration it effected was, in this respect, a homecoming: the law returned to the position from which the preliminary objection of 1975 should never have dislodged it. The second move was comparative, and it was made with a judge's instinct for the telling parallel. In Liversidge v Anderson, decided in 1941, the House of Lords had deferred to a Home Secretary's detention of a man under wartime regulations, over the dissent of Lord Atkin, who accused the majority of being more executive-minded than the executive. Atkin was vindicated by posterity and ignored in his lifetime, his dissent becoming the canonical text on judicial duty in emergencies while the majority speeches mouldered. Khanna J wrote with that precedent in the room, and the correspondence between the two dissents, across thirty-five years and two legal systems, is too exact to be coincidence: the same deference by the majority, the same insistence by the dissenter that words in a statute and a constitution mean what they say even in a crisis, the same certainty that the future would reverse the valuation. India completed the pattern the English case had sketched, because Puttaswamy did formally what English law never needed to do, and overruled the majority in terms.

The third move was the deepest, and it is the one on which the whole subsequent law of Article 21 has been built, whether or not the builders acknowledged the debt in every case. Khanna J denied that the Constitution is the source of the right to life and personal liberty, and he grounded the denial not in theology but in the structure of a legal order: the Constitution, on his reading, recognises and guarantees rights that any system calling itself a government of laws must presuppose, since the alternative is a State whose power over life and liberty is limited only by its own enactments, which is to say limited by nothing it cannot change. From that ground, the remedial conclusion followed without strain. A Presidential Order could suspend the enforcement of the articles it named. It could not repeal the presupposition, and it could not make an unauthorised detention authorised. The detenu who pleaded non-compliance or mala fides was therefore not smuggling a suspended right into court under a statutory label. He was invoking the presupposition itself, through the ordinary channel of a writ directed at the custodian's authority. Critics of the dissent, in 1976 and since, have pressed the point that this reasoning constitutionalises natural law by the back door, and the objection is a real one that a serious reader should entertain rather than wave away. The answer the subsequent law has given is pragmatic and complete: when the nine judges of Puttaswamy came to state the foundation of the privacy right and of Article 21 itself, they adopted the dissent's ground in its own terms, holding that life and personal liberty are inalienable rights recognised by the Constitution and not conferred by it. A premise that the Court's largest benches now recite as orthodoxy cannot be dismissed as one judge's philosophy. It has become the doctrine, and the dissent's method, precedent, comparison and structure in that order, is a large part of how it got there.

## The Strongest Case for the Majority, Stated Fairly

A stance of evenhandedness is worth nothing if it is applied only to the side the writer favours, and the majority in ADM Jabalpur has a case, which should be put at its strongest before it is answered. It begins with the text. Article 359 is not an oversight or a trap; it is a deliberate provision, placed in the Constitution by its framers, who had lived through the freedom struggle and knew exactly what preventive detention was, and who nevertheless wrote into Part XVIII a machinery by which the enforcement of fundamental rights could be suspended in an emergency, naming no exception. The framers of the Indian Constitution also wrote Article 22, with its express toleration of preventive detention, a toleration no comparable democratic constitution of the era contained. A judge asked in 1976 to hold that the emergency machinery could not do the very thing it appeared designed to do was being asked to perform major constitutional surgery in the middle of the emergency itself, and judicial restraint in such a moment is not a synonym for cowardice. It has a principled pedigree: courts that rewrite emergency provisions under pressure may win a season's applause and lose the settlement that makes their ordinary authority possible. The majority could also point to the practice of other democracies in wartime, where courts of high reputation had deferred to executive detention on reasoning not unlike its own, and to the consideration that the merits of individual detentions, involving security assessments the executive alone was placed to make, were the least justiciable material a court could be offered in a crisis.

The strongest version continues with an argument about candour. The alternative reading, the detenus' reading, preserved the form of judicial review while the emergency lasted, but it did so by a distinction, between testing an order and enforcing a right, that the majority regarded as artificial in the detention context, since the only practical content of the testing was release. A court that adopts an artificial distinction to soften a constitutional provision it dislikes has not defended the Constitution; it has evaded it, and evasions of that kind corrode the authority on which all judicial protection of liberty ultimately rests. On this view, the four judges chose the honest course: they said what the text, as they read it, required, they said it without disguise, and they left the remedy where the Constitution had left it, with the political branches and with the people who would judge those branches when the emergency ended. There is even a grim defence of the result on institutional grounds: that a judgment so stark, which concealed nothing, made the stakes of the emergency provisions impossible to miss, and thereby armed the reformers who later amended them, whereas a fudged decision might have normalised the suspension and left the text unreformed. Versions of this argument, that the majority's clarity was its unintended service, have been made by commentators who have no sympathy for the decision itself.

The answer, when it comes, must meet that case and not a caricature of it, and it comes in three parts. The first is that the text did not compel the result. Article 359 suspends the right to move a court for the enforcement of named rights. The detenus did not move a court for the enforcement of Article 21; they moved it for the enforcement of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act against the authority that claimed its shelter. Whether that characterisation is artificial is a question of judgment, not of compulsion, and the High Courts of the country, reading the same text in the same emergency, predominantly judged it the other way. A provision genuinely incapable of bearing the detenus' meaning would not have persuaded court after court that it bore exactly that meaning. The second part is that the majority's own premises reached beyond the text. Nothing in Article 359 declares Article 21 to be the sole repository of the right to life and liberty, and nothing in it addresses the antecedent question whether executive action requires legal authority. Those propositions were supplied by the majority, not found in the article, and a court that supplies the premise that decides the case cannot then plead that the text decided it. The third part is that the institutional defence proves too much. Courts do not ordinarily justify a refusal of jurisdiction by the excellence of the reforms the refusal may provoke, and the judges who lived with the decision did not, in their later reflections, advance that defence on their own behalf. The criticism of ADM Jabalpur that endures, in the writings of H. M. Seervai in his Constitutional Law of India and in the wider literature the case generated, is precisely calibrated to this point: not that the majority misread a word, but that it read a remedial suspension as a licence, supplied the premise the licence required, and refused a remedy the text had not clearly forbidden. That criticism stands. The majority's argument was serious, and it was wrong.

## The Price of a Dissent: Supersession and Resignation

The institutional sequel to the judgment arrived on a schedule that removed any doubt about how dissents of this kind were priced. Under the convention of seniority that governed appointments to the office of Chief Justice of India, Khanna J, as the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court after Chief Justice Ray, stood next in line for the chief justiceship when Ray CJ retired in January 1977. The government passed him over. Justice Beg, a member of the ADM Jabalpur majority, was appointed Chief Justice instead. Khanna J resigned from the Court. The supersession was the third such departure from the seniority convention in the decade, and its meaning was read identically by every observer, friendly or hostile: the dissent in the Habeas Corpus case had been noted, and the institution's senior dissenter would not lead it. The government of the day defended the appointment on stated grounds of its prerogative in matters of appointment, and defenders of the supersession pointed out, accurately, that seniority was a convention rather than a constitutional command. Both things are true, and neither answers the point that matters for the study of this case, which is the signal the episode sent to every judge who would thereafter be asked to decide a liberty case against a determined executive: the signal that the price schedule for dissent ran up to the summit of a judicial career, and that the price would be collected in public.

### Why was Justice Khanna superseded in January 1977?

Because the seniority convention made him next in line when Chief Justice Ray retired that month, and the government chose not to appoint the judge who had dissented in the Habeas Corpus case, appointing Justice Beg of the majority as Chief Justice instead. Khanna J resigned, and the supersession was read across the profession as the price of the dissent.

The dissent he had written the previous April, in the stated expectation that it would cost him the chief justiceship, was thus followed by the exact consequence he had foreseen when he chose to write it.

The later life completes the record and should be stated without ornament. Khanna J did not withdraw into bitterness or obscurity. He served a brief spell as Union Law Minister in 1979, in the government that followed the Janata period's fragmentation, a posting that placed the dissenter, for a short interval, in charge of the ministry that speaks for the law in the cabinet. His portrait hangs in the Supreme Court, an honour the institution accords its own, and his dissent entered the training of generations of lawyers as the standing example of the difference between a court's order and a court's conscience. When, decades later, a nine-judge bench came to overrule the case, the opinion that performed the overruling paused over the dissent with a respect that benches do not always extend to the dead: the position Khanna J had taken alone was treated as the position the law should have taken all along. Supersession had denied him the chief justiceship of the Court he served. It had not denied him the authorship of its most honoured opinion, and the exchange, viewed across the whole arc, was not one the government that priced his dissent would have chosen to advertise.

There is a wider institutional question here, and the stance of this series requires that it be treated as genuinely open rather than as settled by sentiment. Judicial independence is protected by many devices, security of tenure, salary charged on the Consolidated Fund, removal only by impeachment, and the ADM Jabalpur aftermath exposed the soft point that none of those devices reaches: the distribution of honours and elevations within a career, which lies in executive gift and which can be withheld without any judge being removed, threatened, or even criticised. A system can keep every formal guarantee and still teach its judges, by a single publicised supersession, where favour lies. The reforms and controversies about judicial appointments that have occupied Indian public law for decades afterwards, including the collegium system and the litigation over the National Judicial Appointments Commission, all conduct their arguments in the shadow of episodes of this kind, and a reader who understands the Khanna supersession understands why the appointment question in India is never merely administrative. It is the question of who prices dissent, and at what rate.

## What the Case Did Not Decide

The limits of a decision are part of its meaning, and ADM Jabalpur has been credited and debited with a great deal that its operative order never touched. It did not amend the Constitution. It did not strike down or validate the Maintenance of Internal Security Act as a whole, and the collateral questions about the Act's harsher provisions were left, in substance, without a forum rather than answered on their merits. It did not hold that fundamental rights had ceased to exist, and the distinction is not pedantry: the Presidential Order suspended the right to move a court for the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22, and the majority's own reasoning, for all its severity, proceeded on the footing that enforcement was the casualty. Popular accounts that say the Supreme Court suspended fundamental rights in 1976 misstate the instrument and, in misstating it, miss the very distinction on which Khanna J's dissent was built and on which the case's eventual correction partly turned. Precision here is a form of respect for the losing side of the argument, whose whole position was that the gap between a right and its remedy was where the law still lived.

Nor did the case decide anything about ordinary times, or about detentions outside the shadow of a proclaimed emergency and a subsisting Article 359 order, or about the power of courts to examine detention under statutes generally. Its ratio was chained to its instruments. That is one reason the decades that followed could route around it so extensively without formally disturbing it: the emergency provisions were amended, Article 21 was rebuilt by interpretation into a provision of entirely different dimensions, and the specific conjunction of text and order that had produced the result ceased to be reproducible, while the judgment itself sat undisturbed in the reports, authoritative in form and avoided in practice. The result is a curiosity of the common law that students of precedent should study carefully, because it shows the two distinct deaths a precedent can die. It can be overruled, by a bench of sufficient strength, in terms. Or it can be abandoned in place, distinguished, ignored, and disapproved in the literature, until its formal overruling, when it comes, registers a fact that the profession has long since absorbed. ADM Jabalpur died both deaths, in that order reversed: abandoned first, in spirit, within a few years of its delivery, and overruled, in form, only after forty-one years.

The relationship of the case to the doctrine of basic structure deserves a paragraph of its own, because the two are entangled in the public memory and were entangled in fact. Kesavananda Bharati, decided in 1973, had held that Parliament's amending power does not extend to damaging the basic structure of the Constitution, a doctrine whose full architecture is traced in the series study of [the basic structure doctrine](/2010/02/01/basic-structure-doctrine-explained/). The ADM Jabalpur bench sat three years after Kesavananda, in a courtroom where the ink of that struggle between Court and Parliament was barely dry, and the emergency litigation cannot be understood without the atmosphere that struggle created: a government that had already superseded judges once, in 1973, after an unwelcome decision, and a Court aware that its authority rested on settlements that determined executives could reopen. The majority in ADM Jabalpur did not rest its conclusion on any rejection of basic structure, and the case is not authority against the doctrine. But the shadow is part of the explanation contemporaries offered for the Court's caution, and an honest history records the shadow while refusing to let it decide the verdict.

## The Undoing of ADM Jabalpur

The correction of ADM Jabalpur was not an event. It was a sequence, spread across four decades and three different kinds of instrument, constitutional amendment, judicial reinterpretation, and formal overruling, with a fourth element, personal and institutional reckoning, running alongside. The sequence is the article's findable artifact, set out in the table that follows this discussion, and its lesson is the namable claim with which this article began: overruling a decision and preventing its recurrence are different projects, and India, to its credit, eventually undertook both. The political correction came first and fastest. The Emergency was withdrawn in March 1977, the government that had proclaimed it was defeated at the general election that followed, and the judgment stood discredited in public life within two years of its delivery, condemned in Parliament, in the press, and in the profession's own journals. None of that touched its status as law. Discredit is a political condition. Precedent is a legal one. The gap between the two is where the rest of the sequence unfolds.

The judicial rebuilding began almost at once, and it began with Article 21 itself. In Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided in January 1978, a seven-judge bench re-read Article 21 from its foundations: the procedure that deprives a person of life or personal liberty must be just, fair and reasonable, not arbitrary or oppressive; Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive silos but interpenetrating guarantees, so that a law touching liberty must satisfy the equality and freedom articles as well. The silo reading of fundamental rights, on which the majority in ADM Jabalpur had implicitly relied when it treated Article 21 as a self-contained repository whose suspension ended the inquiry, was dismantled in terms. Maneka Gandhi did not overrule ADM Jabalpur, which concerned a different question and could not have been overruled by implication without confusion, but it removed the intellectual ground from beneath the judgment, and every subsequent expansion of Article 21, into livelihood, health, environment, education and privacy, widened the distance between the living Constitution and the closed door of 1976.

The legislative correction addressed the recurrence, and it is the step that matters most for anyone asking whether the case could happen again. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, enacted in 1978 and brought into force in stages across 1979, amended Article 359 to provide that the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended by a Presidential Order, whatever the emergency; the full account of what that amendment restored and what it left unrestored is given in the series study of [the 44th Amendment and the undoing of the Emergency settlement](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/). The amendment did not purport to overrule the judgment, which concerned an order lawfully issued under the earlier text. It did something more durable: it withdrew the instrument. A future President, proclaiming a future emergency, cannot name Article 21 in an Article 359 order, and a future bench asked to repeat the ADM Jabalpur reasoning would have to begin by explaining away the constitutional text that now forbids the premise. Between them, Maneka Gandhi and the Forty-fourth Amendment had, by 1979, made the decision practically sterile. It governed a conjunction of proclamation, order and text that could no longer occur. What remained was the formality, and the formality waited.

The reckoning, when it came, came first from within the majority itself. Justice Bhagwati, who had concurred in 1976 and who went on to become Chief Justice of India and one of the architects of public interest litigation, returned to the judgment in later life without evasion. In an interview in 2011, thirty-five years after the decision, he said in substance that the Supreme Court should be ashamed of the ADM Jabalpur judgment, that he pleaded guilty to his part in it, and that he did not know why he had yielded, describing his concurrence as a weakness he regretted and a judgment that had been against his conscience. Justice Chandrachud, by then long retired from the chief justiceship he had held for over seven years, is likewise reported to have expressed regret for his part in the majority in an address delivered after the Emergency, saying that he regretted not having had the courage to lay down his office rather than join the conclusion he reached. These statements do not change the law. They change the record on which the institution judges itself, and they matter to the student of the case for a precise reason: the two judges who expressed regret were not marginal figures distancing themselves from an aberration, but two of the most consequential judges of their generation, and their confessions established, from the inside, that the majority's reasoning had not commanded even its own authors' settled conviction. A precedent disowned by its makers, rebuilt around by amendment and reinterpretation, and condemned in the literature stood for decades in that condition, overruled by everyone except a court.

| Still true | Position after all corrections | The case remains the standing study of how a locus standi ruling can close a legal system to a class of grievances | The questions it never reached: the merits of the Emergency detentions themselves, which no corrective step ever adjudicated |

The formal end arrived in 2017, inside a case about something else. K. S. Puttaswamy v Union of India, the privacy case, required a nine-judge bench to determine whether the Constitution recognises a fundamental right to privacy, and that inquiry led the bench back through the precedents in which the Court had read the fundamental rights narrowly, ADM Jabalpur among them. The detailed treatment of that judgment and of the privacy holding belongs to the series study of [Puttaswamy, privacy and the Aadhaar verdict](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/), which owns those questions; what belongs here is the overruling itself, which was express, unqualified and unanimous in its rejection of the 1976 majority. The opinions in Puttaswamy held that the majority judgments in ADM Jabalpur were seriously flawed, and that life and personal liberty are inalienable rights, not creations of the Constitution but recognitions by it of rights that inhere in every human being. Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, writing for a plurality of the bench, addressed the case his father had decided forty-one years earlier and closed it in a sentence that leaves nothing to implication: ADM Jabalpur must be and is accordingly overruled. The son overruled the father, in terms, on the very premise the father had accepted, that Article 21 is the source rather than the recognition of the right. With that sentence, the forty-one-year overrule was complete, and the dissent of Khanna J, the appeal to the intelligence of a future day, stood answered by the tribunal it had named.

## The Years of Avoidance: A Precedent Nobody Would Use

Between the sterile correctness of 1979, when amendment and reinterpretation had made the decision irreproducible, and the formality of 2017, when a court finally buried it, ADM Jabalpur lived the strange half-life of a precedent that bound in theory and persuaded nobody. The mechanics of that half-life are worth studying, because they show how a common law system digests a decision it is ashamed of but not yet equipped to overrule. Overruling required a bench larger than five, and Constitution Benches of greater strength are not assembled to tidy the reports; they are assembled because a live case compels the question. For four decades no live case compelled it, precisely because the Forty-fourth Amendment had removed the factual predicate. The decision therefore survived by the same logic that had produced it: the system's formal rules, about bench strength and the occasions for reference, protected a ruling whose substance the system had abandoned. Counsel learned the etiquette quickly. ADM Jabalpur was cited, when it was cited at all, by the side that expected to lose the citation, or by a court in the course of explaining why some adjacent proposition did not follow, or by historians of the Court rather than by advocates seeking a result. Its appearance in a judgment became a reliable signal that the passage containing it would be about the Emergency, the judicial role, or the sources of rights in the abstract, anywhere, in short, except in the operative reasoning that decided the case at hand.

The reckoning outside the courts ran faster and deeper. The Shah Commission of Inquiry, appointed by the government that succeeded the Emergency regime to examine the excesses committed during it, reported in 1978 and documented, across volumes, the manner in which the detention power had been used: orders signed without application of mind, grounds that dissolved on inspection, a machinery of arrest operating as an instrument of political management. The Commission was not a court and its findings decided no one's rights, but its record performed one function the courts had declined to perform in 1976: it examined the detentions. Read against that record, the majority's refusal of examination acquired its permanent context. The question the High Courts had been forbidden to ask, whether these orders were under and in compliance with the Act and free of mala fides, was asked by a commission of inquiry two years later, and the answers it returned did not suggest that examination would have embarrassed the detenus. The profession's own verdict formed in the same years and hardened into consensus. H. M. Seervai's Constitutional Law of India, the treatise to which Indian constitutional lawyers of that generation repaired before any other, treated the majority's reasoning as a failure that the available text had not required, and the treatise's authority carried the criticism into chambers and classrooms where journal articles did not reach. Granville Austin, the historian of the Constitution whose Working a Democratic Constitution remains the standard narrative of the period, recorded the verdict of the profession in a metaphor that has attached itself to the case: that the majority judges, in their separate opinions, had contrived to be hanged separately rather than together. Bar councils, law journals and the memoirs of the counsel who had argued the case, among them accounts by lawyers who had appeared for the detenus and who described the courtroom of 1976 for a generation that had not seen it, completed the informal overruling. By any measure except the law reports, the case was dead before some of its judges had retired.

Inside the judiciary, the avoidance took the form of a tacit quarantine, punctuated by moments of open self-criticism from the institution's most senior voices. Judges writing in the detention cases of the first post-Emergency years, Krishna Iyer J prominent among them, used the occasions those cases offered to write about personal liberty in a register that amounted to a repudiation of the emergency jurisprudence, without a bench of the strength the formal act required. Successive Chief Justices, in addresses to the Bar and in lectures, referred to the case as the institution's cautionary memory, the episode against which the Court's claim to be a counter-majoritarian guardian had to be re-earned. That re-earning is the context in which the great expansion of the Court's jurisdiction in the following decades belongs: public interest litigation, the epistolary jurisdiction, the reading of Directive Principles into Article 21, the whole apparatus by which the post-Emergency Court rebuilt its standing with the public, was constructed by judges several of whom had sat in or around the case of 1976 and who understood, as an institutional matter, what the construction was for. None of this disturbed the precedent's formal existence. All of it explains why, when the privacy reference finally gave a bench of nine both the occasion and the necessity, the overruling occupied only a small part of a very long set of opinions and met no resistance from any quarter of the Court. The burial was brief because the mourning, and the repudiation, had been conducted over forty years.

A reader tracing this half-life should also notice what it cost, because the avoidance was not free. A precedent that is universally disapproved and never overruled leaves a residue of uncertainty in every adjacent doctrine: counsel advising on emergency powers had to report that the locus standi holding stood, technically, in the reports; students had to be taught a case and then taught to disregard it; and foreign courts and comparative scholars, reading the Indian reports without the profession's oral tradition of shame, could and did cite ADM Jabalpur as India's position on emergency detention long after India had ceased to regard it as such. The formal overruling of 2017 was therefore not the redundant ceremony the decades of avoidance might suggest. It was the settlement of a debt the reports had carried since 1976, and the delay in settling it is the durable institutional lesson of the case, as instructive as the error that created the debt. Systems correct their worst decisions through several channels at once, political, legislative, interpretive and professional, and the channels do not synchronise. The judgment of a court is not finally corrected until a court corrects it, and a system that finds the formal correction easy to postpone will discover, as India did, that postponement has a price measured in the currency the system can least afford: the accuracy of its own record about itself.

## A Worked Application: One Petition in Three Legal Eras

The practical meaning of the doctrine becomes clearest when a single, simple case is carried through the three legal eras the sequence created, and the exercise that follows invents no authority and decides no real matter; it applies, to an unadorned set of facts, the rules this article has already established from the record. Take a detenu held under a preventive detention statute on grounds that, on the face of the order, recite the statutory formula without connecting material, and suppose the order was in truth signed to silence a critic of the administration rather than to prevent any act the statute names. In the legal world of 1976, with a proclamation in force and an Article 359 order naming Articles 14, 21 and 22, the detenu's wife files a habeas corpus petition in the High Court pleading exactly those defects: non-application of mind, absence of the statutory nexus, mala fides. Under ADM Jabalpur the petition is not maintainable and is dismissed at the threshold, without the grounds being called for, without the file being produced, without any court asking whether the order is under the Act. The defect in the order is not held to be curable or incurable. It is held to be unexaminable, and the detention continues for the life of the order and its renewals, answerable to the advisory board machinery of the statute and to nothing else. That is the era's complete answer, and its completeness is the point: the system did not malfunction in reaching it, it functioned exactly as the precedent directed.

Carry the same petition into the legal world after 1979. The Forty-fourth Amendment has made the enforcement of Article 21 non-suspendable, so even if an emergency were proclaimed and an Article 359 order issued, the order could not name Article 21, and the doorway the 1976 majority closed is held open by the constitutional text itself. The petition is maintainable on the statute and on Article 21 together. Under the Article 21 that Maneka Gandhi rebuilt, the detaining procedure must be just, fair and reasonable, and under the ordinary detention jurisprudence the courts administer in every decade, an order whose grounds bear no live link to the statute's objects, or which was passed for a purpose foreign to the Act, is liable to be quashed, with the detenu's release as the ordinary consequence. The same facts that in 1976 could not be pleaded are, in this era, the standard grounds on which detention orders are routinely struck down, and the advocate's craft consists not in finding a door but in proving the defects, on a record the detaining authority must now produce and defend. Carry the petition, finally, into the legal world after 2017. Nothing about its maintainability changes, for the amendment had already secured that. What changes is the premise available to the court that hears it. If the State were to argue, in some new emergency's litigation, that the right at stake is the Constitution's gift and sleeps when the Constitution's remedial machinery sleeps, the answer is now supplied by the largest available authority: the premise was the error of ADM Jabalpur, the error was overruled by nine judges, and life and personal liberty are inalienable rights the Constitution recognises and does not create. The three eras thus divide the case's legacy with unusual neatness. The first era shows the holding. The second shows the amendment doing the practical work. The third shows the overruling doing the doctrinal work, and a reader who keeps the division in view will never again mistake a statement that the case has been overruled for a statement about which instrument did what.

## The Position as It Stands: Doctrine After the Overruling

The settled position can be stated with a completeness that would have astonished the counsel who argued the case in 1976. The right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 is not a gift of the constitutional text, and its enforcement cannot be suspended by any Presidential Order, because the Forty-fourth Amendment removed Articles 20 and 21 from the reach of Article 359 altogether. A law or executive action depriving a person of life or liberty must answer to a procedure that is just, fair and reasonable, and must survive the interlocking tests of Articles 14 and 19 as Maneka Gandhi constructed them. A detention order must comply with the statute that authorises it, and a detenu may test that compliance, and the bona fides of the order, before the High Court under Article 226 and before the Supreme Court under Article 32, in emergency and out of it, so far as Articles 20 and 21 are engaged. ADM Jabalpur is not a weakened precedent or a distinguished one. It is an overruled one, its majority opinions declared seriously flawed by a bench of nine, and an advocate who cited it for its reasoning would be citing a corpse. What survives of the case is its use as anti-authority: the standing example, in arguments about judicial duty, of the consequence of locating rights in a single text and remedies in a single doorway.

What remains genuinely open should be described structurally, as the discipline of this framework requires, rather than dramatised. Article 359 survives, and it still permits the suspension of the enforcement of fundamental rights other than Articles 20 and 21 during a proclaimed emergency; a future emergency litigation would therefore be fought on different ground, around the other named rights and around the limits of the proclamation itself, and the doctrines that would decide it, the reviewability of a proclamation's basis, the basic structure limits on emergency power, have been developed since 1976 but never tested in a comparable crisis. The ordinary law of preventive detention also survives, in the National Security Act and the State statutes, with its Article 22 safeguards of grounds, representation and advisory board review, and the courts' supervision of that ordinary law, its insistence on live links between grounds and objects, on the supply of materials, on the unexplained delay that vitiates an order, is the day-to-day descendant of the questions the Emergency petitions tried to ask. None of that ordinary law was created by the overruling of ADM Jabalpur, and a reader who wants the practical law of detention will find it in the detention statutes and their case law. The overruling settled the constitutional premise. The premise was the case.

### Which provisions now bar a repetition of the ADM Jabalpur route?

Two provisions and one precedent. Article 359, as the Forty-fourth Amendment rewrote it, forbids any Presidential Order suspending the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21. Maneka Gandhi's reading of Article 21 requires any depriving procedure to be just, fair and reasonable. And Puttaswamy overruled the sole-repository premise on which the 1976 route depended.

The residual risks are structural rather than doctrinal: suspension of other rights, detention under ordinary statutes, and the willingness of a future bench under pressure, which no text can guarantee.

That answer, with its division between the doctrinal and the structural, is the honest one, and it maps the difference between what law can do and what only institutions can do. Doctrine can be repaired by amendment and overruling, and in this instance it was, completely. Institutional nerve cannot be amended into existence. The judges of 1976 had texts that permitted a braver answer and chose the safer one; the judges of some future crisis will have texts that compel a braver answer and will still have to choose to give it. The lesson the case teaches the advocate is therefore double. Plead the text, because after 1978 the text is on liberty's side in a way it was not before. And plead the case itself, because ADM Jabalpur, overruled and disowned, remains the most eloquent available demonstration of what the alternative to judicial courage costs, stated not by liberty's pamphleteers but by the record of the Court that paid it.

## How to Read the Judgment: A Working Method

The judgment rewards a particular reading discipline, and students and advocates approaching it for the first time will get more from the file if they observe a sequence. Read the operative order first, in its single sentence, and hold its exact scope in mind against every summary encountered afterwards; most errors about the case are errors about that sentence. Then read the opinions separately rather than as a composite majority, because Ray CJ's positivism, Beg J's confidence in the executive, Chandrachud J's candour about hardship, and Bhagwati J's structural reading of Article 359 are four different judgments wearing one result, and they have worn differently across time. Then read the dissent against the majority's premise rather than against its conclusion: locate the sole-repository proposition in each majority opinion, and watch the dissent refuse it at the root. Finally, read the correction sequence as part of the case, Maneka Gandhi for the premise, the Forty-fourth Amendment for the instrument, Puttaswamy for the burial, because in Indian constitutional law a case is not fully read until its afterlife is read with it. Seminar leaders and candidates who convert that sequence into teaching materials, the four opinions, the exchange, the dissent and the four corrective steps, often assemble the file as a slide deck for classroom use, and a deck of that kind, containing detenu names and case papers, can be opened and presented straight from the browser with [the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which renders the slides on the device itself and sends no file to any server.

A word belongs here about the report itself, because most readers meet the case in a law report, cited as (1976) 2 SCC 521, and reports shape understanding in ways their editors rarely intend. The headnote of a long, multi-opinion judgment is a compression performed by the report's editors, and the compressions of this case, repeated across digests, databases and coaching materials for five decades, are one channel through which the imprecise popular version, that the Court suspended fundamental rights, or that it upheld the Emergency, has replicated itself while the operative order sat unread a page away. The discipline this article has urged, to begin from the order's sentence and to test every summary against it, applies with special force to headnotes, which are written for retrieval and not for authority. The same caution governs the citation of the opinions in argument. There are five opinions, not two: the composite figure of the majority, so convenient in debate, dissolves on inspection into Ray CJ's positivism, Beg J's trust, Chandrachud J's austerity and Bhagwati J's structuralism, and an advocate who attributes a sentence to the majority that appears in only one of the four opinions will be corrected by any bench that has done the reading. Precision of attribution is not pedantry in a case whose whole misfortune began with a premise attributed, by four judges, to a text that did not contain it. The reader who finishes the report with that lesson, that in constitutional law the unattributed premise is the dangerous one, has extracted from an unhappy judgment the professional habit it exists, in the end, to teach.

The practical consequence of the case for anyone advising on detention follows from the same discipline. The case stands for nothing that can be used against a detenu in the legal order that exists after its overruling, and its only forensic use is offensive, in the rhetorical rather than the technical sense: it is the citation an advocate reaches for when a court is invited to defer, to narrow a doorway, to treat a pleaded ground as a disguised request for something forbidden. Its presence in an argument signals that the invitation has been issued before, accepted once, and repented at leisure by nearly everyone concerned. Used that way, sparingly and exactly, it retains a force that many live precedents lack. Used loosely, as a synonym for the Emergency or as a decoration on any argument about liberty, it spends that force and misdescribes the record. The description in these pages, of a narrow question, a contestable premise, an available alternative, a priced dissent, and a forty-one-year wait for the formality of burial, is offered as the version that survives checking, and checking, against the text of the order, the composition of the bench, and the terms of the overruling, is the respect this unhappy case is owed.

## The Verdict: A Narrow Question, a Wide Failure

The verdict of this article can be given in the terms the evidence supports. ADM Jabalpur was a defensible answer to a question nobody had asked, and an indefensible answer to the question that was actually framed. The question framed was whether a detenu could test his detention order against the statute that purported to authorise it. The answer the Constitution permitted was yes, as most of the High Courts held; the answer the Court gave was no, on a premise about the source of rights that the text did not contain and that a nine-judge bench, forty-one years later, rejected as seriously flawed. No statute was changed by the decision and no statute could have produced it; the failure was judicial, a failure of reading before it was a failure of nerve, and the distinction matters because readings can be corrected by later courts while nerve must be found again in every generation. What the case finally teaches, to the legislator who drafts emergency provisions, to the advocate who frames a detention challenge, and to the judge who must decide whether a doorway is open, is that the law of liberty is decided at the doorway: in locus standi rulings, maintainability objections, and the quiet premises about where rights come from, long before it is decided in the grand propositions. The grand propositions were all present in 1976, in the Preamble, in Part III, in the memory of the freedom struggle. The doorway was shut anyway. Remembering how, and by what reasoning, and at what price to the one judge who said so, is the use of this unhappy and indispensable case.

The general account given here is legal information and analysis of a decided case and of the constitutional provisions it construed, written for study; it is not advice on any individual matter, and anyone facing a detention order or advising a detenu must work from the statute and the record of that matter itself, where limitation periods and forum rules are unforgiving.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the question before the court in ADM Jabalpur?

The question was one of locus standi under a subsisting Presidential Order. The Order of 27 June 1975, issued under Article 359(1), had suspended the right to move any court for the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22 during the Emergency. Detenus held under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act had petitioned the High Courts under Article 226, arguing not that Article 21 should be enforced, but that their detention orders did not comply with the detaining statute, or were illegal, or were mala fide. The Supreme Court had to decide whether such a petition was maintainable at all in the face of the Order.

### Q: What did the majority hold in the Habeas Corpus case?

The majority of four, Chief Justice Ray with Beg, Chandrachud and Bhagwati JJ, held that in view of the Presidential Order no person had any locus standi to move a writ petition under Article 226 for habeas corpus, or any other writ, order or direction, to challenge the legality of a detention order on the ground that it was not under or in compliance with the Act, or was illegal, or was vitiated by mala fides, factual or legal, or rested on extraneous considerations. Every pleaded ground was named and foreclosed, and the High Courts that had held such petitions maintainable were reversed.

### Q: What did Justice Khanna say in his dissent?

Khanna J held that Article 359 suspends the enforcement of rights, not the rights themselves, and that the right to life and personal liberty does not depend on Article 21 alone, because it is an antecedent value of any legal order and not a gift of the constitutional text. It followed that the State has no power to deprive a person of life or liberty without authority of law even when the enforcement of Article 21 stands suspended, and that a detenu could therefore ask a High Court to test whether his detention complied with the detaining statute or was mala fide. He dissented alone.

### Q: What happened to Justice Khanna afterwards?

When Chief Justice Ray retired in January 1977, Khanna J was the senior-most judge and stood next in line for the chief justiceship. The government superseded him, appointing Justice Beg of the ADM Jabalpur majority instead. Khanna J resigned from the Court.

He later served a brief spell as Union Law Minister in 1979. His dissent, written in the stated expectation that it would cost him the chief justiceship, became the most honoured opinion in the Court's history, and the nine-judge bench that overruled the case treated his position as the one the law should have taken.

### Q: Has ADM Jabalpur been overruled?

Yes, expressly and in terms. In K. S. Puttaswamy v Union of India, decided in 2017 by a nine-judge bench on the question whether privacy is a fundamental right, the Court held that the majority judgments in ADM Jabalpur were seriously flawed and overruled the decision. Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, whose father had been in the 1976 majority, wrote the sentence that closed the case: ADM Jabalpur must be and is accordingly overruled. The overruling was not implicit or a matter of inference. It adopted the core of Khanna J's dissent, holding that life and personal liberty are inalienable rights recognised by the Constitution rather than created by it.

### Q: Why is ADM Jabalpur called the darkest hour of the Supreme Court?

The description reflects what the Court did at the moment of maximum need. During a proclaimed Emergency, with opposition leaders and thousands of others detained without trial, relatives asked the courts only to test whether the detention orders complied with the detaining statute. The High Courts had largely held that they could. The Supreme Court held that nobody had standing to ask, accepting a reasoning under which even a detention ordered in bad faith, or a killing by an officer acting from personal enmity, would have no judicial remedy while the suspension lasted. A court declining jurisdiction over liberty at the one moment when only courts stood between the detenu and the executive is the failure the phrase records.

### Q: Could a decision like ADM Jabalpur happen again?

On the same legal materials, no. The Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act amended Article 359 so that the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended by a Presidential Order, which withdraws the instrument on which the 1976 ruling depended. Maneka Gandhi then rebuilt Article 21 so that any depriving procedure must be just, fair and reasonable, and Puttaswamy overruled the sole-repository premise itself. A future bench would have to defy the amended text and a nine-judge precedent to repeat the result. The risks that remain are structural: emergencies still permit suspension of other rights, preventive detention statutes remain on the book, and no text can supply judicial nerve.

### Q: Did any ADM Jabalpur judge later express regret?

Yes. Justice Bhagwati, who had concurred in the majority and later became Chief Justice of India, said in an interview in 2011 that the Supreme Court should be ashamed of the ADM Jabalpur judgment, that he pleaded guilty to his part in it, and that he did not know why he had yielded, describing his concurrence as a weakness and the judgment as one that had gone against his conscience. Justice Chandrachud, the longest-serving Chief Justice, is also reported to have expressed regret after the Emergency, saying that he regretted not having laid down his office rather than join the conclusion. No comparable statement came from the other majority judges.

### Q: Who were the five judges who decided ADM Jabalpur?

The Constitution Bench that decided Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla on 28 April 1976 consisted of Chief Justice A. N. Ray and Justices M. H. Beg, Y. V. Chandrachud, P. N. Bhagwati and H. R. Khanna. The first four wrote separate opinions concurring in the operative order for the Union, so the decision is described as four to one. Khanna J dissented alone. The composition matters to the case's afterlife: Justice Beg succeeded Ray as Chief Justice over Khanna's seniority, Justice Chandrachud became the longest-serving Chief Justice, and Justice Bhagwati later became Chief Justice and publicly regretted his concurrence.

### Q: What did Attorney General Niren De concede during the ADM Jabalpur arguments?

Arguing for the Union, Attorney General Niren De accepted the consequence of his own submission when Khanna J pressed him on it. If no court could be moved for the enforcement of Article 21 while the Presidential Order operated, Khanna J asked whether there would be a remedy if a police officer took the life of a detenu from personal enmity. The Attorney General answered, in substance, that consistently with the suspension there would be no judicial remedy for the duration of the Emergency. The exchange is the most quoted passage of the hearing because it stated the price of the majority's premise in plain words, through the government's own law officer.

### Q: Did ADM Jabalpur suspend fundamental rights themselves?

No, and the distinction is the one Khanna J's dissent was built on. The Presidential Order under Article 359(1) suspended the right to move any court for the enforcement of Articles 14, 21 and 22, and pending proceedings for that enforcement. It did not delete those articles, amend Part III, or declare the conduct they forbid to be lawful. The majority proceeded on the footing that enforcement was the casualty, and held that because Article 21 was the sole repository of the right to life and liberty, losing its enforcement left a detenu with nothing a court could protect. Accounts that say the Court suspended fundamental rights misstate the instrument and miss the precise point in dispute.

### Q: What role did Section 16A of MISA play in ADM Jabalpur?

Section 16A, inserted into the Maintenance of Internal Security Act during the Emergency, was the statutory background against which the petitions were fought. In its operative effect it allowed the detaining authority to withhold the grounds of detention from the detenu where disclosure was considered against the public interest, and it barred disclosure of the grounds and of the detenu's representation in court proceedings. The detenu therefore faced the Presidential Order above the statute and Section 16A inside it: no grounds to answer, and then, after ADM Jabalpur, no court entitled to ask for them. The case itself, however, was decided on locus standi under Article 359, not on the validity of Section 16A.

### Q: Which cases began the undoing of ADM Jabalpur before Puttaswamy?

The first judicial step was Maneka Gandhi v Union of India in 1978, where a seven-judge bench held that a procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be just, fair and reasonable, and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 interpenetrate rather than stand in exclusive silos. That dismantled the intellectual premise of the 1976 majority without formally overruling its locus standi holding. The legislative step taken alongside it, the Forty-fourth Amendment, was not a case at all. Between Maneka Gandhi and the privacy litigation, later benches repeatedly described ADM Jabalpur with disapproval and declined to extend it, so that by the time Puttaswamy sat in 2017 the formal overruling registered a position the law had long since absorbed.

### Q: What did Justice D Y Chandrachud write about ADM Jabalpur in Puttaswamy?

Writing for a plurality of the nine-judge bench in K. S. Puttaswamy v Union of India in 2017, Justice D. Y. Chandrachud held that the majority judgments in ADM Jabalpur were seriously flawed, because life and personal liberty are inalienable rights that the Constitution recognises rather than creates, and he closed the question in a single sentence: ADM Jabalpur must be and is accordingly overruled. The passage carries a weight beyond its doctrine, because Justice Y. V. Chandrachud, his father, had been one of the four judges in the 1976 majority. The overruling adopted, in substance, the reasoning of Khanna J's dissent on the source of the right.

### Q: Why is the case called ADM Jabalpur?

The name is the name of the lead appeal. ADM stands for Additional District Magistrate, the detaining authority in Jabalpur, in Madhya Pradesh, who stood as appellant against Shivkant Shukla, the respondent connected with the detention questioned before the Madhya Pradesh High Court. Habeas corpus petitions by relatives of persons detained under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act had been decided in High Courts across the country, most of them in the detenus' favour on the preliminary objection, and the Union and the detaining authorities appealed. The appeals were clubbed before a Constitution Bench, and the Jabalpur appeal, standing first, gave the whole batch the name under which the case is cited and remembered.

### Q: What is locus standi and why did it decide ADM Jabalpur?

Locus standi is the entitlement of a person to be heard by a court at all, the right to stand in the doorway before any argument on the merits begins. ADM Jabalpur was decided entirely at that doorway. The Court did not hold that the detention orders were lawful, did not examine whether they complied with the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, and did not find them free of mala fides. It held that no person had locus standi to move a High Court under Article 226 to raise those questions while the Presidential Order operated. That is why the case is the standing lesson in how a maintainability ruling, framed in dry procedural vocabulary, can decide the substance of liberty for everyone outside the door.

### Q: Did the majority in ADM Jabalpur examine whether the detentions were lawful?

No. The operative order was jurisdictional, and it was drawn to exclude examination: no person had locus standi to challenge a detention order on the ground that it was not under or in compliance with the Act, or was illegal, or was vitiated by mala fides, factual or legal, or rested on extraneous considerations. The individual detentions before the High Courts were never tested on their facts by the Supreme Court, and no detaining authority was found to have acted in good faith. This is also why the later corrective steps, amendment, reinterpretation and overruling, never adjudicated the merits of the Emergency detentions either. The merits were the question the legal system, at every stage, declined to reach.

### Q: How did Maneka Gandhi change the Article 21 that ADM Jabalpur had read?

The ADM Jabalpur majority had treated Article 21 as the sole repository of the right to life and personal liberty, a self-contained provision whose suspension ended judicial inquiry. Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided by seven judges in January 1978, rebuilt the article on different foundations. It held that the procedure established by law for depriving a person of life or liberty must be right, just and fair, not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive, and that Articles 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive but interpenetrate, so a law touching liberty must also satisfy equality and freedom. After Maneka Gandhi, Article 21 could no longer be read as the narrow silo the 1976 reasoning had assumed.

### Q: What is the citation of the ADM Jabalpur judgment?

The judgment is cited as Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla, (1976) 2 SCC 521, and in the All India Reporter as AIR 1976 SC 1207. It was decided on 28 April 1976 by a Constitution Bench of five judges, and it is popularly known simply as the Habeas Corpus case. The SCC citation is the one used in modern practice and in the opinions that later overruled it. Readers should treat the headnotes attached to the report with care: several of the loose claims about the case in circulation, including the claim that it suspended fundamental rights, come from headnote compression rather than from the operative order the bench actually made.

### Q: Does any part of ADM Jabalpur survive as good law?

No part of its reasoning survives as authority a court would follow. The nine-judge bench in Puttaswamy overruled the decision and declared its majority opinions seriously flawed, and the premise on which it rested, that Article 21 is the sole repository of the right to life and liberty, has been displaced both by that overruling and by the Forty-fourth Amendment's removal of Articles 20 and 21 from Article 359. What survives is the case's use as anti-authority and as a teaching record: the operative order's text, the Attorney General's concession, and Khanna J's dissent are still read, but they are read as the history of an error and its correction, not as law.
