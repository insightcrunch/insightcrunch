## The Cheap Seat Across an Ocean: How Low-Cost Long-Haul Airlines Work

Low-cost long-haul airlines work by selling a seat across an ocean at a fare far below a legacy carrier fare, stripping out frills, packing in more seats, and charging separately for bags, meals, and seat selection. The model fails for a structural reason rather than a managerial one: on long routes the costs nobody can cut, including fuel, crew rest, aircraft ownership, and time itself, dominate the total cost of a flight, so the saving shrinks as distance grows until the fare that fills the aircraft no longer covers the flight.

![A widebody aircraft cabin configured with dense economy seating on a long-haul route - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-68.webp)

The puzzle begins with a fare that looks impossible. A crossing of the Atlantic covers roughly 3,500 miles between London and New York, and a passenger comparing prices on that corridor encounters two very different numbers for the same physical act of sitting in a tube over water for seven hours. One carrier prices the seat as part of a network, with connections, lounges, a premium cabin, and cargo in the hold underwriting the economics. Another carrier prices the seat almost alone, asking only whether the fare, plus fees for extras, exceeds the cost of producing that single seat on that single flight. The difference lies not in the physics of flight but in which costs each business model can remove and which costs remain untouched no matter how disciplined the operator becomes.

Rigas Doganis, in The Airline Business in 2006, set out the underlying structure with clarity: an airline sells perishable capacity, because an empty seat at departure has zero value afterward, while most costs of the flight are fixed once the schedule is published. That perishability pushes every carrier toward filling seats, but it pushes a low-fare carrier harder, because a low fare needs a higher load factor to produce the same revenue from the same aircraft. On a short route, a carrier can recover from a weak flight within hours by filling the next departure. On a long route, the aircraft commits to a single sector for half a day or more, and a weak flight consumes the machine, the crew, and the fuel for that entire block of time with no second chance on the same day.

The history of the model confirms the arithmetic across five decades. Freddie Laker launched the Skytrain service between London and New York in 1977 with walk-on fares far below the regulated prices of the period, and Laker Airways collapsed in 1982. People Express crossed the Atlantic from 1983 and was absorbed into a larger carrier in 1987. AirAsia X, founded in 2007, passed through repeated restructurings. WOW air ceased operations in March 2019, and Primera Air ceased operations in October 2018. Norwegian built a large long-haul operation from 2013 and withdrew from long-haul flying in a restructuring completed across 2020 and 2021. Norse Atlantic, founded by Bjorn Tore Larsen in 2021 with a first flight in June 2022, ran the same sequence again in full. Each section that follows isolates one cost or one revenue line, names its mechanism, and shows how distance alters its weight in the total. Names, fleets, and decades differ. The sequence repeats: entry at a striking fare, rapid growth, a season or a fuel spike that exposes thin margins, then contraction or collapse.

## What a Long-Haul Flight Actually Costs

A long-haul flight begins spending money long before a passenger reaches the gate. The aircraft itself carries an ownership cost, whether a purchase price financed over years or a lease payment owed every month regardless of whether the machine flies. Fuel must be loaded for the sector, for reserves, for diversion to an alternate airport, and for holding in poor weather. Flight crew and cabin crew must be paid, positioned, and rested under legal limits. Maintenance accrues by flight hour and by cycle, the take-off and landing pair that stresses engines and airframes. Airports charge for landing, parking, and passenger handling, and air navigation services charge for passage through controlled airspace along the route. The flight is a bundle of obligations, most of them triggered by distance and time rather than by the number of passengers on board.

The distinction between fixed and variable cost decides how a fare behaves. A passenger adds little variable cost: a small amount of fuel burned because of extra weight, a meal if one is included, and processing through booking and check-in systems. The remaining cost stands whether the cabin holds 200 passengers or 290 passengers. Doganis made the point in The Airline Business in 2006 that this cost structure gives airlines high operating leverage, meaning that small changes in load factor or fare produce large changes in profit or loss. A legacy carrier spreads that leverage across premium fares and cargo revenue. A low-cost long-haul carrier deliberately removes those buffers and then must fill almost every seat at a fare high enough, after ancillary fees, to carry obligations that barely respond to occupancy at all.

Consider the anatomy of a single transatlantic sector flown by a widebody aircraft with roughly 300 seats. The sector lasts about eight hours in one direction. Fuel consumption for such a sector runs into tens of tonnes, and fuel alone commonly represents the largest single operating line, ahead of crew and ownership. Crew cost reflects not only the hours in the air but also allowances, accommodation at the destination, and the rest period that removes the crew from productive duty after arrival. Ownership cost reflects a machine far larger and more expensive than a short-haul narrowbody: a widebody costs several times more to acquire, and its lease rate prices that scale every month. None of those lines shrinks because the ticket was sold cheaply. The fare is a marketing decision. The cost bundle is an engineering and legal reality, and the gap between the two is where the model lives or dies.

Distance also changes the order of the lines. On a flight of one hour, airport charges and turnaround handling weigh heavily because they recur every sector, while fuel stays modest. On a flight of ten hours, fuel, crew time, and ownership time dominate, while the single departure and single arrival spread their charges across a far larger total. Peter Morrell, writing in Research in Transportation Economics in 2008, examined whether long-haul low-cost airlines could be successful and identified exactly this shift as the central problem: the elements a budget carrier traditionally attacks form a smaller proportion of long-haul cost, so even perfect execution on those elements moves the total by less. The mechanism matters more than the label. A carrier does not fail because a spreadsheet contains a line called fuel. A carrier fails because the proportion of cost open to attack contracts with every added mile, while the proportion closed to attack expands.

## The Unit of Sale: One Seat Over One Mile

Airlines compare themselves through a unit that strips away size and route length: the cost of moving one seat one mile. The measure allows a carrier flying small aircraft on short routes to stand beside a carrier flying widebody aircraft across oceans, because both totals are divided down to the same denominator. The gap between the two numbers, multiplied by millions of seat miles, becomes the profit or loss of the operation. A difference of a fraction of a cent per seat mile, repeated across a fleet flying daily, separates a sustainable carrier from a failing one within a single season.

### What is cost per available seat mile?

Cost per available seat mile is total operating cost divided by available seat miles, meaning seats offered multiplied by miles flown, occupied or not. The measure exposes efficiency independent of sales success. A carrier lowers the figure by cutting cost, adding seats, or flying each seat farther, and raises it whenever expensive capacity sits empty or underused.

The denominator deserves attention because it can flatter as easily as it can reveal. Flying a seat farther increases available seat miles without adding a departure, so long sectors mechanically dilute fixed departure costs across more miles and can show a lower cost per available seat mile than short sectors on the same aircraft type. Morrell noted in 2008 that this dilution misleads casual comparison: a lower unit cost on a long route does not mean a healthier business, because revenue per seat mile also falls with distance. Passengers pay less per mile on long journeys than on short ones across the whole industry, since few travellers would pay a short-haul per-mile rate multiplied across 5,000 miles. Both lines descend with distance. The commercial question is which line descends faster, and the long-haul low-cost record suggests that revenue descends faster than a stripped-down cost base can follow.

Load factor completes the unit picture. Cost is calculated on seats offered, but revenue arrives only on seats sold, so a carrier offering a seat at a unit cost of eight cents and selling only 80 percent of seats must recover the full cost from the paying 80 percent. The effective cost per paying passenger rises by a quarter at that load factor. Legacy carriers tolerate a lower load factor because premium passengers pay several times the economy fare for a small share of the cabin floor. A low-cost long-haul carrier, selling a cabin of near-uniform low fares, has no such cushion and needs load factors persistently in the high eighties or beyond. Doganis observed in 2006 that break-even load factor is the sharpest single test of an airline model, and on long routes the test tightens because each unsold seat represents more miles of unrecovered cost than an unsold seat on a short hop.

The discipline of the denominator protects against a common error in public discussion of cheap fares. A headline fare compares a single seat price against nothing at all. The unit comparison asks a harder question: across a season, across a fleet, does revenue per seat mile exceed cost per available seat mile by a margin wide enough to absorb disruption, seasonality, and debt? Carriers that answer that question honestly tend to grow slowly. Carriers that answer it with hope tend to grow quickly and then appear in the history sections of articles on the model.

## Where the Short-Haul Model Gets Its Savings

The short-haul low-cost model works because a short flight contains a large amount of removable waste. Traditional carriers built their short routes around hub connections, printed tickets sold through agents, free catering, generous turnaround times, and mixed fleets. Each habit carried a cost, and each cost could be attacked. A point-to-point carrier selling directly online, turning an aircraft around in 25 minutes, flying one aircraft type, charging for bags, and using secondary airports could remove a striking share of the total cost of a one-hour flight. Ryanair in Europe and Southwest Airlines in the United States proved the scale of the prize across decades, filling aircraft at fares legacy rivals struggled to match while still earning profits through most cycles. The savings were real, repeatable, and large relative to the cost of a short sector.

Turnaround speed sits at the centre of the mechanism. A narrowbody aircraft on the ground earns nothing, so cutting ground time from an hour to 25 minutes allows the same machine to fly more sectors per day, spreading ownership cost across more seat miles. Short sectors multiply the benefit: a carrier flying eight sectors daily gains far more from fast turns than a carrier flying one long sector, where the turn is a small fraction of the block time. Direct distribution removed agent commissions that once consumed a meaningful share of a cheap ticket. Single-fleet operation cut training, spares, and maintenance complexity. Dense seating added seats without adding flights. Ancillary charging unbundled the ticket so that a passenger carrying no bag and eating no meal stopped paying for services built into a legacy fare.

The regulatory opening made the experiment possible in the first place. The [Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 guide](/2016/05/01/airline-deregulation-act-1978-guide/) explains how ending federal control of routes and fares in the United States allowed new entrants to test models that the regulated era had kept off the map. Europe followed with its own liberalisation across the following two decades, and the short-haul revolution arrived in both markets once entry, pricing freedom, and access to airports aligned. The lesson of that era is often misread. Deregulation removed legal barriers to low fares. It did not remove the physical and contractual costs of flight, and the short-haul model succeeded because removable commercial waste, not physics, had been the binding constraint on short routes.

Transferring the model to long routes breaks the proportions that made it work. A 25-minute saving on the ground is marginal beside a ten-hour sector. Agent commissions, already shrunk by online sales across the industry, offer little further saving on any route length. Catering on a long flight is harder to strip without damaging the product, because a passenger crossing an ocean for ten hours expects food in a way that a passenger on a 50-minute hop does not, and charging for every item across a captive ten-hour cabin generates resistance as well as revenue. Secondary airports exist on some long-haul corridors but are scarcer, since widebody aircraft need longer runways, larger terminals, and border facilities that many secondary fields lack. Morrell concluded in 2008 that the transferable savings on long-haul routes amounted to a far smaller cost advantage than the 40 to 50 percent advantage short-haul carriers had enjoyed against legacy rivals on short sectors, with estimates of the long-haul edge often placed nearer 20 percent before revenue weaknesses were counted.

## Density: More Seats in the Same Tube

Seat density is the most visible tool in the low-cost workshop. An aircraft fuselage is a fixed tube with a fixed cost of moving through the air, so every additional seat divides that cost more finely. A widebody configured with 300 seats spreads fuel, crew, and ownership across 300 fares. The same aircraft configured with 380 seats spreads near-identical flight costs across 80 additional fares, and the cost per available seat mile falls sharply even though the cost of the flight barely changes. Extra passengers add weight and therefore some fuel burn, and extra seats add weight of their own, but the increments are small beside the fixed cost of the sector. Density is the one lever that works on long routes almost as powerfully as on short ones, which explains why every serious low-cost long-haul attempt has configured aircraft at or near maximum certified density.

The method of gaining seats matters. Seat pitch, the distance between one seatback and the next, can fall from the 34 inches common in legacy economy cabins toward 28 or 29 inches in dense configurations. Seats can be made slimmer, with thinner cushions and frames that reclaim an inch or two per row without changing pitch on paper. On widebody aircraft, adding a seat to each row changes the geometry entirely: a fuselage laid out nine abreast can be pushed to ten abreast with narrower seats and aisles. Ten rows gaining one seat each add ten seats. A small pitch reduction across 40 rows can add several rows. Combined, configuration choices can raise capacity by a fifth or more on the same airframe, transforming the denominator of every cost calculation the carrier performs.

Physics and regulation set the limits of the trade. Emergency evacuation rules require that all passengers leave the aircraft within 90 seconds through half the available exits, capping capacity regardless of how many seats could physically fit. Seat width cannot fall below the width of actual passengers without producing complaints, refunds, and reputational damage that eventually reach the booking curve. On long sectors the trade sharpens, because a passenger tolerates a tight seat for one hour in a way that same passenger resists across nine hours, overnight, with sleep attempted upright in a narrow space. Doganis noted in 2006 that product quality and price form a single judgement in the mind of a buyer, and on long routes the quality penalty of density grows with time spent in the seat, while the price benefit stays fixed at the moment of purchase.

Density also interacts with weight and range in a way short-haul flying rarely exposes. A full cabin of passengers and bags on a long sector pushes the aircraft toward its maximum take-off weight, and fuel for the longest routes competes directly with payload. A carrier may face a choice between selling every seat and carrying enough fuel for a distant alternate, or between a full cabin and the cargo a legacy rival sells in the same hold. Seat blocking, where the last rows are left empty on the longest sectors, quietly returns some of the density advantage on exactly the routes where the advantage was most needed. The arithmetic on a brochure assumes a configuration. The arithmetic in operation includes weather, weight, and the occasional empty seat that physics imposes.

## Utilisation and the Turnaround Problem

Utilisation measures how many hours per day an aircraft spends earning revenue in the air rather than sitting on the ground. Ownership and lease costs run by the calendar, not by the flight hour, so an aircraft flying 16 hours daily spreads its monthly cost across far more seat miles than an identical aircraft flying 10 hours daily. Short-haul low-cost carriers built much of their advantage on this single variable, scheduling first departures early, turning aircraft in 25 minutes, and keeping machines in motion until late evening. A narrowbody flying eight sectors a day converts ground discipline directly into unit cost. The mechanism is simple and brutal: the rent is due either way, and only hours in the air dilute it.

### Why does aircraft utilisation matter more on long routes?

Aircraft utilisation matters more on long routes because a widebody costs far more per day than a narrowbody, while schedules, time zones, airport curfews, and crew rest leave long-haul fleets fewer usable hours. Every idle hour on an expensive aircraft destroys more value, and a single late arrival can erase an entire day of planned flying.

Long routes fight utilisation through structure rather than laziness. A flight from London to Los Angeles consumes about 11 hours in one direction, and the return sector, positioning, and ground time at both ends push a full rotation beyond a single operating day. Time zones compress the usable departure windows: a westbound crossing must leave Europe in daytime to arrive at a reasonable hour, and the eastbound return typically flies overnight, so departure banks cluster into narrow bands that leave aircraft waiting between them. Night curfews at major airports close further options. A long-haul fleet often cannot, because the next legal, commercially sensible departure window sits hours away from the moment the aircraft becomes available.

Norwegian illustrated the cost of the constraint during its long-haul expansion from 2013 onward. A widebody fleet spread across bases in Europe and North America faced rotations in which aircraft waited on the ground for schedule connectivity rather than for any technical reason, and reported utilisation on long-haul fleets across the industry commonly sits several hours per day below the best narrowbody fleets. Morrell, in 2008, treated utilisation as one of the decisive gaps between the short-haul template and long-haul reality, because a low-cost carrier paying market lease rates for a widebody cannot afford narrowbody economics at widebody prices with fewer hours to spread them across. The lease payment for a large twin-aisle aircraft can exceed the payment for a narrowbody by a multiple, so each idle hour carries a multiple of the penalty as well.

Utilisation also explains why growth tempts long-haul entrants into error. Adding routes can raise utilisation by giving aircraft somewhere productive to go in otherwise idle windows, so expansion looks like a cure for the cost problem. Bjorn Kjos expanded Norwegian long-haul rapidly for reasons that included this logic, filling schedules across the Atlantic and beyond. The trap is that each added route must itself fill a large aircraft at adequate fares in both directions across all seasons. Growth that raises utilisation while lowering load factor or fare quality trades one denominator problem for another, and the combined arithmetic can worsen even as the fleet looks busier. Busy aircraft are not the same as profitable aircraft, and the distinction sits at the heart of several failures examined in later sections.

## Fuel: The Cost That Scales With Distance

Fuel is the line that distance multiplies most directly. An aircraft burns fuel in proportion to weight, speed, and time in the air, and a long sector holds all three at high levels for many hours. A widebody crossing the Atlantic burns tens of tonnes of kerosene in a single direction, and ultra-long sectors beyond 12 hours burn far more, because the aircraft must carry fuel to carry fuel: kerosene loaded for the final hours of flight adds weight that the engines must lift for the entire journey. Engineers call the effect fuel burn compounding, and it places a rising marginal cost on each additional hour of range. No sales technique, configuration choice, or management method removes the chemistry of combustion or the physics of lifting weight through air at altitude.

The share of fuel in total cost is what makes the line decisive for the low-cost model. On short sectors, fuel might represent a modest fraction of total cost beside airport charges, handling, and crew, so a carrier attacking those other lines can outrun fuel exposure. On long sectors, fuel commonly rises toward a quarter or a third of operating cost, and in periods of high oil prices the share climbs higher still. The mechanism by which crude markets translate into a pump and pipeline price for jet fuel is set out in the [guide to how oil prices are set](/2026/10/09/how-oil-prices-are-set/), and an airline buying kerosene at an airport pays a price built from that crude benchmark plus refining, transport, and supply margins. A low-cost carrier has no structural advantage in that market. Kerosene sells at the same price to a disciplined entrant and to a legacy rival of far greater scale, and the larger rival often buys better, through volume, storage, and longer supplier relationships.

Hedging, the practice of fixing fuel prices in advance through financial contracts, smooths the exposure without removing it. A carrier hedged through a price spike gains temporary protection, and a carrier hedged through a price fall pays above market while rivals buy cheaply, as several European carriers discovered across past oil cycles. Hedging also demands cash collateral and financial sophistication that thinly capitalised entrants struggle to supply. Southwest Airlines famously gained from well-timed hedges in an earlier era of high prices, an exception that required a strong balance sheet built on short-haul profits. A long-haul entrant burning far more fuel per departure, on margins far thinner, faces the exposure with fewer reserves. Morrell identified fuel price vulnerability in 2008 as a standing weakness of the long-haul low-cost form, because the model concentrates risk in the one input whose price the carrier neither controls nor avoids.

Primera Air, which ceased operations in October 2018 after expanding into transatlantic services, and WOW air, which ended operations in March 2019, both operated through periods in which fuel and financing pressures exposed thin long-haul margins, and neither failure required fuel alone as an explanation. The pattern is more precise than a single-cause story. Fuel acts as the amplifier: a model with a wide cost advantage absorbs a fuel spike as reduced profit, while a model with a narrow advantage absorbs the same spike as losses, covenant pressure, and lessor anxiety. Distance decides the size of the amplifier, because distance decides how much of every departure is kerosene.

## Crew, Rest Rules and the Price of Time Zones

A long flight consumes crew in a way a short flight never does. Two pilots can operate a short sector and fly several more sectors in the same duty period, spreading their cost across hundreds of passengers on multiple departures. A sector of ten hours may require three or four pilots so that each pilot receives in-flight rest, and cabin crew numbers scale with aircraft size and safety requirements for evacuation and service. After arrival across multiple time zones, legal rest rules remove the entire crew from duty for an extended period, often including a full local night or more at the destination. The carrier pays salaries, allowances, hotel rooms, and transport during that rest, while the crew produces no revenue. A replacement crew must be positioned or rested and ready for the return sector, so a single rotation can involve two full crews for one round trip.

Flight time limitation schemes exist because fatigue degrades judgement, and regulators in Europe, the United States, and other jurisdictions set maximum duty periods, minimum rest, and cumulative limits that no commercial pressure can lawfully override. The rules are a safety floor, not a negotiable cost line. Doganis discussed crew productivity in The Airline Business in 2006 as one of the areas where management method matters, through rostering efficiency, base structure, and industrial agreements, while the legal core of rest remains fixed for every operator on a route. A legacy carrier and a low-cost carrier flying the same sector face the same augmented crew requirement and the same minimum rest at the destination. The low-cost carrier can trim allowances, base crews in lower-cost cities, and roster tightly, but the savings operate at the margin of a cost block whose size is set by sector length and law.

Basing strategy shows both the opportunity and the controversy inside crew cost. Norwegian based some long-haul crews outside its home market during its expansion, seeking lower employment costs and wider recruitment, and the practice drew sustained criticism from labour organisations and political attention in several countries. The mechanism was transparent: crew cost per hour differs by labour market, and a carrier selling against rivals based in high-cost countries gains by employing under cheaper contracts. The limits were equally transparent. Safety regulation follows the operating licence and the route, training standards must match the fleet, and reputational and legal challenges accumulate when a labour model becomes a public argument. A saving that depends on regulatory arbitrage carries its own risk premium, and several jurisdictions tightened rules or scrutiny in response to such models, narrowing the advantage over time.

The cumulative effect is a crew cost per available seat mile that falls with sector length only until augmentation and layover thresholds are crossed, then rises in steps. Each step is predictable, documented, and identical for every competitor, which is why crew cost belongs with fuel and ownership among the costs distance multiplies until they cannot be stripped. A carrier can administer the steps efficiently or wastefully. No carrier in the record from Laker in 1977 to Norse Atlantic in 2026 administered them away.

## The Aircraft: Buying, Leasing and the Widebody Trap

Long-haul flying requires widebody aircraft, and widebody aircraft change the financial character of an airline. A new large twin-aisle aircraft carries a list price well above 200 million dollars, with negotiated prices lower but still enormous beside a narrowbody. Few entrants buy such machines outright. Most lease them, paying a monthly rate that reflects the aircraft value, the lessor assessment of the carrier credit risk, and the expected resale or re-lease value of the type. Leasing converts a purchase into a fixed monthly obligation that continues through weak seasons, fuel spikes, and demand shocks. The obligation is denominated often in dollars while ticket revenue arrives in pounds, euros, kroner, or ringgit, adding currency exposure to the fixed charge.

The trap inside the widebody choice is indivisibility. A short-haul carrier can add capacity in increments of roughly 180 seats and can redeploy a narrowbody across dozens of routes if one market weakens. A widebody adds 300 or more seats in a single indivisible unit and can serve only routes with runways, demand, and range to match. Each new aircraft is a large bet placed on a small number of city pairs. AirAsia X built its model from 2007 around high-density Airbus A330 aircraft on Asian and Australian routes, and its repeated restructurings reflected in part the difficulty of filling large aircraft profitably across seasons on a limited network. Norwegian ordered Boeing 787 aircraft at scale for its expansion from 2013, gaining fuel-efficient machines whose size still demanded high load factors on every route, in every month, to justify their lease and ownership economics.

Engine and maintenance realities deepen the commitment. Widebody engines are among the most expensive components in commercial aviation, and overhaul events cost millions of dollars per engine, accrued across flying hours and cycles. Norwegian suffered a documented operational blow when Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 engine issues on the Boeing 787 fleet required inspections and groundings across the industry, forcing leased replacement capacity and schedule disruption. A low-cost carrier with thin reserves absorbs such an event with less slack than a legacy group holding spare aircraft and deeper maintenance contracts. Technical risk scales with fleet specialisation as well: a single-type widebody fleet concentrates exposure to any defect affecting that type, while a multi-type fleet reintroduces the complexity costs the model tries to avoid.

Residual value and lessor confidence form the final turn of the trap. Lessors price risk from evidence, and each public failure of a long-haul entrant raises the rate or the deposit demanded from the next entrant, a mechanism through which Laker in 1982, WOW air in 2019, and Norwegian restructuring in 2021 raised the cost of capital for successors including Norse Atlantic, founded in 2021 into a market that had watched the pattern repeat. When a carrier weakens, lessors move early to repossess aircraft, because a widebody placed promptly with a stronger operator protects its value, while a machine left with a failing carrier deteriorates in both condition and marketability. The speed of that withdrawal explains why long-haul collapses look sudden from the outside. The aircraft are the balance sheet, the aircraft are mobile, and the owners of the aircraft have every incentive to move first.

## Revenue: Fares, Ancillaries and the Missing Premium Cabin

Revenue on a long-haul flight comes from three sources: fares, ancillary charges, and cargo, with premium fares acting as a multiplier on the first source for carriers that offer them. A low-cost long-haul carrier builds revenue almost entirely from the first two. The base fare is set low to stimulate demand and fill a large cabin. Ancillary revenue then rebuilds the ticket price piece by piece through checked bags, seat selection, meals, priority boarding, and extra legroom, with each charge carrying a high margin because the cost of providing most ancillary items sits well below the price charged. On short routes, ancillary revenue transformed the model by letting a carrier advertise a fare below the true average payment. On long routes, the same technique operates, but the base cost to be recovered is far larger, so ancillary income must climb further before the flight breaks even.

Passenger willingness to pay for extras also behaves differently across a long journey. A traveller on a one-hour flight can skip food, travel with hand baggage only, and accept an assigned seat, so unbundling separates passengers cleanly into those who pay and those who cost little to carry. A traveller crossing an ocean for a two-week trip usually needs checked baggage, expects at least one meal across ten hours, and cares more about seat location on an overnight sector. More passengers buy the extras, which raises ancillary revenue per passenger, but the carrier also faces stronger resistance when essentials of a long journey feel monetised, and regulators in several markets require the headline fare to remain honestly comparable.

The missing premium cabin is the largest revenue difference between the models. On a legacy long-haul flight, business class passengers may occupy a tenth of the seats while contributing a far larger share of passenger revenue, because a lie-flat seat on an overnight crossing sells for several thousand dollars against a few hundred for an economy seat. Premium economy adds a second tier of passengers paying a meaningful premium for modest extra space. Doganis quantified the disproportion in 2006: front cabins subsidise rear cabins on long routes, allowing legacy carriers to price economy seats aggressively while the flight as a whole remains profitable. A low-cost carrier that removes premium cabins removes the subsidy and must then recover the entire cost of the flight from economy fares and fees alone, at exactly the moment when density has already pushed those fares toward their floor.

The interaction between premium absence and seasonality is severe. Leisure demand, which fills most low-cost long-haul cabins, peaks in summer and around holidays and thins sharply in winter on many Atlantic and leisure corridors, while business demand, which fills premium cabins, distributes more evenly across the year for legacy rivals. A carrier without premium revenue therefore holds the most seasonal revenue profile on the route with the least seasonal cost profile, since leases, salaries, and many charges continue through quiet months. Norwegian long-haul network included heavily seasonal leisure routes, and winter losses against fixed widebody costs formed a recurring feature of its long-haul results before withdrawal in 2021. Filling a winter aircraft by cutting fares meets the break-even problem directly: the fare that fills the last 50 seats may sit below the average cost of the flight, so a full aircraft can still lose money while appearing successful at the gate.


## The Network Problem: Feed, Frequency and Thin Routes

A long-haul route rarely fills an aircraft from the city at one end alone. A legacy carrier departing Frankfurt for Chicago draws passengers from dozens of spokes, each short flight delivering travellers who transfer onto the widebody, and the same machine works in reverse at the far end through partners and its own onward services. It gathers thin streams of demand from places that could never support a transatlantic departure of their own and concentrates them into one departure dense enough to fill 300 seats at fares that cover the crossing. A low-cost entrant without a feeder network must find every passenger inside the catchment of a single airport, which narrows the market to people who live near the field, plan far ahead, and travel for reasons flexible enough to fit a sparse timetable.

Frequency compounds the disadvantage. A business traveller choosing between a daily departure and a service flown 3 times a week does not compare fares alone, because the schedule itself carries value: a missed meeting, a cancelled flight with no same day alternative, or a return date that does not exist each imposes a cost the ticket price never shows. Legacy groups answer that problem with banks of arrivals and departures timed to connect. The low-cost operator faces a hard trade. Adding frequency on a thin route multiplies the number of seats that must be sold before any of them earns a margin, while holding frequency low keeps the route unattractive to the higher fare passengers whose bookings would have steadied revenue across the week.

Aircraft size locks the problem in place. Short-haul economics reward a single type such as the Boeing 737 or the Airbus A320, flown in dense formation across many short sectors, because a 180 seat aircraft can be filled on a wide range of city pairs. Long-haul flying demands a widebody with 2 aisles and, in the configurations discussed by Rigas Doganis in The Airline Business (2006), commonly 250 to 350 seats, since a smaller aircraft carries nearly the same crew, fuel system complexity, and airport footprint while spreading those costs over far fewer passengers. The minimum viable departure is therefore large. A route that generates 150 willing passengers a day at low fares is a healthy short-haul route and an impossible long-haul one, because half an empty widebody earns half the revenue against almost the whole cost of the flight.

Interlining and alliance membership deepen the legacy advantage in ways a fare comparison hides. A through ticket sold by a partner in another continent, a baggage agreement that moves a suitcase across 3 carriers without the passenger touching it, and a frequent flyer currency that a traveller has accumulated over years all steer bookings toward network carriers before price is even compared. Peter Morrell, writing in Research in Transportation Economics (2008), treated network access as one of the structural gaps between long-haul low-cost entrants and incumbent groups, separate from the cost per seat each side achieved on the aircraft itself.

Secondary airports, the classic short-haul answer to high charges, help less across an ocean. The saving also shrinks in proportion, because airport charges form a smaller share of a long flight than of a short one, a relationship the cost table in a later section sets out in full. The network problem therefore has no single fix. Feed, frequency, gauge, and partnerships each favour the incumbent, and each must be solved with the same limited capital at the same time.

## Seasonality and the Direction of Demand

Leisure demand across the North Atlantic moves in a pronounced annual wave. Schools close, daylight lengthens, and millions of travellers who will accept a low fare in July will not cross an ocean in February at any price a carrier can profitably print. A long-haul leisure carrier holds a far narrower hand. Its aircraft are large, its routes are few, and the alternative destinations within widebody range from a northern European base in winter are limited to a small set of sun markets that many competitors chase at the same moment.

The fixed costs refuse to follow demand downward. Lease rentals on a widebody fall due in every month of the year, insurance and scheduled maintenance continue whether the aircraft flies or sits, and crew contracts carry minimum payments that a quiet February cannot suspend. When load factors fall in the low season, revenue per departure drops steeply, because the passengers who vanish are not replaced by freight or by premium traffic on a leisure configured aircraft. When fares are cut to stimulate winter demand, the carrier discovers the floor beneath the exercise: a seat sold below the cost of the fuel, catering, and handling attached to that passenger adds a loss rather than diluting one, and a widebody filled with such seats completes an expensive crossing for less than the flight cost to operate.

Direction makes the wound deeper. Leisure flows are unbalanced by week and by season, strong outbound at the start of a holiday period and strong inbound at its end, with soft demand running against the flow in between. A network carrier smooths that imbalance with connecting traffic, cargo booked in the weaker direction, and passengers travelling for work or family reasons whose calendars ignore the holiday pattern. A point to point leisure operator has none of those buffers in strength. An aircraft that crosses full in one direction and half empty on the return earns, across the rotation, a load factor in the low seventies at best, and the fare achieved on the full leg must then carry part of the empty leg as well. Doganis (2006) identified seasonal imbalance as a defining weakness of charter and leisure scheduled flying long before the low-cost long-haul wave, because the aircraft, unlike the passenger, must make the return journey on schedule.

Aircraft ownership structures sharpen the seasonal trap. A carrier that leases additional widebodies to capture the summer peak owns that capacity through the winter trough as well, since lessors write contracts in years rather than seasons. A carrier that sizes its fleet for winter abandons the peak revenue that was supposed to pay for the year, leaving the strongest months to competitors with larger fleets. Parking an aircraft for a season saves fuel and some crew cost, yet storage, return to service checks, and continued rent consume much of the saving, and a parked widebody earns nothing against rent that can exceed $1,000,000 a month for a modern type. Seasonality therefore does not merely lower the average fare a long-haul leisure carrier achieves. It attacks the utilisation on which the whole cost case rests, a connection the utilisation discussion in Part A established and the worked flight in a later section prices in full.

## Cargo in the Belly: The Subsidy Legacy Carriers Keep

### How much revenue does belly cargo contribute on a long flight?

On a widebody crossing, belly cargo commonly contributes revenue equal to roughly 5 to 15 percent of passenger revenue, and on freight dense routes the share runs higher. Legacy carriers book that income before departure through freight contracts, which lowers the passenger fare needed to cover the flight.

Every widebody carries 2 revenue spaces, the cabin above the floor and the hold beneath it, and the 2 spaces obey different economics. Passengers buy a seat for a particular departure and vanish as customers the moment the aircraft lands, while freight shippers buy weight and volume on a schedule, often under contracts that run for months and cover many rotations. A Boeing 777 or an Airbus A330 on a long sector can carry 15 to 20 tonnes of cargo in the belly after passenger baggage is loaded, in containers and pallets shaped to the hold, and that capacity exists whether a carrier chooses to sell it or not. The fuel penalty for carrying freight is real but modest against the revenue, since the aircraft makes the crossing in any case and the marginal burn rises with weight rather than doubling with the load.

Legacy groups built organisations to harvest that space. Freight divisions sell capacity to forwarders, who consolidate shipments from hundreds of businesses into palletised loads delivered to the airport on a timetable matched to departures. The mechanism matters for passenger fares because cargo revenue enters the route calculation before a single ticket is sold. A legacy carrier can price the final cabin seats aggressively, even below the average passenger cost, when the hold beneath those passengers has already contributed a substantial share of the flight revenue. Morrell (2008) counted cargo among the revenue advantages that a passenger only low-cost entrant forfeits, and the forfeiture grows with stage length because belly capacity grows with aircraft size and sector distance.

Low-cost long-haul carriers have usually left that income uncollected, for reasons rooted in organisation rather than in the aircraft. Selling freight requires a sales force in each market, handling contracts that can load and break down pallets within the turnaround, customs and security processes for goods rather than people, and a claims system for damaged or delayed shipments. Tight turnarounds, prized on short routes, conflict with freight handling on long ones, since a pallet built incorrectly or a container delayed at the warehouse holds a departure worth far more than the shipment. Some entrants have carried mail or light freight opportunistically, yet opportunistic loads earn opportunistic rates, well below the contracted yields a freight division extracts from the same hold space.

The gap also distorts how competition looks from the passenger side. When a legacy carrier matches a low headline fare on a route where its aircraft carry contracted freight, an observer may conclude that the incumbent has learned to match the entrant on cost. The likelier reading is that the incumbent is spending a second revenue stream the entrant does not possess, buying time until the entrant exhausts its capital. Cargo therefore functions as a quiet subsidy inside the legacy model, invisible on the ticket, decisive in the route accounts, and almost impossible for a lean passenger only operator to replicate without ceasing to be lean.

## The Table That Settles the Argument

One table settles the central argument of the whole subject, because it separates the costs distance inflates from the costs a determined operator can compress, and shows that the 2 groups move in opposite directions as a route lengthens. On a short sector, the compressible group forms a large share of the total cost of a seat, so a carrier that attacks airport charges, distribution, turnaround time, and service frills can open a wide gap against a legacy rival burdened by older habits. On a long sector, fuel, rest bound crew cost, and the capital cost of a widebody consumed for half a day on a single crossing swell to dominate the total, and those are the rows where the table records the least room to cut. The low-cost method does not weaken with distance because managers lose discipline. It weakens because the share of the cost base the method can reach shrinks beneath it.

| Cost Element | Share Behaviour as Distance Grows | Can a Low-Cost Carrier Cut It | Consequence on Long Routes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fuel | Rises steeply and dominates the total | Only marginally through newer aircraft and density | The largest cost answers to physics and the oil price rather than to management |
| Flight and cabin crew | Rises as rest rules add extra crew sets | Barely since rest minima are set by regulation | A long crossing carries crew who are paid to rest inside the aircraft |
| Aircraft ownership and leasing | Rises as one crossing consumes half a day | Partly through secondhand aircraft and hard lease negotiation | Capital cost per seat stays high when daily utilisation falls |
| Airport and navigation charges | Falls as a share of the total | Yes through secondary airports and negotiation | Real savings shrink in proportion until they barely move the fare |
| Maintenance | Rises with aircraft value and cycle complexity | Partly through fleet commonality and outsourcing | Widebody checks and engine care set a floor no operator escapes |
| Distribution and service | Falls as a share of the total | Yes through direct sales and unbundled service | The classic savings remain available yet cover a shrinking slice of cost |

The rows also explain why short-haul success misleads founders and investors who study it as a template. Ryanair and Southwest built their margins in the lower rows of the table, where charges, distribution, and service choices differ sharply between operators, and in rapid turnarounds that belong to the ownership row on short sectors. Doganis (2006) organised airline costs in exactly this spirit, separating the elements management controls from the elements the operating environment imposes, and the separation grows more unfavourable to the entrant with every additional 1,000 miles of stage length.

The table returns later in the worked flight, where each row becomes a sum of money attached to one crossing, and in the closing argument about what would have to change for the model to hold. Any claim that a new entrant has found a cheaper way to fly long-haul should be required to name the row it has moved. A claim that names no row, or that names only distribution and service, describes a saving measured in single dollars against a cost measured in hundreds, and the sections that follow test the historical record against that standard.

## A Worked Flight: One Crossing Priced Seat by Seat

Abstractions about cost shares persuade few readers, so consider one concrete crossing priced from the ground up. The aircraft is a Boeing 787-9 configured with 344 seats, a density a determined low-cost operator might choose, flying 3,450 miles between London and New York with 310 passengers aboard. The distance, the seat count, and the passenger count stay fixed through every line that follows, and each cost element appears once, at one value, so the arithmetic can be checked rather than admired. The figures are illustrative rather than drawn from any single carrier account.

Fuel for the crossing comes to $28,400, the largest single element, burned across 7 to 8 hours of flight by an aircraft whose weight includes the fuel for the final hours carried through the first ones. Flight and cabin crew cost $9,800, covering the augmented flight deck crew that rest rules require on a sector of this length, the cabin team sized to the seat count, allowances, and hotel rest at the far end. The ownership charge allocated to this departure is $14,600, the lease and capital cost of a widebody divided across the limited number of sectors such an aircraft completes in a day. Airport and navigation charges total $7,900, gathering departure and arrival fees, en route charges for the airspace crossed, ground handling, and the cost of the gate and the ramp. Maintenance contributes $6,300, an allocation for line checks, component wear, and the heavy inspections whose cost arrives in large sums at long intervals and must be accrued against every flight. Distribution and service close the account at $3,100, covering direct sales systems, payment costs, catering bought by passengers or bundled at minimal level, and passenger handling administration.

The flight therefore costs $70,100 to operate. Divided by the 344 seats offered, the cost stands at $204 for each available seat, whether a passenger sits in it or not. Divided by the 310 passengers who boarded, the cost rises to $226 for each passenger carried, because every empty seat transfers its share of the same fixed flight cost onto the passengers who did travel. The aircraft, the crew, the fuel for the airframe itself, and the charges all leave London regardless of the booking curve, so the difference between a profitable crossing and a losing one is decided almost entirely by how many of the seats convert into paying passengers and at what average return each one produces.

Set revenue against that cost with equal discipline. Suppose the average passenger produces $189 in fare and $31 in ancillary purchases, bags, seat selection, food, and priority services, for a combined return of $220. Across 310 passengers the flight collects $68,200, which falls $1,900 short of the $70,100 the crossing cost to operate. The shortfall looks small, and on a stronger day, with a fuller aircraft or a firmer average fare, the same flight earns a margin. Yet the example was built generously, with a modern fuel efficient type, a dense cabin, and a load factor above 90 percent, and it still lands beneath the line. Remove 30 passengers in a weak month and the loss widens sharply, since revenue falls by thousands while cost barely moves. That sensitivity, visible in one worked crossing, is the arithmetic every carrier in the following sections met at fleet scale.

## The First Generation: Laker, People Express and the Atlantic

Freddie Laker launched the Skytrain service of Laker Airways in 1977, selling walk up transatlantic seats between London Gatwick and New York at fares far below the regulated prices of the flag carriers, and filling his McDonnell Douglas DC-10 aircraft with passengers the incumbent system had priced out of the sky. The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 in the United States and the loosening of British policy opened the legal space for the experiment, yet the economics rested on the same foundations the worked flight priced: a large aircraft, a long sector, and a fare set by the need to fill seats rather than by the cost of carrying the passengers in them. Skytrain proved demand beyond dispute.

Laker Airways collapsed in 1982, and the causes named at the time already contain the structural pattern in early form. A recession cut discretionary travel, the value of sterling moved against dollar denominated costs such as fuel and aircraft finance, competitors held fares down on the routes where Laker flew while keeping stronger yields elsewhere in their networks, and debts taken on for expansion fell due against falling revenue. Read together, they describe a carrier with no second revenue stream, no feeder network, no premium cabin to cushion a fare war, and a cost base dominated by items priced in a foreign currency and set by distance. The network carriers could lose money on a contested route and recover it across a system. Laker had only the contested routes, so the fare war reached the whole company at once. The mechanism is worth naming precisely: cross subsidy inside a network turns price competition into a local expense for the incumbent and an existential threat for the entrant, even when the entrant operates the individual flight as efficiently as the incumbent does.

People Express repeated the experiment from the American side, beginning transatlantic service in 1983 from Newark with Boeing 747 aircraft bought at low prices, offering fares that again filled very large aircraft with price sensitive travellers. The carrier had built its domestic reputation on radical simplicity, low labour cost, and minimal service, and the long-haul operation exposed where each of those strengths thins with distance. A 747 carries the cost logic of the largest row in the table at maximum scale, and filling it outside peak periods demanded fares that sank toward the marginal cost of the seat. Service failures and operational strain damaged the reputation that low fares alone could not protect, debts from rapid expansion mounted, and the airline was absorbed into a larger rival system in 1987, its independent existence over within 4 years of its first crossing.

The first generation therefore proved 2 things at once, and later founders remembered only the first. The supply of that travel at a profit, with the aircraft, fuel prices, and labour rules of the period, was not established by either attempt. Laker and People Express each flew full aircraft into insolvency or absorption, which is the signature outcome of the model: the product succeeds with passengers while the arithmetic fails in the accounts, because load factor measures popularity and only yield against unit cost measures survival.

## The Second Generation: AirAsia X, Norwegian and the Widebody Bet

The second generation arrived with better aircraft and the same table of costs. AirAsia X, founded in 2007 and built on the short-haul strength of the AirAsia group in Malaysia, applied a proven regional low-cost system to long sectors from Kuala Lumpur toward Australia, China, Japan, and, for a period, Europe. The experiment tested a hopeful variation: perhaps feed from a powerful short-haul network, the very asset Laker lacked, would repair the model. Feed helped at the margin, gathering passengers from across Southeast Asia onto widebody departures, yet the European services were withdrawn and the carrier passed through repeated restructurings, because feed solves the problem of filling seats without solving the problem of what each filled seat returns against fuel, crew rest, and widebody capital cost across 10 hours of flying. The mechanism separates cleanly here. A feeder network raises load factor, and load factor is only half the survival equation; yield per passenger against cost per passenger decides the other half.

Norwegian Air Shuttle made the largest and most instructive bet. Under Bjorn Kjos, the carrier expanded into long-haul flying from 2013, ordering Boeing 787 aircraft, opening bases across Europe and the United States, and selling transatlantic fares that again demonstrated enormous public appetite for cheap crossings. The 787 cut fuel burn per seat against older widebodies, secondary airports in the New York area and elsewhere trimmed charges, and dense configurations spread costs widely. Then the upper rows of the table asserted themselves. Engine problems on the 787 fleet grounded aircraft and forced expensive wet leases of substitute capacity, expansion had been financed with heavy debt and lease commitments, and a long-haul network spread across many thin routes multiplied the network problem instead of concentrating demand on a few dense ones.

The restructuring of Norwegian between 2020 and 2021 ended its long-haul operation entirely, while the short-haul business survived in reduced form. The same management culture, brand, and sales machinery produced a viable airline on short sectors and an unsustainable one on long sectors, which isolates distance itself as the variable that changed. Morrell (2008), writing before the Norwegian expansion began, had set out the conditions a long-haul low-cost carrier would need: dense routes, high utilisation, restrained growth, and revenue beyond the basic fare. The Norwegian record reads as a test of that list conducted at great expense, with the conditions met partially and briefly rather than together and permanently.

WOW air of Iceland and Primera Air, both of which ceased operations in the period before Norwegian withdrew, with WOW ending in March 2019 and Primera in October 2018, filled in the smaller scale version of the pattern. Each used geography or low labour and aircraft costs to offer cheap transatlantic seats, each filled aircraft in peak season, and each failed when financing tightened against a cost base that left no reserve. The second generation thus improved every element the first generation had handled badly, from aircraft efficiency to brand to feed, and arrived at the same destination by a longer road. Better execution delayed the arithmetic and enlarged the scale on which it operated, without repealing it.

## Norse Atlantic and the Limits of Starting Again

The Atlantic has repeatedly attracted a fresh attempt built from the remains of the previous one, and the pattern repeated in documented form when Norse Atlantic suspended all flights on November 26, 2026, and its board stated an intention to file for bankruptcy absent urgent short-term financing, per the company statement. That single dated sentence belongs in the record as history, and the analytical interest lies in what a restart of this kind tests. A new carrier formed after a predecessor withdraws begins with advantages that look, in a prospectus, like answers to the old failure. Widebody aircraft released by a shrinking rival can be leased on softer terms than new orders commanded. Crews with long-haul experience are available without training pipelines built from nothing.

Each advantage is real, and each addresses a row near the bottom of the cost table or a line in the financing account rather than the structure of the flight itself. Cheaper leases reduce the ownership row without removing it, since a widebody resting between crossings still consumes capital every day of the year. Experienced crews still rest under the same regulations, in the same hotel rooms, at the same allowances, because rest law follows the route and the human body rather than the employer. Fuel for a crossing between London and New York or Oslo and Florida burns by the tonne according to weight and weather, indifferent to the youth of the company buying it. The restart therefore begins the race from a better position on the costs that matter least and an identical position on the costs that matter most, which is why the history of the Atlantic shows new entrants outliving their predecessors by years rather than by generations.

The deeper limit is strategic. A restarted carrier must choose the same routes the predecessor chose, because those are the routes where low fare demand concentrates, and it must price against the same network rivals whose cargo income, feed, and premium cabins survived the previous contest intact. Nothing in the failure of one low-cost operator weakens the legacy groups waiting on the route. Every collapse releases modern widebodies into the leasing market at depressed rates, and those rates tempt the next founding team into believing the ownership row has been permanently repriced, when the discount is in fact the market clearing price of a failed experiment, available precisely because the previous operator could not make the same aircraft pay.

Starting again also inherits a demand problem disguised as an opportunity. The passengers a failed carrier leaves behind are the most price sensitive travellers in the market, loyal to a fare level rather than to an airline, and a successor that courts them must open at fares close to the ones that failed to sustain the predecessor. Raising fares to a survivable level surrenders the founding claim of the business, while holding fares at the inherited level imports the old loss rate into a new set of accounts. The limit of starting again is therefore not imagination, capital, or effort, each of which the Atlantic attempts have possessed in quantity. The limit is that a restart resets the company while leaving the route, the aircraft, the regulations, and the rivals exactly where the failure left them.

## What Legacy Carriers Learned and Copied

Legacy carriers studied the low-cost challenge for decades and responded by disassembling it, adopting the elements that worked inside a network and discarding the elements that only functioned as part of a separate low-cost company. The clearest transfers came from the lower rows of the cost table. Checked baggage, once folded into the standard fare, became a separate charge on many network airlines, first on domestic and short-haul services and then, in lighter form, on long-haul economy products. Seat selection, priority boarding, and paid meals in economy followed the same path, converting services that legacy carriers had supplied by default into optional purchases.

Cabin densification travelled the same route. Network airlines added seats to widebody aircraft by narrowing seat width, reducing pitch in economy, and reconfiguring galleys and lavatories, narrowing the cost per seat gap that entrants had opened with density alone. A legacy Boeing 777 that once flew with fewer than 300 seats in many configurations came to fly with well over 300 in high density layouts at several groups, while a premium economy cabin inserted between economy and business protected the yield at the front of the aircraft. The mechanism deserves attention because it reverses the usual story of disruption. The incumbent did not need to become a low-cost carrier. It needed to lower its economy cost per seat toward the entrant level while preserving the revenue layers above economy, and densification plus unbundling achieved exactly that combination on the routes where the 2 models competed directly.

Distribution and sales practice completed the transfer. Legacy groups built direct digital sales channels that matched the low-cost refusal of commission heavy intermediaries, used fare families to display a stripped basic product beside richer bundles on the same screen, and learned to sell the stripped product aggressively on price comparison terms while steering repeat and corporate travellers toward the bundles. Doganis (2006) described the convergence of the 2 models as each borrowed the methods of the other, and on long-haul routes the borrowing ran mostly in one direction, because the legacy side had more layers to protect and more revenue with which to fund the transition. The entrant, by contrast, could copy almost nothing back. Adding a premium cabin, a freight division, or a feeder network meant adding the cost structure the model existed to avoid, a symmetry that explains why convergence strengthened incumbents instead of rescuing challengers.

The lasting legacy of the low-cost long-haul attempts may therefore sit inside the aircraft of their rivals. Every passenger who pays separately for a bag on a network airline, sits in a denser economy cabin, or compares a basic fare against a bundle is using a product reshaped by Laker, by People Express, and by the carriers that followed them onto the Atlantic and across Asia. The entrants failed as companies while succeeding as a source of methods, and the distinction matters for judging the record fairly. A model can be commercially unsustainable in the form its pioneers gave it and still permanently change the industry it attacked, by forcing incumbents to price each element of the journey separately and to defend, route by route, a cost position they once held by custom alone.
## Basic Economy and the Hybrid Answer

Basic economy is a fare, not an airline, and the distinction explains why the hybrid answer has proved more durable than the pure model. A network carrier that adds a stripped fare class to a long flight keeps everything that makes the flight survivable. The premium cabin still sits at the front of the same aircraft. The cargo hold still carries freight beneath the same passengers. The loyalty programme still fills seats in weak months, and the connecting network still delivers passengers from dozens of cities into one departure. The low fare, in that setting, is a pricing decision applied to a seat that might otherwise fly empty. The cost of carrying one more passenger in an unsold seat is small: a meal withheld, a bag charged for, a modest amount of extra fuel. The cost of operating the flight exists whether that seat sells or not.

A standalone low-cost carrier faces the opposite position. Every seat must help pay for the whole flight, because no premium cabin, no freight contract and no connecting bank stands behind the fare. Where a network carrier can discount the margin of a service, the standalone carrier must discount the core of the service, and the core is where fuel, crew and aircraft ownership sit. Basic economy therefore captures much of the same demand without carrying the same risk. The carrier keeps the revenue layers that a pure operator gives away, and those layers are worth more, not less, as distance grows.

Hybrid carriers occupy the ground between the two forms, and their record shows both the attraction and the limit of the compromise. A hybrid typically adds some of what the pure model removes: a better seat for a fee, a modest premium cabin, partnerships that supply connecting passengers, or a loyalty scheme that steadies demand between seasons. Each addition raises revenue per passenger. Each addition also raises cost, complexity and weight, and moves the operation closer to the network model it set out to undercut. The question is whether the added revenue outruns the added cost on a route where the uncuttable share of total cost is already high, and on many routes the answer has been that the hybrid survives by becoming, piece by piece, a smaller version of the carrier it challenged.

That outcome carries a lesson about where the low-cost advantage lives. The advantage is strongest where a service can be simplified without touching the expensive core of the operation. Short flights allow that separation because ground handling, turnaround time, distribution and service frills form a large slice of a small total. Long flights defeat the separation because simplification reaches the expensive core quickly and then stops. A hybrid respects the boundary by keeping a hand on premium revenue and partner feed. A pure operator tests the boundary by surrendering both, and the fare must then do work that, on a long sector, no fare at a filling price has reliably done.

## The Gulf and Asian Variations

The Gulf and Asian variations appear, at first sight, to break the pattern, and a closer reading shows how much support each variation draws from its setting. The large Gulf carriers are not low-cost airlines in the strict sense. Their service is full, their cabins are divided, and their networks are built around connecting banks at a single hub. What they share with the low-cost model is a low unit cost, reached by a different road: geography that places Europe, Asia and Africa within one stop, very high aircraft utilisation across time zones, dense configurations on many routes, and airport infrastructure built at a scale that keeps transfer costs down. Their advantage is structural, but the structure is a hub system with premium and cargo revenue intact, not a stripped long-haul fare standing alone.

Geography does heavy work in that system. A standalone point to point operator has no such pool. Each route must fill its own aircraft, in both directions, across the whole year. The Gulf model therefore answers the feed problem that defeats pure long-haul operators, and answers it with the very machinery, connections, transfers and schedule banks, that the low-cost model was designed to remove. Calling the result a low-cost success confuses a low unit cost with a low-cost structure, and the denominator matters: cost per passenger kilometre falls when a network spreads fixed costs over enormous connecting volume, which is a different achievement from cutting the cost of one direct flight.

The Asian variations sit closer to the model and show its workable range. Scoot operates inside the Singapore Airlines group, and Zipair operates inside the Japan Airlines group, while AirAsia X grew from the AirAsia short-haul system in Kuala Lumpur. Group membership changes the arithmetic in concrete ways. A parent group can supply feed from its own short-haul network, negotiate aircraft and maintenance from a stronger balance sheet, and keep a brand and sales channel that a new entrant must build from nothing. The low-cost long-haul unit, in that form, is a division inside a larger system rather than a lone company betting its survival on a handful of widebody aircraft.

Stage length does the rest. Much Asian low-cost long-haul flying sits in a band of roughly five to nine hours, linking large cities across a region with dense populations and strong year round demand. That band is long enough for density and ancillary revenue to matter, yet short enough that crew rest penalties, utilisation losses and fuel share stay below the levels reached on a 12 hour or 14 hour sector. The variations that endure tend to shorten the problem, share the problem with a group, or surround the problem with hub revenue. None of the three amounts to a lone operator solving the full problem on the longest routes at the lowest fares, and the distinction is the point.

## When the Model Works: Dense Leisure Routes and Secondary Airports

### Which long-haul routes can a low-cost carrier actually sustain?

A low-cost carrier can sustain routes where demand stays dense in both directions across most of the year, flight time stays near six to nine hours, a secondary airport keeps charges low, and the same aircraft can return without a long idle gap between rotations each day.

Density is the first condition, and density means more than a large city at each end. A sustainable route needs enough passengers who will travel on the days and at the hours a small fleet can offer, because a carrier with few aircraft cannot spread demand across a bank of departures. Leisure demand helps when the destination draws visitors in both directions or across a long season: a holiday market with inbound and outbound flows, a route serving people visiting family, or a city pair tied to events and study as well as tourism. A route that depends on one flow, in one direction, in one season, leaves the operator selling the return leg into a weak market at a fare that still has to cover a full crossing.

Secondary airports supply the second condition, within limits. Lower charges, faster handling and less congestion can trim real money from each rotation, and a secondary field near a large city can still draw a full aircraft when the fare gap is wide. The saving, though, is one of the costs that shrinks as a share of the total when distance grows. Airport charges matter greatly on a short sector, where they rival the fuel bill. On a long sector, fuel and crew dominate, and a cheaper departure point cannot close a gap opened by those larger elements. Secondary airports also carry a revenue cost: business travellers value central fields and frequent schedules, and the passengers most willing to accept a distant airport are often the passengers least willing to pay for extras on board.

Aircraft scheduling supplies the third condition, and the route pattern decides whether it can be met. A sector near seven or eight hours can allow an aircraft to cross, turn and return inside a working pattern that keeps utilisation respectable, especially where time zones place the return departure at a saleable hour. A much longer sector breaks the pattern. The aircraft arrives when the home market is asleep, waits for a departure window passengers will buy, and loses the hours that a short-haul fleet converts into extra sectors. Routes in the middle band therefore suit the model in a way the longest routes do not, and many of the more durable operations have clustered, deliberately or by necessity, inside that band.

The fourth condition is restraint in the cabin and the timetable. A sustainable operation on a dense leisure route tends to keep one aircraft type, a tight configuration, a small number of routes flown often rather than many routes flown thinly, and a fare structure that treats ancillary revenue as a supplement rather than a rescue. Restraint also protects the balance sheet from the trap that has caught larger attempts: each new route adds a full season of risk before it adds a proven return, and a small fleet has no spare aircraft when a new route underperforms while an established route needs cover.

## The Complication: Is Failure in the Model or in the Financing?

The strongest objection to a structural account deserves its best form, because the record gives it real material. Defenders of Norwegian made the case in a consistent way during and after its long-haul expansion. Bjorn Kjos, the founder of Norwegian Air Shuttle, presented long-haul growth as a natural extension of a proven short-haul system, backed by new and efficient widebody aircraft. On that reading, the idea was sound and the damage came from the balance sheet: heavy borrowing to fund a fleet that arrived faster than the routes matured, and a debt load that left no room for a bad season. Analysts who stress financing tell a similar story across several failures, pointing to thin capital and demanding lease rates.

Given full weight, the objection runs further. A disciplined operator, the argument holds, would grow slowly, keep debt modest, choose only thick leisure routes, configure aircraft densely, and build cash in strong summers against weak winters. Nothing in the failures named in the historical record, the objection continues, proves that such an operator cannot hold, because no failed carrier matches that description. If the cause sits in the financing, then the model stands acquitted, and the lesson for the next entrant is discipline rather than retreat.

The answer begins by granting the objection its strongest ground. Financing plainly decides a great deal. Capital structure decides how long a carrier can endure a weak winter, whether a fuel spike becomes a crisis or a bad quarter, and whether a fleet problem can be absorbed or ends the company. Any account that treated money management as irrelevant would fail against the record, and the structural account does not need that claim. The thesis narrows instead: financing decides when a carrier dies, while unit arithmetic decides why rescue capital keeps refusing to arrive, and why each rescue that does arrive demands a smaller network, a retreat from long routes, or absorption into a larger group.

That narrowing is where the evidence separates the two explanations. A company with sound economics and bad financing attracts buyers, because the underlying operation, once relieved of debt, earns its keep. Long-haul low-cost restructurings have repeatedly produced the opposite result. Norwegian survived its restructuring of 2020 to 2021 by ending long-haul flying altogether and rebuilding around short routes, which is the market conceding that the short-haul core was worth saving and the long-haul network was not. AirAsia X restructured around a reduced network after long negotiations with creditors and lessors. Norse Atlantic, founded by Bjorn Tore Larsen with experienced management and modern aircraft, still failed to make the long-haul arithmetic pay.

The financing objection also struggles with the direction of causation. Leverage was not an accident visited on otherwise healthy operations. Growth at speed was the strategy the model seemed to require, because a small long-haul fleet carries ruinous unit costs: too few aircraft to spread training, maintenance and management, too little presence to fill seats without deep discounting, and no spare capacity when an aircraft fails. Scale promised relief, so carriers bought scale with debt, and the debt then exposed the thin margin that scale was meant to cure. Slow growth avoids the debt trap and keeps the unit cost trap. That fork, repeated across decades and across owners with very different habits, is the signature of a structural problem wearing a financing costume, and it is why the objection, taken at full strength, ends by confirming the narrower thesis it set out to defeat.

## Bankruptcy, Leasing and the Speed of Collapse

Long-haul low-cost carriers tend to collapse quickly, and the speed follows from the way the assets and the cash are arranged. Most of the fleet is leased rather than owned, so the carrier holds few assets that a lender can treat as security, while rent on widebody aircraft falls due on a fixed calendar regardless of ticket sales. Cash arrives from passengers in advance of travel, which flatters the balance sheet in a growing season and reverses without warning when bookings slow, refunds rise or card processors hold back funds against the risk of non delivery. A company can therefore look liquid in a strong month and face a wall within weeks, because the money in the bank partly belongs, in substance, to passengers who have not yet flown and to lessors whose claims do not pause.

Leasing cuts both ways across the life of the company. At entry, leasing is the enabler: a new carrier can field modern widebody aircraft without raising the purchase price of a fleet, and can present a credible network within a short period. At the point of failure, the same structure accelerates events. Lessors move to protect aircraft that represent large individual values, and an aircraft repossessed or grounded cannot be replaced by a smaller type on a long route. The mechanics that govern a court supervised restructuring in the United States follow the sequence set out in the guide to [how prepackaged bankruptcy works](/2026/10/22/how-prepackaged-bankruptcy-works/), but an airline adds a complication that a retailer or a manufacturer does not face in the same form: the product is a promise to move people on fixed dates, and the promise loses value the moment confidence in delivery falters.

Confidence is an operating asset in aviation in a literal sense. A passenger choosing between carriers for a flight months ahead weighs the risk of cancellation alongside the fare, and a carrier reported to be seeking emergency funds will watch forward bookings soften at exactly the moment cash is shortest. The spiral needs no misconduct and no sudden change in costs. A financing headline reduces revenue, reduced revenue tightens cash, tight cash produces more alarming headlines, and the distance between a going concern and a suspension of operations can close in a single season. Network carriers enter the same spiral with more defences: owned assets, loyalty programme value, cargo contracts, government significance on national routes, and premium revenue that keeps corporate accounts loyal while leisure bookings waver.

Geography makes airline failure harder to stage than failure in most industries. Aircraft, crews, maintenance bases and passenger obligations sit in several jurisdictions at once, each with its own rules on repossession, employee claims and consumer refunds. A carrier that stops flying leaves passengers abroad, aircraft at foreign stations and crews out of position, and the cost of an orderly wind down can exceed the cash available, which is one reason collapses so often arrive as abrupt suspensions rather than managed retreats. Laker Airways in 1982, WOW air in 2019 and Primera Air in 2018 each ended with little warning to passengers holding tickets. The pattern is not a series of separate management scandals. A leased fleet, advance ticket cash, confidence sensitive bookings and obligations scattered across borders form a structure in which failure, once it starts, has very few slow lanes.

## Reading an Airline Claim: Costs, Denominators and Forecasts

Airline claims are usually true in the narrow sense and misleading in the comparison they invite, so the reading skill is to ask which denominator carries the number. Cost per available seat mile divides total cost by the seats offered multiplied by the miles flown, and the figure falls automatically as sectors lengthen, because fixed costs of a departure spread over more miles. A carrier can therefore report a falling unit cost while the cost of each flight rises steeply. Cost per passenger kilometre asks a different question, since only filled seats enter the second measure, and a low fare that fills an aircraft can still lose money if the fare per kilometre sits below the cost per kilometre of the seats actually sold. Rigas Doganis, in The Airline Business, set out the long-haul cost structure in these terms, and the distinction between seats offered and seats sold remains the first check on any claim built from unit costs.

The artifact table in the middle of the argument shows how each cost element behaves as distance grows, and that behaviour is the test to apply to a promotional claim. A claim that celebrates savings on distribution, catering or airport choice points at elements whose share of the total falls on long routes. A claim that stays silent on fuel, crew rest and aircraft ownership avoids the elements whose share rises. Reading the table against a launch announcement settles much of the dispute without any inside knowledge: count the savings the announcement names, place each one in its row, and ask whether the named savings sit in the part of the cost base that distance enlarges or the part that distance shrinks. Most launch claims cluster, on inspection, in the shrinking part.

Load factor claims need the same treatment. A load factor of 90 percent sounds like proof of health, and on a short route with strong yields it often accompanies profit. On a long route, the figure says nothing about the price paid to fill those seats, the season in which the figure was measured, or the direction of the flying. A carrier can fill an aircraft across an ocean at fares below cost for months, and the fuller the aircraft, the larger the loss per departure in absolute terms. Yield, meaning revenue per passenger kilometre, has to stand beside load factor before either number means anything, and a claim that offers one without the other has chosen its evidence. Peter Morrell, in research published in 2008 on whether long-haul low-cost airlines can succeed, treated load factor and yield together for exactly that reason, and the pairing remains the honest standard.

Forecasts deserve a record check rather than repetition. The transport evidence standard asks what a forecaster predicted before, and long-haul low-cost history supplies a long trail of confident projections: launch plans that assumed rapid fleet growth, high utilisation on the longest sectors, premium level ancillary spending from passengers who had chosen the cheapest fare, and year round demand on routes with a pronounced summer peak. A forecast that assumes away seasonality, or that projects short-haul utilisation onto 12 hour sectors, has not made an arithmetic error so much as declined to use the available record. The disciplined reader asks for the assumption beneath the headline number, compares the assumption with the last carrier that relied on it, and treats agreement between the two as a warning rather than as support.

## What Would Have to Change for the Model to Hold

A permanent version of the model needs changes that alter the ratio at the heart of the argument, not changes that merely help a carrier reach the next season. The first candidate is aircraft economics. A step change in fuel burn per seat, of the kind that a new generation of aircraft delivers in part, lowers the largest single cost on a long sector. The gain, though, arrives for every competitor at once. Network carriers buy the same efficient aircraft, place premium cabins inside them, and keep their cargo and feed advantages while their fuel bill falls by a similar proportion. A technology improvement that lifts all operators preserves the relative gap even as it lowers absolute costs, and the history of widebody development shows that pattern repeating: each new type made long-haul flying cheaper for everyone and left the stripped fare model still chasing a moving target.

The second candidate is the ownership cost of the aircraft itself. Cheaper leasing, longer lease terms matched to route maturity, or a fall in widebody values could narrow the fixed burden that a small fleet carries. The table referenced in the reading section makes the limit plain: ownership is one rising element among several, and relief on one element leaves fuel and crew untouched. Crew cost on long routes is not waste waiting for a cleverer manager. Rest at the far end, augmented crews on the longest sectors and pay for time away from base are the price of operating across time zones with human beings, and that price scales with distance in a way that software cannot repeal.

Demand would have to change as well, and demand changes are the least reliable foundation for a business model. A permanent model needs leisure demand that stays high across the year or a passenger mix willing to pay for a premium seat inside a low-cost brand. The first would require travel patterns to flatten in a way that climate, school calendars and working life resist. The second dissolves the model from inside, since a carrier with a successful premium cabin, strong loyalty revenue and partner feed has rebuilt the network structure under a new name. Cargo offers a partial version of the same bargain: a low-cost carrier that built a serious freight business on passenger routes would add a revenue layer that distance rewards, but freight demands sales networks, handling contracts and schedule reliability aimed at shippers, and each requirement pulls the operation further from the simple model that produced the low fare.

What remains is a narrower and more honest possibility. The model holds where the problem is kept small: sectors in the middle distance band, dense leisure demand, group support or feed partnerships, one fleet type, and growth paced to cash rather than to ambition. Inside those limits, low-cost long-haul flying has operated for extended periods and can continue to operate. Outside those limits, the changes required are so large, and so available to competitors, that the model in its pure form would still face the same arithmetic with better tools. A change counts as a solution only if it widens the gap between what a stripped operator saves and what distance costs, and no change in aircraft, leasing, labour or demand has yet done that on the longest routes.

## The Practical Takeaway

Reading a fare claim well starts with the distance. Take the headline fare, note the sector length beside it, and ask which costs that distance fixes before any carrier choice enters the picture: fuel for the crossing, crew with rest at the far end, and an aircraft committed for the better part of a day. A fare that looks startling on a short sector may be ordinary arithmetic. A fare that looks startling on a 12 hour sector deserves a slower reading, because the fixed share of the cost is larger and the room for a genuine saving is smaller.

The second step is to separate the seat from the journey. A lead fare usually prices the seat alone, with bags, seat selection, food and flexibility sold apart. Adding the elements a particular journey requires gives the honest total, and comparing that total, rather than the headline, against a network carrier fare on the same route shows what the saving actually buys. The comparison also runs in the other direction: if the stripped total and the full service total nearly meet, the difference between the offers lies in schedule, airport and protection when a flight is cancelled, and those features have value that a fare table does not display.

A further step is to read the small print of the operation behind the fare. A carrier flying one aircraft type, on a route served several times a week, with a departure and a return that both sit at hours passengers accept, presents a different claim from a carrier offering a single weekly rotation on a mixed fleet. Density in the cabin belongs in the same reading. A very dense configuration lowers cost per seat and also signals how much of the saving came from space rather than from efficiency, while a fare paired with a long list of paid extras signals how much of the saving came from unbundling rather than from a lower cost base. Each one locates the saving, and locating the saving is the skill the earlier sections built: a saving found in distribution or in service can be genuine and still small, while a saving claimed against fuel or crew on a long sector needs evidence that a timetable, a fleet list or a published account can supply.

The third step is to read the season and the schedule as part of the price. A very low fare offered for a winter departure on a summer leisure route tells a story about demand that the fare alone hides, and a single weekly frequency tells a story about what happens when a flight is disrupted: the next departure may sit several days away. None of that reading requires a judgement about whether a given fare represents good value for a particular traveller, and no part of the reading points toward a booking decision. The skill is narrower and more durable. You can take any long-haul fare, divide the claim into the costs distance fixes and the costs a carrier can cut, check the season and the frequency behind the number, and form a view of whether the price rests on a saving or on a hope. A fare built on a saving can be repeated. A fare built on hope tends to vanish with the carrier that offered it.

## The Structural Lesson

Strip the names away and the pattern reduces to a ratio. On a short flight, the expenses an operator can remove make up a generous share of everything the flight costs, so removing them creates a fare gap wide enough to build a company inside. On a long flight, the expenses set by physics, by human rest and by the price of a large aircraft over many hours make up most of the total, so the same discipline creates a gap too narrow to hold a company for long. The carriers change, the aircraft improve, the founders differ in temperament and talent, and the ratio waits at the end of every attempt, unchanged by the quality of the effort spent against it.

That is why the failures rhyme without being identical. Laker Airways tested the idea with the tools of the late 1970s, People Express carried the test into the deregulated 1980s, AirAsia X and Norwegian rebuilt the attempt around efficient widebody aircraft and dense cabins, and Norse Atlantic ran the attempt again with the lessons of each earlier failure available in full. Each attempt added something real: better aircraft, sharper distribution, denser seating, harder won knowledge about seasonality and feed. Each addition lowered cost or raised revenue at the margin, and the margin was never where the problem lived. The problem lived in the share of cost that no addition could reach, and that share grows with every mile added to the route.

The repetition also disciplines how each new attempt should be read. A launch announcement will name the savings first, because savings are the part of the story a founder controls: a denser cabin, a cheaper airport, a direct sales channel, a younger fleet. The structural reading reverses the order and asks about the parts no founder controls: the fuel a crossing burns, the rest a crew requires, the rent a widebody aircraft commands, and the winter demand on a leisure route. Where those fixed elements leave a margin, an entrant has a business to build. Where those elements consume the fare before the controllable savings begin, an entrant has a promotion with an airline attached, and the end of the story is a matter of financing and timing rather than of doubt. Holding the two parts apart, in that order, prevents both errors that surround the subject: the error of treating every failure as proof that cheap long flights are impossible in any form, and the error of treating every launch as proof that the arithmetic has changed.

The wider implication reaches beyond aviation, to any market where a challenger promises to remove the cost of a service: the promise holds only where the removable part is large, and distance, time and physics decide that share long before any manager takes a decision.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do low-cost long-haul airlines make money?
A low-cost long-haul airline makes money only when a very full aircraft spreads a largely fixed flight cost across enough seats sold at fares that still cover fuel, crew, leasing and airport charges. The model combines dense seating, a single aircraft type, fees for bags, meals and seat selection, and fares set low enough to fill seats that legacy carriers leave empty. Norwegian built its long-haul operation from 2013 around the fuel efficient Boeing 787 and high seat counts for exactly that reason. The margin stays thin because the saving comes from service and density rather than from the largest costs. One weak season, one fuel spike or a run of half empty departures can erase a year of careful savings, which is why profit depends on load factor and discipline more than on the headline fare.

### Q: Why are long-haul flights harder to discount than short flights?
Short flights give a budget carrier many costs it can cut: quick turnarounds, high daily aircraft use, simple catering and crews who return home the same day. Distance removes most of that room. A crossing of 3,500 miles burns far more fuel, needs augmented crews and rest periods, ties up an expensive widebody aircraft for a whole day and still pays navigation and airport charges that scale with weight and distance. Rigas Doganis set out the structure in The Airline Business in 2006: the share of cost open to a low-cost operator shrinks as sectors lengthen. Ryanair thrives on sectors near 1,000 miles where cuttable costs dominate. Across an ocean, the uncuttable costs dominate instead, so the discount that fills the aircraft can fall below the cost of operating it.

### Q: What is a good load factor for a long-haul airline?
Load factor measures the share of available seats filled by paying passengers, and long-haul economics push the useful figure high. A legacy carrier with premium cabins and belly cargo can profit at load factors in the low 80 percent range because each seat earns more and freight shares the flight cost. A low-cost carrier selling cheap economy seats needs figures nearer 90 percent, sustained across the whole season rather than on peak weekends alone. Peter Morrell examined the model in a 2008 study and stressed how little slack the arithmetic leaves. A single departure at 70 percent can consume the surplus from several full ones, because fuel, crew and leasing charges barely change with empty seats. Consistency matters more than a record month: a carrier that fills 92 percent in August and 68 percent in February averages into loss.

### Q: Why did Laker Airways fail?
Laker Airways launched its Skytrain service between London and New York in 1977, selling walk-on fares far below the regulated prices of the flag carriers, and the service proved that unmet demand for cheap Atlantic travel was real. The airline collapsed in 1982 under a combination of heavy debt taken on to expand its fleet, a recession that cut traffic, a strong dollar that raised its dollar denominated costs against sterling revenue, and aggressive fare matching by larger rivals with deeper reserves. Freddie Laker had built the model on high utilisation and low overheads, yet the balance sheet carried fixed obligations that empty winter seats could not service. The failure established the pattern repeated by later entrants: the fare idea worked, the aircraft filled in season, and the financing broke when revenue fell faster than costs could follow.

### Q: Why did Norwegian stop flying long-haul routes?
Norwegian expanded onto long-haul routes from 2013 under Bjorn Kjos, ordering Boeing 787 aircraft and opening bases across Europe and North America at a pace that built a large network before the operation matured. Low fares filled aircraft, but the company accumulated heavy debt, faced engine problems that grounded part of the 787 fleet, and carried losses that left little reserve when traffic collapsed during the pandemic period. In its 2021 restructuring Norwegian ended long-haul flying entirely, returned widebody aircraft to lessors and refocused on short-haul routes in the Nordic region, where turnarounds are fast and the low-cost model holds. The decision was an admission about arithmetic as much as finance: short sectors let the same management, fleet discipline and cost base produce margins that intercontinental sectors never sustained long enough to fund the debt they required.

### Q: How much of an airline ticket price is fuel?
Fuel typically represents roughly a quarter to a third of a long-haul carrier's operating cost, and the ticket price has to recover that share along with crew, aircraft ownership, maintenance and airport charges. The proportion moves with the oil price, the age and efficiency of the fleet and the length of the route, since longer sectors burn more fuel per seat even in efficient aircraft such as the Boeing 787. A legacy carrier and a budget carrier buy jet fuel in the same markets at similar prices, so fuel is the cost the low-cost model can least influence. Hedging can delay a price rise for months, and efficient engines can trim burn by a meaningful margin, but neither changes the underlying share. When oil prices climb, roughly a quarter to a third of the cost base climbs with them, and fares must follow or losses widen.

### Q: What is ancillary revenue on a long-haul flight?
Ancillary revenue is everything a passenger pays beyond the base fare: checked bags, seat selection, meals and drinks, priority boarding, extra legroom seats, onboard sales and change fees. On a long flight the opportunity grows because a passenger spending 8 hours aboard will pay for food, comfort and entertainment that a 1 hour passenger skips. AirAsia X, founded in 2007, built its model around a low headline fare with meals, baggage and seat assignments sold separately, turning the cabin into a sequence of small transactions. Ancillaries matter because they carry high margins: a meal sold for 15 dollars costs far less to provide, and a bag fee involves almost no extra fuel. They cannot close the whole gap, however. Even strong ancillary income per passenger remains small beside fuel and leasing costs on an intercontinental sector.

### Q: Why do budget airlines avoid premium cabins?
A premium cabin earns high revenue per seat but consumes the floor space of several economy seats, needs separate service, heavier seats, dedicated crew attention and sales channels aimed at business travellers who book late and demand frequency. Budget carriers avoid that complexity because their model depends on one dense cabin, fast servicing and a single simple product. The trade carries a cost: on long routes, premium passengers often supply a disproportionate share of a legacy carrier's revenue, cross-subsidising its economy cabin and letting it match low fares selectively. A budget widebody such as the Airbus A330 aircraft flown by AirAsia X in dense layouts gives up that revenue entirely. Every seat must then pay its own way from economy fares and ancillary sales, which is why the low-cost long-haul aircraft needs both higher density and higher load factors than its legacy rival.

### Q: How does seat density affect passenger comfort and cost?
Seat density spreads the fixed cost of a flight across more passengers, so each added row lowers the cost per seat even though total flight cost barely changes. A Boeing 787 configured with 344 seats by Norse Atlantic divides fuel, crew and leasing charges far more ways than the same aircraft type flown with 250 seats by a legacy carrier. The price of density is space: seat pitch shrinks toward 29 or 30 inches, aisles narrow, and nine seats across replace eight in some widebody layouts, which passengers notice sharply on sectors of 8 to 11 hours. Comfort limits therefore set a ceiling on the saving. Beyond a certain density, passengers defect to rivals, demand compensation in lower fares, or avoid repeat bookings, and the carrier trades away the repeat leisure demand its network depends on for a one-time gain in seat count.

### Q: What happens to passengers when a long-haul airline collapses?
When a long-haul airline collapses, its flights stop, and passengers holding tickets for later departures become creditors of an insolvent company rather than customers with a guaranteed journey home. Passengers who paid by credit card often recover the fare through chargeback schemes run by card networks, and passengers who bought a package holiday through a bonded operator gain repatriation and refund protection under schemes such as ATOL in the United Kingdom. Travellers who booked flight-only tickets directly, as many WOW air passengers did when that carrier ceased operations in March 2019, generally had to buy replacement tickets at walk-up prices and pursue refunds through the administration process, often recovering little. Governments sometimes coordinate rescue fares with rival airlines, which offer discounted seats to stranded travellers, but no general legal duty requires a competitor to carry passengers of a failed carrier free of charge.

### Q: Why do low-cost airlines lease aircraft instead of buying them?
Leasing converts a huge purchase price into a monthly payment, preserves cash, and lets a young carrier grow without raising the full capital cost of a widebody fleet. A new Boeing 787 carries a list price well above 200 million dollars, while an operating lease spreads that burden and returns the residual value risk to the lessor. Leasing also speeds entry: aircraft can arrive faster than a purchase programme financed order by order. The weakness appears in downturns, because lease payments continue through empty winter months and weak seasons, and a carrier cannot sell an aircraft it does not own to raise cash. Norwegian returned its leased 787 fleet during its 2021 restructuring only through negotiation with lessors inside a formal insolvency process. Leasing lowers the barrier to starting a long-haul airline while raising the fixed monthly bill that its fares must always cover.

### Q: How does belly cargo help legacy airlines compete?
Widebody aircraft carry freight in the hold beneath the passenger cabin, and on dense trade routes that belly cargo earns revenue that shares the cost of the flight. A legacy carrier flying between major hubs can fill its hold with time sensitive freight, mail and perishables sold through an established cargo sales network, sometimes covering a substantial share of trip cost before a single passenger boards. That income lets the legacy carrier price economy seats aggressively on the same route, because the seat does not need to carry the full cost alone. A low-cost long-haul carrier usually lacks freight sales contracts, handling arrangements and the schedule reliability forwarders demand, and point to point leisure routes generate weaker freight demand than hub routes anyway. WOW air and other leisure focused entrants therefore fought rivals whose aircraft were partly paid for by a hold they could not fill as profitably.

### Q: What is the difference between a low-cost carrier and a legacy carrier?
A legacy carrier descends from the regulated era of national flag airlines and builds its model on hub networks, connecting passengers, multiple cabin classes, loyalty programmes, alliances and cargo. British Airways and Lufthansa sell frequency, connections and premium service as much as the seat itself. A low-cost carrier strips that structure away: one cabin class or a simple version of it, point to point routes, fees for extras, direct online sales and a single aircraft family to cut training and maintenance cost. Southwest Airlines proved the low-cost formula on domestic routes from the 1970s onward, and Ryanair refined it in Europe. The distinction blurs at the edges, since legacy groups launch budget brands and budget carriers add connections, but the cost base differs in kind: the legacy model buys revenue richness with complexity, and the low-cost model buys cheapness with simplicity.

### Q: Why do cheap transatlantic flights keep appearing and disappearing?
Cheap transatlantic flights recur because the demand is genuine and each generation of aircraft promises to change the economics: Freddie Laker proved the market in 1977, People Express crossed from 1983, and Norwegian, WOW air, Primera Air and Norse Atlantic each followed with newer, more efficient aircraft and the same basic promise. Entry looks attractive in strong summers, when aircraft fill at fares that cover costs and growth hides weak winter performance. Disappearance follows a repeated sequence: expansion funded by debt or lease commitments, a fuel rise or demand shock, cash reserves too thin to survive the low season, and then a sudden stop, as with Primera Air in October 2018 and WOW air in March 2019. Deregulation under the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 keeps entry legally open, so new founders keep testing whether discipline or technology has finally moved the underlying arithmetic.

### Q: How do exchange rates affect long-haul airline costs?
A long-haul airline earns revenue in the currencies of both ends of its routes but pays many of its largest bills in United States dollars: jet fuel is priced in dollars, aircraft leases are usually denominated in dollars, and much maintenance and insurance pricing follows the dollar as well. When a carrier's home currency weakens, its dollar costs rise in local terms while a large share of ticket revenue stays fixed in the weaker currency, squeezing margin without any change in operations. Laker Airways suffered exactly that mismatch in the early 1980s, borrowing and paying in dollars while earning much of its revenue in sterling. Hedging currency exposure for a season or a year can soften the effect, but a small carrier with thin reserves pays more for protection and can rarely hedge its whole cost base, so exchange rate swings remain a structural risk of intercontinental flying.

### Q: Can a low-cost airline survive on leisure routes alone?
Leisure routes offer the strongest case for the model: demand is price sensitive, passengers book ahead, accept secondary airports and travel with bags and extras that generate ancillary income. Dense holiday routes to Florida, the Caribbean or Southeast Asia can fill large aircraft at high load factors for much of the year. The weakness is rhythm. Leisure demand peaks in school holidays and summer, collapses in midweek winter periods, and rarely includes the business travellers who pay high late booking fares that smooth a legacy carrier's week. AirAsia X built its network on such routes from 2007 and still passed through repeated restructurings. Survival on leisure alone demands conservative growth, strong cash reserves built in peak season, and routes with counter seasonal demand in both hemispheres. Few carriers have held that discipline long enough, because growth is what attracts the capital that funds the fleet.

### Q: What is basic economy and how is it different from a budget airline?
Basic economy is a fare class sold by a legacy carrier, not a separate airline. The passenger flies on the same aircraft, with the same crew, safety system and network as every other economy passenger, but pays less in exchange for restrictions: no seat selection, limited changes, last boarding and sometimes reduced baggage allowance. United Airlines introduced its basic economy product in 2017 to match budget fares on contested routes without rebuilding its cost base. The difference matters because the legacy carrier keeps premium cabins, cargo revenue and connecting traffic on the same flight, so the discounted seat is a pricing decision rather than a survival condition. A standalone budget airline must cover the whole flight from its low fares. Basic economy therefore lets an incumbent defend price sensitive passengers selectively, route by route, while the budget rival has to win the entire aircraft economics on every departure.

### Q: Why do long-haul aircraft sit unused for part of the day?
Long-haul schedules are governed by time zones, airport curfews and the departure times passengers will accept. An aircraft leaving London in the morning reaches New York in the early afternoon local time, and its return departure that suits passengers leaves in the evening, so the aircraft waits on the ground between rotations. Night curfews at many European airports and slot rules at congested hubs narrow the options further. A short-haul aircraft at Ryanair can complete 6 or more sectors in a day with 25 minute turnarounds, while a widebody on intercontinental work may manage one return trip in 24 hours. Every idle hour still carries leasing and ownership cost, which is then divided across fewer flying hours and fewer seats sold. Scheduling tricks such as overnight flights in one direction recover some use, but geography and the clock impose a ceiling no operator has removed.

### Q: How do airport charges differ for long-haul flights?
Airport charges usually scale with aircraft weight and passenger count, so a widebody departure pays far more in landing and parking fees than a narrowbody departure, and long-haul passengers also generate higher terminal, security and border processing costs that airports recover through per passenger charges. Navigation charges add a second layer, since en route fees in many regions are calculated from distance flown and aircraft weight, which means a long sector accumulates charges a short sector never reaches. Low-cost carriers cut airport cost on short routes by choosing secondary airports with low fees and fast turnarounds, an option that narrows on long routes because passengers expect primary gateways and because fewer secondary fields can handle widebody aircraft and border formalities for 300 passengers. The result is that airport and navigation charges, a quarter or less of a short-haul saving opportunity, become another largely fixed burden across an ocean.

### Q: What would make low-cost long-haul flying sustainable?
Sustainability would require the gap between cuttable and uncuttable costs to close, and several changes could narrow it: markedly more fuel efficient aircraft that reduce the quarter to a third of operating cost that fuel represents, denser leisure demand spread across the year, secondary airports able to handle widebodies at low charges, and fleets financed conservatively enough to survive weak winters without emergency capital. Discipline matters as much as technology. A carrier growing slowly on a small set of thick routes, holding cash from peak season against the low season, and resisting the prestige of flag carrier networks would start from the strongest position the model allows. Norwegian, AirAsia X and Norse Atlantic each had part of that formula and lacked another part. The record since Laker Airways in 1977 suggests the model becomes durable only when an operator treats a thin margin as permanent and plans the whole business around surviving its worst season.
