The standard account of the 1960s food crisis in India begins with rain. Two monsoons failed in succession, in 1965 and again in 1966, and foodgrain production fell from 89.4 million tonnes in 1964-65 to 72.3 million tonnes in 1965-66, a collapse of about a fifth in a single harvest year. Ships carrying American wheat under the PL-480 programme crossed the Pacific through 1966 and 1967, the rupee was devalued in June 1966, and within three years the high-yielding varieties associated with C. Subramaniam and M.S. Swaminathan had begun the recovery that celebratory literature calls the Green Revolution. Every element of that account is factually grounded, and the account still misleads. The monsoon failure was the trigger of the crisis, not its substance. The substance was political: a famine in Bihar that the administrative machine handled late and badly, a negotiation in Washington that priced Indian policy autonomy in grain, a technocratic alliance that reorganised farm policy over the objections of an older Congress inheritance, and a general election in February 1967 in which voters across eight states removed the Congress from office. The claim this article defends is the brief's own: the 1960s food crisis was when the Nehruvian state's limits became politically visible, and the 1967 verdict was the legible consequence.

## The Harvest Numbers Behind the 1960s Food Crisis in India

The scale of the production collapse deserves precise statement, because the political argument depends on the size of the hole the state had to fill. The official production series, reproduced in the Economic Survey and in the Planning Commission's plan documents, records foodgrain output of 89.4 million tonnes in 1964-65, a record harvest that had briefly encouraged talk of approaching self-sufficiency. Output then fell to 72.3 million tonnes in 1965-66 and recovered only to about 74 million tonnes in 1966-67, when the drought repeated across Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh. Two consecutive harvests ran roughly fifteen million tonnes below the 1964-65 level, in a country whose population at the 1961 census count was approaching 440 million and growing by more than ten million people a year. The Third Five-Year Plan, which ran from 1961 to 1966, had targeted foodgrain production above 100 million tonnes by its final year. The gap between the plan target and the 1965-66 harvest approached thirty million tonnes, and no administrative effort could close a gap of that order from domestic stocks that did not exist.

Imports filled part of the gap, at volumes that redefined the meaning of dependence. India imported roughly ten million tonnes of foodgrains in 1966, the largest single-year import in the country's history to that point, and the overwhelming share arrived from the United States under PL-480, the Agricultural Trade Development and Assistance Act of 1954. Lester Brown, the United States Department of Agriculture economist whose memoranda circulated in Washington during 1965 and 1966, calculated that American shipments in 1966 amounted to about a fifth of the United States wheat crop, moved on some six hundred ship voyages. A state that had defined its economic sovereignty through planning, and whose founding generation had treated food dependence as a colonial inheritance to be abolished, was feeding its cities from another country's surplus disposal programme. The political charge of that fact ran through every scene of the crisis, and it explains why [the Nehruvian-era institutional fragility framework](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/) matters for reading the episode: the settlement's confidence rested on a state that claimed it could plan production, and the harvest figures of 1965-66 announced, in public, that the claim had failed at the most basic task a state performs.

### Why did Indian foodgrain production fall so sharply in 1965-66?

Production fell because the 1965 monsoon delivered rainfall well below the long-period average across the rain-fed tracts where most Indian grain grew, and the harvest dropped from 89.4 million tonnes in 1964-65 to 72.3 million tonnes in 1965-66. Irrigation covered under a fifth of the cropped area, so a failed monsoon translated almost directly into a failed harvest.

The deeper exposure was structural, and the drought merely revealed it. Indian agriculture in 1965 still depended on monsoon rainfall across more than four-fifths of its cropped area, fertiliser application per hectare stood among the lowest in Asia, and the land reforms of the 1950s had redistributed little operational land outside a handful of states. Francine Frankel, in India's Political Economy 1947-2004 (1978, updated 2005), documents how the Nehruvian strategy had concentrated investment and administrative attention on heavy industry during the Second and Third Plans, treating agriculture as a sector that institutional reform and community development would lift without equivalent capital. The Community Development Programme, launched in 1952 with village-level workers across the country, had diffused its effort across every rural objective at once and raised yields only marginally. Atul Kohli, in State-Directed Development (2004), reads the same record as the predictable output of a centralised development model whose coherence at the commanding heights coexisted with thin delivery capacity in the districts. When the rains failed, there was no buffer: buffer stocks were small, the public distribution system covered mainly the larger cities, and the state governments that constitutionally owned agriculture as a subject possessed neither the grain nor the money to improvise a substitute harvest.

## The Road into Scarcity: How the Settlement Managed Food Before 1965

Dependence did not begin with the drought. India had imported American grain under PL-480 since the first agreement of 1956, signed when the Second Five-Year Plan's foreign exchange appetite collided with a mediocre harvest, and the four-year agreement of May 1960 committed the United States to supply sixteen million tonnes of foodgrains, a volume that already made the programme a structural feature of Indian food management rather than an emergency device. The political class of the early 1960s understood the exposure and spoke about it in the language of a deadline: self-sufficiency by the end of the Third Plan was the stated objective of the food ministry under S.K. Patil and of the Planning Commission's perspective documents. The instruments chosen to reach the deadline reveal the settlement's instincts. The Intensive Agricultural District Programme, launched in 1960 with Ford Foundation encouragement and modelled on the package approach of concentrating fertiliser, credit and extension in selected districts, anticipated the later high-yielding strategy in miniature, and its modest results showed both the promise of concentration and the cost of applying it inside an administrative machine built for uniform thin coverage.

The deeper difficulty sat in the plan's own hierarchy of purposes. The Mahalanobis strategy that governed the Second and Third Plans treated agriculture's task as releasing food and labour for industrialisation at stable prices, and it rated investment in heavy industry as the growth engine whose returns would eventually mechanise the farm. What the strategy never built was a shock absorber. Buffer stocking remained rudimentary, with the union government's stocks rarely covering more than a few weeks of public distribution offtake. Food zones, drawn to restrict the movement of grain between states and regions in the name of procurement and equity, fragmented the national market into compartments whose prices diverged violently whenever one compartment's harvest failed. State governments guarded their zones and their procurement levies jealously, and the union food ministry's conferences with chief ministers through the early 1960s record a recurring argument about movement restrictions that the crisis of 1966 would settle by catastrophe rather than by agreement.

Two wars then consumed the margin that remained. The 1962 war with China raised defence outlays and produced the emergency financial measures of 1963, and the 1965 war with Pakistan brought the suspension of American and consortium aid at the precise moment the first drought harvest was being counted. Lal Bahadur Shastri's government, in office from June 1964, had already shifted the rhetorical balance toward the farm with the slogan Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan, and Shastri appointed C. Subramaniam to food and agriculture in 1964 with a mandate to prepare an agricultural price and production policy. The Agricultural Prices Commission and the Food Corporation of India both date from January 1965, products of that mandate. The settlement, in other words, had begun correcting its farm neglect a full season before the monsoon failed, and the correction had produced instruments but not yet grain. Timing is a political fact of the first order: the crisis struck in the interval between the diagnosis and the remedy, when the state could describe exactly what it intended to do and could not yet do it.

Shastri's own food management in 1965 deserves record, because the crisis's first chapter belongs to his government and not to his successor's. Facing the failed 1965 harvest and the aid suspension after the September war, Shastri appealed to households to miss a meal a week, the appeal remembered as the Monday fast campaign, and his government tightened rationing in the cities while negotiating the resumption of PL-480 shipments that Washington had paused during the fighting. The appeal's moral register, a prime minister asking families to eat less so that the nation could eat, marked how far the situation had moved from the plan documents' arithmetic of targets. Shastri died at Tashkent on 11 January 1966, hours after signing the declaration that ended the war's formal phase, and the food emergency passed to Indira Gandhi together with the premiership. She inherited the pipeline, the prices and the drought in a single portfolio, and her government's first parliamentary test, the Lok Sabha food debates of January and February 1966, took place before she had appointed a single official of her own choosing to the food ministry she inherited from Shastri's cabinet, Subramaniam included.

## Scene One: Bihar, Where Scarcity Became Famine

The crisis acquired a name and a geography in Bihar. The drought of 1966 struck a state of some 46 million people at the 1961 census, among the poorest in the union, where the kharif crop withered in district after district across the central and southern plateau and the plains north of the Ganga. Bihar's own foodgrain harvest fell sharply across the two drought years, and by the autumn of 1966 the Bihar administration of Chief Minister K.B. Sahay confronted districts in which the landless labourers who formed the bottom of the rural order had neither wages nor grain. The Times of India and the Statesman carried dated reporting through the winter of 1966-67 from Gaya, Palamau, Hazaribagh and Munger describing villages where families had sold cattle, utensils and roofing material, and where migration toward the coalfields and toward Calcutta had begun. The reporting matters as evidence because it fixed the famine in the public record while official language still preferred the vocabulary of scarcity and drought.

The mortality figures require the care the stance of this article demands. The brief for this article speaks of mortality in the hundreds of thousands, and that magnitude circulates in retrospective accounts of the Bihar famine. The officially recorded figure is far smaller: government records counted 2,353 deaths from starvation across the drought-affected states, a figure that relief workers and journalists on the ground disputed at the time and that demographic historians have continued to contest, since deaths from diseases that malnutrition carries with it were recorded under other heads. What is not contested is the destitution: hundreds of thousands of people in Bihar survived 1966-67 on relief kitchens, gratuitous relief and wage work on public works, and the state's own famine declaration, issued only in 1967 after the Sahay ministry had fallen, conceded that conditions in the worst districts met the threshold of famine under the Bihar Famine Code. Paul R. Brass, in "The Political Uses of Crisis: The Bihar Famine of 1966-1967", published in the Journal of Asian Studies, showed how the declaration itself became a political contest, with politicians at each level choosing the word famine or the word scarcity according to the aid and the blame each word carried. The gap between the lived emergency and the administrative vocabulary is the first piece of political substance the agricultural framing hides.

### Why did Bihar suffer more severely than other drought-hit states in 1966-67?

Bihar suffered most because the drought struck a rural order with the thinnest reserves in India: a landless labour force paid in kind, low irrigation cover outside the canal colonies, weak rural credit after two poor harvests, and a state administration whose relief machinery activated months after the crop failure was visible in district reports.

The centre-state machinery then failed in sequence, and the sequence is documented. Agriculture and food sat in the State List of the Constitution, while the money, the imports and the buffer stocks sat with the union government in New Delhi, a division that [the federal design of the Indian Constitution](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) had drawn for normal times and that an emergency exposed as a design for delay. The Bihar ministry of K.B. Sahay, facing a general election due in February 1967, hesitated through the autumn of 1966 to proclaim famine conditions, since proclamation admitted failure; the union government of Indira Gandhi, in office from 24 January 1966 after Lal Bahadur Shastri's death at Tashkent on 11 January 1966, pressed Bihar for sharper reporting while rationing releases against competing claims from Maharashtra, Gujarat, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh. Relief works opened unevenly, test works meant to screen the genuinely destitute screened out the weakest instead in several districts Brass examined, and the voluntary effort organised through the Bihar Relief Committee under Jayaprakash Narayan's chairmanship moved grain and money faster in some blocks than the official channel beside it. A parallel relief state run by a man who held no office was a standing rebuke to the official one, and voters in Bihar could see both.

Why hunger concentrated where it did is a question about Bihar's rural structure, and the structure explains the famine's social map. The households that slid first into destitution were the landless and near-landless labourers, a vast class in central and south Bihar paid substantially in kind at harvest and dependent on employment that a failed crop extinguished at the moment food prices peaked. Sharecroppers working under insecure batai arrangements stood one rung above them and fell nearly as fast, their share of a failed harvest amounting to nothing while their obligations to the landholder survived. Small holders with a few acres and no irrigation sold assets in sequence, cattle first, then brass and cloth, then in the famine's worst pockets the roof itself, a sequence the relief literature from Gaya and Palamau records with terrible regularity. Rural credit, which in a normal scarcity year carried such households to the next harvest, contracted exactly when it was needed, since the moneylender's grain was also the village's reserve and its price rose with everyone else's desperation. Amartya Sen's entitlement vocabulary, developed partly from cases of this kind, names the mechanism precisely: the famine's victims did not live where food was absent from the earth in any simple sense, they lived where their command over food, through wages, shares and credit, had collapsed together. Bihar's land relations, among the least reformed in India after the zamindari abolition of the 1950s transferred title without disturbing the operational hierarchy beneath it, had pre-drawn the map of who would hunger when the rain failed. The drought was weather. The vulnerability was legislation's unfinished business, and the opposition of 1966-67 said so in the Assembly and on the stump.

The Bihar administration's hesitation had a bureaucratic anatomy worth dissecting, because the delay was produced by a machine working as designed rather than by individual failure alone. Under the Famine Code tradition that Bihar inherited, a district officer's declaration process ran through crop inspections, test relief works and reports on migration and crime, each stage generating the paper that authorised the next release of funds. The design assumed a slow emergency measured in seasons and a treasury entitled to proof. The drought of 1966 moved at the speed of a failed crop and an empty grain basket, and the proof accumulated in the bodies of the destitute before it accumulated in the files. Relief officers on the ground, as the IARI field volumes record, improvised around the code where conscience or local pressure demanded, opening works on verbal sanction and feeding kitchens ahead of authorisation, and the improvisation itself became evidence at the political level: a system that functions only when its officers break it has disclosed its design fault more completely than any commission could. The union government's Famine Committee machinery in New Delhi reviewed state reports against import availability, which tied the tempo of Bihar's relief to the tempo of Washington's shipping decisions three levels up the chain, and the citizen at the end of the chain experienced the whole architecture as a single fact, the lateness of help.

Mortality argument in Bihar ran along a parallel track that the political reading must handle without flinching. The state government's spokesmen through early 1967 maintained that starvation deaths were not occurring, pointing to the relief works and the absence of the mass mortality that had marked the Bengal famine of 1943, in which some three million people died within living memory of every senior administrator of 1966. The comparison with 1943 did real work in the official defence, and it contains a truth the article concedes: the relief effort of 1966-67, for all its lateness, prevented a mortality catastrophe of the colonial pattern, and the prevention owed something to democratic politics itself, since an elected ministry facing voters and a free press could not let districts starve in silence as a colonial administration had done in Bengal. The concession strengthens rather than weakens the political reading. Precisely because the democratic state could not permit 1943, its lesser failures acquired political price tags: every shortcoming that stopped short of catastrophe still presented itself to voters as a broken promise rather than as fate. The opposition's achievement in 1966-67 was to hold the government to the republic's own standard, and the government's defence, that matters might have been worse under the Raj, confessed how far the standard had slipped from the one the founding generation had set at Avadi and in the plan documents.

The second drought year widened the crisis's map beyond Bihar and changed its political chemistry. The monsoon of 1966 failed across a broad arc that took in eastern Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Gujarat and parts of Maharashtra and Orissa, so that by the winter of 1966-67 the union government faced scarcity claims from a majority of states at once, each pressing against the same import pipeline and the same Food Corporation stocks. The competition among states for the centre's grain is a documented feature of the season's food ministry conferences, and it corroded the solidarity the Congress system had supplied when one party governed at both levels: Congress chief ministers lobbied against Congress ministers in New Delhi with a freedom that prefigured the defections of the following year. Scarcity in one state is a relief problem. Scarcity in ten states at once, in an election season, is a crisis of the union's design, and the administrative record of 1966-67 shows the centre behaving accordingly, rationing releases by political weight as much as by measured need and drawing from opposition-ruled or opposition-leaning regions the accusation of partisan distribution that would echo through the campaign. The accusation's fairness varied by state and by month, and the article does not adopt it wholesale; its political effect, however, did not depend on its fairness. It depended on its plausibility to households whose ration had thinned while a neighbouring district's had not, and plausibility of that kind is the currency in which verdicts like February 1967 are denominated.

## The Relief State on Trial in Bihar

The field evidence from Bihar survives in an under-cited archive that the celebratory Green Revolution literature largely passed over. During 1966 and 1967, teams associated with the Indian Agricultural Research Institute under M.S. Swaminathan's direction compiled field-survey volumes on conditions in the drought districts, recording crop loss, water scarcity, migration and the working of relief in village-level detail; the volumes rest in the IARI Library in New Delhi. Read beside the correspondence columns of the Statesman from the winter of 1966-67, the surveys describe a state whose information moved upward slowly and whose instructions moved downward slower: district officers reported scarcity in the language the Famine Code required before funds could flow, Patna weighed the electoral cost of the famine word, and New Delhi weighed Bihar's claims against a national import pipeline that Washington controlled. The three levels of the federation each behaved rationally within their incentives, and the sum of the behaviour was a relief effort that arrived after the hunger.

The episode also fixed a durable political vocabulary. The phrase ship-to-mouth, used in the press and in parliamentary exchanges of 1966 to describe an economy living on grain that ships delivered month to month, condensed the humiliation into two words. Members across parties used the Lok Sabha debates of January and February 1966 on the food situation, held within weeks of Indira Gandhi taking office, to arraign two decades of farm policy, and the debates record opposition speakers returning repeatedly to the contrast between the Avadi promise of a socialist pattern and the ration queue. That contrast had been available since 1955 as rhetoric; the debates of early 1966 gave it a harvest figure and a queue of witnesses. Readers tracing how the promise and the performance diverged will find the anatomy in [the Avadi rhetoric-practice gap](/2019/02/05/avadi-resolution-socialist-pattern-1955/), and the food debates show the gap being read aloud in the chamber by members who had voted for the promise.

The Bihar archive also poses a practical problem that relief administrators of 1966 would recognise in a different key. The record of the famine survives scattered across famine code returns, survey volumes, ration statistics and committee accounts, in formats that defeat quick comparison, and anyone assembling the district evidence into a single working file faces the same fragmentation the relief officers faced in paper. Researchers handling such mixed bundles of returns and reports can open them through [an online viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which is a small modern convenience against a documentary record whose disorder is itself part of the evidence: a state that filed its hunger in incompatible formats had not yet decided what the hunger meant.

The archival lesson generalises beyond Bihar. A centralised planning state earns legitimacy from outcomes it claims to command, and it loses legitimacy at the same speed when outcomes it claimed to command collapse in public view. The Nehruvian state had not promised merely growth; the plan documents had promised food security as the foundation of the development project, and the First Plan's relative success in agriculture had been the settlement's early proof. By 1966 the proof had inverted. The state could still mobilise, as the relief operation eventually showed at scale, but it mobilised late, under foreign supply, and through improvisation that included voluntary committees outside its own chain of command. Each qualification attached itself to the verdict voters delivered in February 1967.

The human detail of the Bihar relief operation survives in sources that sit awkwardly beside the aggregate statistics, and the awkwardness is instructive. The Bihar Relief Committee's own reports, and the account by Graham Chapman in Tiger on a Rein, his 1969 report on the famine, describe relief kitchens serving thin gruel to queues that formed before dawn in Gaya district, school feeding programmes that doubled as attendance registers for tracing missing children, and deep tubewell drilling campaigns that raced the summer of 1967 to put drinking water into villages whose wells had failed. Peace Corps volunteers and Indian university students on national service camps worked inside the voluntary effort, which meant that the most visible face of relief in some blocks belonged to organisations with no place in the Constitution's scheme of food administration. The official machine, by contrast, spoke the language of the scarcity manuals inherited from the Famine Codes of the 1880s, with their tests of distress designed for a colonial state determined to spend as little as scarcity allowed. A republic that had promised its citizens a welfare state was administering hunger through instruments drafted to protect a treasury, and the anachronism was not lost on the journalists who filed from Patna through the winter.

Bihar's politics carried the famine into the state election with unusual directness. The Sahay ministry's management became the opposition's central exhibit, and the non-Congress parties that combined in the United Front campaigned on the relief record village by village. Jayaprakash Narayan, whose committee had fed people the ministry had failed to reach, held a moral authority in the state that no party could match, and his refusal to convert that authority into candidacy left it available as a judgement hovering over the contest. When the United Front ministry under Mahamaya Prasad Sinha took office in March 1967 and issued the famine declaration the Congress had withheld, the sequence completed itself in public: the word famine arrived in Bihar with the change of government, confirming Brass's argument that the naming of the crisis had been a political act all along. The declaration unlocked fuller relief obligations and central assistance, which is to say that the administrative category followed the electoral verdict rather than preceding it. For a citizen of Gaya district who had queued at a relief kitchen in December 1966, the lesson required no theory to decode. The state had known, and had waited to say so until the voters had spoken.

## Scene Two: PL-480 and the Price of American Grain

The second scene ran through Washington, and its documentary trail sits in the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library in New Delhi: the 1966 PL-480 agreement texts and the correspondence of L.K. Jha, the Prime Minister's principal secretary, and B.K. Nehru, India's ambassador to the United States, who conducted the negotiation on the Indian side. The counterpart across the table was the administration of Lyndon B. Johnson, with Secretary of Agriculture Orville Freeman managing the food aid programme and Chester Bowles, the American ambassador in New Delhi, pressing Washington to treat Indian food security as a strategic asset. The negotiation's Indian objective was simple to state and hard to win: firm, multi-year commitments of American grain at volumes near ten million tonnes a year, so that rationing, relief and price policy in India could plan against a known supply. The American objective was equally legible. Johnson, angered by Indian criticism of the Vietnam War and determined to extract policy change as the price of grain, refused long commitments and released shipments in short tranches, a method his own officials called the short tether.

The short tether converted food into leverage with a precision that Indian negotiators recorded with bitterness in the correspondence the NMML preserves. Month to month through 1966, the Indian embassy and the Prime Minister's secretariat calculated shipping schedules against port capacity at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Visakhapatnam, against rail movement into the deficit states, and against the ration commitments of the fair price shop network, which the union government expanded past twenty thousand additional shops during the crisis years. A delayed tranche did not register in Washington as a statistic; it registered in Patna and Lucknow as empty shops. B.K. Nehru's memoir, Nice Guys Finish Second (1997), recounts the negotiation from inside the embassy and records the Indian reading plainly: the administration in Washington intended the dependence itself to discipline Indian policy, on Vietnam, on foreign investment, and above all on agriculture, where American officials demanded a reordering of Indian priorities toward fertiliser, irrigation, seed and price incentives as the stated condition of continued supply. The demand arrived dressed as technical advice, and the Indian government of Indira Gandhi substantially accepted it, because the alternative to acceptance was hunger in an election year.

The mechanics of the programme deepened the dependence they financed, and the mechanics belong in the political account. PL-480 Title I shipments were sold for rupees deposited in American-controlled counterpart accounts in India, funds whose uses required agreement with the United States embassy, so the grain arrived trailing a financial presence inside the Indian budget itself. By the mid-1960s the accumulated counterpart balances were large enough to figure in monetary argument in New Delhi, and Indian economists disputed whether the rupee deposits fuelled the inflation the food shortage had started. The programme's Title II grants fed the relief and school feeding operations directly, which meant American surplus stood behind the voluntary effort in Bihar as well as behind the ration shops. Dependence of this texture cannot be summarised as a trade flow. It was an institutional interpenetration, negotiated file by file, and the negotiators on both sides understood that each agreement renewed a relationship neither could exit without cost. Johnson's innovation in 1966 was to make the renewal monthly and conditional, converting a settled interpenetration into an open lever, and the Indian files at the NMML record the exact dates on which the lever was pulled.

The war of September 1965 had already rehearsed the lever's use. Washington suspended aid to both combatants when India and Pakistan went to war, and the suspension caught the food pipeline in the first drought season, teaching New Delhi before Johnson's tether tightened that the supply line ran through American strategic judgement and could be closed by it. The resumption after the Tashkent declaration of January 1966 came with the reform agenda attached, and the sequence, suspension, resumption, conditions, taught Indian negotiators the full grammar of the relationship in a single year. India's food security establishment carried the lesson into doctrine: the buffer stocking norms, the diversification of supply sources and the eventual insistence on domestic procurement surpluses that mark later food policy all argue, in administrative language, that 1966 must not recur. When a state's granary policy is written as a refusal to repeat a diplomatic humiliation, the agricultural and the political have merged beyond separation, and the historian's task is simply to read the merger in the documents that record it.

### What did Lyndon Johnson's short tether policy mean for India in 1966?

The short tether meant that the Johnson administration approved PL-480 grain shipments to India in short, month-to-month tranches instead of the multi-year commitment New Delhi requested. Each release required fresh Indian assurances on farm policy, which kept India's ration system, relief planning and foreign policy under continuous American pressure through 1966 and into 1967.

The autonomy cost deserves exact statement, because [the foreign-policy autonomy constraints](/2019/03/04/nehru-foreign-policy-domestic-politics/) that the Nehru years had treated as a founding achievement were the currency in which the grain was paid for. Non-alignment had promised that India would take aid from both blocs and instructions from neither. In 1966 the promise met its material limit: a country importing a fifth of its foodgrain consumption from one supplier, on that supplier's monthly discretion, could criticise the supplier's war only within limits the supplier enforced. Indira Gandhi's public statements on Vietnam grew measurably more careful during the months of heaviest dependence, a shift American and Indian officials both registered, and the Indian vote and voice in international forums bent around the shipping schedule. The point is not that food aid purchased obedience in any crude transaction. The point is structural: the Nehruvian state's claim to autonomous judgement rested on a material base that two failed monsoons dissolved, and the dissolution was visible to every diplomat and editor watching the negotiation. Francine Frankel's political economy of the period treats the PL-480 relationship as the hinge on which Indian economic policy turned in 1966, and the correspondence supports her: the same files that schedule wheat also schedule policy.

Indira Gandhi carried the negotiation to Washington in person at the end of March 1966, in a visit whose choreography recorded the dependence as faithfully as any cable. Received by President Johnson on 28 March 1966, she obtained the resumption and expansion of food shipments and the release of aid held in suspense since the 1965 war, and the joint statements of the visit spoke the language of partnership in development. American reporting of the visit, and the Indian opposition's commentary on it, fastened on the same asymmetry from opposite directions: the Prime Minister of India had travelled to secure her country's food supply from a president who released it in tranches. Chester Bowles, whose diaries and dispatches from his second ambassadorship in New Delhi argue the American case for generous treatment, warned Washington that squeezing India on food would destroy the constituency for the very reforms Washington demanded. The warning identifies the negotiation's real structure. The United States wanted an India that reordered its farm policy toward American advice and remained a functioning democracy in Asia; the short tether pursued the first objective by methods that corroded the second, since every delayed ship weakened the elected government the reforms were supposed to strengthen. Orville Freeman's own journeys to India in 1966, inspecting drought districts and pressing the new agricultural strategy, embodied the fusion of relief and direction: the agriculture secretary of a foreign power touring famine districts as an inspecting authority was a sight Indian newspapers recorded without needing to editorialise.

Inside India, the negotiation's terms leaked steadily into public argument through 1966, and the leaks did political work no communiqué could undo. The press reported the American demand for a fertiliser policy open to foreign investment, the World Bank's conditions for consortium aid, and the embassy's scrutiny of Indian statements on Vietnam, and each report converted a technical file into a sovereignty story. The union government's difficulty was not that the conditions were unreasonable in substance; several, on fertiliser, prices and seed, matched what Subramaniam already intended. The difficulty was sequence and authorship. A reform announced after an American demand read as obedience even when it repeated an Indian intention, and the government of 1966 lost control of the authorship of its own farm policy in the public mind. That loss is a measurable political fact with a long half-life: the suspicion that economic policy in New Delhi answered to Washington, planted in the devaluation season, structured opposition rhetoric for a generation and disciplined every later Indian negotiator who handled an aid or lending file.

## Devaluation in June 1966: The Concession Delhi Could Not Disguise

On 6 June 1966, the government of Indira Gandhi devalued the rupee, moving the exchange rate from 4.76 rupees to 7.50 rupees to the dollar, a devaluation of 36.5 percent in the value of the currency. The decision had been urged on India by the World Bank and the Aid India Consortium as a condition of resumed non-project assistance, which had been suspended during and after the 1965 war with Pakistan, and it travelled in the same negotiating season as the PL-480 assurances on farm policy. Finance Minister Sachin Chaudhuri carried the decision in cabinet, L.K. Jha carried it between New Delhi and Washington, and the government presented it domestically as a sovereign measure to promote exports and discipline imports. The presentation persuaded few. The Lok Sabha and the press treated the devaluation through the winter session of 1966 as the visible receipt for the invisible negotiation: the price, denominated in national prestige, of grain and aid. K. Hanumanthaiya and other Congress members joined opposition speakers in charging that the government had acted under foreign dictation, and the charge stuck to the ministry for the remainder of its term.

The economic returns disappointed the decision's defenders on their own terms, and the disappointment compounded the political cost. Exports did not surge, because the drought had cut the exportable surplus of the farm commodities India sold and because devaluation raised the rupee cost of the imports industry required; the export subsidy and import liberalisation measures that accompanied the decision were partially reversed within two years under domestic pressure. What endured was the precedent and the memory. Ashok Mitra, who served in the economic administration of the period and wrote about the devaluation's politics afterward, treated June 1966 as the moment the planning state's claim to chart an independent course lost its audience inside the country itself. For the argument of this article, the devaluation belongs inside the food crisis and not beside it: the chain from failed monsoon to import dependence to aid conditionality to currency concession is a single causal chain, and each link was forged in the same twelve months. An agricultural framing that ends with the Green Revolution's later tonnage cannot explain why a currency decision in June 1966 requires a famine in Bihar to be intelligible. A political framing explains it in one pass.

The devaluation also split the economic administration in ways that map the crisis's reach into the state's own house. Ashok Mehta, deputy chairman of the Planning Commission and the decision's principal internal advocate, defended the rate change as the unavoidable price of resuming aid and correcting an overvalued currency, and his defence stated the technocratic case at its strongest: the exchange rate was a policy instrument, not a national honour, and a government that refused to move it would sacrifice imports, fertiliser and growth to prestige. The counter-case, pressed inside the Congress parliamentary party and by the party's left, held that the decision's timing, announced under consortium pressure in a famine season, destroyed the distinction between instrument and honour in the public mind whatever the economics said. Both cases were honestly arguable, which is precisely why the episode damaged the government: a decision that required a seminar to defend was being judged in a ration queue. The Planning Commission itself emerged weakened, its Fourth Plan draft overtaken by the annual plan improvisation of 1966 to 1969 that administrators called the plan holiday, and a development state that paused its own planning for three years had conceded the food crisis's institutional victory in its central citadel.

## Prices, Rationing and the Urban Queue

Between the farm and the famine stood the price of grain, and the price mechanism transmitted the crisis to households that never saw a drought district. Food articles led the rise in wholesale prices through 1965 and 1966, and the cost of the coarse grains and pulses that formed the diet of the poor rose fastest where supply was thinnest. Statutory rationing covered the largest cities, where the fair price shop network issued fixed quantities at controlled rates, while smaller towns and the countryside depended on informal arrangements and the open market's mercy. The queue outside the ration shop became the urban form of the crisis, photographed and reported as relentlessly as the Bihar relief works, and it reached a constituency the Congress could least afford to alienate: salaried employees, teachers, clerks and industrial workers whose money wages the price rise cut month by month. Dearness allowance agitations and strikes over food prices ran through the industrial calendar of 1966, and the West Bengal food movement of February and March 1966, in which protests over rice supply met police firing and left demonstrators dead, announced that scarcity politics had acquired a street form in the cities two full seasons before the general election.

The food movement in West Bengal deserves its sentence in the chain of evidence, because it shows scarcity radicalising an organised left well before the verdict of 1967 reorganised the party map. The Left Front parties of the period campaigned on levy, procurement and price, charging the Congress ministry of Prafulla Chandra Sen with protecting hoarders while the ration deteriorated, and the agitation's suppression supplied the grievance on which the United Front fought the 1967 election in the state and won. A similar grammar operated wherever prices outran wages: the crisis translated macroeconomic failure into a household experience renewed at every purchase, which is the most efficient form of political education a democracy provides. The Congress entered the election season of 1966-67 defending not an abstraction called the economy but the price of rice in Calcutta, of wheat in Kanpur and of millet in the Deccan towns, and it defended them with the shipping schedule of a foreign aid programme in its hand.

Procurement told the same story from the market's side. The state governments' levy and purchase operations, on which the public distribution system depended for its domestic share, shrank as the harvest shrank, since farmers with little surplus sold into open markets at prices the controlled procurement rate could not match. The Food Corporation's godowns accordingly filled with imports rather than with Indian grain through 1966, and the ration system's dependence on the American pipeline became a physical arrangement visible in every port and railhead. The arrangement had a quiet constitutional consequence that administrators of the period registered: food, a state subject, was being supplied to the states by a union corporation out of foreign shipments negotiated by the union's diplomats, so the federal balance in the food sector tilted toward the centre for as long as the emergency lasted, and the tilt outlasted the emergency in the Corporation's permanent expansion. Deficit states that resented the centre's conditions in 1966 nonetheless queued for its grain, and the queue reordered centre-state bargaining in ways the formal Constitution nowhere describes. Political scientists of the following decade, studying the centralisation they associated with Indira Gandhi's later governments, sometimes dated the process from her decisions. The food files suggest an earlier and less voluntary beginning: centralisation by hunger, accepted by the states themselves because the alternative was an empty ration shop in an election year.

## Scene Three: Subramaniam, Swaminathan and the Technocratic Turn

The third scene is the one the standard account knows best, and it still yields political substance when read as a contest inside the state rather than as a laboratory triumph. C. Subramaniam, who took the food and agriculture portfolio in Lal Bahadur Shastri's cabinet in 1964 and held it under Indira Gandhi until 1967, arrived in the ministry as a politician with an administrator's reputation from his years in Madras and at the union steel and heavy industries portfolios. M.S. Swaminathan, leading the genetics and plant breeding work at the Indian Agricultural Research Institute in New Delhi, had since the early 1950s argued that India's yield problem was a problem of plant architecture and inputs, soluble by science rather than by exhortation. Their correspondence between 1964 and 1967, preserved in the IARI archives, documents an alliance being built deliberately: a minister who could carry cabinet decisions on fertiliser, credit and price, and a scientist who could specify what those decisions should buy. The alliance's wager was that dwarf wheat varieties bred by Norman Borlaug's programme in Mexico, tested at IARI from 1963 after Borlaug's visit that March, could multiply Indian wheat yields when combined with fertiliser and assured water, and that a price guarantee would persuade farmers to risk the new package.

The wager became policy under crisis pressure, and the dates show the pressure working. The government adopted the new agricultural strategy in 1965 and launched the High Yielding Varieties Programme from the kharif season of 1966, concentrating seed, fertiliser and credit in districts with assured irrigation rather than spreading them thinly across the country. Subramaniam announced a remunerative support price policy through the Agricultural Prices Commission, created in 1965, and the Food Corporation of India, created in January 1965, gave the union government a procurement and distribution instrument of its own. In 1966 India placed what was then among the largest seed purchases in agricultural history, importing Mexican dwarf wheat seed in bulk for the rabi sowing, and the wheat harvest of 1967-68 jumped to about 16.5 million tonnes from about 11 million tonnes in the drought years, lifting total foodgrain production to about 95 million tonnes. The numbers vindicated the alliance's agronomy completely, and the vindication is not in dispute in this article.

### How did the high-yielding varieties programme change Indian farm policy in 1966?

The programme replaced the older strategy of spreading extension effort evenly across all districts with a concentrated bet: dwarf seed, fertiliser, credit and a guaranteed support price were bundled into selected districts with assured irrigation, beginning with the kharif season of 1966. Production risk moved from the state's exhortation to the farmer's calculation, and procurement acquired a price instrument.

What the agronomy displaced is the political point. The Gandhian and early Nehruvian inheritance had imagined agricultural transformation arriving through the village community: cooperative farming, endorsed in the Congress resolution at Nagpur in January 1959, panchayat-led development, and the extension worker persuading the village to change together. The Subramaniam and Swaminathan strategy bypassed the village community as the unit of change and addressed the individual cultivator with a price and a packet of seed. Within the Congress, the displacement had named opponents and named defenders, and the argument ran along the party's oldest fault line between its cooperative rhetoric and its landlord and rich-peasant base in the districts. Ashutosh Varshney, in Democracy, Development, and the Countryside (1995), traces how the new strategy's success created a politically organised farm interest in the surplus regions, Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, whose demands on prices, inputs and procurement would discipline every later government. The technocratic alliance therefore did more than raise yields. It selected which countryside would prosper first, and in selecting, it wrote the electoral geography of the next generation into farm policy. [The Kamaraj Plan's organisational refresh failure](/2019/02/27/kamaraj-plan-1963-congress/) had already shown a Congress unable to renew its own machinery; the farm strategy of 1966 shows the same party governing around its machinery through a minister and a scientist, effective and unaccountable in equal measure.

The alliance had institutional midwives whose names belong in the record, because the technocratic turn travelled on foundation money and demonstration plots as well as on cabinet paper. The Rockefeller Foundation had worked in Indian agriculture since the 1950s, its officers in correspondence with the Indian Agricultural Research Institute on maize and sorghum before the wheat breakthrough, and the Ford Foundation financed extension and the intensive district experiments that rehearsed the package method. Borlaug's 1963 visit, arranged through these channels, inspected trial plots at Delhi, Ludhiana and Pantnagar and reported that the dwarf material could transform Indian wheat if seed multiplication and fertiliser supply kept pace. Swaminathan then organised the demonstration programme that carried the argument out of the institute: national demonstrations on farmers' own fields from 1964, in which small plots under the new package out-yielded the surrounding crop in full view of the cultivators who would have to risk the change. The demonstration plot was a political instrument disguised as agronomy. It persuaded without a ministry circular, and it created the constituency of adopting farmers whose results in the rabi of 1966-67 and the record wheat harvest of 1967-68 made the strategy irreversible before the party that owned it had finished arguing about its philosophy.

Subramaniam's part in the alliance is easily understated by accounts that cast him as the scientist's ministerial sponsor. His contribution was a theory of the farmer. Against the extension tradition that treated the cultivator as a conservative to be educated, Subramaniam argued in cabinet and in print that the Indian farmer was a calculating producer who responded to price and risk exactly as producers elsewhere did, and that policy failed when it asked him to adopt new methods at a loss. The support price, the fertiliser supply and the seed packet were one argument in three forms, and the argument quietly reversed the moral economy of the Avadi inheritance, in which the countryside figured as the object of the state's uplift. In the Subramaniam design the countryside figured as a market of decision-makers whose aggregated choices the state had to make profitable. The reversal, more than any single variety, is what later decades inherited: procurement politics, support price agitations and the organised farm lobbies of the surplus states all conduct their business inside the frame Subramaniam built in the famine years.

The harvest sequence that followed the programme's launch supplies the technocratic alliance's best evidence, and its geography supplies the political reading's. Wheat led the recovery, production rising from about 11 million tonnes in the drought years to about 16.5 million tonnes in 1967-68 and past 20 million tonnes by 1969-70, concentrated overwhelmingly in Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, where canal and tubewell irrigation let the dwarf varieties drink. Punjab's marketed surplus filled the Food Corporation's procurement godowns and the central pool, converting the state into the republic's granary and its farmers into a constituency with a national claim. Rice followed more slowly, its high-yielding varieties from the International Rice Research Institute at Los Baños in the Philippines proving fussier in Indian delta conditions, so the recovery's map stayed lopsided toward the wheat belt through the period this article covers. The lopsidedness mattered politically in both directions. In the surplus northwest it created the prosperous, organised, price-conscious peasantry whose later mobilisations, from the Bharatiya Kisan Union's campaigns to the siege politics of support price announcements, descend in a direct line from the settlement Subramaniam designed. In the deficit east, and above all in Bihar, it confirmed the experience of abandonment that the famine had taught, and the contrast between the granary and the famine state entered the rhetoric of regional grievance where it remained available to every later movement that charged New Delhi with governing for the surplus.

Swaminathan's own trajectory after 1967 illustrates how completely the alliance's frame captured the state. The geneticist moved from the institute's laboratories into the direction of Indian agricultural research itself, and the scientific establishment he built treated the crisis years as its founding charter: scarcity had proved that research was national security, a claim no finance ministry of the period seriously contested when budgets were drawn. Subramaniam, defeated in the 1967 election season's wreckage of Congress fortunes in Madras state politics and out of the food portfolio in the cabinet reshuffle that followed the verdict, carried the argument into later portfolios and into his writings, where the famine years appear as the education of a minister who learned that production answered to incentives. The two men's later honours, and the celebratory literature that grew around them, completed the agricultural reading's conquest of the episode's memory: statues and commemorations remember the seed, and forget the ship, the queue and the verdict. Recovering the political substance is not an act of ingratitude toward the recovery's authors. It is the restoration of the scene in which their achievement became necessary, and of the citizens whose hunger set the scene.

The package's economics carried a dependency of their own, and honesty about the recovery requires naming it. Dwarf varieties yielded their miracles only under heavy fertilisation, and India's fertiliser production in 1966 covered a fraction of the requirement the programme created, so the seed strategy multiplied fertiliser imports on the heels of the grain imports it was meant to end. The union government fought the period's sharpest industrial policy battle over this bottleneck, negotiating for large new fertiliser capacity with foreign consortia while the terms on pricing and distribution control became a sovereignty argument in miniature, replaying the PL-480 season's anxieties inside a factory file. Distribution of fertiliser was partially decontrolled in 1966 as part of the reform package, against the instincts of the control-minded administration, and the decision's presence in the same season as devaluation and the price announcements shows the package's coherence: seed, nutrient, water and price moved together or not at all. Irrigation set the strategy's hard frontier, since the dwarf plant's thirst confined the early programme to the canal commands and the tubewell tracts, and the minors and field channels of the older canal systems, starved of maintenance funds through the plan years, delivered water too unreliably for the package in district after district that the strategy had marked as promising. The Green Revolution's first map was therefore drawn as much by the public works balance sheet of the 1950s as by the genetics of the 1960s, one more route by which the settlement's earlier choices governed its crisis.

## Grain Against the States: Food Zones and the Federal Friction

The crisis also ran a stress test through the federal distribution of food power, and the results belong in the political ledger. Food zones, maintained to let the union government procure in surplus regions and ration in deficit ones, broke down under scarcity into competitive hoarding by state administrations. Surplus states resisted the movement of grain across their borders and pressed for higher procurement prices and levy concessions, while deficit states charged the centre with favouring the surplus regions that fed the cities. Chief ministers' conferences on food through 1965 and 1966, chaired by the union food minister, recorded the friction in formal session: Andhra Pradesh and Madras guarding their rice, Punjab bargaining over wheat procurement, West Bengal demanding imports against its urban ration. The union government held the import pipeline and the Food Corporation's stocks, which made the centre the crisis's quartermaster, yet the Constitution left agriculture and intrastate trade with the states, which made the centre a quartermaster without command of the territory. Subramaniam's successors inherited the same geometry, and the food crisis fixed it as a permanent feature: every later argument over support prices, inter-state movement and central procurement replays, in calmer weather, the quarrel the drought forced into the open.

The friction had a party dimension that sharpened the 1967 outcome. Because the Congress governed both the centre and most states through 1966, the quarrel over grain was a quarrel inside one party conducted across levels of government, and it taught state-level Congress leaders the habit of blaming New Delhi that they carried into opposition or defection after the verdict. Charan Singh's break in Uttar Pradesh, executed in April 1967, grew from soil that included his long advocacy of the cultivator against the centre's industrial and procurement priorities. The federal lesson of the crisis therefore outlasted the famine: food had demonstrated that the Indian union's economic constitution distributed responsibility in a pattern that scarcity would always find out, and no government after 1967 could treat the food file as a technical file again.

## The Cooperative Road Not Taken

The counterfactual disciplines the argument, and the article takes it seriously rather than as decoration. A cooperative-led transformation had institutional sponsors of real standing in 1964: the cooperative ministry tradition associated with Vaikunthbhai Mehta, the Sarvodaya network around Jayaprakash Narayan, and the Community Development bureaucracy with its village-level workers in more than five thousand blocks. What the cooperative road lacked in 1965 was time and a harvest. Cooperation reorganises incentives across seasons; famine operates across weeks. Subramaniam's circle argued, in the correspondence and in cabinet, that a state facing ten million tonnes of imports could not stake survival on institutional redesign whose yield effects lay years away, and the argument was correct on its own terms. The political reading adds what the correctness concealed. Choosing the technocratic road under emergency conditions ratified a hierarchy inside the Nehruvian inheritance, science over association, the expert over the panchayat, that the founding generation had left deliberately unresolved among [the directive principles' promise of a welfare state](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/) and the cooperative language of the Avadi settlement. Emergencies settle arguments that constitutions defer, and they settle them in favour of whoever can deliver by the next season. The food crisis settled this one.

The settlement carried a cost that arrived on a longer fuse, and Varshney's analysis names it. Because the high-yielding package required assured water and purchased inputs, its early map was the map of existing advantage: the canal colonies and tubewell districts of the northwest and the deltas of the south. Bihar, whose famine had forced the strategy's adoption, received the package late and thinly, since its irrigation cover and rural credit could not support the bundle. The Green Revolution's geography therefore reproduced, inside the recovery, the inequality the crisis had exposed between states. A political history that begins with Bihar's hunger and ends with Punjab's wheat surpluses has not changed the subject; the distance between the two is the subject, and it structured centre-state argument over procurement, prices and water for decades.

## The Opposition Learns the Language of Hunger

No account of the crisis's political substance is complete without the parties that converted scarcity into organisation between 1963 and 1967, for the verdict of February 1967 had authors as well as causes. Ram Manohar Lohia, the Socialist leader whose parliamentary interventions had made the cost of living a standing indictment since his election to the Lok Sabha in 1963, campaigned on prices with an arithmetic his audiences could repeat: his famous contrast between the daily income of the mass of Indians and the expenditure on the Prime Minister's establishment turned the food question into a question about the republic's priorities. The Socialist parties carried food agitation into the districts through 1965 and 1966, organising grain marches and gheraos of food offices that taught local activists the geography of the ration system they would later administer in coalition governments. The Bharatiya Jana Sangh pressed the same grievance from the right, attacking imports and devaluation as surrenders of economic sovereignty and building its urban trader and middle-class base on the price question. The Swatantra Party, founded in 1959 by C. Rajagopalachari and already the Congress's free-market critic, treated the controls that scarcity multiplied, permits, levies, movement restrictions, as proof of its founding argument, and its 1967 performance, emerging as the largest single opposition party in the Lok Sabha with 44 seats, rewarded the consistency.

The Communist parties divided the crisis between them according to their 1964 split. The Communist Party of India, aligned increasingly with the Congress on the national plane, muted its food agitation in deference to electoral understandings, while the Communist Party of India (Marxist) built the West Bengal food movement of 1966 into its state organisation and reaped the United Front victory of 1967. The contrast across the split is a small controlled experiment in scarcity politics: the party that organised hunger grew, and the party that subordinated hunger to alliance arithmetic shrank in the state where the two competed directly. Inside the Congress, the lesson registered at every level. District Congress committees reported through 1966 that the food file was costing the party its rural machinery, as local leaders who had to face the ration queue daily lost standing to opposition organisers who arrived with a grievance and a programme. The Kamaraj organisation, rebuilt after 1963 as an election machine, found itself campaigning in 1967 on the defensive in constituency after constituency, explaining queues rather than announcing projects. Political organisations learn from scarcity the way states do, publicly and at cost, and the opposition's education in the language of hunger during 1965 and 1966 is the organisational bridge between the monsoon failure and the February verdict. The drought supplied the grievance; Lohia's socialists, the Jana Sangh, Swatantra and the Bengal left supplied the grammar in which the grievance became votes.

## Scene Four: The Verdict of February 1967

The fourth general election, held in February 1967, translated the crisis into arithmetic. The Congress won 283 of 520 Lok Sabha seats, a seat share of about 54 percent, against 361 seats and about 73 percent in 1962, on a vote share that fell from about 45 percent to about 41 percent. The parliamentary figure, though stark, understates the rupture, because the state results broke the Congress system open. The Congress lost office in eight states. In Bihar, the United Front coalition that displaced the Sahay ministry brought Mahamaya Prasad Sinha to the chief ministership in March 1967. In Uttar Pradesh, Charan Singh left the Congress and formed a government in April 1967 at the head of a Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalition. In Madras, C.N. Annadurai's Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam won a majority that ended Congress rule in the state permanently. Coalitions of the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal pattern, ideologically miscellaneous alliances united by anti-Congress arithmetic, took office across the Hindi belt, and defections then unmade and remade ministries with a speed that gave the era its vocabulary of floor-crossing.

Attribution requires the care the charter of this article imposes, and the strongest form of the sceptical reading deserves its sentence. The 1967 verdict had many causes beyond food: two wars and their taxes, rising prices across commodities, the organisational decay that the Kamaraj Plan of 1963 had tried and failed to reverse, the succession struggles after Nehru's death in May 1964 and Shastri's death in January 1966, and regional movements, above all the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Madras, whose roots long predated the drought. Rajni Kothari's account of the Congress system treats 1967 as the moment opposition parties learned to combine against the dominant party, a learning process internal to politics. All of that stands. The food crisis claim is narrower and stronger for its narrowness: scarcity, prices and relief failures supplied the verdict's emotional register and its geography. The Congress collapse ran deepest where the crisis had bitten hardest and where relief had visibly failed, in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh, and the opposition campaigns of 1966-67 returned to the ration shop, the price of grain and the American ship with a consistency that the campaign literature of the season documents. A verdict can have several causes and still have a centre of gravity. In February 1967 the centre of gravity was food.

The election also closed the succession question in an unexpected register. Indira Gandhi, chosen by the Congress parliamentary party in January 1966 over Morarji Desai by 355 votes to 169, had been treated by the party's senior organisers, the group the press called the Syndicate, as a manageable tenant of an office they controlled. The diminished majority of 1967 made her dependent on the same organisers while teaching her the lesson she acted on across the following decade: that a leader's security lay in a direct appeal over the organisation's head, of the kind the food crisis had forced her to practise in relief tours and radio broadcasts through 1966. The line from the crisis to the populist reorganisation of Congress politics after 1969 runs through this lesson, and it is one more piece of political substance that a tonnage table cannot hold.

The verdict's internal Congress consequences completed the translation of scarcity into structure. The diminished majority converted the Syndicate of senior organisers, K. Kamaraj, S. Nijalingappa, Atulya Ghosh and their colleagues, from kingmakers into coalition managers on whom the Prime Minister depended for every division, and the dependence embittered both sides of the arrangement. Kamaraj himself lost his parliamentary contest at Virudhunagar in 1967, a defeat that symbolised the organisation's exposure: the man who had managed successions could not manage his own constituency in the scarcity election. Morarji Desai entered the cabinet as deputy prime minister and finance minister under a truce whose terms the weakened leadership could impose and the strengthened Desai could accept, postponing a contest the food crisis had made inevitable by demonstrating that the old machine could no longer deliver the country. Within two years the postponed contest arrived as the split of 1969, fought over bank nationalisation and the presidential election, and the standard narratives of that split begin in 1969. The chain this article has documented begins it earlier, in the ration queues and relief districts of 1966, where the authority that the split merely redistributed had already been spent.

A word on method closes the scene. The claim that food centred the 1967 verdict does not rest on the seat arithmetic alone, which cannot speak, but on the convergence of the campaign record, the geography of the losses and the testimony of participants collected in the political science of the period, including the election studies that the Centre for the Study of Developing Societies built from 1967 onward under Rajni Kothari's direction. Where the evidence supports a narrower statement, this article has made the narrower statement, and where participants disagree, the disagreement stands attributed in the text. That discipline is what separates a political reading of the crisis from a polemic about it, and it is the discipline the harvest figures, the correspondence and the debates can sustain.

The state results repay a slower reading, state by state, because the pattern of the losses maps the crisis's geography with unusual fidelity. In Kerala, the United Front led by the Communist Party of India (Marxist) swept the March 1967 election and E.M.S. Namboodiripad returned as chief minister at the head of a coalition that included the party that had split from his own three years earlier, a verdict delivered in India's most literate state on a campaign in which rice supply and price stood at the centre. In West Bengal, the United Front under Ajoy Mukherjee displaced the Congress ministry that had fired on food demonstrators in 1966, and the ministry fell within the year to the centre's intervention, teaching the state its own lesson about the limits of verdicts. In Punjab, reorganised in November 1966 into the Punjabi-speaking state the Akali Dal had demanded, the Akali-led coalition under Gurnam Singh took office, joining the language question's settlement to the scarcity verdict in a single ministry. In Orissa, a Swatantra and Jana Congress coalition under Rajendra Narayan Singh Deo ended Congress rule. In Rajasthan the Congress fell just short of a majority and the governor's invitation to it to form the government, amid opposition claims of a rival majority, produced the period's first great controversy over gubernatorial discretion. Madhya Pradesh began under a Congress ministry that defections converted into a Samyukta Vidhayak Dal government by July 1967 under Govind Narayan Singh. Read as a list, the results describe a party system cracking along every available seam. Read against the food map, they describe something more precise: the states where scarcity, prices or relief had dominated the preceding eighteen months produced the deepest ruptures, and the two states where the Congress survived comfortably were states where the crisis had bitten least or where its organisation had other resources. Correlation is not the whole of causation, and this article has already conceded the verdict's other causes, but the map is evidence no honest adjudication can set aside.

## What the Crisis Revealed About the Nehruvian State

Draw the four scenes together and the revelation has three parts, each anchored in the evidence above. First, the planning state could allocate investment across five-year horizons and could not improvise across five-week emergencies. The famine machinery that failed in Bihar was colonial in design, built around declarations and codes, while the plan machinery that commanded prestige had no emergency arm; the state had organised itself for the future it preferred and not for the harvest it received. Atul Kohli's State-Directed Development (2004) generalises the point as the Nehruvian model's central asymmetry, a coherent apex resting on a fragmented and weakly penetrative base, and Bihar in 1966 is his argument photographed at the moment of stress. Second, the state's external autonomy proved to be a function of its granaries. Non-alignment survived 1966 as doctrine and bent as practice, because doctrine does not eat. Third, the state's adaptive capacity, when it came, arrived through a channel the model had not designed: a minister and a scientist allied across the boundary between politics and expertise, governing through price and seed rather than through the plan's institutional machinery. The adaptation vindicated the alliance and indicted the design in the same act.

The revelation travelled forward along two tracks that the following decade made visible, and tracing them completes the long arc the brief assigns to this article's secondary framework. The first track is institutional. The plan holiday of 1966 to 1969, three annual plans improvised while the Fourth Plan waited, marked the planning idea's loss of command over the state's own calendar, and when the Fourth Plan finally launched in 1969 it did so into a polity whose centre of economic gravity had shifted from the Planning Commission's perspective to the Prime Minister's secretariat and the politics of the moment. The food crisis did not kill planning; the targets, the commission and the five-year rhythm all survived. What died was the presumption that the plan stood above politics as the state's rational self, entitled to obedience because it was a plan. After 1966 every economic instrument in India, the support price, the procurement levy, the buffer stock, the fertiliser subsidy, was understood by its handlers as a political instrument first, and the understanding began in the famine season when the plan's food chapter failed in public.

The second track is the language of legitimacy itself. The Congress that fought the 1967 election still spoke the settlement's dialect of development, targets and national endeavour, and lost. The Congress that rebuilt after the 1969 split spoke a different dialect, of direct promise to the poor, and its master slogan of 1971, Garibi Hatao, abolish poverty, is unintelligible except as an answer to the question the food crisis had posed: what does the state say to a citizen it could not feed? Indira Gandhi's populist reorganisation of her party's appeal, the bank nationalisation of July 1969 presented as a blow against concentrated wealth, the abolition of privy purses, the direct identification of the leader with the hungry, all belong to the education the crisis administered. The brief for this article directs attention to the 1969 split and the post-1971 centralisation as the later exhibits of limits first revealed in 1966, and the direction is sound: a state whose administrative base had failed in Bihar and whose party machine had failed at the polls rebuilt its authority around the leader's person, because the person was the instrument that had survived the testing. Personality-as-institution, the frame the following pillar applies to the Indira years, has its prehistory in the ration queue. The food crisis is where the Nehruvian state's impersonal claims were weighed, found light, and replaced by a politics that promised in the first person.

The House Thesis frame sharpens the point into the series' own vocabulary. The food crisis operationalised the third founding tension, between the rights the Constitution promised and the development that was supposed to make the promises good, at the level of the ration queue. [The three founding tensions that the Assembly left open](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) had been negotiated in 1949 as text; in 1966 they were renegotiated as grain, and the party that had authored the text discovered that authorship conferred no immunity. The rhetoric-practice gap that the Avadi session of 1955 had opened between the socialist pattern's language and the economy's performance stopped being a debating point in 1966 and became a lived experience distributed by district. States learn their limits in public or not at all; the Indian state learned this one in the most public medium there is, an empty plate, and every later government inherited the knowledge.

A comparative glance steadies the adjudication, because India was not the only state whose food dependence became a diplomatic instrument in the 1960s, and the comparison isolates what was distinctive in the Indian case. Egypt under Nasser drew heavily on the same American surplus programme and felt the same leverage when Washington slowed shipments during the Johnson years, and the parallel confirms that the short tether expressed a general technique of food power rather than a particular animus against New Delhi. The Indian distinctiveness lay in the domestic receiver. India held competitive elections on schedule through the emergency, possessed a press that reported the negotiation's terms as sovereignty news, and fielded opposition parties organised enough to convert the dependence into votes within eighteen months. Food power over Egypt pressured a presidency; food power over India pressured a government that then faced its electorate in February 1967 with the shipping schedule as campaign literature. The technique met democracy, and democracy is what converted an episode of aid diplomacy into a permanent alteration of the party system. International relations accounts of PL-480 that stop at the water's edge miss the conversion, and agricultural accounts that stop at the seed miss it from the other side. The political reading is the only one that follows the grain all the way from the American farm belt to the Bihar ballot, which is the journey the grain itself made.

## The Four-Scene Political-Substance Map

## Resolution Is Not Revelation: The Adaptation Counterargument

The strongest counter to the political-crisis reading must be met on its best evidence, because the counter is substantially true about tonnage. The Green Revolution worked. Wheat production roughly doubled within five years of the 1967-68 harvest, foodgrain output crossed 100 million tonnes by 1970-71, imports shrank, buffer stocks became policy, and India never again stood on a short tether of the 1966 kind. A reading that stresses limits, the counter argues, mistakes a painful transition for a structural verdict: the Nehruvian state bent under the drought, borrowed, devalued, reorganised its farm strategy and emerged feeding itself, which is a record of adaptation that weaker states could not have matched. Swaminathan's own retrospective writings present the crisis years in this register, as the forge of a scientific agriculture, and the production series supports every figure he cites.

Amartya Sen's celebrated argument belongs inside this adjudication, because it supplies the counter's most sophisticated form and, properly read, supports the political reading's core. In Poverty and Famines (1981), Sen argued that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, since rulers who must face elections and newspapers cannot let starvation proceed unobserved, and he treated independent India's record, including the Bihar emergency of 1966-67, as evidence: drought struck, entitlements collapsed for the landless, and a large famine was averted by public action that political pressure forced. Sen's entitlement approach also explains the Bihar pattern better than the tonnage does, since people starved amid a national food economy that still moved grain, their command over food having failed with their wages and employment. Accept Sen whole, and the conclusion follows that the crisis's meaning was political in its very mechanism: what saved Bihar was not the plan but the pressure, the press, the opposition and the approaching election, the accountability machinery of the democracy acting on a state whose administrative machinery had stalled. The political-crisis reading does not compete with Sen's account. It is Sen's account, extended from the relief operation to the verdict that the relief operation's shortcomings then produced. Where Sen stops at the famine averted, this article follows the same pressure into February 1967 and asks what it did to the party system, and the answer is written in the seat arithmetic of eight lost states.

The distinction that answers the counter is the one the brief directs: crisis-resolution is not crisis-revelation, and the two run on different clocks. Resolution asks whether the grain eventually arrived, and it did. Revelation asks what the interval between failure and remedy disclosed about the state's design, and the disclosure did not expire when the harvest recovered. The centre-state delay built into the food machinery, the autonomy that dissolved with the granaries, the organisational hollowness that forced government around the party machine: each disclosed weakness reappeared in the politics of the following decade, in the Congress split of 1969, in the centralisation after 1971, and in the recurring centre-state contests over prices and procurement that Varshney documents. A state can solve a problem and still have been shown, by the solving, to be a different state from the one its rhetoric described. The food crisis showed India that difference, in public, before the recovery arrived to soften the memory. That is why the political reading survives the tonnage, and why the tonnage, honestly weighed, cannot carry the agricultural reading by itself.

One further test separates the political reading from a merely rhetorical one, and the crisis passes it: the state's own behaviour changed in the directions the revelation indicated. Buffer stocking became a standing commitment rather than an aspiration, the Food Corporation grew into a permanent logistics arm, support price announcements acquired the status of a political calendar no government could skip, and the import dependence of 1966 entered the strategic memory as a humiliation to be engineered away, shaping fertiliser, seed and irrigation policy for two decades. Governments do not reorganise themselves around lessons they have not learned. The reorganisation is the state's signature on the revelation's authenticity, written in budgets and institutions rather than in editorials, and it corroborates from inside the reading that the crisis was a constitutional event in the life of the republic, the moment the development state discovered the distance between its promises and its instruments and began, unevenly, to close it.

The three scholarly frames the brief assigns to this article can be set against each other on the evidence assembled, because the adjudication among them is where the political reading earns its keep. Francine Frankel's India's Political Economy 1947-2004 reads the crisis through the gradualist strategy of the Nehruvian settlement, in which the Congress's need to hold a coalition of landlords, industrialists and the poor together had postponed every hard choice, land reform, taxation of agricultural incomes, procurement discipline, until scarcity presented the bill for all of them at once. Her frame explains the crisis's origins best: the vulnerability of 1965 was manufactured across two decades of postponed reform, and the postponement was political in its marrow. Atul Kohli's State-Directed Development explains the crisis's administration best, the coherent apex and fragmented base through which a state that could draft a perspective plan could not move grain into Gaya on time. Ashutosh Varshney's Democracy, Development, and the Countryside explains the crisis's aftermath best, the emergence of the surplus-region farmer as an organised political force inside the frame the technocratic settlement built. The article's political-crisis reading does not choose among the three so much as order them in the sequence the events impose: Frankel for the manufacture of vulnerability, Kohli for the failure of delivery, Varshney for the reconstruction that followed, and the four scenes as the connective tissue that carries one frame into the next. What none of the three frames, taken alone, supplies is the chain itself, the passage from postponed reform through failed relief and foreign leverage to electoral verdict, and supplying that chain is the original analytical move this article was assigned to make. The agricultural-emergency reading, against which the whole argument contends, appears in this company as the thinnest of the available frames, not because its facts are wrong but because its facts are the smallest subset: a monsoon, a shortfall, a seed. Everything in the episode that decided what kind of republic India would be happened outside that subset, in the ministries, embassies, relief queues and polling stations where the political substance accumulated.

## The Legible Consequence

The 1960s food crisis began as weather and ended as knowledge. Between the failed monsoon of 1965 and the verdict of February 1967, the Indian state was measured at the three points where its founding claims were largest: its promise to feed, its promise to judge independently of foreign pressure, and its promise to transform the countryside through the community the founders had imagined. At each point the measurement fell short of the promise in ways that named men and dated documents record: a famine declared late in Bihar, a currency devalued under negotiation in June 1966, a cooperative inheritance set aside for a packet of dwarf seed. The recovery that followed was real, and it belonged to the alliance of C. Subramaniam and M.S. Swaminathan and to the farmers of the surplus districts who carried the new package. The revelation belonged to everyone. The Nehruvian state's limits became politically visible in 1966, and visibility, once achieved in a democracy with a free press and a general election on the calendar, converts directly into consequence. The 1967 verdict was that consequence, legible then and legible still.

The proof that the lesson lodged permanently in the republic's practice arrived with the next great drought, in Maharashtra between 1970 and 1973, when a state government facing scarcity on the Bihar scale answered with the instrument the earlier failure had implied: a guarantee of relief employment on public works, organised before destitution rather than after it, which matured into the Maharashtra Employment Guarantee Scheme and stands in the genealogy of every later Indian commitment to work as the form relief takes in a democracy. The line from the Bihar famine to the employment guarantee runs through the same discovery Sen formalised, that in a republic the hungry possess a weapon the colonial subject lacked, the capacity to punish. Bihar in 1966 taught the weapon's existence to both sides of the relationship, to the citizens who used it in February 1967 and to the state that rebuilt its relief doctrine so as never to face it unprepared again. That double education, administered by a failed monsoon and completed at a general election, is the food crisis's deepest political substance, and it is the reason an episode filed in the agricultural histories belongs at the centre of the political history of the Nehruvian state's end.

The evidence for every link in the chain sits in named places, and the article closes by returning the reader to them: the production series in the plan documents and the Economic Survey; the negotiation in the agreement texts and correspondence preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library; the ministerial alliance in the correspondence held in the IARI archives and in the field-survey volumes of 1966-67 shelved in the IARI Library; the argument in the Lok Sabha debates of January and February 1966; and the verdict in the Election Commission's statistical reports for the fourth general election. A political reading that can be audited against its archives invites the strongest form of disagreement, disagreement on evidence, and that invitation, extended to every rival account from the celebratory to the sceptical, is the article's final claim on the reader's trust.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the 1960s food crisis in India?

The 1960s food crisis was the scarcity emergency produced when the monsoons of 1965 and 1966 failed in succession and foodgrain production fell from 89.4 million tonnes in 1964-65 to 72.3 million tonnes in 1965-66, recovering only slightly the following year. India imported about ten million tonnes of grain in 1966, mostly American wheat under PL-480, while Bihar slid into famine conditions and food prices rose sharply across the country. The crisis forced the June 1966 devaluation, a reordering of farm policy toward high-yielding varieties, and it fed directly into the Congress losses in the general election of February 1967.

### Q: What is PL-480 food aid?

PL-480 was the United States food aid programme created by the Agricultural Trade Development and Assistance Act of 1954, known in its later branding as Food for Peace. It shipped American surplus grain to friendly countries on concessional terms, with payment arrangements that spared scarce foreign exchange. India became its largest recipient, and by the mid-1960s American wheat under PL-480 covered a substantial share of Indian consumption. Because President Lyndon Johnson released shipments in short tranches during 1966, the programme also functioned as leverage, linking continued grain to Indian acceptance of American advice on farm policy and economic management.

### Q: Who started the Green Revolution in India?

The Green Revolution in India was started politically by C. Subramaniam, the food and agriculture minister from 1964 to 1967, and scientifically by M.S. Swaminathan, the plant geneticist at the Indian Agricultural Research Institute who championed the Mexican dwarf wheat varieties bred by Norman Borlaug. Subramaniam carried the cabinet decisions on seed imports, fertiliser, credit and support prices, while Swaminathan's trials from 1963 onward demonstrated that the dwarf varieties could multiply yields under Indian conditions. The High Yielding Varieties Programme launched in 1966 joined their work into national policy, and the wheat harvest of 1967-68 confirmed the wager.

### Q: What was the Bihar famine?

The Bihar famine was the famine condition that developed across drought-stricken Bihar districts during 1966 and 1967, after two failed harvests left landless labourers without wages or grain. The state administration of Chief Minister K.B. Sahay hesitated to declare famine before the February 1967 election, voluntary relief organised through the Bihar Relief Committee under Jayaprakash Narayan moved alongside the official effort, and famine was formally declared in 1967 after the ministry changed. Officially recorded starvation deaths numbered 2,353 across the drought states, a figure relief workers disputed, while mass destitution and migration were documented in the press and in IARI field surveys.

### Q: How did the food crisis affect the 1967 elections?

The food crisis shaped the February 1967 elections by making scarcity, grain prices and relief failures the campaign's dominant register, above all in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh where the crisis had bitten hardest. The Congress fell from 361 Lok Sabha seats in 1962 to 283 in 1967, its seat share dropping from about 73 percent to about 54 percent, and it lost office in eight states to opposition coalitions of the Samyukta Vidhayak Dal pattern and to the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Madras. Other causes contributed, including prices, wars and organisational decay, but food supplied the verdict's emotional centre of gravity.

### Q: Who was C. Subramaniam?

C. Subramaniam was the union food and agriculture minister from 1964 to 1967, serving under Lal Bahadur Shastri and then Indira Gandhi, and the political architect of India's new agricultural strategy. A senior Congress figure from Madras with earlier charge of the steel and heavy industries portfolios, he allied with the geneticist M.S. Swaminathan to back the Mexican dwarf wheat varieties, pushed fertiliser production and imports, created the price and procurement instruments of the Agricultural Prices Commission and the Food Corporation of India, and carried the High Yielding Varieties Programme through cabinet. His tenure joined the PL-480 emergency to the Green Revolution's launch.

### Q: Did the food crisis force rupee devaluation?

The food crisis was a central cause of the rupee devaluation of 6 June 1966, when the government moved the exchange rate from 4.76 to 7.50 rupees to the dollar, a fall of 36.5 percent. The devaluation was urged by the World Bank and the Aid India Consortium as a condition for resumed assistance, and it travelled in the same negotiating season as the PL-480 talks in which India accepted American advice on farm policy. Hunger had made India dependent on foreign grain and aid, and dependence priced the currency. Opposition and Congress speakers alike treated the decision as a receipt issued under foreign pressure.

### Q: Why did the United States suspend aid to India during the 1965 war?

The United States suspended aid to both India and Pakistan when the two countries went to war in September 1965, applying an even-handed embargo that in practice struck India's food pipeline in the first drought season. The suspension halted consortium assistance and paused PL-480 shipments at the moment the failed 1965 harvest was being counted, teaching New Delhi that the supply line ran through American strategic judgement and could be closed by it. Shipments resumed after the Tashkent declaration of January 1966, with the reform agenda attached, so the episode rehearsed in miniature the leverage that President Johnson's short tether then applied through 1966.

### Q: What role did L.K. Jha and B.K. Nehru play in the food negotiations?

L.K. Jha, principal secretary to the Prime Minister, and B.K. Nehru, India's ambassador to the United States, conducted the Indian side of the 1966 food negotiations with the Johnson administration. Their correspondence, preserved at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, scheduled wheat shipments against Indian port capacity, rail movement and ration commitments while pressing for firm multi-year supply commitments that Washington refused. B.K. Nehru later recounted the talks in his 1997 memoir Nice Guys Finish Second, recording the Indian reading that the short tether was meant to discipline Indian policy. Together they managed the diplomacy of dependence at its most exposed point.

### Q: How much foodgrain did India import in 1966?

India imported roughly ten million tonnes of foodgrains in 1966, the largest single-year import effort in the country's history up to that point, with the overwhelming share arriving from the United States under PL-480. The United States Department of Agriculture economist Lester Brown calculated that American shipments that year amounted to about a fifth of the United States wheat crop, carried on some six hundred ship voyages. The imports fed the ration shops of the cities and the relief works of the drought districts, and the scale of the operation fixed the phrase ship-to-mouth in the political vocabulary of the period.

### Q: What did the Lok Sabha debates of early 1966 reveal about the food situation?

The Lok Sabha debates on the food situation in January and February 1966, held within weeks of Indira Gandhi becoming Prime Minister on 24 January 1966, revealed a chamber treating scarcity as an indictment of two decades of farm policy rather than as an act of weather. Members across parties quoted harvest figures, ration queues and price movements, and opposition speakers returned repeatedly to the contrast between the Avadi promise of a socialist pattern and the queues outside fair price shops. The debates record the moment the food question moved from ministry files into open political contest, months before the famine declaration in Bihar.

### Q: Why did the Congress hesitate to declare famine in Bihar in 1966?

The Congress ministry of Bihar under Chief Minister K.B. Sahay hesitated to declare famine through the autumn of 1966 because the Famine Code's declaration carried consequences the ministry wished to avoid months before the February 1967 general election: admitting famine admitted administrative failure, triggered obligatory relief expenditure, and handed the opposition a certified verdict on the government's record. Paul R. Brass showed in his study of the Bihar famine that politicians at each level chose between the words scarcity and famine according to the aid and blame each word carried. The declaration came in 1967, after the Sahay ministry had fallen.

### Q: What was the Food Corporation of India, and why was it created in 1965?

The Food Corporation of India was the union government's procurement and distribution agency, created in January 1965 as the state-level operations of the new food policy took shape under C. Subramaniam. It gave New Delhi an instrument of its own for buying grain at announced support prices, holding buffer stocks and moving supplies into the public distribution system, instead of depending entirely on state administrations and private trade. Its creation marked the centre's decision to treat food management as a permanent national function. During the 1966-67 scarcity the Corporation's stocks and logistics formed the backbone of the official relief and ration effort.

### Q: How did the Bihar Relief Committee differ from official relief work?

The Bihar Relief Committee, the voluntary organisation chaired by Jayaprakash Narayan, differed from official relief in speed, flexibility and accountability. It raised funds and grain from voluntary agencies in India and abroad, ran feeding and relief works through local committees, and published its own reporting from the drought districts, while the official machine waited on Famine Code declarations, budget releases and instructions moving between Patna and New Delhi. In several blocks the Committee's operations reached villages before the government's did. The sight of a parallel relief effort run by a man holding no office became a political fact in itself, and opposition speakers used it against the ministry.

### Q: What did Paul Brass argue about the politics of the Bihar famine?

Paul R. Brass argued, in "The Political Uses of Crisis: The Bihar Famine of 1966-1967" in the Journal of Asian Studies, that the Bihar famine was shaped by the politics of definition as much as by the drought itself. Governments, parties and relief agencies chose between the vocabularies of scarcity, drought and famine according to what each word unlocked in aid or assigned in blame, and the seriousness of the emergency was accepted only gradually and reluctantly by the institutions responsible for relief. His study showed how rhetoric determined the quantity, timing and recipients of assistance, making the famine a case study in how crises are politically constructed and used.

### Q: Which states did the Congress lose in the 1967 state elections?

In the state elections held alongside the February 1967 general election, the Congress lost office in eight states. The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam under C.N. Annadurai won Madras outright, ending Congress rule there permanently. Opposition fronts and Samyukta Vidhayak Dal coalitions displaced Congress ministries across the Hindi belt, with Mahamaya Prasad Sinha becoming chief minister in Bihar in March 1967 and Charan Singh, who had left the Congress, heading the government in Uttar Pradesh from April 1967. Kerala, Punjab, West Bengal and Orissa also passed out of Congress hands, breaking the pattern of one-party dominance that had held since independence.

### Q: What was the Agricultural Prices Commission, and what did it change in 1965?

The Agricultural Prices Commission was created in 1965 to advise the union government on support and procurement prices for farm produce, and it changed Indian farm policy by giving price a permanent institutional place in production decisions. Under the new agricultural strategy of C. Subramaniam, announced minimum support prices told cultivators before sowing that the state would buy at a remunerative rate, converting the high-yielding varieties package from an exhortation into a calculation. The Commission's price announcements, joined to Food Corporation of India procurement, underwrote the risk farmers took on dwarf seed and fertiliser from the kharif season of 1966 onward.

### Q: How did food prices behave during the crisis years of 1965 to 1967?

Food prices rose sharply through the crisis years as scarcity met inelastic demand and thin public stocks. With production down by roughly a fifth in 1965-66 and imports arriving on a monthly American schedule, open-market grain prices in the deficit states climbed far above the controlled rates at fair price shops, and the queues and premiums outside those shops became the crisis's most visible daily image. Price rise, rather than absolute absence of grain, was how most urban and landless households experienced the emergency, which is why opposition campaigns in 1966-67 spoke as much about the cost of food as about its quantity.

### Q: What did Atul Kohli argue about the limits of the Nehruvian development model?

Atul Kohli argued in State-Directed Development (2004) that the Nehruvian development model combined a coherent, purposeful apex with a fragmented and weakly penetrative administrative base, so the state could design five-year strategies more readily than it could deliver outcomes in the districts. The food crisis displayed the asymmetry under stress: the Planning Commission could set a foodgrain target above 100 million tonnes while the relief machinery of Bihar worked through a colonial Famine Code and waited on declarations. Kohli's reading treats the centralised model's limits as structural features present from the start, which scarcity exposed rather than created.

### Q: Why does the political reading of the food crisis survive the Green Revolution's success?

The political reading survives because resolution and revelation answer different questions. The Green Revolution resolved the grain shortage: wheat output roughly doubled within five years of the 1967-68 harvest and imports shrank. Revelation concerns what the interval between failure and remedy disclosed about the state, and that disclosure did not expire with the recovery. The centre-state delays in food administration, the foreign-policy autonomy that thinned as the granaries emptied, and the party organisation bypassed by a minister and a scientist all reappeared in the politics of the following decade, from the Congress split of 1969 to the centralisation after 1971.
