A French army of roughly thirty six thousand men landed on the Egyptian coast west of Alexandria on 1 July 1798, took the city the next morning, destroyed the country's ruling military elite three weeks later within sight of the pyramids, and occupied Cairo. Thirty eight months afterward the survivors of that army were loaded onto British transports and shipped home, having lost the country, lost their fleet, lost their commander, lost their second commander to an assassin, and lost somewhere between a third and a half of their number to battle, disease, and the desert. Measured against its own objectives, the Egyptian campaign is one of the most complete strategic failures of the revolutionary wars.

![Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 and why the campaign failed - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-40.webp)

It is also the single most consequential three years in Egyptian history between the Ottoman conquest and the building of the Suez Canal. The invasion destroyed the political order that had run the country for two and a half centuries, exposed to everyone including the Ottomans that Egypt could be taken by a European power in a fortnight, opened the country to European commercial and diplomatic penetration that never afterward receded, produced the scholarly enterprise that would decipher hieroglyphs and invent an academic discipline, and created the exact power vacuum that an Albanian officer in the Ottoman expeditionary force stepped into four years later.

That gap between outcome and consequence is what this article exists to explain, and the framework for it can be called the brilliant-failure thesis. The campaign was tactically impressive and strategically incoherent from the moment it was conceived. Napoleon could beat any force Egypt or the Ottomans put in front of him and could not solve the one problem that decided the war, which was that an army on the far side of a sea it does not control is a hostage rather than an expedition. Everything the invasion achieved, it achieved by accident, through the wreckage it left behind rather than through anything it set out to do.

The popular image is the opposite: a triumphant young general, the cavalry charge broken at the pyramids, forty centuries looking down, scholars measuring temples, a conqueror who chose to move on to greater things. Nearly every element of that picture is either false or a reversal of the actual sequence. Napoleon did not move on to greater things from a position of strength. He abandoned a trapped army in a ship that slipped past a British blockade, and he did it because the campaign was lost and the political opportunity at home was closing.

## Why France looked at Egypt at all

The invasion was not an improvisation, and understanding its origins requires separating three different motives held by three different parties, none of which fully overlapped.

The oldest was a French strategic tradition. Proposals to seize Egypt had circulated in French policy circles since the seventeenth century, when the philosopher and diplomat Leibniz reportedly urged such a scheme on Louis XIV as an alternative to war in Europe, and the idea resurfaced repeatedly through the eighteenth century. France had extensive commercial interests in the Levant, a large merchant community at Alexandria and Cairo, and a long standing consular presence, and French merchants had spent decades complaining about the exactions and unpredictability of the Mamluk households who actually governed the province. The commercial argument was that Egypt was a rich country being wasted by its rulers and that French control would turn it into a colony worth more than the sugar islands.

The second motive was strategic and specific to the war with Britain. After the peace settlements of 1797 left France dominant on the continent and Britain undefeated at sea, the problem for the Directory was how to strike at an enemy it could not reach. A direct invasion of England was examined and judged impracticable given British naval superiority in the Channel. Egypt offered an indirect route: a French base astride the isthmus between the Mediterranean and the Red Sea would threaten the route to British India, could serve as a springboard for cooperation with Indian rulers hostile to British expansion, and would cost Britain its Levant trade.

The third motive was personal and political. The Directory had a general of enormous popularity and uncertain loyalty who had just conquered Italy largely on his own authority, negotiated a treaty with Austria without instruction, and returned to Paris as the most famous man in France. Sending him to Egypt removed him from domestic politics while putting him somewhere he might do the Republic some good. The foreign minister Talleyrand supported the project energetically and promised to go to Istanbul himself to reconcile the Ottoman sultan to the seizure of his province, a promise he never attempted to keep, which left the expedition without the diplomatic cover it had been planned around.

### What did the Directory expect to gain from Egypt?

A colony to replace the lost Caribbean sugar trade, a base threatening Britain's route to India, the removal of an overmighty general from Paris, and a war fought at someone else's expense. The objectives were plural, poorly ranked, and never reconciled, which is why no one could say what would count as success.

## The India question, honestly assessed

The claim that Napoleon invaded Egypt to reach India is the most repeated explanation and the one most in need of qualification, because it is partly true and frequently overstated.

What is documented is that the threat to India featured prominently in the argument made to the Directory, that Napoleon corresponded with Tipu Sultan of Mysore, who was then fighting the British in southern India, and that after reaching Egypt he investigated the Suez isthmus and the Red Sea route personally. The British certainly believed the threat was real, which is why they redeployed naval strength to the Mediterranean and why an Indian army under David Baird was eventually landed on the Red Sea coast.

What is not credible is the idea of a French army marching from Cairo to Delhi. The logistics were impossible with the resources available, and Napoleon never assembled anything resembling the force or the shipping such a project would require. The realistic version of the India argument is narrower and more sensible: a French Egypt would hold the short route between Europe and the Indian Ocean, would give France a naval and commercial position on the Red Sea, and would be a standing strategic threat that forced Britain to divert resources. That is a real objective, and it was destroyed on 1 August 1798 along with the fleet.

The deeper problem was that these aims required Egypt to be held permanently, and nobody in Paris had worked out how a permanent garrison on the far side of a British-controlled sea would be supplied, reinforced, or relieved. The campaign was planned as though the Mediterranean were a French lake, which it had not been since 1793 and would not be again.

## The expedition that sailed

The force assembled at Toulon and the other southern French and Italian ports in the spring of 1798 was one of the largest amphibious undertakings attempted to that point, and its composition tells you what the planners thought they were doing.

The army numbered roughly thirty six thousand soldiers, a figure that appears in various forms across the sources and should be treated as an order of magnitude rather than a muster roll; estimates in the literature range from about thirty four thousand to about forty thousand depending on whether sailors, support personnel, and later reinforcements are counted. It was a good army, drawn substantially from the Army of Italy, and it came with the officers who would carry the Napoleonic wars for the next fifteen years, among them Kleber, Desaix, Lannes, Murat, Berthier, and Menou.

The naval escort was thirteen ships of the line under Admiral Brueys, with the enormous one hundred and eighteen gun flagship Orient, supported by frigates and roughly four hundred transports. The convoy was therefore vast, slow, and extremely vulnerable, which is the fact around which the entire campaign's fate turned.

The expedition also carried a civilian component with no precedent in military history: a commission of sciences and arts, roughly one hundred and sixty scholars, engineers, surveyors, naturalists, draftsmen, printers, and orientalists, with a printing press capable of setting Arabic type. Their presence signals that the venture was conceived as colonization rather than raid, and the enterprise they conducted became the most celebrated by-product of the entire campaign, treated separately later in this cluster.

Secrecy was maintained to a remarkable degree. The destination was concealed from almost everyone including most of the senior officers, and the British, who knew a great armament was fitting out at Toulon, spent months guessing at targets that included Ireland, Portugal, Naples, and Constantinople.

## Malta and the crossing Nelson missed

The fleet sailed on 19 May 1798 and made its first stop at Malta, held since the sixteenth century by the Knights of St John, a crusading order by then reduced to a wealthy anachronism governing a fortified island of enormous strategic value.

The island fell on 12 June after a token resistance. The Knights were divided, a significant proportion of them were French and unwilling to fight their countrymen, and the fortifications that had held off an Ottoman siege in 1565 were manned by a garrison with no appetite for a defense. Napoleon spent a week reorganizing the island's government, abolishing the order's rule, seizing the treasury and the accumulated plate and jewels of the knights, and installing a French garrison. Malta was a genuine strategic prize, commanding the central Mediterranean, and holding it mattered more to French naval position than anything that happened afterward in Egypt.

Then came the crossing, and the most consequential piece of luck in the campaign. Nelson, detached with a squadron to hunt the French armament, reached Toulon after it had sailed, guessed correctly that Egypt was the destination, and raced there ahead of the convoy. He arrived off Alexandria on 28 June, found nothing, concluded he had guessed wrong, and sailed north toward Anatolia. The French convoy, moving far more slowly, arrived three days later. The two fleets had passed within a short distance of each other in poor visibility without either sighting the other.

Had Nelson found the convoy at sea, the campaign would have ended in the first week with the destruction of thirteen ships of the line and four hundred transports carrying an army. That it did not is why there was an Egyptian campaign at all, and it is worth registering how narrow the margin was, because the same admiral found the fleet a month later at anchor and produced exactly that outcome.

## The landing and the storming of Alexandria

Napoleon learned from a captured vessel that a British squadron had been off the coast days earlier, and the news decided him to land immediately rather than wait for better conditions or scout a proper anchorage.

The disembarkation began on the night of 1 July 1798 at Marabout, a stretch of open beach west of Alexandria, in a heavy surf and without a harbor. It was chaotic and costly, boats were swamped and men drowned, and the army came ashore wet, disorganized, without cavalry horses and without artillery of any weight. The decision to land in those conditions was a calculated risk driven by the fear of being caught at sea, and it was the correct call given what Nelson would have done to a convoy under sail.

Alexandria itself was a shock to men raised on classical education. The city that had held perhaps half a million people under the Ptolemies and Romans was a decayed town of a few thousand, its ancient harbor silted, its monuments quarried, its walls in poor repair, its population dependent on a canal from the Nile that functioned intermittently. The French stormed it on the morning of 2 July against a determined but hopeless defense by the local garrison and militia, taking losses that included a wound to Kleber.

Napoleon issued his first proclamation to the Egyptian population from Alexandria. It announced that the French came as friends of the sultan and enemies only of the Mamluk beys, that they respected Islam more than the Mamluks did, and that Egyptians should cooperate. It was printed in Arabic on the expedition's press, and its language and reception deserve separate treatment because it set a pattern the occupation never escaped.

## The march on Cairo

The move inland began almost immediately, and the desert march from Alexandria toward the Nile at Damanhur and Rahmaniya was the army's first real education in the country it had come to conquer.

Napoleon split his force, sending one division along the coast to Rosetta and the main body across the desert to strike the Nile directly. The desert leg was short by the standards of what armies later marched, but it was conducted in July, in wool uniforms, by men who had come ashore with inadequate water discipline and no reliable guides, through country where the wells were few and had often been fouled by the retreating population. Men died of heat and thirst, stragglers were killed by bedouin who shadowed the column, and the psychological effect on a European army seeing this landscape for the first time was severe. Officers' memoirs from the campaign record suicides, and the mood on reaching the Nile was closer to relief than to triumph.

At the river the army found water, melons, and a functioning agricultural landscape, and its condition improved rapidly. From Rahmaniya it moved south along the Nile toward Cairo in the heat, with the flotilla of small craft carrying supplies and the scholars keeping pace on the river.

## Shubra Khit and the answer to cavalry

The first serious engagement came on 13 July at Shubra Khit, and although it was small compared with what followed it is the tactically decisive moment of the campaign, because it established that the French formation worked.

A Mamluk force under Murad Bey, supported by a river flotilla, attacked the French column. Napoleon formed his divisions into large squares, each several thousand men with artillery at the corners and the baggage, the scholars, and the wounded in the middle. The cavalry charged, could not break into the formation, took heavy fire from muskets and canister at close range, and withdrew. French losses were minimal.

The lesson runs through everything that followed. Heavy cavalry cannot break disciplined infantry that holds its formation and has artillery support, because a horse will not charge home into a hedge of bayonets and because canister at a hundred yards destroys a mounted formation faster than it can close. This was not new knowledge in Europe, where infantry squares had been standard practice for generations, but it was decisive in Egypt because the Mamluk military system, described in detail in the article on [the Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt](/2013/09/01/mamluk-beys-of-ottoman-egypt/), had produced superb individual horsemen and nothing else. There was no French answer required beyond doing what French infantry already knew how to do.

Eight days later the same tactical problem was posed on a far larger scale outside Cairo and produced the same answer, this time in front of the pyramids. That engagement, its actual location, what Napoleon did and did not say before it, and how it destroyed bey power in a single afternoon are the subject of the article on [the battle of the pyramids](/2013/11/01/battle-of-the-pyramids/), and this article does not re-narrate it. What matters here is the strategic result: Murad Bey fled south into Upper Egypt with the remains of his household, Ibrahim Bey withdrew east toward Syria, and the French entered Cairo on 24 July 1798 essentially unopposed.

In three weeks from landing, Napoleon had destroyed the field army of the country, occupied its capital, and made himself master of the Nile valley from the Delta to the approaches of Upper Egypt. By the ordinary standards of military history that is an extraordinarily successful campaign. Eight days later it ceased to matter.

## The Battle of the Nile

On the evening of 1 August 1798 Nelson found the French fleet anchored in Aboukir Bay, twenty miles northeast of Alexandria, and attacked immediately in failing light.

Brueys had been unable to bring his ships into Alexandria's silted harbor and had moored in line in the open bay, relying on shoal water inshore to protect one flank. The disposition had a fatal flaw: the ships were anchored with enough space and enough water inshore for an enemy to pass between them and the shoal. Nelson's captains, some without orders and on their own initiative, took part of the British line inside the French line, so that French ships were engaged on both sides simultaneously by an enemy attacking a fleet that was not cleared for action on its landward side.

The battle ran through the night. The Orient caught fire and blew up in an explosion reported as visible and audible for many miles, taking Brueys and most of her crew. By morning of 2 August, of thirteen French ships of the line, eleven had been captured or destroyed. Two escaped under Admiral Villeneuve, who would meet Nelson again at Trafalgar. British losses were comparatively light and Nelson himself was wounded in the head.

### Why did the Battle of the Nile matter so much?

Because it converted an expedition into a siege. Without a fleet the French could not be reinforced, supplied, paid, or withdrawn, and any strategic value Egypt held for France depended on communication with France. The army kept winning battles and stopped having any purpose, and the Ottomans and British now knew it was trapped.

## What the loss of the fleet actually meant

The consequences cascaded outward from Aboukir Bay over the following months, and setting them out in order shows why every subsequent French victory was strategically empty.

Reinforcement ended. Whatever happened to the army in Egypt, no more men, horses, guns, or munitions would arrive except in single vessels running the blockade. Attrition became permanent, and an army that cannot replace losses is on a timer regardless of how many engagements it wins.

Supply and money ended. The expedition had counted on drawing what it needed from France and on the Mediterranean trade. Now everything had to be extracted from Egypt itself, which meant taxing a population the proclamations had promised to liberate, seizing property, and imposing forced contributions on merchants and notables. An occupation that needs to fund itself from the occupied becomes a predatory administration whatever its intentions, and the French occupation did so within months.

Withdrawal ended. The army could not leave. This is the underappreciated consequence and the one that explains the strange desperation of the next two years, including the Syrian expedition. A commander who cannot retreat must either win outright or wait to be destroyed.

Diplomacy collapsed. The Ottoman sultan Selim III, who had been assured by Talleyrand's unkept promises that France remained friendly and was merely disciplining rebellious Mamluks on his behalf, declared war in September 1798 and allied with Britain and, extraordinarily, with Russia, the empire's habitual enemy. The fiction that the French came as agents of the sultan against the beys, on which the entire political strategy of the occupation rested, was publicly dead from that moment.

And the strategic rationale evaporated. A French Egypt threatened British India only if France could use it, which required ships. After 1 August the occupation threatened nothing. It had become an expensive garrison holding a province France could neither exploit nor defend nor abandon.

## Governing an occupied country

The French administration of Egypt lasted three years and is more interesting than its military record, because it is the first attempt by a modern European state to govern an Arab Muslim society directly, and many of the methods later used across the region were first tried here.

Napoleon's approach was to rule through local intermediaries while keeping all real authority in French hands. He created a divan in Cairo composed of religious scholars, merchants, and notables, and similar councils in the provinces, and he gave them consultative functions over taxation, grain supply, and public order. The men he appointed were mostly shaykhs of al-Azhar, the natural leadership of Cairo society and the one group with standing among the population after the beys had fled. Their position was impossible: they were held responsible by the French for keeping the city quiet and by their own community for collaborating with an infidel occupation.

Fiscal policy was the occupation's central problem and its central failure. The French inherited a revenue system built on tax farming, described in the article on [how Egypt was governed by the Ottomans](/2013/07/01/egypt-under-ottoman-rule/), and they needed far more from it than the beys had taken, because the beys had at least spent locally while the French were funding a stranded army. They abolished some tax farms, confiscated the property of absent beys, levied enormous forced contributions on Cairo's merchants, imposed a house tax, and eventually resorted to demanding payment from the very notables sitting on their divan. They also employed Coptic fiscal administrators, notably Muallim Yaqub, who had served the beys in the same capacity, which deepened communal tensions that outlasted the occupation.

There were genuine administrative innovations alongside the extraction. The French conducted surveys, attempted a cadastral register, introduced street lighting and sanitation measures in Cairo, established quarantine procedures, set up hospitals, founded the Institut d'Egypte in August 1798 as a working scientific academy, and produced the first Arabic-language printed material of an official character in the country. Whether these amounted to modernization or to the administrative apparatus of a colony is a fair question, and the honest answer is that they were both, and that almost none of them survived the withdrawal except as a demonstration that such things could be done.

## The proclamations and the politics of religion

The most discussed aspect of the occupation's political strategy is Napoleon's appeal to Islam, and it needs handling carefully because both the cynical and the credulous readings miss what was actually going on.

The proclamation issued at Alexandria and reissued in Cairo declared that the French were true Muslims in the sense that they had overthrown a pope, destroyed the authority of priests, and worshipped one God; that they came as friends of the Ottoman sultan; and that they had arrived to free Egypt from the tyranny of the Mamluk beys, who were foreigners and oppressors. The text invoked God, used Quranic phrasing, and was printed in Arabic.

Egyptian reaction is documented with unusual clarity because al-Jabarti, the Cairo chronicler, subjected the Arabic text to a detailed and withering critique of its grammar, its theology, and its logic. He noted the errors of construction, observed that the claim to be Muslims sat oddly with men who neither prayed nor fasted nor abstained from wine, and pointed out that liberation from tyranny delivered by an occupying army that immediately levied contributions was a curious kind of freedom. His verdict, written by a man of the religious establishment who would serve on the French divan, is the single best piece of evidence on how the occupation was received by educated Egyptians.

Napoleon also took part in public Islamic festivals, cultivated the shaykhs, and at one point floated the possibility that the army might convert, a suggestion the ulama reportedly met by asking about circumcision and wine, which ended the discussion. The purpose throughout was instrumental. The French needed the religious establishment to keep Cairo calm and needed a legitimating story that did not depend on French republicanism, which meant nothing in Egypt. It half worked for about three months, until the tax demands made the contradiction unignorable.

## Desaix and the conquest of Upper Egypt

The campaign that historians of the expedition rate most highly as a military performance is the one that attracts the least attention, and it ran for the better part of a year up the Nile.

Murad Bey had escaped south after the destruction of his army outside Cairo, and as long as he remained at large with cavalry and bedouin allies, the French hold on the valley was provisional and the Cairo grain supply was insecure. In late August 1798 Napoleon sent Louis Desaix south with a division of a few thousand men to finish him.

The pursuit lasted into 1799 and covered the length of Upper Egypt to the First Cataract and beyond. Desaix fought a series of engagements, at Sediman in October 1798 where the square again broke a Mamluk charge, at Samhud, and repeatedly thereafter, and each time Murad withdrew, reassembled, and reappeared elsewhere. This is the classic problem of a conventional force pursuing a mobile enemy in open country: the French could win any battle Murad accepted and could not compel him to accept one, and their infantry could not catch cavalry. Desaix eventually adapted by acquiring horses, using the river flotilla for mobility and supply, and establishing garrisoned posts, which is recognizably a counterinsurgency method.

The division's condition deteriorated badly. It was operating far from Cairo, in extreme heat, short of supplies, with ophthalmia blinding men in numbers and dysentery constant, under harassment from tribal forces that did not need to fight pitched battles to inflict losses. Desaix's handling of it earned him a reputation among Egyptians recorded in the sources as the just sultan, a label that reflected his discipline over his troops and his dealings with local communities rather than any softness in the campaign itself.

Militarily the operation succeeded: by 1799 Upper Egypt was under French control, the grain moved, and Murad was reduced to negotiating. It also carried the expedition's scholars, including Vivant Denon, past the great temple sites of the south, and Denon's drawings of Dendera, Thebes, Esna, and Philae, made under campaign conditions, became the most influential single body of images the expedition produced.

## What the occupation was like for the soldiers

An army of thirty six thousand Europeans living for three years in a country none of them had chosen to visit produced an extensive literature of complaint, and it is worth registering because it explains the occupation's behavior.

They had expected wealth and found a hot, unfamiliar country where the food disagreed with them, the water made them ill, the wine ran out, and the ordinary comforts of a European garrison did not exist. They were cut off from home, receiving no letters and sending none that arrived, and after August 1798 they knew they could not leave. Pay was irregular because the army was funding itself from a resentful population. Morale problems appeared early and never resolved, and the correspondence intercepted by the British and published in London for propaganda purposes gave the enemy a detailed and accurate picture of an unhappy army.

The relationship with the population deteriorated along predictable lines. Soldiers requisitioned, and requisition from people who have little is indistinguishable from robbery. Religious offense was constant and often unintentional, in a society where the boundaries the French kept crossing were not visible to them. Reprisals for attacks on stragglers were collective and harsh. Some French soldiers married Egyptian women, some converted, and a small number stayed behind in 1801, but the general pattern was an army in a country it did not understand, doing what such armies do.

## The Suez survey and the canal that was not dug

One episode from the occupation deserves separate notice because of what it began.

In December 1798 Napoleon rode to Suez with a party including engineers and surveyors to examine the isthmus and look for traces of the ancient canal that had once connected the Nile to the Red Sea. The party found remains of the old works and conducted a survey under the engineer Jacques-Marie Le Pere, who continued the investigation over the following two years.

Le Pere's survey concluded, incorrectly, that the Red Sea stood significantly higher than the Mediterranean, a measurement error that discouraged canal projects for decades. The error was eventually corrected by later survey work, and the idea itself did not die. The canal that was built through the isthmus in the 1860s traces its modern engineering lineage directly to this survey, and the French involvement in that project drew on a connection to Egypt that the expedition established.

Napoleon also nearly drowned on the trip, caught by the returning tide while examining the shallows, which is a small detail that the memoirists could not resist and which has nothing to do with anything.

## The Cairo revolt of October 1798

On 21 October 1798 Cairo rose, and the suppression of that rising defined the occupation's character for the remainder of its life.

The immediate trigger was the imposition of a new property tax on Cairo households, which fell on exactly the urban classes the French had been courting, on top of forced contributions already extracted. Behind it lay three months of accumulating grievance: the Ottoman declaration of war, which removed any doubt that the French were enemies of the sultan and of Islam; the presence of an occupying army whose soldiers behaved as occupying soldiers do; and the collapse of the pretense that the French had come as liberators.

The rising was serious. French soldiers and officials were killed in the streets, the general Dupuy, commandant of Cairo, was among the dead, and the insurgents fortified themselves in the old quarters with the mosque of al-Azhar as a center of resistance. The French response was to bring artillery onto the heights of the Citadel and bombard the city, then to storm the quarters street by street. Al-Azhar was entered by cavalry, and al-Jabarti's account of what was done inside the mosque, the destruction of books and furnishings and the treatment of the precinct, is the passage in his chronicle that has done more than anything else to fix the occupation's reputation in Egyptian memory.

Casualties among the population ran into the thousands. Napoleon followed the bombardment with executions of identified leaders, then with a deliberate policy of conciliation toward the surviving shaykhs, restoring the divan and cultivating those who had stayed neutral. The combination of overwhelming reprisal followed by calculated leniency became the occupation's standing method.

What the revolt proved was that the political strategy had failed. The French could hold Egypt by force and could not govern it by consent, and from October 1798 onward every subsequent decision was made by a command that knew the population was hostile and the country would have to be garrisoned rather than administered.

## The Syrian campaign

In February 1799 Napoleon marched roughly thirteen thousand men out of Egypt into Ottoman Syria, and the reasons for this apparently reckless move follow directly from the strategic trap the army was in.

An Ottoman army was assembling in Syria under Ahmad al-Jazzar, the formidable governor of Acre, with the intention of invading Egypt overland, while a second Ottoman force was expected by sea with British naval support. Attacking the land threat before it could combine with the sea threat was militarily orthodox. There was also a wider calculation: a victorious French army in Syria might raise local support, detach Ottoman provinces, and open a path that would restore some strategic meaning to the whole enterprise. And there was the simple need to do something, because an army waiting in Egypt to be attacked was an army waiting to disintegrate.

The advance went well at first. The fort at El Arish fell in February, Gaza was taken, and the French reached Jaffa in early March. Jaffa was stormed on 7 March after the defenders refused terms, and the sack that followed was severe. What happened next is one of the darkest episodes of Napoleon's career: several thousand prisoners, the figure is usually given as between two and three thousand and is disputed, were marched to the beach over the following days and shot or bayoneted, on the stated grounds that the army could neither feed nor guard them and that many had been paroled at El Arish and had broken their parole. The killings are documented by French participants and are not seriously contested as fact, only as to numbers and justification.

Plague appeared at Jaffa, having already been present in the region, and from that point the campaign carried an epidemic inside it.

## The siege of Acre

The French reached Acre on 19 March 1799 and stayed in front of it for two months, and the failure there ended any possibility that the campaign could be redeemed.

Acre was a fortified coastal city held by al-Jazzar with a substantial garrison, and it was resupplied continuously from the sea by a British squadron under Sir Sidney Smith. The critical event had already happened before the siege began: Smith intercepted the French flotilla carrying the siege artillery down the coast and captured the guns. Napoleon was therefore attempting to reduce a fortress with field artillery, which cannot breach masonry, against a defense that was being reinforced, fed, and advised from ships he could not touch.

The French made repeated assaults over eight weeks. They mined, they stormed breaches, they got into the works more than once and were thrown out. The defense was stiffened by a French emigre engineer, Antoine de Phelippeaux, who had been a contemporary of Napoleon at military school and who directed the fortification work until he died during the siege. Casualties mounted, plague spread through the camp, and the general Caffarelli was among the dead.

A relieving Ottoman army was destroyed at Mount Tabor on 16 April, where Kleber's division held off vastly superior numbers in square until Napoleon arrived on the flank, which was another tactical triumph with no strategic effect whatever. On 21 May, with the plague spreading and the season advancing, Napoleon raised the siege and marched back to Egypt.

### Why did the siege of Acre fail?

Because a fortress supplied by sea cannot be taken by an army without siege guns or naval power, and the British had captured the French siege train before it arrived. Napoleon had field artillery that could not breach walls, no way to cut the defenders' resupply, and an epidemic in his camp.

The retreat through Palestine and Sinai was worse than the advance. The wounded and the plague-stricken were carried or abandoned, and the visit Napoleon paid to the plague hospital at Jaffa on the way back, later turned into a famous painting, was accompanied by a documented discussion of whether the men who could not be moved should be given a fatal dose of opium. The army reached Cairo in June 1799 having lost roughly a third of the force that set out and having achieved nothing.

## Aboukir 1799: the last victory

In July 1799 the seaborne Ottoman force that the Syrian expedition had been meant to forestall landed at Aboukir, escorted by British and Russian ships, and seized the fort and the peninsula.

Napoleon concentrated rapidly and attacked on 25 July. The Ottoman army, commanded by Mustafa Pasha, had landed without cavalry of consequence and had put itself in a position with the sea at its back and no line of retreat. The French assault, with Murat's cavalry driving into the broken infantry, destroyed it. Large numbers of Ottoman soldiers drowned attempting to reach the ships, Mustafa Pasha was captured, and the French recovered the position within weeks.

It was a genuinely impressive battlefield performance and it changed nothing, which is the pattern of the whole campaign after August 1798. Another Ottoman army could be assembled and shipped. The French army could not be replaced. Victory bought time and nothing else.

It did, however, hand Napoleon something valuable: a recent, unambiguous, reportable triumph. Within a month he used it.

## Why Napoleon left

On the night of 22 August 1799 Napoleon boarded a frigate at Alexandria with a small party of chosen officers and the scholars Monge and Berthollet, slipped past the British blockade, and sailed for France. He left a letter appointing Kleber to command, and Kleber learned of his own appointment and his commander's departure after the ship had gone.

The conventional charge is desertion, and it is not unfair, but the reasoning is worth setting out because it was not panic.

He had learned from newspapers taken from a captured or intercepted vessel, and from Sidney Smith who had allowed such papers to reach him, that the political and military situation in Europe had collapsed for France. The Second Coalition had driven the French out of Italy, the Directory was discredited and unstable, and the conquests of 1796 and 1797 were being lost. A general with a reputation intact and a recent victory to report had an opening in Paris that would not stay open.

He also knew the Egyptian campaign was finished. The fleet was gone, Syria had failed, the population was hostile, the army was shrinking, and no relief was possible. Staying meant presiding over a slow defeat and eventual capitulation, which is precisely what happened to his successors. The calculation was cold and it was correct on its own terms: he preserved a reputation that a surrender in Egypt would have destroyed, reached France in October, and overthrew the Directory in the coup of 18 Brumaire in November.

The men left behind understood exactly what had been done to them. Kleber's reported remarks about his commander were scathing, and his correspondence to the Directory set out the army's true condition in terms that Napoleon's own dispatches had concealed.

## Kleber, El Arish, and Heliopolis

Kleber was a capable and realistic soldier who assessed the situation on taking command and concluded that the army had to be got out of Egypt.

He opened negotiations through Sir Sidney Smith and concluded the Convention of El Arish on 24 January 1800, under which the French army would evacuate Egypt with its arms and equipment and be transported home in British and Ottoman ships. It was a reasonable settlement that would have ended the occupation with the army intact.

The British government repudiated it. Admiral Lord Keith, Smith's superior, informed Kleber that the French must surrender as prisoners of war rather than be repatriated, on the grounds that returning a veteran army of thirty thousand men to France was a strategic gift Britain had no reason to make. The repudiation reached Kleber after the French had already begun evacuating positions, with an Ottoman army advancing on the understanding that the convention held.

Kleber's response was to fight. On 20 March 1800 he attacked the far larger Ottoman army at Heliopolis outside Cairo and destroyed it, then put down a second Cairo rising that had broken out in the belief that the French were leaving, an operation that involved weeks of fighting in the Bulaq quarter and considerable destruction. By the spring of 1800 the French had reestablished control of the country more firmly than at any point since 1798.

On 14 June 1800 Kleber was stabbed to death in the garden of his headquarters in Cairo by Suleiman al-Halabi, a young Syrian student from Aleppo who had come to Egypt for the purpose. The assassin was tried, and executed by impalement after having his hand burned, a punishment the French carried out publicly. On the same day, by coincidence, Desaix was killed at Marengo in Italy, which removed at a stroke the two generals most identified with the campaign.

## Menou and the British landing

Command passed to Jacques-Francois Menou, the senior divisional general present, and the choice was unfortunate.

Menou had genuinely embraced the country. He had converted to Islam, taken the name Abdallah, married an Egyptian woman from Rosetta, and believed sincerely in the project of a permanent French Egypt organized as a colony. His conviction made him the officer least willing to consider evacuation and, as it turned out, the one least capable of handling the military crisis that arrived.

A British expeditionary force under Sir Ralph Abercromby landed at Aboukir on 8 March 1801, executing an opposed amphibious assault against defended beaches with a competence that had been rehearsed for weeks. Menou, who had dispersed his forces and was slow to concentrate, allowed the British to establish themselves and move on Alexandria.

The decisive engagement was fought on 21 March 1801 outside Alexandria, near the ruins the French called Canope. Menou attacked and was repulsed in hard fighting that included a French cavalry attack breaking into a British line and being destroyed. Abercromby was mortally wounded and died a week later. The French withdrew into Alexandria, and the strategic position collapsed.

A second front then opened that is frequently omitted from accounts of the campaign. A British force from India under Major General David Baird, including sepoy regiments of the Indian Army, crossed the Red Sea, landed at Qusayr in the summer of 1801, marched across the eastern desert to the Nile, and moved north. The India that the expedition had been launched to threaten sent an army to help finish it.

## The capitulation

The end came in two stages. The French garrison in Cairo, cut off and surrounded by British and Ottoman forces, capitulated in June 1801 and was repatriated. Menou held out in Alexandria until 2 September 1801, when he surrendered on terms.

The terms permitted the French to return to France with their personal property and with the honors of war, and they produced one celebrated dispute. The British demanded the collections, maps, drawings, and antiquities assembled by the scholars, on the grounds that these were the property of the defeated state. The savants resisted furiously, with threats to destroy their papers rather than surrender them, and the eventual compromise let them keep their notes, drawings, and natural history collections while the large antiquities passed to Britain. The most consequential item in that transfer was a black granodiorite slab found by French soldiers at Rosetta in 1799, whose journey and decipherment are the subject of the article on [the Rosetta Stone and the deciphering of Egypt](/2014/01/01/rosetta-stone-deciphering-egypt/).

The last French troops left Egypt in the autumn of 1801, three years and two months after landing.

## Counting the cost

The campaign's human cost is difficult to establish precisely and easy to underestimate, because battle casualties were the smallest part of it.

Of the roughly thirty six thousand men who landed in 1798, the number repatriated in 1801 is usually given in the region of twenty thousand, with reinforcements and non-combatants complicating any clean comparison. The losses were dominated by disease rather than by combat. Bubonic plague ran through the army repeatedly, most severely during and after the Syrian expedition, and it killed soldiers in numbers no battle approached. Dysentery and other enteric diseases were endemic. Trachoma and other forms of ophthalmia blinded large numbers permanently, a condition so associated with the campaign that it entered European medical literature under names referencing Egypt.

Egyptian losses are harder still to quantify and were certainly larger. They include the dead of the battlefield engagements, the thousands killed in the two Cairo risings and their suppression, the destruction of property and livestock, the disruption of the agricultural cycle in a country where a missed planting means hunger a year later, and the effects of three years of extraction by an army that had to fund itself locally. Al-Jabarti's chronicle registers the cumulative weight of this in a way no casualty table can.

### What killed most of the French soldiers in Egypt?

Disease, by a wide margin. Plague devastated the army during the Syrian campaign and recurred afterward, dysentery was constant, and ophthalmia blinded many who survived. Battle losses across three years were modest by the standards of the European wars, because the French won nearly every engagement they fought.

## Why the campaign failed

Assembling the causes in order of weight produces a clear hierarchy, and the top of it was fixed before the fleet sailed.

The first and decisive cause was sea power. France launched an overseas expedition into a sea controlled by an enemy navy, and no amount of battlefield skill compensates for that. The Battle of the Nile did not create the vulnerability; it exposed one that had existed since the day the convoy left Toulon. Every subsequent problem, the inability to reinforce, the need to tax the occupied population, the impossibility of withdrawal, the loss of the siege train before Acre, followed from the same root.

The second was the absence of a political strategy that could survive contact with reality. The occupation's legitimating story required Ottoman acquiescence, which Talleyrand had promised and never sought, and required that the French appear as liberators, which their fiscal needs made impossible within months. Once the sultan declared war in September 1798, the French were an infidel army occupying a Muslim province against the sovereign's will, and there was no version of the proclamations that could talk around that.

The third was objectives that were never ranked. Was Egypt a colony to be developed, a base against India, a bargaining chip for a future peace, or a place to park a dangerous general? The expedition was sold to different constituencies as each of these, and a commander cannot allocate resources against four incompatible goals. The clearest symptom is that nobody in Paris ever specified what would constitute success or when the army might come home.

The fourth was the mismatch between the army's capabilities and the tasks it faced. It was an exceptional field army. It had no siege train adequate to reduce a fortified port, no naval component after August 1798, no cavalry in any quantity until it could requisition horses locally, and no medical capacity against epidemic disease. It could win any battle in the open and could not take Acre, could not blockade a coast, and could not keep itself alive.

The fifth was the hostility of the population, which was not inevitable and was largely self-inflicted. The Mamluk beys had been unpopular, and a genuinely lighter administration might have found a degree of acquiescence. What the French had to do instead, because they were cut off, was extract harder than the regime they had replaced, while their soldiers gave constant offense and their reprisals after the risings were massive. By 1799 the occupation was garrisoning a country rather than governing one, which is a permanently losing position.

### Could the French have held Egypt?

Only with naval control of the eastern Mediterranean, which France did not have and could not obtain. With a secure sea route, reinforcement and supply were possible and the occupation might have consolidated. Without one, the army was besieged from August 1798 onward and the only question was the date of its surrender.

The table below sets the aims against the outcomes, which is the quickest way to see how completely the strategic ledger came out against the expedition even as the battlefield ledger came out in its favor.

| Element | What was intended | What happened | Deciding evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diplomatic cover | Ottoman acquiescence brokered at Istanbul | Sultan Selim III declared war in September 1798 and allied with Britain and Russia | Talleyrand never went to Istanbul; the alliance is on the record |
| The landing | Secure Alexandria and the Delta | Achieved in two days, 1 to 2 July 1798 | The city fell to assault with light French losses |

## What the invasion destroyed

The occupation's most important effect was the one it accomplished in its first month and then reinforced for three years: the shattering of the Mamluk bey households as a governing force.

Before 1798 the beys were the effective government of Egypt, holding the tax farms, the cavalry, and the offices that mattered while the Ottoman governor held a seal. The French smashed their field army, drove Murad into Upper Egypt where he was pursued relentlessly by Desaix through a punishing series of campaigns up the Nile, drove Ibrahim into Syria, seized their property, and dismantled the tax farms that financed them. Murad eventually came to terms with the French and was left governing part of Upper Egypt on their behalf before dying of plague in 1801.

By the time the French left, the households had lost their revenue base, their accumulated capital, a large proportion of their trained cavalry, and the political monopoly that had let them dictate to governors. They had also lost something less tangible and more important: the presumption of invincibility inside Egypt. An elite that could be destroyed in an afternoon by foreigners is an elite that its own subjects, its bedouin allies, and its Ottoman overlord will calculate differently about afterward.

That the beys remained a factor until 1811 obscures how much had changed. What returned after 1801 was a set of weakened, competing, cash-poor households in a country now containing an Ottoman expeditionary army, British forces for a time, and an assertive Cairo population that had learned during the occupation that it could riot, negotiate, and depose. The beys were no longer the only power in Egypt, and they were never again the strongest.

## What the invasion exposed

The second effect was informational, and it ran in every direction at once.

To Europe, the campaign demonstrated that a modest European force could take an Ottoman province and hold it against everything the empire could send, and that the Ottoman military system was two generations behind. This was not a secret afterward. The eastern question, the long nineteenth-century diplomatic problem of what would happen to Ottoman territories and who would get them, is conventionally dated from around this moment for exactly that reason.

To Istanbul, the lesson was that the empire could not defend its most valuable province and had needed British and Russian help to recover it. The reforming impulse that ran through Ottoman policy in the following decades, the new-model army projects and the administrative restructuring, drew part of its urgency from what 1798 had revealed.

To Egyptians, the lesson was the one that mattered most. Al-Jabarti's chronicle registers, alongside the outrage, a clear-eyed recognition of French technical and organizational capacity: the printing press setting Arabic type, the instruments, the medical practice, the discipline of the troops under fire, the speed with which they built and surveyed. He did not admire the occupation and he did not pretend that what he had seen was unimpressive. A generation of Egyptian administrators formed their view of what a state could do partly from watching one operate in their country for three years.

## The opening of Egypt

The third effect was the permanent entry of European power into Egyptian affairs, which had a commercial, diplomatic, and eventually a political dimension.

Before 1798 European states dealt with Egypt as a distant Ottoman province through consuls who negotiated with whichever bey was dominant. After 1801 Britain and France treated Egypt as an object of great-power competition in its own right, with resident agents whose influence on Egyptian politics was direct and often decisive. British and French rivalry over Egypt structures the next century and a half of the country's history, running through the Muhammad Ali crisis of the 1830s, the construction of the Suez Canal, the debt commissions, and the occupation of 1882.

Commercially the occupation had wrecked more than it built, but it had also surveyed, mapped, and catalogued the country in detail, and that information was available afterward to anyone interested in what Egypt could produce. The nineteenth-century transformation of Egyptian agriculture toward export crops had many causes, and traced in the article on [trade and decline in Ottoman Egypt](/2013/08/01/trade-decline-ottoman-egypt/) is the older commercial pattern it replaced, but the French survey work is where systematic European knowledge of Egyptian agricultural capacity begins.

## The scholarly consequence

The fourth effect is the one the campaign is now best remembered for, and it is genuinely enormous, though it belongs in detail to a separate article.

The commission of scholars spent three years measuring, drawing, excavating, collecting, and describing, and the resulting publication, issued over decades, established both the modern European image of ancient Egypt and the foundations of an academic discipline. The discovery of the Rosetta Stone during fortification work at Rosetta in 1799 supplied the key that would unlock hieroglyphic writing two decades later. The institute founded in Cairo in 1798 was the first modern scientific academy in the Arab world.

None of this was a French gift, and framing it as one is a nineteenth-century habit worth resisting. It was a by-product of a colonial expedition, conducted without Egyptian consent, and its most valuable single object was taken to London as a spoil of war. It was also, on its own terms, an extraordinary piece of collective scholarship whose accuracy in recording monuments that were subsequently damaged or destroyed makes it indispensable. Both things are true. The full account is in the article on [how Napoleon's scholars revealed Egypt](/2013/12/01/napoleon-scholars-rediscovered-egypt/).

## The occupation and Egypt's religious minorities

One of the occupation's least discussed and most damaging legacies concerns the position of Egypt's Christian and Jewish communities, and it deserves stating carefully because it is easy to get wrong in either direction.

The French did not arrive intending to reorganize communal relations. What they did was recruit administrators where they could find competent ones, and the fiscal administration of Ottoman Egypt had long employed Coptic Christian scribes and accountants, who understood the registers and the land-tax categories better than anyone. The most prominent, Muallim Yaqub, had served the beys in exactly the same capacity and now served the French, eventually raising an auxiliary formation and leaving Egypt with the army in 1801.

The French also suspended some of the legal distinctions of Ottoman practice, including restrictions on dress and on the public conduct of non-Muslim worship, less out of principle than from indifference to categories they did not recognize.

The consequences fell on the communities themselves. Visible association with an infidel occupation, in a society where the communal order had been stable for centuries, exposed Copts and Jews to resentment that had no outlet while the French were present and considerable outlet once they left. Al-Jabarti registers the shift in tone. The episode did not create communal tension in Egypt, which had its own long history, but it introduced a specific and lasting pattern in which European powers claimed a protective interest in local Christian populations and local Christians were suspected of foreign attachment. That pattern runs through the nineteenth century and well beyond it.

## Grain, flood, and the occupation economy

An army of thirty six thousand men eating in a country whose food supply depended on a single annual flood was an economic event in itself, and the occupation's fiscal record cannot be separated from the agricultural cycle.

Egypt's grain system was seasonal and unforgiving. The flood filled the basins from late summer, planting followed the recession, and the harvest came in spring, which meant that disruption at any stage produced hunger a full year later rather than immediately. The French arrived in July 1798, at the start of the flood, fought their campaigns across the countryside through the following two years, requisitioned draft animals and grain, and interrupted the labor of villages in the districts where fighting or pursuit occurred.

They also understood the problem and tried to manage it, which is to their credit and did not solve it. The Cairo divan's most consistent business was grain supply, the French surveyed the canals and attempted repairs in some districts, and Desaix's campaign in Upper Egypt was motivated substantially by securing the southern grain that fed the capital. None of it could compensate for an occupation that needed to extract more revenue than the regime it replaced while conducting military operations in the country that produced the food.

## The vacuum and the man who filled it

The most consequential single result of the invasion was something no one intended and nobody could have predicted from the campaign's own logic.

When the French left in 1801, Egypt contained four armed parties and no settled authority: the surviving bey households, weakened and competing; the Ottoman expeditionary army sent to expel the French, including a contingent of Albanian irregulars; a British force that withdrew in 1803; and the population and religious establishment of Cairo, which had spent three years discovering that it could act politically.

Istanbul tried to reassert direct control and failed, partly through an attempt to trap and kill the bey leaders that succeeded only in deepening the disorder. The Albanian contingent, unpaid and cohesive, became the most reliable armed force in the country, and its second in command built systematic relationships with the Cairo merchants and with the shaykhs of al-Azhar. In 1805 the ulama, exercising exactly the political weight they had learned they possessed, demanded the removal of the sitting governor and the appointment of that officer in his place.

His name was Muhammad Ali, and the state he built, examined in the article on [Muhammad Ali and the birth of modern Egypt](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/), was constructed out of the lessons of what he had watched. He centralized revenue, abolished the tax farms, conscripted peasants into drilled infantry, hired European instructors, built an artillery establishment and a navy, and in 1811 destroyed the Mamluk households entirely.

The causal chain is direct and worth stating plainly. Without the French invasion the bey households would not have been broken; without their breaking Istanbul would not have sent an expeditionary force; without that force the Albanian contingent and its officer would not have been in Egypt; and without three years of occupation the Cairo religious establishment would not have had either the confidence or the precedent to make and unmake governors. Napoleon did not intend to create modern Egypt. He cleared the ground for someone else to.

## How Egyptians remembered it

The Egyptian memory of the occupation is not the European one, and reading the campaign only through French memoirs produces a badly distorted picture.

The indispensable source is al-Jabarti, whose chronicle covers the occupation year by year from inside Cairo. He wrote more than one account of the period, and his perspective is that of a scholar of the religious establishment who served on the French divan, observed the occupiers closely, and judged them severely. He records the bombardment of the city and the entry into al-Azhar, the executions, the exactions, the behavior of soldiers, and the humiliation of being governed by people who did not share the fundamental assumptions of the society they ruled. He also records the printing press, the instruments, the hospitals, and the discipline, without contradiction, because both were what he saw.

Popular memory in Egypt fixed on the risings rather than on the battles, which is the correct emphasis from an Egyptian standpoint: the Cairo revolts were the moments when Egyptians acted rather than were acted upon. Suleiman al-Halabi, who killed Kleber, occupies an ambiguous position, remembered variously as an assassin, a martyr, and an instrument of others.

What did not happen is the emergence of any Egyptian enthusiasm for the occupation, then or later. The notion, occasionally advanced in European writing, that the French brought enlightenment to a grateful population is unsupported by any Egyptian source. What the occupation produced among Egyptians who thought about it was a sharpened awareness of the gap in capability between their government and a European state, which is a different thing entirely from gratitude.

## How historians have judged it

Scholarly assessment of the campaign has moved considerably, and knowing where it has moved is useful for anyone writing about it.

Nineteenth-century French writing, working from the memoirs of participants and from the great scholarly publication, treated the expedition as a civilizing mission interrupted by British perfidy, and this framing shaped European understanding for generations. British writing treated it as an overreach defeated by naval superiority, which is accurate as far as it goes and incurious about everything else.

Twentieth-century work complicated both. Historians examining the Ottoman and Egyptian sources, among them scholars working on al-Jabarti and on the Ottoman archives, established that the province was neither the stagnant backwater nor the blank slate the French accounts assumed. Henry Laurens, in the most detailed modern French treatment, reconstructed the expedition's political and intellectual context in a way that takes the colonial project seriously as a project rather than as an adventure. Juan Cole's study of the occupation reads the campaign substantially through Egyptian and Arabic evidence and through the experience of occupation, and it is the most direct corrective to the heroic version.

The interpretive question that remains genuinely open is how much of nineteenth-century Egyptian change should be attributed to the invasion. The older view treated 1798 as the beginning of modern Egypt, the moment the country was jolted into the modern world. The revisionist view points out that Ottoman Egypt had its own dynamics of change before the French arrived, that Muhammad Ali's methods owed as much to Ottoman reform models as to French example, and that crediting a three-year occupation with a century of transformation is a piece of European self-flattery. The balanced position, and the one this article takes, is that the invasion did not cause Egyptian modernization but destroyed the specific political arrangement that was preventing it, which is a real and large effect stated at its correct size.

## What the French actually found

The expedition arrived carrying a picture of Egypt drawn from classical authors and travelers' accounts, and the gap between that picture and the country in front of them shaped everything they did.

They expected a fabulously rich province badly governed. They found a country of perhaps three to four million people, a figure the savants attempted to establish and which remains an estimate, with agriculture concentrated in the Nile valley and Delta, a large and genuinely impressive capital, and a level of rural poverty that shocked officers who had campaigned in Italy. The irrigation system, which depended on coordinated maintenance of canals and dikes, was in poor condition in many districts, a direct consequence of a tax-farming regime that rewarded extraction and penalized investment.

They expected ancient monuments and found them, though not where the classical sources suggested and often buried, quarried, or reused. Alexandria was a ruin with a living town attached. Cairo, by contrast, was among the largest cities in the world with a dense and sophisticated urban economy, guilds, an enormous commercial quarter, and a great religious university, and French accounts of it are considerably more respectful than their accounts of the countryside.

They expected to be welcomed against the beys and were not. The assumption that a population oppressed by a foreign military caste would prefer a different set of foreigners was the campaign's foundational political error, and it rested on not understanding that the beys, whatever else they were, were Muslims operating inside a legitimate Ottoman frame, while the French were not.

They expected to feed and pay an army from local resources and found a fiscal system they did not understand, administered in a language they could not read, through categories that took them a year to learn. Much of the occupation's administrative effort went into simply working out who owed what, which is why the surveys and registers occupy so much of the record.

## What the campaign taught European armies

The Egyptian expedition is a case study that entered professional military education almost immediately, and the lessons drawn from it are visible in how later campaigns were planned.

The first was about combined operations. An overseas expedition is a naval undertaking that happens to carry soldiers, and the decisive planning variable is not the quality of the army but the security of the sea route. British planning for later amphibious operations reflects this understanding thoroughly; the 1801 landing at Aboukir, conducted with rehearsed boat waves, naval gunfire support, and a clear beach organization, is often cited as the first modern opposed landing, and it was executed by the side that had learned the lesson at French expense.

The second was about the infantry square, which the campaign demonstrated on an unusually pure test case. Against cavalry with no artillery support, a square of steady infantry is effectively invulnerable. The French squares at Shubra Khit and outside Cairo are the textbook illustration, and the tactic's limits, which are that a square is a slow and passive formation vulnerable to artillery, were also visible in that the French could not use it to pursue or destroy a mobile enemy who declined to charge.

The third was about siege capability, and it is the lesson the campaign teaches by failure. Napoleon before Acre had an excellent army, a competent engineer corps, and no heavy guns, and the result was eight weeks of casualties for nothing. Armies plan siege trains as a separate logistical problem afterward.

The fourth was medical, and it took longer to absorb. The campaign's losses to plague, dysentery, and ophthalmia vastly exceeded its battle losses, and the French medical service, which included capable men such as the surgeon Larrey, learned a great deal about field hospitals, quarantine, and the management of epidemic disease in an army under the worst possible conditions. That accumulated experience fed into military medicine for decades.

## Was it a colonial project?

The question matters because the answer determines how the campaign should be classified, and the evidence points clearly in one direction while the rhetoric points in another.

The rhetoric was liberationist. The proclamations promised freedom from Mamluk tyranny, the institute was presented as an enterprise of universal science, and French writing afterward framed the whole venture as the carrying of enlightenment to a country that had lost its ancient glory.

The structure was colonial in every particular. The expedition brought administrators, surveyors, and a printing press because it intended to stay. It established a government in which all executive authority rested with French officers and Egyptians held consultative positions only. It reorganized taxation to fund the metropolitan power's army. It conducted a comprehensive survey of the country's resources, population, and antiquities of precisely the kind a colonial administration requires. Menou's plans, drawn up in earnest, envisaged permanent French settlement and the reorganization of landholding. And the antiquities collected were regarded as transferable property, which is why their fate became a clause in a capitulation agreement.

The honest classification is that this was an early modern European colonial occupation conducted with revolutionary vocabulary, and that the vocabulary was not merely cynical. Many of the participants, Menou most obviously, believed both things at once: that they were improving a country and that they were taking it. That combination is the characteristic posture of nineteenth-century colonialism, and the Egyptian expedition is where it first appears in fully developed form in the Arab world, which is a large part of why the campaign has attracted so much attention from historians of empire.

## The counterfactual worth considering

Historians are rightly wary of counterfactuals, but one is analytically useful here because it isolates the causal weight of the naval question.

Suppose Brueys had taken the fleet to Corfu or back to Toulon after the landing, as Napoleon had in fact authorized if a safe Egyptian anchorage could not be found, and the ships had survived. France would have retained thirteen ships of the line in the Mediterranean, the army would have had a communication route that was contested rather than closed, reinforcement would have been difficult rather than impossible, and the strategic threat to the Levant trade and the route to India would have remained live.

Would the occupation then have lasted? Probably longer, and possibly much longer, but the political problems would not have gone away. The Ottoman declaration of war was driven by the seizure of a province, not by the naval outcome. The hostility of the population came from taxation and religion, not from Aboukir Bay. A supplied French Egypt would have been a contested colony fighting a long counterinsurgency against Ottoman-backed resistance, which is an expensive commitment for a France that was simultaneously fighting Austria, Russia, and Britain in Europe.

The value of the exercise is in showing what the naval disaster did and did not cause. It did not cause the political failure, which was designed into the expedition. It converted a difficult colonial project into an immediate military catastrophe and compressed a decade of probable attrition into three years. The campaign was going to fail. Nelson determined how, and how fast.

## The other side of the war

Accounts written from the French records make the campaign look like a French story with a British naval interruption, which understates how much the opposing coalition did and how unusual it was.

The Ottoman response was faster and more serious than the French had assumed it would be. Selim III declared war in September 1798, and the empire raised two field armies, one in Syria under the governor of Acre and one to be shipped to Egypt, while also mobilizing the religious authority of the sultanate by framing the war as a defense of Muslim territory against an infidel invasion. The Syrian army was the one Napoleon marched north to forestall; the seaborne army was the one destroyed at Aboukir in 1799 and the one Kleber destroyed again at Heliopolis in 1800. Ottoman forces lost heavily every time they met the French in the field and kept coming, which is the strategic point: the empire could absorb defeats that the French could not.

The British contribution was naval first and military later. Nelson's destruction of the fleet was the single decisive act of the war, and the follow-on blockade that cut the French off was sustained for three years at considerable cost. Sidney Smith's squadron on the Syrian coast did two things that shaped the campaign directly: it captured the French siege train bound for Acre, and it supplied and stiffened the defense of the city. The land expedition under Abercromby in 1801 was a carefully prepared operation rather than an improvisation, and it was joined by the force from India under Baird approaching from the Red Sea.

The Russian element is the most surprising. Russia and the Ottoman Empire had fought each other repeatedly through the eighteenth century, and the alliance of 1798 was a direct product of French overreach: a Russian squadron passed the straits with Ottoman consent, operated in the eastern Mediterranean, and took the Ionian Islands from France. An invasion intended to break Britain instead produced a temporary alignment of Britain, the Ottoman Empire, and Russia against France, which is a diplomatic outcome as comprehensively adverse as any in the period.

The coalition's coordination was imperfect, and the repudiation of the El Arish convention shows the strain between a British government pursuing a war against France and a British naval officer on the spot pursuing the quickest removal of a French army from Egypt. But it held, and it recovered the province, which is more than the alliance managed in most theatres of that war.

## How we know: the sources for the campaign

The Egyptian expedition is one of the best documented military operations before the age of photography, and the abundance creates its own problems.

French participant memoirs are numerous and were largely written after the fact by men with reputations to manage, several of them under a regime in which the expedition's commander had become emperor. They are indispensable for detail and unreliable for judgment, particularly on the Jaffa killings, the plague hospital, and the circumstances of Napoleon's departure, where the accounts diverge in ways that track the writers' later political positions.

Official French correspondence, including Napoleon's dispatches and the army's administrative records, survives extensively and is valuable precisely because it was written during events rather than afterward. It should be read with the knowledge that Napoleon's dispatches were composed for political effect from the start, and that his own reporting of the campaign's condition was consistently more optimistic than Kleber's assessments written weeks later.

The Egyptian side is carried by al-Jabarti above all, whose year-by-year chronicle is the only substantial contemporary Arabic narrative of the occupation from inside Cairo. Other Arabic material includes correspondence, legal records from the religious courts, and the documents of the divan, and the court registers in particular allow property seizures and forced contributions to be traced concretely rather than through complaint.

British and Ottoman records supply the other two perspectives: Admiralty and army correspondence, Nelson's and Sidney Smith's dispatches, and Ottoman state documents concerning the declaration of war, the expeditionary forces, and the settlement afterward.

The scholarly publication produced by the commission is a source of a different type. It records the country as it was at a specific moment with a thoroughness nothing else approaches, and it is simultaneously a document of the colonial gaze that requires reading critically as evidence about the observers as well as the observed.

## The myths worth correcting

Several persistent stories about this campaign are either false or badly distorted, and clearing them away improves the picture.

The image of a triumphant Napoleon in Egypt is the largest distortion. He won every battle he fought there and lost the campaign comprehensively, and he left it by slipping away from an army he never saw again. The heroic framing comes from imperial-era painting and from memoirs written under his rule, not from the record of 1798 to 1801.

The claim that French artillery shot the nose off the Sphinx is false. The nose was already missing long before 1798, and a description from the fifteenth century by the Egyptian historian al-Maqrizi attributes the damage to a much earlier act of iconoclasm. Drawings made before the French arrival show the monument noseless.

The idea that Napoleon converted to Islam is a confusion with Menou, who did convert, married an Egyptian woman, and took the name Abdallah. Napoleon flirted with the possibility rhetorically and never approached it seriously.

The notion that the expedition discovered ancient Egypt requires heavy qualification. European travelers had been describing and drawing Egyptian monuments for centuries, and Egyptians had lived among them continuously. What the commission did was record them systematically and at scale, which is a real achievement correctly described.

The belief that the Rosetta Stone was deciphered by the French expedition compresses two decades of work by several people in several countries into a single event. The stone was found in 1799 and surrendered to Britain in 1801; the decipherment came later and elsewhere.

And the assumption that the campaign was a rash adventure of a young general acting alone misstates the politics. It was approved by the Directory, advocated by the foreign ministry, prepared over months, and rooted in a French strategic tradition a century old. The failures were institutional, not merely personal.

## The verdict

The brilliant-failure thesis holds on both halves, and the two halves need stating separately because collapsing them is what produces the bad popular account.

On the military and strategic side, the invasion failed completely. Every objective that could be named in Paris in the spring of 1798 went unachieved. Egypt was not turned into a French colony. Britain's route to India was not threatened for more than a month. The Levant trade was not secured. The Ottoman Empire was not conciliated but driven into alliance with France's two principal enemies. The army was not preserved as a strategic asset but consumed, and its survivors came home in British ships. The campaign cost France a fleet, a large veteran army's effective strength, and its position in the eastern Mediterranean, and it gained France nothing that outlasted 1801.

The tactical record is the opposite and should not be used to soften the strategic one. The French won essentially every land engagement they fought over three years, frequently against much larger forces, at Shubra Khit, outside Cairo, at Mount Tabor, at Aboukir, at Heliopolis. The formations worked, the artillery was handled well, the divisional commanders were excellent. None of it mattered, and the reason it did not matter is the single most transferable lesson the campaign offers: tactical excellence cannot repair a strategy that has no answer to the enemy's decisive advantage. Britain's decisive advantage was the fleet, and France had chosen to fight where the fleet decided everything.

On the consequential side, the invasion was among the most transformative events in Egyptian history. It broke the bey households that had governed the country since the Ottoman conquest, and their destruction in 1811 by Muhammad Ali was the completion of a demolition the French began. It exposed Ottoman military weakness to everyone including the Ottomans. It brought European power into Egyptian politics permanently, in a form that ran from consular influence through debt supervision to outright occupation in 1882. It produced the scholarly enterprise that founded a discipline and unlocked a writing system. And it created the exact conditions in which an Albanian officer could take a country.

Holding both findings together is the whole analytical task. The invasion mattered enormously and succeeded at nothing, and the reason it mattered was the wreckage rather than the achievement. A campaign can be a complete failure on its own terms and still be the hinge on which a country's history turns, because history does not reward intentions and does not require that consequences follow from anyone's plan.

The final judgment on Napoleon's part in it is narrower than either the admirers or the detractors usually allow. He conducted the campaign about as well as it could have been conducted after August 1798, and it should not have been attempted in the form it took, and he attempted it anyway because the objectives were never clear enough for anyone to ask the one question that would have stopped it. What happens to this army if we lose control of the sea? The answer was available before the fleet left Toulon. Nobody asked.

## Using this for study and revision

The campaign is dense with dates and engagements that blur together, and the way to hold it is by causal structure rather than by sequence. Four anchors carry almost everything: the landing on 1 July 1798, the Battle of the Nile on 1 and 2 August 1798, Napoleon's departure in August 1799, and the capitulation of Alexandria on 2 September 1801. Everything else hangs from those.

The analytical points worth fixing are the distinction between tactical success and strategic failure, the five causes of failure in order of weight, the difference between what the campaign intended and what it caused, and the specific chain that connects the invasion to the rise of Muhammad Ali. If you can argue those four, you can handle any question an examiner is likely to put about 1798.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why did Napoleon invade Egypt?

Three motives converged rather than one. France had a century-old strategic tradition of viewing Egypt as a potential colony, backed by substantial commercial interests in the Levant and by decades of merchant complaints about the Mamluk regime. There was a war aim: after peace on the continent left Britain undefeated at sea and a direct invasion of England judged impracticable, Egypt offered an indirect route to hurt Britain by threatening the short passage toward India and its Levant trade. And there was domestic politics: the Directory had an enormously popular general of uncertain loyalty and was glad to send him somewhere far away where he might also do the Republic some good. The foreign minister Talleyrand promoted the project and promised to reconcile the Ottoman sultan to it, a promise he never attempted, which left the expedition without the diplomatic cover it had been built around.

### Q: What year did Napoleon land in Egypt?

The army landed on 1 July 1798 at Marabout, an open beach west of Alexandria, and stormed Alexandria the following morning. The fleet had sailed from Toulon on 19 May, seized Malta on 12 June, and crossed the Mediterranean without being intercepted. The disembarkation was made at night in heavy surf without a harbor, and it cost men and equipment, because Napoleon had learned from a captured vessel that a British squadron had been off the coast days earlier and refused to risk being caught at sea. The occupation that began that week lasted until the capitulation of Alexandria on 2 September 1801, so the French presence in Egypt ran three years and two months in total, though Napoleon himself was present for only fourteen months of it.

### Q: How big was Napoleon's army in Egypt?

Roughly thirty six thousand soldiers, and the figure should be treated as an order of magnitude rather than a precise count, since estimates in the literature range from about thirty four thousand to about forty thousand depending on whether sailors, support staff, and later arrivals are included. The naval escort was thirteen ships of the line under Admiral Brueys, with the hundred and eighteen gun flagship Orient, supported by frigates and roughly four hundred transports. The army was drawn substantially from the veteran Army of Italy and included officers who would lead French armies for the next fifteen years. It also carried a civilian commission of around one hundred and sixty scholars, engineers, surveyors, and draftsmen, with a press capable of setting Arabic type.

### Q: What did Napoleon want in Egypt?

He wanted a permanent French possession, not a raid. The presence of surveyors, administrators, a printing press, and a scientific commission signals an intention to stay, and the administrative effort the French poured into registers, cadastral surveys, and tax reorganization makes the colonial purpose unambiguous. Within that, he wanted a base on the isthmus between the Mediterranean and the Red Sea that would give France a position on the route toward the Indian Ocean, a source of agricultural wealth to replace lost Caribbean trade, and a strategic threat that forced Britain to divert resources. He also wanted, personally, a campaign spectacular enough to keep his reputation growing while he waited for the political situation in Paris to produce an opening.

### Q: How did Napoleon justify the invasion?

Publicly he claimed to come as a friend of the Ottoman sultan and an enemy only of the Mamluk beys, presenting the French as liberating Egypt from a foreign military caste that oppressed it. His Arabic proclamation, printed on the expedition's press, went further and argued that the French were closer to true believers than the Mamluks were, since they had overthrown a pope and destroyed the authority of priests. The Cairo chronicler al-Jabarti dismantled the text in detail, criticizing its grammar, its theology, and the logic of liberation delivered by an army that immediately levied contributions. The justification collapsed entirely when the sultan declared war in September 1798, after which the French were an infidel army occupying a province against its sovereign's will.

### Q: Was Napoleon's Egyptian campaign a success or failure?

A tactical success and a strategic failure, and the two should not be collapsed. The French won essentially every land engagement they fought across three years, often against far larger forces, and their infantry formations and artillery handling were excellent. Not one of the campaign's stated objectives was achieved. Egypt did not become a French colony, Britain's route to India was threatened for barely a month, the Levant trade was not secured, the Ottoman Empire was driven into alliance with Britain and Russia, and the army was consumed rather than preserved. France lost a fleet, a veteran army's effective strength, and its position in the eastern Mediterranean, and gained nothing that outlasted 1801. The lasting significance lies entirely in the consequences, not in the outcome.

### Q: How did the British defeat Napoleon in Egypt?

Primarily at sea, and then on land three years later. Nelson found the French fleet anchored in Aboukir Bay on 1 August 1798 and destroyed it overnight, taking or burning eleven of thirteen ships of the line, which cut the French army off from reinforcement, supply, pay, and any possibility of withdrawal. A sustained blockade kept it cut off. Sir Sidney Smith's squadron then captured the French siege train bound for Acre and supplied the defense of that city, which broke the Syrian campaign. The land campaign came in 1801, when Sir Ralph Abercromby executed a carefully rehearsed opposed landing at Aboukir in March, defeated Menou outside Alexandria on the twenty first, and was joined by a force from India that crossed the Red Sea and marched from Qusayr.

### Q: Why did Napoleon leave Egypt?

Because the campaign was lost and an opportunity at home was closing. He learned from intercepted European newspapers that France had suffered severe reverses, that the conquests of 1796 and 1797 were being lost, and that the Directory was discredited and unstable. He also knew his own position was hopeless: the fleet was gone, the Syrian expedition had failed at Acre, the population was hostile after two risings, the army was shrinking to disease, and no relief could reach him. Staying meant presiding over a slow defeat and eventual capitulation, which is exactly what happened to his successors. He sailed from Alexandria on the night of 22 August 1799 with a small chosen party, slipped past the blockade, reached France in October, and overthrew the Directory in November.

### Q: What was the Battle of the Nile?

The naval action fought in Aboukir Bay on the night of 1 to 2 August 1798, twenty miles northeast of Alexandria, and the decisive event of the whole campaign. Unable to enter Alexandria's silted harbor, Admiral Brueys had moored his thirteen ships of the line in a line in the open bay, relying on shoal water to protect the landward flank. Nelson attacked immediately in failing light, and several of his captains took part of the British line inside the French line, so that French ships were engaged simultaneously on both sides while their landward guns were not cleared for action. The flagship Orient caught fire and exploded. By morning eleven French ships of the line had been captured or destroyed and only two escaped.

### Q: Did the French capture Malta on the way?

Yes, on 12 June 1798, and it was a genuine strategic prize. The island had been held since the sixteenth century by the Knights of St John, by then a wealthy anachronism governing fortifications of enormous value with a garrison that had no appetite for a defense. A significant proportion of the knights were themselves French and unwilling to fight their countrymen, and resistance was token. Napoleon spent about a week abolishing the order's rule, reorganizing the island's government, seizing the treasury and the accumulated plate and jewels, and installing a French garrison. Malta commands the central Mediterranean and mattered more to the French naval position than anything that happened afterward in Egypt, which is why its later loss to Britain was so consequential.

### Q: What happened at the siege of Acre?

The French reached the fortified coastal city on 19 March 1799 and besieged it for two months without taking it. Acre was held by Ahmad al-Jazzar, the formidable Ottoman governor, and was continuously resupplied from the sea by a British squadron under Sir Sidney Smith, who had already captured the French siege artillery in transit down the coast. Napoleon was therefore trying to breach masonry with field guns that cannot do it, against a defense being fed, reinforced, and advised from ships he could not touch. The French mined, stormed breaches repeatedly, and got into the works more than once before being thrown out. Plague spread through the camp, a French emigre engineer named Phelippeaux directed the defense works until he died, and the siege was raised on 21 May 1799.

### Q: Who commanded the French army after Napoleon sailed home?

Jean-Baptiste Kleber, who learned of his own appointment only after the ship had gone and whose reported remarks about his commander were scathing. Kleber assessed the position realistically, concluded the army had to be extracted, and negotiated the Convention of El Arish in January 1800 for an evacuation with arms and honors. When the British government repudiated it he attacked and destroyed a much larger Ottoman army at Heliopolis in March 1800, then suppressed a second Cairo rising. He was assassinated in his headquarters garden on 14 June 1800 by Suleiman al-Halabi, a young Syrian from Aleppo. Command then passed to Jacques-Francois Menou, who had converted to Islam, married an Egyptian woman, believed in permanent French settlement, and handled the British invasion of 1801 poorly.

### Q: What was the Cairo revolt against the French?

The rising of 21 October 1798, triggered immediately by a new property tax on Cairo households imposed on top of forced contributions, and driven more deeply by the Ottoman declaration of war, the behavior of the occupying troops, and the collapse of the claim that the French had come as liberators. French soldiers and officials were killed in the streets, including Dupuy, the commandant of Cairo, and insurgents fortified the old quarters with al-Azhar as a center of resistance. The French brought artillery onto the Citadel heights, bombarded the city, and then cleared the quarters street by street, entering al-Azhar with cavalry. Al-Jabarti's account of what was done inside the mosque precinct has shaped Egyptian memory of the occupation more than any other passage. Casualties among the population ran into the thousands.

### Q: Did plague strike the French army?

Repeatedly and devastatingly, and it killed far more French soldiers than any battle did. Bubonic plague was present in the region and appeared in the army at Jaffa during the Syrian expedition in March 1799, then spread through the camp before Acre and traveled back to Egypt with the retreating column. Napoleon's visit to the plague hospital at Jaffa, later made famous by a painting, was accompanied by a documented discussion of whether men too ill to be moved should be given a fatal dose of opium. Alongside plague, dysentery and other enteric diseases were constant, and trachoma and related forms of ophthalmia blinded large numbers of soldiers permanently, a condition so associated with the campaign that it entered European medical writing under names referencing Egypt.

### Q: When did the French finally leave Egypt?

The occupation ended in stages during 1801. A British force landed at Aboukir on 8 March, defeated Menou outside Alexandria on 21 March in a battle where the British commander Abercromby was mortally wounded, and a second British force from India crossed the Red Sea and marched from Qusayr. The French garrison in Cairo, cut off and surrounded, capitulated in June 1801 and was repatriated. Menou held out in Alexandria until 2 September 1801, surrendering on terms that allowed the French to return home with the honors of war. The last troops sailed that autumn, three years and two months after the landing. A dispute over the scholars' collections formed part of the surrender terms, and the large antiquities, including the Rosetta Stone, passed to Britain.

### Q: What was the Convention of El Arish?

An evacuation agreement concluded on 24 January 1800 between Kleber and Sir Sidney Smith, under which the French army would leave Egypt with its arms and equipment and be carried home in British and Ottoman ships. It was a sensible settlement that would have ended the occupation with the army intact and saved two more years of fighting. The British government repudiated it. Admiral Lord Keith, Smith's superior, informed Kleber that the French must surrender as prisoners of war instead, on the reasoning that returning thirty thousand veterans to France was a strategic gift Britain had no reason to make. The repudiation arrived after the French had already begun evacuating positions and an Ottoman army was advancing on the understanding that the convention held, which is why Kleber fought at Heliopolis.

### Q: Did Napoleon claim to be a friend of Islam?

He did, repeatedly and instrumentally. His Arabic proclamations invoked God, used Quranic phrasing, claimed that the French had overthrown a pope and destroyed the authority of priests, and argued that they respected Islam more than the Mamluk beys did. He took part in public Islamic festivals, cultivated the shaykhs of al-Azhar, appointed them to his divan, and at one point floated the possibility that the army might convert, a suggestion the ulama reportedly met by raising circumcision and wine, which ended the conversation. The purpose was to secure the cooperation of the religious establishment and to supply a legitimating story that did not depend on French republicanism, which meant nothing in Egypt. He never converted. Menou, his eventual successor in command, actually did.

### Q: Was the invasion really aimed at British India?

Partly, and the claim is usually overstated. The threat to India featured prominently in the argument made to the Directory, Napoleon corresponded with Tipu Sultan of Mysore, who was then fighting the British in southern India, and after arriving he personally investigated the Suez isthmus and the Red Sea route. The British took the threat seriously enough to redeploy naval strength and eventually to land an Indian army on the Red Sea coast. What is not credible is a French army marching from Cairo to Delhi, for which the logistics were impossible and for which no force or shipping was ever assembled. The realistic version is narrower: a French Egypt would hold the short route to the Indian Ocean and force Britain to divert resources permanently. That objective died with the fleet.

### Q: How did the French administer the country they occupied?

Through local intermediaries with all real authority kept in French hands. Napoleon created a divan in Cairo of religious scholars, merchants, and notables, with similar councils in the provinces, giving them consultative roles over taxation, grain, and public order while French officers decided everything. Most appointees were shaykhs of al-Azhar, held responsible by the French for keeping the city quiet and by their own community for collaborating. Fiscal policy was the central failure: cut off from France, the army had to extract more than the Mamluk regime had, through confiscations, forced contributions on merchants, and a house tax that triggered the October rising. Alongside the extraction came surveys, registers, street lighting, quarantine measures, hospitals, and the Institut d'Egypte, founded in August 1798.

### Q: What happened at Aboukir in 1799?

The land battle of 25 July 1799, not to be confused with the naval action in the same bay a year earlier. An Ottoman army had been landed at Aboukir under British and Russian naval escort and had seized the fort and peninsula, intending to advance on Cairo. Napoleon concentrated rapidly and attacked. The Ottoman force had landed without cavalry of consequence and had placed itself with the sea at its back and no line of retreat, and the French assault, with Murat's cavalry driving into broken infantry, destroyed it. Large numbers drowned trying to reach the ships and the commander Mustafa Pasha was captured. It was an impressive battlefield performance that changed nothing strategically, but it gave Napoleon a recent, reportable victory, and within a month he used it and sailed for France.
