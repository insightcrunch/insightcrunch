In September 1970 a Marxist coalition won a national election in Chile and did something no Marxist movement had done before: it took office through the ballot box and announced that it would transform the country's property relations by legal means, using the constitution it had sworn to uphold. Salvador Allende, the coalition's candidate and a lifelong parliamentarian, became president of a republic whose congress, courts, press and armed forces remained in other hands. The experiment that followed, known as the Chilean road to socialism, was not improvised. It was an explicit strategic claim, argued for years inside the Chilean left, about how socialism could be reached in a country with strong institutions: not by storming the state but by winning it, piece by piece, through elections and law. That claim is why the case matters beyond its own tragedy. The argument between reform and revolution, which runs through the whole history of Marxism, has many theoretical texts and very few controlled tests. Chile between 1970 and 1973 is the principal piece of evidence in that argument, the one case where the democratic road was attempted in full, by a government that meant it, and ended by a military coup. Everything written about the case since has been, in some measure, a dispute over what the evidence shows.

![The presidential palace in Santiago and the documentary record of the Chilean road, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The dispute has usually been conducted as a quarrel between two single causes. One tradition holds that the government was destroyed from outside, by a hostile superpower that financed its enemies and strangled its economy, and that nothing internal to the experiment decided the outcome. The opposing tradition holds that the government destroyed itself, by an economic programme that produced chaos and by a revolutionary rhetoric that terrified the middle of the country into the arms of the military. Both traditions can quote documents. Both are incomplete, and the incompleteness is not a matter of missing facts but of missing structure. The Chilean government faced three pressures at once: an economic sequence that turned from expansion to crisis within two years, an external campaign of destabilisation that is established in declassified records, and a division inside its own coalition over the very legality on which its strategy rested. Any account that selects one pressure and discards the other two will explain a Chile that did not exist. This article works through all three, sets them in the three pressures table that organises the analysis, and arrives at the case's durable lesson, which concerns neither Washington nor inflation in the first instance. The lesson is that the road required two things the government never possessed together: a majority, and a disciplined coalition. Allende governed with a plurality and an exposed flank, and the narrow room between those two facts is where the Chilean road was lost.

## The Chilean road to socialism: the claim the case was testing

The Chilean road to socialism, in its canonical definition, refers to the programme of the Popular Unity government elected in 1970, which sought to transform property relations through constitutional, electoral and legal means while respecting existing institutions, and which was ended by a military coup in September 1973. Each element of that definition carried weight. The transformation was to be structural, not managerial: copper, banking and the commanding heights of industry were to pass into social ownership, and the great landed estates were to be broken up. The means were to be constitutional: expropriations under laws already on the books or passed through congress, purchases negotiated where law required, and no recourse to the armed seizure of power. The respect for institutions was not decorative. It was the strategy's core, the feature that distinguished the Chilean claim from every revolutionary precedent and made it interesting to socialists far beyond Chile, including the Communist parties of Western Europe that were then developing their own doctrines of a democratic transition.

The claim had a genealogy. The Chilean Communist Party had argued since the 1950s that the country's institutional stability and broad suffrage made a peaceful road conceivable, and the Socialist Party, Allende's own, had moved between insurrectionary language and electoral practice for a generation. Allende himself had contested the presidency three times before 1970 and had come close in 1964, when the Christian Democrats defeated him with the support of the right and of the United States. The coalition that finally carried him, Popular Unity, joined Socialists and Communists with the Radical Party, smaller left formations and, for a time, dissident Christian Democrats. Its Basic Programme, published before the election, set out the transformation in unusual detail for a campaign document: a social property area of nationalised monopolies, a mixed area, a private area left intact, agrarian reform, a single chamber legislature to replace the existing congress, and a foreign policy of independence from both superpower blocs. Voters were not deceived about the destination. What the programme promised was a transition to socialism conducted inside the republic's forms, and the electorate that gave the coalition its plurality knew the destination had been named.

Why Chile, of all countries, produced this experiment is a question about institutions. Chile possessed what most of Latin America lacked: a long record of competitive elections, a professional military that had stayed out of government for four decades, a literate electorate organised into disciplined parties, and a left with deep roots in the nitrate and copper unions of the north. The same institutions that made the peaceful road plausible also defined its constraints, since a congress elected under the old order, a judiciary formed under it and an officer corps socialised by it would all survive the election that brought the left to the palace. The reform and revolution debate, treated in full in the series article on the [reform or revolution debate](/2013/02/01/reform-or-revolution-debate/), had been conducted for seventy years as an argument about texts and about Russia. Chile converted it into an argument about a sitting government, with a budget, a central bank and a general staff. That conversion is the case's permanent value to the student of Marxism: propositions that had lived as doctrine acquired addresses, dates and consequences.

### What did the coalition promise in its basic programme?

A structural transformation conducted by law: nationalisation of copper and the large monopolies into a social property area, state control of banking, completion of the agrarian reform, and a new constitution to follow, all pursued through elections and existing institutions rather than through any seizure of power by force.

The programme's architecture deserves attention because later controversy often flattened it into a promise of general expropriation. The three property areas were a deliberate signal. The social area would hold the commanding heights: the copper mines, the banks, the foreign trade apparatus and a defined list of monopoly enterprises. The mixed area would combine state and private capital in ventures the state judged strategic. The private area, which by the programme's own account would include the great majority of firms, small workshops, farms below the expropriation ceiling and the whole world of small commerce, was to remain private and was promised protection. The distinction mattered politically, because the government's survival depended on persuading small proprietors that the transformation had a boundary they could see, and it mattered analytically, because the fiercest struggles of the following years were fought precisely over where the boundary ran: which enterprises belonged on the list, and what happened when workers occupied a factory that the list did not name. A programme can be read in its silences as well as its clauses, and the Basic Programme was silent on the question that would eventually consume it: what the government should do when its own supporters moved faster than the law it had sworn to keep.

The foreign dimension of the programme was equally deliberate. Chile's copper was mined overwhelmingly by two American companies, Anaconda and Kennecott, whose position in the economy was the standing symbol of dependence in Chilean political argument across the spectrum. Nationalising copper was not a left eccentricity; it was a national aspiration that the preceding Christian Democratic government had already advanced through partial purchases, under the banner of Chileanisation. Popular Unity proposed to complete the process and to conduct foreign policy without alignment, restoring relations with Cuba, China and the socialist states while maintaining trade with the West. The breadth of the copper consensus, demonstrated within a year in a congressional vote that no party opposed, is one of the facts that any adequate account of the period must absorb, because it shows that the confrontation which eventually destroyed the government was not, at its root, a confrontation over expropriation as such. Something else polarised Chile between 1971 and 1973, and identifying that something is the analytical work of this article.

## A plurality, not a majority: the electoral position

The election of 4 September 1970 produced the result around which everything else in the case turns. Three candidates divided the vote. Allende, for Popular Unity, finished first. Jorge Alessandri, the conservative former president backed by the National Party, finished a close second. Radomiro Tomic, the Christian Democrat, finished third. The coalition's candidate had won the largest share of the votes cast and had fallen well short of half of them. Under the Chilean constitution, when no candidate held an absolute majority, the choice between the two leading candidates fell to congress, which by long convention confirmed the plurality winner. The convention held, but not before a crisis that set the pattern for the three years to come: the right explored ways to block confirmation, the Christian Democrats demanded constitutional guarantees as the price of their votes, and the commander in chief of the army, General René Schneider, was assassinated in an attempted kidnapping by right wing plotters who hoped to provoke military intervention and prevent the inauguration. Schneider died defending the constitutional doctrine that bore his name, that the armed forces obeyed the constitution and did not choose governments. His murderers included men in contact with American intelligence, a connection the declassified record later documented. Allende was confirmed by congress in October and inaugurated on 3 November 1970, having accepted a Statute of Democratic Guarantees that wrote Christian Democratic conditions on press freedom, education and the apolitical character of the armed forces into the constitutional order.

### Did Allende win a majority?

no. He won a plurality in the presidential election of September 1970 and was confirmed by congress under the constitutional procedure. The government never held a majority in congress, which is central to understanding the constraints on its programme. Every major measure therefore required negotiation or decree authority the opposition contested.

The arithmetic of that position governed the government's life. In congress, Popular Unity held a minority in both chambers, facing a Christian Democratic centre that began as a negotiating partner and ended as an adversary, and a right that was hostile from the first day. The government's legislative programme therefore moved at the speed of the centre's tolerance. When the centre cooperated, as on copper, the transformation advanced with overwhelming legitimacy. When the centre refused, the government fell back on legal instruments inherited from earlier decades: decree laws, requisition powers, and the device known as intervention, under which the state could take over the administration of a firm paralysed by dispute or abandonment without formally expropriating it. Each such device was legal in form and contested in substance, and each use of it deepened the opposition's conviction that the government was achieving by administrative ingenuity what it could not achieve by votes. The comptroller general and the courts became battlegrounds, ruling on the legality of interventions and requisitions in case after case. A government with a majority would have rewritten the statutes and ended the argument. A government with a plurality could only argue, and the argument consumed it.

The plurality had a second, quieter consequence. A president carried into office by the largest minority in a three way race governs a country in which most voters chose someone else, and his opponents can plausibly claim that the majority of the nation rejected his programme even as they accept his office. Allende's supporters answered that the combined vote of the two candidates of change exceeded the right's, and that the Christian Democrat programme also promised deep reforms. Both claims were true, and their coexistence defined the politics of the period: a government with a genuine mandate to govern and a contested mandate to transform. The distinction between winning an election and holding state power, which the exam guides in this series ask students to keep separate, is not an abstraction in this case. The palace changed hands in November 1970. The congress, the courts, the central bank's habits, the press and the barracks did not, and the government's three years can be written as the history of that gap.

## The transformation begins: the social property area and the land

The government's first measures moved on the fronts where law and consensus were strongest. The banking system passed largely into state hands through the purchase of shares by the state development corporation, a method chosen because it used market instruments rather than confiscation and because the banks' owners, facing a government determined to control credit, often preferred to sell. The agrarian reform, begun under the Christian Democrats with a law of 1967, accelerated sharply: the estates above the legal ceiling, defined in irrigated hectares, were expropriated at a pace that effectively ended the great latifundio in the Chilean countryside within two years. In industry, the state assembled the social property area from the programme's list of monopolies, using a repertoire of instruments that ranged from negotiated purchase to intervention after labour disputes. By the middle of the government's life the state controlled copper, most of banking, much of foreign trade and a substantial share of industrial production, though the exact boundary of the social area remained contested factory by factory, and the contest over the boundary became one of the period's defining struggles.

### What did the social property area mean in practice?

It meant that a defined set of commanding enterprises, copper mines, banks and listed monopolies, would be owned and run by the state, while mixed and private areas continued beside it. In practice its boundary was fought over factory by factory, since workers occupied plants the list had never named.

The agrarian reform showed both the strength and the exposure of the method. Legally, the reform rested on the predecessor's statute, and its early expropriations were difficult to portray as revolutionary lawlessness. Socially, it dissolved a rural order in which the inquilino, the resident labourer bound to the estate, had lived under the patron's authority for generations, and it replaced that order with reform centres and cooperatives whose performance varied widely. Politically, it was irreversible in the countryside's expectations long before it was complete in law: peasants who had waited decades for land were not disposed to wait for the ministry's timetable, and seizures of farms by rural workers, often encouraged by movements to the government's left, began to outrun the official process. The pattern that would define the whole experiment appeared first in the fields. A legal transformation, proceeding at the speed of its statutes, generated a social movement that experienced the statutes as delay, and the government found itself policing its own supporters to defend the legality that was its strategy's foundation.

The economic policy of the first year belonged to a different register. The government raised wages substantially, froze or controlled prices, expanded public spending and used idle industrial capacity to raise production. The design, associated with the economy minister Pedro Vuskovic, aimed at a redistribution led expansion: purchasing power transferred to wage earners would call forth output from factories running below capacity, employment would rise, and the political base of the transformation would broaden as living standards improved. For a year the design worked on its own terms. Production climbed, unemployment fell, real incomes of the poor rose, and the government's support grew at the municipal elections of April 1971, where the coalition improved on its presidential vote. The opposition press warned that the expansion was consuming reserves and inventories, that the price controls were storing up scarcity, and that the fiscal deficit was being financed by the printing press. The warnings were also true, and they described the same facts from the other end of the sequence. The first year's success was real and it was purchased with the second year's crisis, a relationship the three pressures table sets out in full below.

## Copper: the unanimous vote and what it proves

In July 1971 congress approved the constitutional amendment nationalising the great copper mines, and it approved the measure unanimously. Every party voted for it: the government's coalition, the Christian Democrats who would within two years be demanding the government's resignation, and the National Party of the right. The compensation formula deducted from the companies' payment the excess profits they were judged to have earned since the mid 1950s, a calculation that reduced the compensation owed to the American owners to a figure the government presented as nil or negative, and this deduction, rather than the nationalisation itself, became the point of international dispute. Kennecott and Anaconda carried their claims into foreign courts and into a campaign to obstruct Chilean copper sales and credit abroad. Domestically, however, the vote stands as the period's most instructive document. A congress in which the government held only a minority expropriated the country's commanding industry without a dissenting voice, because copper nationalism was a national position older than the coalition and shared across the spectrum that would soon be at war with itself.

### Why did the copper vote pass without a single vote against it?

Because copper nationalism commanded the whole spectrum. The foreign companies' dominance had been criticised for decades by parties of right, centre and left alike, and the preceding government had already bought into the mines. No deputy wished to be recorded defending the companies against the nation's claim to its principal resource.

The copper vote disciplines every simple account of the period. If the confrontation that destroyed the government had truly turned on expropriation as such, the unanimity of July 1971 is inexplicable. The parties that voted to take the mines without compensation worth the name were not converted socialists; they were nationalists on this question, and they remained what they had been on every other. What divided Chile was not the principle that the state should own copper but the direction and speed of everything else: the interventions in industry, the land seizures, the price controls, the rhetoric of a left flank that spoke of advancing without compromise, and the fear, cultivated assiduously by the opposition press, that the private area's promised boundary would not hold. The government's tragedy can be stated through the copper vote. On the question where it could assemble a national consensus, it achieved total victory within eight months. On the questions where consensus had to be built rather than inherited, it faced a congress it did not control, an economy turning against it, and supporters who treated consensus building as betrayal. The unanimous vote also mattered abroad, in a way the government perhaps undervalued: it demonstrated to Washington that the Chilean congress, including its anti Marxist majority, would not serve as an instrument against nationalisation, and it pushed the conflict onto terrain, credit, courts and covert action, where the rules were murkier.

The excess profits deduction deserves its own paragraph, because it shows the government's method at its most characteristic. The deduction was not an improvisation. It was computed from the companies' own published accounts, under a formula the amendment itself enacted, and it expressed a doctrine: that returns beyond a reasonable rate, extracted over decades from a nation's principal resource, were not property to be compensated but arrears to be recovered. Allende, presenting the calculation, observed that on the government's figures the companies owed Chile rather than the reverse. Foreign lawyers disputed the arithmetic and the principle, and the dispute followed Chilean copper shipments through European courts for years. Yet the legalism of the manoeuvre is the point. Even at its most radical, the government's signature measure was drafted as law, voted as law and defended as law, which is precisely what the classical objection to the democratic road must explain away: the road's most expropriating act was also its most constitutional.

## The three pressures table

The analysis of this article is organised by the three pressures table, set out below. Its rows are the three forces that bore on the government simultaneously: the economic sequence, the internal division and the external action. Its columns record what occurred, the character of the evidence, the contribution each pressure made to the outcome, and what a different course would have required. The table's discipline is that no row is allowed to explain the whole. Read down the contribution column and the interaction appears: the economic crisis enlarged the audience for external destabilisation, the internal division prevented the government from answering the crisis with the consolidation its strategy required, and the external action raised the price of every error the other two pressures produced. Historians who privilege one row are not inventing their evidence. They are truncating the table.

Two features of the table deserve emphasis before the narrative resumes. The first is the asymmetry of the fourth column. Two of the three pressures were, at least in principle, within the reach of the government's own choices: a different economic course and a different coalition discipline were available, at prices the government judged too high. The third pressure was not within its reach at all, and honesty requires saying so. A government can deny a destabilisation campaign its occasions; it cannot vote the campaign out of existence. The second feature is the direction of causation between the rows. The table lists them separately for clarity, but in the lived sequence they compounded. Shortages made the truck owners' strike of October 1972 possible as a national event rather than a trade dispute. The strike made the parallel organs of the industrial belts expand, as workers organised supply and production themselves. The expansion of those organs confirmed the centre's fear of a dual power. The centre's fear was then available, documented and quotable, to every external actor who wished to present the government as a prelude to dictatorship. Single cause accounts fail not because their causes are imaginary but because the causes in this case were a system.

## The economic sequence: from expansion to crisis

The crisis that consumed the government's second and third years had several authors, and the honest account distributes responsibility before it assigns weight. The expansion of the first year was financed in ways that could not continue: reserves spent, inventories run down, a fiscal deficit covered by money creation, and price controls that held official prices below the level at which goods would be supplied. When the controls bit, goods vanished from legal shops and reappeared on the parallel market at prices that mocked the official index. The state's distribution agency, created to supply working class districts, became a political institution in its own right, and the queues for basic goods became the opposition's most effective recruiting ground among housewives of the middle strata, whose protests, beginning with the march of the empty pots in December 1971, gave the resistance a domestic and feminine face that the government struggled to answer. The copper price, on which the budget depended, fell from the heights of the first year. Foreign credit, on which Chile had relied to finance imports, contracted sharply under American pressure, so that spare parts for American made machinery and vehicles grew scarce across the economy. Investment by Chilean private capital stopped almost entirely, as owners who feared expropriation consumed or exported their capital instead. Each element had its own logic; together they formed a siege economy in which the government's promises and the citizens' experience diverged further every month.

### How did the shortages change the politics of the middle strata?

Scarcity converted abstract argument into daily experience. Shopkeepers, transport owners, professionals and housewives who had not voted for the right encountered the transformation as queues, missing goods and a currency losing value, and many concluded that the government could not run the economy it proposed to remake.

The October 1972 stoppage showed the crisis becoming a contest over the state's functioning. The truck owners, a numerous and strategically placed body of small proprietors organised in a powerful confederation, struck over grievances that mixed the economic and the political, and the stoppage spread to shopkeepers, some professional associations and parts of the countryside. A country built along a single mountain spine, dependent on road haulage for food distribution, felt the strike in its stomach within days. The government survived the month by improvisation: volunteers and loyal workers moved supplies, the military entered the cabinet in the person of the army commander, General Carlos Prats, whose presence signalled that the armed forces would guarantee order while the government negotiated, and the strike ended in an uneasy settlement. But the lesson each side drew was corrosive. The opposition learned that a paralysing strike could bring the government to the edge and that the armed forces, once admitted to the cabinet as arbiters, had become political actors with a veto. The government's left learned that only the organised workers of the industrial belts had kept the country fed, and concluded that those organs, not the ministries, were the real seat of power. Both lessons pointed away from the constitutional centre the strategy required.

### What did the October transport stoppage reveal about the government's reach?

It revealed that the state the government headed could not by itself move food through the country against an organised refusal, and that survival depended on improvised worker organisation and on the army's arbitration. Each rescue therefore strengthened the two forces, parallel power and military arbitration, that the road could not absorb.

The government's economic management in its final year was a holding operation conducted under conditions no finance minister would choose. A second and larger stoppage by the truck owners began in the winter of 1973 and continued until the coup. Rationing was discussed and never systematically imposed, partly from administrative incapacity and partly because the government feared that formal rationing would concede the opposition's portrait of collapse. Inflation, measured by the official index the government itself published, accelerated beyond anything in the country's experience, and wages chased prices in a spiral that dissolved the first year's gains for the very workers the transformation was meant to serve. The black market became the real price system for many goods, which meant that the state's proudest instrument, its control of distribution, functioned as a subsidy to those with dollars and connections. It is in this context that the economic explanation of the fall must be weighed. The crisis was not a pretext invented by the government's enemies; it was a material fact experienced by millions, and it destroyed the asset on which a democratic road most depends, the belief of the uncommitted that the government is competent to govern. Yet the crisis was not a purely internal product either. The credit squeeze, the copper litigation and the financing of the opposition press and parties were documented components of it, and a finance minister facing the same domestic errors without the external siege would have faced a hard year rather than a strangulation. The three pressures table exists precisely to hold those two sentences together.

## The flank: the left outside the government's discipline

The least covered element of the Chilean case, and the most analytically important, is the division inside the left over the strategy itself. Popular Unity was a coalition, not a party, and its components did not agree about what they were doing. The Communist Party, the coalition's most disciplined organisation, held the line of the programme with near military consistency: legality, gradual advance, alliance with the centre where possible, and no provocation of the armed forces. Allende himself, a Socialist formed by forty years of parliamentary life, governed from the same conviction, and his authority over the coalition rested on it. But the Socialist Party, formally the president's own, contained a powerful current that regarded the institutional road as a phase to be outgrown, spoke the language of revolutionary advance, and resisted the compromises that consolidation required. Outside the coalition stood the Movement of the Revolutionary Left, the MIR, founded by students and former Socialists in the previous decade, which held that the legal road was an illusion, encouraged the direct seizure of farms and factories, built influence among the landless and the urban poor, and preached the construction of popular power as an alternative to the state the government was trying to operate. The MIR was small beside the coalition's parties. Its strategic weight was far larger than its membership, because its actions were attributed, by every frightened observer in the centre, to the government it claimed to be pushing forward.

### Why did the government insist on legality even as its flank broke it?

Because legality was not a tactic the government could suspend but the strategy itself. The road's claim was that transformation could proceed inside the constitution; a government that licensed seizures outside the law would have surrendered its distinguishing argument, frightened the centre it needed and handed the military its pretext.

The seizures were the flank's signature act. In the countryside, land occupations pressed the agrarian reform beyond its statutory pace and sometimes beyond its boundaries, taking farms beneath the expropriation ceiling and estates already assigned to other beneficiaries. In the towns, workers occupied factories that the social property list did not include, and having occupied them, demanded that the state intervene and take them over, confronting the government with a choice between ratifying an illegality committed in its name or sending police against its own supporters. Ministers chose, case by case, and every choice cost something: ratification confirmed the opposition's charge that the private area's boundary was fiction, while enforcement confirmed the flank's charge that the government had become the caretaker of the old order. The cordones industriales grew out of this pressure. In the industrial belts of Santiago, beginning with the Cerrillos cordon during the October stoppage, workers from neighbouring factories formed coordinating committees to keep production and supply running, to defend plants against closure and sabotage, and to organise the life of their districts. At their best the cordones were the most impressive popular organisations of the period, proof of a working class capable of administering production. At the same time they were, in embryo, an alternative authority: they answered to assemblies rather than to ministries, they discussed arming themselves, and the government's enemies could point to them as the skeleton of a parallel state being assembled inside the republic.

### What were the cordones industriales, and why did they alarm more than the opposition?

They were coordinating committees of workers across neighbouring factories in the industrial belts, formed to sustain production, supply and defence during the stoppages. They alarmed the centre because they looked like a parallel authority growing inside the republic, organised, territorial and answerable to assemblies rather than to the government.

The strategic contradiction can now be named precisely, and naming it is this article's analytical contribution. The democratic road, as the government itself understood it, required the progressive consolidation of a majority: the plurality of 1970 had to grow, through competent government and visible improvement, into the broader support that would let the transformation proceed with legitimacy and eventually rewrite the constitution by consent. Every element of that consolidation pointed toward reassurance of the middle strata, the small proprietors, the professionals, the Christian Democratic electorate whose party might yet be split or won. Every action of the flank pointed the other way. Each land seizure told a small farmer that his farm might be next; each factory occupation told a small industrialist that the list was no protection; each speech about advancing without compromise told the centre that the government's promises were a holding operation behind which the real revolution gathered. The flank believed it was accelerating history. Its measurable effect was to accelerate the consolidation of the government's enemies, to drive the Christian Democrats from negotiation toward resistance, and to supply the coup's eventual authors with the portrait of chaos and dual power that their proclamations would use. The plurality and the flank is the name this article gives the trap: the Chilean road required winning a majority it did not have while restraining a left flank it did not control, and the two requirements worked against each other at every step, since the energy that filled the government's rallies was the same energy that emptied its majority.

Allende understood the trap and fought it with the instruments he had. He refused to legalise the seizures wholesale and refused to crush them; he negotiated, delayed, exhorted and occasionally enforced, and he protected the MIR's legality even as its leaders denounced his gradualism, because a government of the left that began by banning the left's revolutionaries would have split its own camp. The Communist Party supplied the discipline the coalition lacked, and paid for it in the flank's contempt. The president's room for manoeuvre narrowed with each crisis, because the flank's actions were cumulative in the centre's memory while his reassurances were perishable. By the final winter the argument inside the coalition had hardened into two irreconcilable readings of the same facts. One side held that only a pause, a negotiated consolidation with the Christian Democrats, could save the government and the road. The other held that pauses were surrenders, that the confrontation was coming regardless, and that the government's task was to deepen the transformation and organise the power to defend it. The dialogue of the deaf, as participants later called it, continued into the last weeks, and it was never resolved, because resolving it would have required a coalition discipline that the coalition, by its composition, did not possess.

## The external dimension: what the declassified record establishes

The external pressure on the Chilean government is not a matter of inference or of the government's propaganda. It is documented in the records of the state that applied it, released through the investigations of the United States Senate in the mid 1970s and through the large declassification programmes of later decades, and the article treats it accordingly: as established history, stated plainly, and then weighed rather than merely denounced. The record begins before the inauguration. In September 1970, days after the election, the president of the United States instructed his national security apparatus to prevent Allende from taking office or to unseat him, and the notes of the director of central intelligence from the meeting of 15 September record the instruction in the language of the room: the president wanted the Chilean economy made to scream. Two tracks followed. The first sought a constitutional or military manoeuvre to block confirmation, the so called Track I. The second, Track II, sought to provoke a military coup directly, through contacts with Chilean officers and support to plotters, and it was within this effort that the arms and money reached the milieu from which Schneider's assassins came, though the agency's own later account held that the specific group that killed the general had been told to stand down days before. The constitutional track failed with the confirmation, the coup track failed with Schneider's death, which rallied the country and the officer corps to legality, and the policy settled into its long form: to make the government's failure certain and visible, by squeezing its credit, sustaining its opposition and keeping contact with its military enemies until conditions ripened.

### What exactly did the declassified documents establish, and what did they not?

They established intent, authorisation and activity: instructions to block the inauguration, the covert financing of opposition parties and their press, pressure against Chilean credit, and continuing contact with officers plotting against the government. They did not establish that the coup itself was ordered or executed by the external power.

The long campaign worked through instruments that left the government's formal sovereignty intact. Multilateral and bilateral credit, on which the Chilean economy depended for imports and spare parts, contracted to a fraction of its former flow under American pressure, in what Chilean officials called the invisible blockade. The copper companies' litigation harassed shipments and deterred buyers. Covert funds sustained the opposition's press, above all the great newspaper El Mercurio, whose survival as an anti government organ through an advertising drought was a material fact of the period, and reached opposition parties and, by the investigators' later findings, the organisational world around the strike movements. A senior American official had stated the doctrine before the election, in a remark the Senate record preserved: he did not see why his country needed to stand by and watch a nation choose communism, as he put it, "due to the irresponsibility of its own people". The sentence matters less for its cynicism than for its clarity about the premise. The Chilean electorate's choice was treated, at the level where the campaign was authorised, as an error to be corrected rather than a fact to be accommodated, and everything else followed from that premise with bureaucratic consistency.

Weighing this pressure requires the same discipline as weighing the others. The destabilisation campaign did not invent the Chilean opposition, which had its own social bases, its own grievances and its own long traditions, nor did it write the government's economic errors, choose its flank's tactics or command the votes of the Chilean congress. Chileans made the crisis in which the campaign operated. What the campaign did was to lower the cost of resistance and raise the cost of government at every margin: a newspaper that would have folded continued to publish, a strike that would have exhausted its funds continued to roll, a finance minister who might have refinanced his debts found the doors closed, and an officer corps contemplating sedition could be confident that the hemisphere's strongest power would welcome the result. Destabilisation of this kind does not need to command events. It needs only to lean, persistently and with resources, on every weakness the target already has, and the three pressures table records its contribution in those terms. The government's enemies were Chilean. Their confidence that time and the world were on their side was substantially imported.

## The opposition's consolidation and the closing of the centre

The Chilean opposition was not born united, and its unification is one of the period's central processes. In 1970 the Christian Democrats had voted to confirm Allende, extracted their statute of guarantees, and contained a substantial current that favoured an understanding with the government on reforms both parties had promised their electorates. The right, by contrast, never accepted the government's legitimacy in substance, and its paramilitary fringe, organised in the movement called Patria y Libertad, moved early to sabotage and provocation. Between these poles stood the gremios, the associations of truck owners, merchants, engineers, doctors and other professionals, which gave the resistance an organisational form independent of the parties and rooted in the daily experience of the crisis. The trajectory of the three years is the story of the centre's migration. Each shock, the seizures, the shortages, the government's recourse to decree and intervention, the rhetoric of the flank, moved Christian Democratic voters and then their leadership away from negotiation. The party's own left wing withered, its presidency passed to men committed to confrontation, and by the final year the two opposition blocs were cooperating in congress, in the streets and in the gremio movements, while a negotiated settlement that might have isolated the right and saved the constitutional order receded beyond reach.

The congressional elections of March 1973 were the period's last measurement of the country, and both camps read them as a kind of victory, which is why they settled nothing. The opposition, which had hoped to win the two thirds of congress needed to remove the president constitutionally, fell short of that threshold and retained its majority. The government, which the opposition press had predicted would collapse under the weight of the crisis, increased its vote over the presidential result and demonstrated that its working class base had not deserted it under scarcity. The arithmetic therefore froze the confrontation. The government could not be removed by the constitutional road its enemies had chosen, and it could not advance its programme through a congress it did not command. What remained, in the logic of the situation, was either negotiation between two forces that had exhausted their trust in each other, or a decision by arms. The opposition's congressional majority chose in August 1973 to declare, by resolution of the chamber of deputies, that the government had violated the constitution, a text which stopped short of any legal effect and functioned instead as an invitation addressed to the armed forces. The government answered that the resolution was itself unconstitutional. The republic's institutions had arrived at the condition the classical objection predicts: each side claiming legality, and no arbiter left whom both would obey.

### What did the March congressional elections settle, and what did they leave open?

They settled that neither camp could win by the rules both had invoked: the opposition kept its majority but missed the supermajority required to remove the president, while the government grew its vote under crisis conditions. They left open the only remaining questions, negotiation or force.

## The armed forces: the apparatus the road never answered

Every account of the Chilean case must eventually arrive at the barracks, because the democratic road's theory of the state met its hardest test there. The strategy assumed that a professional military, formed in a doctrine of obedience to the constitution, would remain neutral while an elected government transformed the society around it, and for the first half of the government the assumption held better than critics expected. General Schneider's doctrine, that the armed forces guaranteed the constitution and did not arbitrate politics, was defended by his successor General Prats at real cost to himself, and the military's entry into the cabinet during the October stoppage was presented, by Prats above all, as a guarantee of the electoral calendar rather than a step toward power. But the assumption contained a difficulty that no goodwill could dissolve. The transformation the government pursued was aimed at the property of the class from which the officer corps was drawn, whose clubs it frequented, whose newspapers it read and whose fears it heard daily as the fears of the nation itself. An army does not need to be ordered into politics when the society that formed it is polarised; it needs only to conclude that the constitution it serves is being destroyed by the government it protects, and the opposition's entire effort in the final year was devoted to supplying that conclusion.

### Why did the constitutionalist generals matter so much to the road's survival?

Because the road's premise was that the coercive apparatus would stay neutral while property relations changed around it. So long as officers of Schneider's and Prats's doctrine commanded, the premise held in practice. Their isolation and removal converted the premise into a hope, and the coup followed within weeks.

The sequence of the final year reads as the dismantling of the premise. In June 1973 a tank regiment, misled by its commander into believing a coup was under way, attacked the presidential palace and was defeated by loyal forces under Prats's personal direction; the tancazo, as the episode was named, killed soldiers and civilians and demonstrated both that a coup faction existed and that the high command still opposed it. In August, after the officers' wives demonstrated outside his house and his fellow generals withdrew their confidence, Prats resigned, broken by the knowledge that he could no longer guarantee his institution's unity. Allende appointed as commander in chief the general he judged most constitutionalist of the remaining candidates, Augusto Pinochet, who within three weeks led the coup. The naval intelligence services had already begun arresting and torturing sailors suspected of left sympathies, an internal purge that told the government the apparatus was preparing itself. In the last days the president considered and prepared a plebiscite, an appeal over the deadlocked institutions to the electorate, which he intended to announce on the day the coup forestalled. Whether the plebiscite would have saved the road or merely chosen its manner of ending is one of the case's permanent counterfactuals. What is documented is the fact that the government's final move was to be another vote, which is the measure of how completely its strategy had foreclosed every other instrument.

## September: the coup and the death of the president

On the morning of 11 September 1973 the armed forces moved as a single institution. The navy seized Valparaíso at dawn, the army surrounded the capital, and the junta demanded the president's resignation and offered him an aircraft into exile. Allende refused, withdrew to the presidential palace of La Moneda with a small guard and a group of loyalists, and broadcast a farewell address to the country over the one radio network still in loyal hands, professing his confidence in his country's future and his refusal to resign the mandate the voters had given him. In the address's most remembered sentence he told the workers of his country, "I have faith in Chile and its destiny". Air force jets then bombed the palace, infantry and armour assaulted it, and by the middle of the afternoon the building was burning and the president was dead. The junta's first accounts spoke of suicide and were widely disbelieved by the government's supporters, for whom murder by the military was the only story consistent with the day. The question was investigated again after the dictatorship, and a forensic investigation completed in 2011, conducted with international specialists and accepted by the courts and by the president's family, established that Allende had taken his own life with the rifle he kept in the palace as the assault closed in. The finding settled the fact without settling the meaning, which the two traditions continue to contest: a death chosen in refusal of surrender, or the final proof that the road had led its author to a room from which no politics could extract him.

### How did Allende die, and how was the question settled?

He died in the presidential palace during the military assault of 11 September 1973. The junta's claim of suicide was contested for decades. An official forensic review concluded in 2011, staffed with specialists from abroad and accepted by the courts, found that he had taken his own life, closing the factual dispute while leaving the interpretations opposed.

The dictatorship that followed ruled Chile for a generation, and this article's treatment of it is governed by a discipline the reader should have stated plainly. The repression that the regime practised, the executions, the disappearances, the detentions and the exile of a substantial part of the political nation, is documented in two official commissions of inquiry appointed after the return of democracy: the National Commission for Truth and Reconciliation, whose report appeared in 1991 and is known by the name of its chairman, Rettig, and the National Commission on Political Imprisonment and Torture, whose report appeared in 2004 and takes its name from its chairman, Valech, and whose work was later extended. Those reports, built from testimony taken under legal mandate, are the appropriate source for the scale of what was done, and this article names them as that source without quoting their figures, because the figures belong to the evidentiary record they assembled and are too often detached from it in polemic. What can be said without numbers is the character of the thing: a regime that treated a defeated political tradition as an enemy population to be extirpated, and that then remade the Chilean economy under the protection of that terror, reversing the nationalisations, returning the estates and opening the country to the market doctrines its advisers imported. The coup therefore did not merely end an experiment. It demonstrated, to every observer drawing lessons, that the question of who commands the apparatus of coercion is not one question among others in a transition, but the question on which the others depend.

### Where should a reader look for the scale of the repression that followed?

To the Rettig Commission, which reported in 1991 on deaths and disappearances, and to the Valech Commission, which reported in 2004 on political imprisonment and torture, both appointed once democracy had returned. Their reports, built from testimony gathered under legal mandate, are the authoritative source for scale.

## The Spanish archive: the record and the English reader

A note on sources belongs in any honest treatment of this case, because the evidence is divided by language in a way that has shaped its English language afterlife. The programme documents of Popular Unity, the coalition's internal polemics, the records of the parties and the greater part of the scholarship were produced in Spanish, and much of the most important remains untranslated or available only in fragments. The declassified documentation of the external power, by contrast, is in English, voluminous and easily searched, and it entered the English language literature early and in bulk. The asymmetry has a predictable effect. An English reader can reconstruct the destabilisation campaign in granular detail from primary sources, while the internal argument of the Chilean left, the argument this article has treated as the case's analytical centre, must be taken largely on the authority of the scholars who have worked the Spanish record. Accounts written in English therefore lean, by the gravity of their archives, toward the external explanation, and readers should correct for the lean consciously. The Chilean debate about Chile, conducted in Santiago's journals and in the exile press across two decades, is richer, more self critical and less settled than the international controversy suggests, and the case's lessons look different when the flank's own theorists and the government's own economists are heard in their own words rather than in summary.

## The strongest objection: the classical case against the democratic road

The objection that any defence of the Chilean road must answer was not invented after the coup. It is the classical position of the revolutionary tradition, restated for Chile by critics who had predicted the outcome while the experiment still lived, and its most rigorous Marxist statement came from Ralph Miliband, who argued in the aftermath that the Chilean ruling class and its military had behaved exactly as a serious theory of the capitalist state would lead one to expect. Stated at full strength, the objection runs as follows. A government that leaves the armed forces, the judiciary and the press in the hands of the class whose property it proposes to expropriate will be removed by them the moment its transformation becomes real. The institutions of the old order are not neutral instruments waiting for an operator; they are staffed, financed and socialised by the order they serve, and their tolerance of a left government is a function of that government's harmlessness. Chile, on this reading, is not a tragedy of errors but a demonstration. The legal road is not a road at all. It is a delay during which the government disarms its own supporters with constitutional scruples while its enemies prepare, and the three years of Popular Unity are the interval between the election and the predictable morning when the jets bombed the palace. Proponents of this objection can point to the record with uncomfortable precision: the courts obstructed, the congress declared against the government, the press campaigned, the gremios paralysed, and the army, courted and flattered by the government to the last week, destroyed it in a day. What, the objection asks, would count as evidence against the classical position, if Chile does not?

### What does the classical objection claim Chile demonstrates?

It claims that the inherited institutions cannot be used to abolish the order that built them: a government that expropriates the possessing class while leaving it the army, the courts and the press will be removed by those instruments, and Chile is offered as the demonstration that legality is a delay, not a road.

The answer begins by separating what the strategy assumed from what it encountered, because the objection's strength depends on confusing the two. The Chilean strategy never assumed that the institutions were neutral. It assumed something narrower and more defensible: that the institutions were divided, that the centre of Chilean politics could be held or won, that the officer corps contained a constitutionalist tradition strong enough to outlast a single government, and that a transformation which grew its majority as it proceeded would raise the political price of a coup beyond what its authors would pay. Each assumption had evidence behind it when the government took office, and some of the evidence strengthened before it collapsed. The copper amendment passed unanimously, which showed that the congress the objection treats as the possessing class's instrument could expropriate that class's most valuable foreign allies without a dissenting vote. The government's vote grew in the municipal contests of its first year and again, under crisis conditions, at the congressional elections of its third, which showed that consent for the transformation could be consolidated by governing and was not a fixed quantity that the election of 1970 had exhausted. The constitutionalist generals held the army to legality through the first coup attempt and through the October stoppage, which showed that the apparatus was not a single fist waiting to fall. A strategy whose premises behave in this fashion for thirty months is not refuted in principle by the events of the thirty fourth. It is defeated in fact, which is a different verdict, and the difference matters for every later attempt to learn from the case.

### What evidence cuts against the claim that consent could never be consolidated?

The unanimous copper vote in a congress the government did not control, the coalition's growth at the municipal polls of its first year and again, amid crisis, at the congressional elections of its third, and the constitutionalist commanders who kept the army legal through the first armed challenge.

Then the concession, which this article makes without softening. The government had no answer to the question of the coercive apparatus, and no proponent of the democratic road has since supplied one. When the assumptions failed, when the centre migrated, the generals were isolated and the institution closed ranks, the strategy contained no second act: no plan for the defence of the government that did not contradict the legality on which the whole claim rested, no loyal force within the state, and a president whose final recourse was a plebiscite the coup forestalled by hours. The objection is wrong to call the road an illusion, because the road demonstrably existed and carried the transformation a long distance. The objection is right about the destination of a road that cannot protect itself, and every subsequent theorist of democratic transition, including the European communists who drew the Chilean lesson into their own doctrines, has had to begin from that admission. The Eurocommunist reflection that followed the coup, in which Enrico Berlinguer and the Italian party concluded that a left government in a divided country required not a bare majority but the broadest democratic consensus and a compromise with the forces of the centre, is the sound of the Chilean concession being absorbed into strategy. It did not solve the problem of the apparatus. It restated the problem as the need never to face it alone.

## Three explanations weighed: impossibility, error and intervention

The literature on the fall of the government has organised itself, across five decades, into three broad positions, and the steelman discipline this series applies requires each to be stated in the form its serious proponents would recognise before any of them is weighed. The first position holds that the road was impossible in principle. Its proponents, of whom Miliband is the most rigorous representative in English, argue that the outcome was written into the structure: a capitalist state does not permit its own abolition by ballot, the Chilean bourgeoisie retained the economic and coercive instruments of its rule throughout, and the government's destruction followed from its project rather than from its mistakes. On this reading the interesting questions are not about 1973 but about why intelligent people believed the interval could end otherwise. The second position holds that specific errors were decisive. Its representative chronicler is Nathaniel Davis, the American ambassador of the final period, whose detailed account, written from inside the opposing camp and all the more valuable for it, distributes blame across the government's economic management, its failure to discipline its left, the opposition's intransigence and his own government's campaign, and concludes that the catastrophe was contingent: a different economic policy, an earlier negotiation with the Christian Democrats, a firmer hand on the flank, and the government completes its term. The third position holds that external action was decisive. Its most famous literary statement is Gabriel García Márquez's account of why Allende had to die, written in the coup's immediate aftermath, and its documentary foundation is the declassified record itself: the instructions, the funding, the blockade. On this reading Chile was a small country whose experiment threatened a precedent, and the precedent was extinguished by the power that could not afford it, using Chilean hands.

Each position commands evidence the others cannot dissolve. The impossibility school is right that the apparatus question had no answer and that the possessing class's tolerance was always conditional. The error school is right that the government made avoidable mistakes with measurable consequences, that the economic sequence was partly self inflicted and that the coalition's indiscipline was a choice renewed daily rather than a fate. The intervention school is right that the documentary record shows a sustained, funded and authorised effort to produce the outcome that occurred, and that no account which treats that effort as background noise deserves to be called complete. The three pressures table with which this article organised its analysis is, in effect, a refusal to let any of the three schools grade its own homework. The pressures interacted. The external campaign's effectiveness was a function of the domestic crisis it amplified; the domestic crisis's depth was a function of errors the campaign's existence made costlier; and the structural impossibility the first school describes became actual only when the other two pressures had isolated the government from the centre whose allegiance was its shield. To ask which cause was decisive, in the sense of the one without which nothing would have happened, is to ask a question the evidence cannot answer, because the removal of any one pressure changes the story beyond recognition. What analysis can do is order the pressures by their relation to the government's choices, and the table's fourth column does exactly that: two pressures were partly the government's to mitigate, one was not, and the case's lesson for any successor lies in the two that were.

## The legal war: decrees, interventions and the comptroller

Beneath the famous events ran a bureaucratic war over the meaning of legality, and it deserves treatment because the democratic road was, in the end, a dispute about law conducted by lawyers. The government's instruments were largely inherited. A requisition statute from the early 1930s, enacted in a brief socialist republic and never repealed, allowed the state to requisition enterprises whose production was interrupted. The intervention device allowed the labour ministry to appoint an administrator to a firm paralysed by dispute. Price and supply powers descended from wartime and postwar legislation. The government used this inherited armoury energetically and, its lawyers insisted, lawfully, to build the social property area where congress would not legislate and to keep factories running where owners had abandoned or sabotaged them. The opposition's lawyers replied that the armoury's purposes had been perverted: statutes written for emergencies were being used as a constitution free expropriation code, and the spirit of the law was being violated by its letter. The comptroller general, an autonomous official charged with reviewing the legality of executive decrees, became a central figure, returning decree after decree for defects the government read as political obstruction. The supreme court, in a public exchange of letters with the president that would have been unthinkable in quieter times, accused the executive of tolerating illegality, and the president answered that the judiciary had tolerated the illegality of the powerful for a century.

The legal war mattered because it consumed the government's scarcest resource, which was the presumption of good faith. Every contested intervention produced a court case, a press campaign and a small proprietor somewhere concluding that his own firm stood one decree from seizure. The government, for its part, learned to see the judiciary not as an arbiter but as a participant, and its supporters spoke increasingly of the legal order as the old order's armour, a conclusion that corroded the strategy's own foundation from within. There is a general lesson here that the case teaches better than any other. A legal transformation must litigate its way through institutions staffed by the order it is transforming, and the litigation is not a distraction from the politics but a principal form of it. The Chilean government won many of its cases and lost the war, because the war's prize was not any single ruling but the country's belief that the rules were still shared. By the final year that belief had drained away on both sides: the opposition's congressional declaration of unconstitutionality and the government's dismissal of it were the formal certificates of a divorce that the legal war had already accomplished.

## The coalition from inside: parties that did not agree

To speak of the government's strategy is already to simplify, because the coalition contained at least two strategies and a permanent argument between them. The Communist Party brought to Popular Unity the habits of a lifetime underground and in the unions: patience, discipline, a preference for the achievable over the proclaimed, and a doctrine of stages in which the present task was the democratic and anti monopoly transformation, not socialism in one leap. Its leaders spoke of consolidating in order to advance, and they meant by consolidation exactly what the flank despised: agreements with the centre, restraint of seizures, protection of small property, and the slow accumulation of majority support. The Socialist Party, by contrast, had declared itself Marxist Leninist at its congress of the late 1960s, had absorbed a generation radicalised by the Cuban example and the student movement, and contained leaders who regarded the Communist counsel of patience as a recipe for strangulation. Its rhetoric ran ahead of the government's practice, and its most prominent figures competed in revolutionary emphasis at rallies where the audience included the very middle strata the programme needed to reassure. The Radical Party, the coalition's historic centre component, haemorrhaged members toward the opposition as the polarisation deepened, and the small parties of the Christian left that had joined in hope of a broader transformation found themselves squeezed between a government they supported and a centre that had been their home.

Allende presided over this argument rather than resolving it, and his method of presiding was itself a cause of the outcome the historians debate. He was a coalition politician of forty years' formation, a former health minister, a senator, a Mason, a man whose authority rested on being the only figure every component would accept, and he used that authority to postpone the coalition's reckoning with itself. Postponement had a logic. A public break with the Socialist left or the MIR would have split the government's base and perhaps brought down the government from within; a public embrace of their line would have detonated the centre and perhaps provoked the military a year earlier. The president chose, month by month, the middle course of exhortation without enforcement, and the middle course's cost was that no one outside the palace could be certain what the government's line was, because the government's line depended on which of its voices an observer heard last. The opposition exploited the uncertainty skilfully, quoting the flank against the president and the president against the flank, and the centre, asked to negotiate with a government whose own camp disputed the negotiation, increasingly declined to try. Coalition discipline is an unglamorous variable, and narratives of the case prefer generals and ambassadors. The three pressures table restores it to the centre of the account, where the participants themselves, in their later testimony, consistently place it.

## The middle strata organised: gremios, shopkeepers and the politics of subsistence

The Chilean middle strata were not a passive audience to the contest between government and opposition. They were organised, numerous and armed with a weapon the period made decisive: the capacity to stop the country's daily life. The gremio movement, the federation of professional and proprietorial associations, gave truck owners, retailers, doctors, engineers and lawyers a common command and a doctrine of their own, the doctrine that the organised professions were the nation and that a government which attacked property attacked society itself. The truck owners were the movement's striking arm, because their vehicles were the country's circulatory system, and their confederation's leaders understood that a stoppage in a country of Chile's geography was not a labour dispute but a siege. The shopkeepers' closures, the doctors' strikes against the proposed national health reforms, the engineers' and lawyers' declarations, built a rhythm of professional refusal that presented itself to its participants as civil resistance and to the government as economic warfare conducted by other means. American funding reached parts of this world, as the Senate investigators later established, and the funding mattered at the margin of endurance. But the gremios were not manufactured. Their members had real grievances, real fears and real organisations of long standing, and any account that treats them as puppets fails to explain why the strings found such willing hands.

The subsistence struggle gave the middle strata's mobilisation its emotional register. The government's distribution committees, the JAP, organised supply in working class districts and were denounced by the opposition as political rationing by party card; the opposition's housewives marched with empty pots through the wealthy districts, and the government denounced them as the theatre of the rich. Both denunciations contained their fragment of truth, and both obscured the underlying fact: scarcity had made the queue the period's central political experience, and the queue radicalised in every direction at once. Workers who queued blamed the hoarders and demanded deeper transformation. Housewives who queued blamed the government and demanded its end. Small merchants caught between controlled prices and vanished supply blamed whoever had made their ledgers impossible. A democratic road runs on the patience of people who did not vote for the destination and must be persuaded, month by month, that the journey is competently conducted. The queues were the monthly verdict on that persuasion, and from the second year onward the verdict was being delivered against the government in districts it had never won and could not afford to lose.

## The other camp: Moscow's caution and Havana's embrace

The Chilean experiment was watched from the socialist world with an attention as intense as Washington's and a commitment considerably more guarded. The Soviet Union recognised the government, traded with it, extended credits on a scale its own documents later showed to be modest beside the need, and counselled, through every channel available to it, the consolidation the Chilean Communists already favoured: broaden the alliance, slow the transformation, avoid the confrontation for which the country was not prepared. Soviet caution had several sources. Moscow doubted that the Chilean road could survive, doubted that a confrontation in the hemisphere could be sustained at acceptable risk, and was conducting its own policy of détente with the United States, in which Chile was a card of small denomination. Chilean requests for the economic support that might have offset the invisible blockade were met with sympathy, studies and partial deliveries, and the gap between the solidarity of the speeches and the arithmetic of the credits became one of the period's quiet scandals inside the left. Cuba's position was warmer and more complicated. Fidel Castro's long visit in the government's first year, a tour of the country that stretched for weeks, delighted the government's supporters and furnished the opposition with a travelling exhibition of the future it predicted. Cuban advice, offered in public speeches and private counsel alike, pressed the government to prepare for the confrontation its enemies were preparing, advice the president heard with gratitude and did not take, because taking it would have dissolved the strategy he had been elected to execute. The Chilean road thus stood alone in a precise sense: the power that wished it dead was committed, the powers that wished it well were careful, and the experiment's survival depended on resources its friends would not supply and its enemies were determined to deny it. The asymmetry belongs in any weighing of the external pressure, because the external dimension of the case was never a contest between two equal interventions. It was a siege conducted against a government whose camp regarded it, at the level of state policy, as an interesting and probably temporary embarrassment.

## The battle for the airwaves and the printed page

No account of the polarisation is complete without the media, because Chile's political war was fought daily in print and on the radio with an intensity that contemporaries compared to a second civil life of the nation. El Mercurio, the country's newspaper of record, owned by the Edwards family and sustained through the advertising boycott of government supporters by covert American funds whose existence its editors denied and the Senate record established, conducted a campaign against the government that combined news selection, economic alarm and the steady amplification of every seizure, shortage and ministerial embarrassment into a portrait of terminal chaos. The government disposed of its own press, the Communist and Socialist dailies, a state television channel and radio networks, and used them with a stridency that its critics inside the coalition sometimes lamented. Between the two propagandas the space for a shared factual world contracted month by month. The same queue was sabotage's proof in one paper and hoarding's proof in the other; the same cordon was popular power or a soviet in embryo; the same general was a constitutionalist or a coup plotter in waiting, depending on the masthead. The government attempted at intervals to use its legal powers against the opposition press, most sharply in the affair of the paper La Segunda and in the long struggle over newsprint supply through the state influenced paper company, and each attempt confirmed the opposition's founding charge that press freedom stood on the government's list of eventual targets. The Statute of Democratic Guarantees, extracted as the price of confirmation, had named the press explicitly. By the final year the guarantees were a text both sides quoted and neither side believed the other intended to honour, which is as good a definition as the case affords of a republic whose common world has already ended before its institutions formally fall.

## Allende: the parliamentarian as president

The man at the centre of the case was himself one of its arguments. Salvador Allende was a physician from Valparaíso who had helped found the Chilean Socialist Party as a young man, served as minister of health in the Popular Front government of the late 1930s, where he wrote a pioneering study of the social determinants of disease, and then spent three decades in the senate, rising to its presidency, while contesting the presidency of the republic in election after election. He was, in the taxonomy of the Latin American left, a rarity: a revolutionary by programme and a parliamentarian by formation, a man who believed the transformation he sought and believed equally that it had to be voted. His political style was the style of the institution that formed him: negotiation, oratory, the long patience of committee and coalition, and a personal courtesy that extended to his enemies and disarmed more than one interviewer sent to find a demagogue. The opposition's portrait of him, as the smiling mask of a coming dictatorship, never fitted the man its own negotiators dealt with, which is one reason the centre's migration took three years rather than three months. His supporters' portrait, of a martyr who foresaw everything, fits no better. He misjudged the officer corps, trusted the constitutionalism of men who were already conspiring, postponed the coalition's reckoning until postponement was the only policy left, and discovered the plebiscite in the final week that a strategy of votes might have brandished in the final year. The case is not a hagiography and does not need one. Its analytical force depends on the government being neither a conspiracy nor a suicide but a serious attempt, led by a serious politician, whose failure therefore instructs.

His conduct on the last morning has entered the record in a form the forensic finding could not diminish. Offered exile by aircraft, he refused it. Offered the forms of resignation, he answered that he would leave the palace only dead, and he spent his last hours in the building his enemies were bombing, broadcasting the farewell that the country's memory has kept entire. Whatever verdict analysis reaches on the road, the manner of its author's death settled the question of his sincerity for the generations that inherited the case, and sincerity, in the politics of memory, is a force of its own. The Chilean left that rebuilt itself under the dictatorship and after it argued about Allende's strategy with increasing freedom as the decades passed, criticising the economic management, the coalition's indiscipline and the illusions about the military with a frankness the exile years had not permitted. It did not argue about whether he had meant what he said. The democratic road's later travellers, in Chile and far beyond it, inherited both the criticism and the certainty, and the tension between them is the case's living form.

## The final weeks: negotiation, plebiscite and the closing of every door

The government's last winter was a sequence of closing doors, each of which had stood open earlier in the story. Negotiation with the Christian Democrats, attempted in earnest in the middle of 1973 with the mediation of the church's cardinal, foundered on the parties' minimum terms: the opposition demanded the effective reversal of the transformation's contested parts and the removal of the ministers who embodied it, while the government offered administrative concessions that its own flank denounced as capitulation and its negotiators knew the centre would refuse. The military cabinet experiment was repeated, with officers entering the government in August in a last effort to guarantee order and the electoral calendar, and collapsed within weeks as the services' internal purges and the navy's arrests announced that the institution was preparing a different guarantee. The second truck owners' stoppage ground through the winter with the gremios now openly demanding the government's end. The chamber's August resolution supplied the legal costume. Inside the coalition, the argument between consolidation and advance reached its final form: the Communist Party and the president's closest advisers drafting the plebiscite appeal, the Socialist leadership and the MIR denouncing the very idea as surrender, and the president, characteristically, holding both drafts in his hand until the morning the choice was made for him. The plebiscite deserves its paragraph in the analytical record, because it was the democratic road's purest possible last move: confronted with institutions that had deadlocked, appeal to the only authority the strategy recognised, the electorate, and let the voters decide between the transformation and its opponents. That the coup arrived on the day appointed for its announcement is the kind of fact that tempts writers into symbolism. Analysis should resist the temptation and keep the drier truth. The plebiscite came in September because every earlier door had been tried in sequence and had closed, and a strategy that reaches its purest move only when no time remains to execute it has confessed, in its timetable, the postponements that consumed it.

## Could the Chilean road have succeeded?

The counterfactual question is unavoidable in this case and usually mishandled, because it is asked as though the answer were a verdict on the strategy's honour. Disciplined counterfactuals change one variable and hold the rest. Run the experiment in that form and the case yields more information than the polemics suggest. Change the economic course: suppose the government had stabilised after the first year, slowed the social area's growth, protected supply and accepted a slower redistribution. The crisis of the second year is milder, the queues shorter, the gremios weaker, the centre slower to migrate. The government still faces a hostile congress, a financed opposition press and an external campaign, and its own flank, denied advance, grows louder or splits. The road survives longer; whether it arrives anywhere is a further question, since stabilisation was precisely the policy the coalition's left existed to prevent. Change the coalition discipline: suppose the seizures had been stopped and the flank held to the programme. The centre's fear subsides, negotiation with the Christian Democrats becomes plausible, and the opposition's portrait of dual power loses its evidence. The government still faces the economic sequence and the credit siege, and it must enforce the discipline against its own supporters in the factories and fields, spending authority it may not recover. Change the external variable: suppose the United States had accommodated itself to the election, as it accommodated itself to other governments it disliked. Credit flows, the press campaign starves, the gremios negotiate rather than besiege, and the officer corps receives no signal that sedition would be welcomed abroad. The government still faces its plurality, its flank and the arithmetic of its own expansion. Each single change improves the prognosis; none guarantees arrival; and the historical government faced all three pressures at full strength simultaneously, which is the three pressures table's whole contention. The counterfactual exercise therefore returns a verdict more precise than either polemic. The road was not impossible. It was narrow, and it narrowed with every month in which the pressures compounded, until by the final winter the remaining corridor was the width of a plebiscite announcement. A strategy can be possible in September of one year and impossible by September of another, and Chile is the case that teaches the difference between a road's existence and its width.

### Why does the case turn on a plurality and a flank rather than on a single cause?

Because each single cause operated through the constraint the name describes. External pressure and economic crisis became fatal when they met a government that could not enlarge its support, because its flank kept frightening the centre, and could not discipline its support, because its majority did not exist.

## The lessons drawn: how the left metabolised the defeat

The Chilean case entered the world left's bloodstream within months of the coup and altered its strategies for a generation, which is a further reason the case repays study in this series. The Italian reflection has already been noted: Berlinguer's conclusion that a governing left in a polarised country needed the widest democratic alliance, and that even a narrow electoral majority would not suffice to govern safely, was drawn explicitly from Santiago's example and became the doctrine of the historic compromise. The French left absorbed the case into its arguments over the common programme and the meaning of a rupture with capitalism conducted by a government of the union of the left. In Britain, the case armed both sides of the Labour argument, the advocates of an alternative economic strategy citing the siege as proof that capital would not permit a gradual transition, and their opponents citing the flank and the queues as proof that gradualism required discipline and consent above all. In Latin America the lesson cut deepest and changed most. The generation that had debated the foco against the party now confronted a third exhibit: a government that had taken power by the road the foco theorists despised and had been destroyed without the means to defend itself. Some drew the conclusion that no road survives without armed capacity. Others, including much of the Chilean left in its long exile and clandestinity, drew the opposite and harder conclusion: that the coalition's own failures of discipline, economics and alliance building were the correctable causes, and that a future attempt would need a majority culture, a respect for the centre's fears and an economic policy that treated supply as a political fact. The Chilean transition that eventually ended the dictatorship was conducted by men and women formed in that second reading, and its famous caution, its negotiated character and its acceptance of gradualism are the case's longest shadow. The Pink Tide that later swept the region, which the series article on the [pink tide marxist analysis](/2019/01/01/pink-tide-marxist-analysis/) examines, governed with the Chilean file open on the desk: wary of the apparatus, attentive to the centre, and divided, as Chile had been divided, over how much of the lesson was prudence and how much was fear.

## The mechanics of the siege economy

The economic sequence rewards a closer mechanical account, because the polemics tend to describe it in moral vocabulary, miracle or sabotage, when its actual texture was a set of interacting mechanisms that a student of the case should be able to trace. The expansion began from slack. Chilean industry entered 1971 with unused capacity, and a redistribution toward wages could call that capacity into production without new investment, which is why the first year's figures of output and employment, whatever index is consulted, move together upward. The mechanism has a built in terminus. When the slack is absorbed, further demand meets capacity, imports and the printing press, and the same policy that produced growth produces shortage and inflation. The government's economists understood the terminus and argued about its date; the political calendar, which required the expansion to continue through the congressional elections, overruled the economic one. Price controls, imposed to protect the redistribution's beneficiaries, suppressed the signal that would have rationed demand and encouraged supply, and as the gap between controlled and real prices widened, the economy sorted itself into a legal circuit of queues and empty shelves and a parallel circuit of availability at punishing prices, with the state's own distribution apparatus struggling to privilege the first circuit's claimants. The fiscal deficit, driven by subsidies, by the social area's losses and by a tax base that the propertied were increasingly unwilling to fill, was financed by emission, and emission in an economy of indexed habits converted itself into inflation with a speed that surprised even the government's critics. Foreign exchange completed the mechanism. Copper earnings fell with the price, food imports rose as agricultural disruption bit, credit lines that had once renewed themselves automatically were cancelled under external pressure, and the reserves accumulated in better years drained away, leaving the central bank to ration dollars among claimants whose machinery, vehicles and medicines depended on them. None of these mechanisms required a conspiracy to operate. All of them were worsened, at identifiable points, by the documented external campaign and by the domestic refusal of the propertied to invest, lend or supply on the old terms. The siege economy is the right name for the result: a condition in which a government's errors and its enemies' efforts become indistinguishable in the citizen's daily experience, and in which the political question, who is doing this to us, has two true answers that cancel each other in the public mind.

## Land, shantytowns and the revolution from below

The transformation's social depth is easy to miss in accounts organised by ministries and generals, and it was in the countryside and the urban margins that the period's hopes and terrors were most concretely lived. The agrarian reform dissolved the fundo, the great estate whose patron had governed rural life as a small state, distributing justice, credit, work and punishment from the big house. For the inquilinos and the landless labourers of the central valley, the reform centres and cooperatives that replaced the estates were an emancipation experienced in the body: the end of deference, of the patron's permission to marry or to travel, of rations in place of wages. The emancipation was uneven, accompanied by falling production on some reformed units, by conflicts between the reform's beneficiaries and the landless who had been left outside it, and by the seizures through which communities pressed the ministry to move faster or wider than its plans. In the south, the land question intersected with the claim of the Mapuche people to territories taken from them in an earlier century, and occupations there carried a charge that the government's legal categories, designed for class rather than for nation, could not comfortably process. In the cities, the pobladores of the shantytowns, organised through land seizures that founded new settlements named for hopes and martyrs, built houses, committees and demands at a pace the housing ministry could not match, and the MIR and the coalition's left competed for their allegiance with the Christian Democrats' older networks. The revolution from below was real, and it is the indispensable background to the argument about the flank. The flank was not a faction's invention. It was the political form taken by millions of people for whom the legal road's timetable was the difference between a farm and another generation of waiting, between a house site and another winter in a relative's room. A government that had restrained them completely would have governed a quieter country and a smaller hope. The tragedy of the case is not that the government failed to choose between its strategy and its base. It is that the choice was genuine, that both horns of it were real, and that the time in which a balance might have been struck was consumed by the other two pressures while the argument proceeded.

## The balance sheet: what the government did and what survived

An assessment of the case that confines itself to the fall misses half the evidence, because the government's record in office is part of what any successor strategy must price. The copper nationalisation endured. The dictatorship, which reversed nearly everything else, did not return the great mines to their foreign owners, and the state copper corporation remained the fiscal spine of every subsequent Chilean government, including the market governments that succeeded the dictatorship; the unanimous vote of 1971 had identified the one transformation that the whole political nation, in the end, regarded as its own. The latifundio did not return either. The dictatorship returned many expropriated estates and crushed the peasant organisations, but the social order of the old countryside, the patron's small state, was gone beyond restoration, and Chilean agriculture was rebuilt on capitalist farms worked by wage labour rather than on the inquilino's servitude. The expansion of education, of health provision and of working class consumption in the government's first period left expectations that later governments of every colour have had to manage. Against this stands the debit side, which the same honesty must enter. The experiment's end delivered the country to a dictatorship that dismantled the labour movement, privatised the social conquests of decades and governed by terror for a generation, and the left that survived it drew the conclusion that a failed transition can cost more than no transition, a conclusion that has shadowed every subsequent attempt in the region. The balance sheet does not settle the argument. It disciplines it, by refusing both the romance in which nothing was achieved and the dismissal in which nothing was lost.

## The state question: what the case did to Marxist theory

Chile's theoretical afterlife runs through the Marxist theory of the state, for which the case became a compulsory exhibit, and the connection is worth drawing explicitly because it explains the case's position in this series. The debate between Miliband and Poulantzas over the nature of the capitalist state, covered by the series article on the [marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/), had been conducted in the abstract when the coup gave it a laboratory. Miliband's Chilean writings argued that the behaviour of the Chilean state, its courts obstructing, its congress declaring, its army finally striking, vindicated the view that state institutions are bound to the possessing class by personnel, socialisation and interest, and that a government which mistakes occupancy of office for possession of power will be educated by events. The structuralist rejoinder held that the lesson was subtler: that the state's relative autonomy is real but conditional, that it held while the transformation respected certain limits and collapsed when the class struggle outside the state raised the stakes beyond them, and that the decisive arena was therefore not the apparatus but the balance of forces in society, which the government had failed to shift in time. Both readings agreed on the fact that mattered, and the agreement is the case's theoretical deposit. Winning an election transfers the government. It does not transfer the state, whose other powers retain their own personnel, doctrines and external connections, and whose neutrality is a political achievement to be constructed and defended, not a constitutional fact to be presumed. Every serious democratic road proposed since has had to specify its answer to the apparatus question in advance, or be understood, by readers formed on Chile, as not yet serious. The specification has never been satisfactorily given, which is why the case remains open in the theory long after it closed in the streets.

## The opposition's two strategies: the soft road and the hard one

The opposition that destroyed the government was as divided about method as the coalition it faced, and the symmetry is instructive. One opposition strategy was constitutional and gradual: use the congressional majority to block, investigate and indict, use the courts and the comptroller to annul the government's instruments, use the gremios and the press to demonstrate ungovernability, and arrive either at the president's resignation under pressure or at his removal by the constitutional supermajority the March elections were meant to supply. This was the strategy of the Christian Democratic leadership for most of the period and of those National Party figures who still preferred legality's costume. The other strategy was the coup, prepared from early on by Patria y Libertad's provocations and by the conspiratorial networks inside and around the officer corps, and it required the first strategy as its antechamber: the constitutional campaign would establish the narrative of a government outside the law, exhaust the centre's scruples and present the eventual military action as restoration rather than rupture. The two strategies competed and cooperated by turns. The soft road's failure at the March elections, when the supermajority was missed, strengthened the hard road's argument that legality had been exhausted. The hard road's premature exposures, the tancazo above all, strengthened the soft road's insistence that the institution was not ready. The chamber's August resolution was the point of their convergence, a legal text whose function was to be read in the barracks. After it, the remaining question was not whether the armed forces would move but which faction of them would command the movement, and the government's intelligence, such as it was, spent the final weeks watching the wrong officers. The symmetry with the government's own division should not be pressed into a false equivalence: one side's flank embarrassed its strategy, the other side's flank executed it. But the structural resemblance is real, and it belongs to the case's general teaching. Polarised systems develop parallel radicalisations, in which each camp's maximalists supply the other camp's argument, and the centre, whose allegiance decides the outcome, is squeezed between two fires it did not light.

## The church, the cardinal and the mediation that failed

Between the camps stood one institution with the standing to mediate, and its failure to do so is the measure of the polarisation's depth. The Chilean Catholic church, led in Santiago by Cardinal Raúl Silva Henríquez, had travelled far from its historic alliance with the right: it had supported the Christian Democratic reform project, distributed its own lands to peasants in advance of the agrarian reform, and regarded the socialist experiment with a sympathy for its aims and an anxiety about its methods that made it the natural broker of any negotiated settlement. The cardinal's mediation in the final winter brought the government's and the Christian Democrats' representatives into the same rooms, and it failed on the substance that every mediation of the period failed on: the opposition's minimum was the transformation's reversal in the contested areas, the government's maximum was the transformation's pause, and between reversal and pause there was no formula that both flanks would permit their negotiators to sign. The church's own trajectory after the coup completes the paragraph with an irony the case specialises in. The institution that could not save the democratic road became, under the dictatorship, the principal shelter of its victims, through the Vicariate of Solidarity whose documentation of the repression fed the commissions of a later generation. Mediation failed in 1973 not because the mediator lacked standing but because mediation requires parties who prefer settlement to victory, and by that winter the decisive actors on every side preferred the risks of victory, or of honourable defeat, to the certainties of compromise. The plebiscite, the negotiation and the mediation were three doors opening onto the same room, and the room was empty.

## The strategic family: social democracy's shadow and the guerrilla alternative

Chile's road belonged to a family of strategies, and its meaning sharpens when the relatives are brought into the frame. To one side stood European social democracy, whose parties had long since traded transformation for management, and whose experience the Chilean Communists studied with a mixture of envy and resolve: envy of the durability that governing within capitalism conferred, resolve not to pay durability's price in abandoned aims. The Chilean claim was precisely that the trade was not compulsory, that a government could keep the destination and still govern, and the comparison with the social democratic record, developed in the series article on [marxism vs social democracy](/2019/11/15/marxism-vs-social-democracy/), is therefore not a courtesy link but the case's own self understanding. To the other side stood the guerrilla road, the strategy of the foco that the Cuban revolution had bequeathed to the region and that the MIR's founders had carried into Chilean politics from their student years. The comparison that the series article on [guerrilla vs electoral strategy](/2019/02/01/guerrilla-vs-electoral-strategy/) develops shows the two roads as mirror images of a single dilemma. The guerrilla road proposed to create the revolutionary majority by arms, dispensing with the electorate's timetable at the price of the population's consent. The electoral road proposed to create it by government, dispensing with arms at the price of exposure to the apparatus. Chile tested the second proposition to destruction in full view of the continent, and the generation that watched drew its conclusions with the smoke still in the air. What no Chilean road of the period could dispense with was the question both strategies were designed to evade: how a minority current, however armed or elected, becomes the majority it needs before the forces it threatens become the majority against it. The plurality and the flank names that question in its electoral form. Its guerrilla form has other names and other graveyards, and the regional guide to which this article belongs, the series survey of [marxism in Latin America](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/), traces the family through all its branches.

## The evidence and its keepers: archives, testimony and the politics of the record

The Chilean case is unusually well documented and unusually contested in its documentation, and a researcher entering it should know the record's shape. The external campaign is documented from the archives of the power that conducted it: the Senate investigation of the mid 1970s, whose published volumes remain the foundation, the intelligence files released under later declassification programmes, and the telephone transcripts and memoranda that surfaced piecemeal across the decades, each release renewing the controversy by adding granularity to a picture whose outline the first investigation had already established. The government's internal record is thinner and more dispersed: coalition documents, the minutes and polemics of the parties, the president's speeches and interviews, the economic data of the central bank and the statistical services, preserved through a dictatorship that had reason to curate the memory of the period it had ended. The testimony literature is vast and divided by the exile: participants wrote their accounts in Mexico City, Paris, East Berlin and Caracas, and the accounts argue with each other as the coalition had argued with itself, so that the historian of the internal division can reconstruct it from the memoirs almost line by line, provided the memoirs are read as pleadings rather than as transcripts. The commissions of the restored democracy added a different order of record, testimony taken under mandate about the repression, whose authority derives from its method rather than from any party's sponsorship. The scholarly literature built on these foundations is correspondingly layered: the first wave of participant history, the documentary wave after the Senate investigation, the economic reassessments as the data were reworked, and the later cultural and social histories that recovered the period's lived texture in the poblaciones and the provinces. A reader who wants the case whole must read across the layers and across the languages, correcting, as the earlier note on the Spanish archive advised, for the gravitational pull of the English record toward the external story.

## The first hundred days and the meaning of the honeymoon

The government's opening season is worth reconstructing because it contained, in embryo, every later development, including the ones that destroyed it. The inauguration itself was a festival of the left's long arrival: the stadium filled, the delegations of the world's socialist movements attending, the new president speaking the language of legality and transformation in a single breath and meaning both. The first measures were chosen for their breadth of consent. Diplomatic relations opened to Cuba and the socialist states, to the applause of the government's base and the tolerable discomfort of the centre. The copper process began its legislative journey. The banks changed hands by purchase. A wage round delivered the redistribution's first instalment, and the shops, stocked from inventories and imports, could honour it. The amnesty for political prisoners of the previous order and the pardon of the young naval and left activists of earlier conspiracies signalled the new government's clemency, and were read by its enemies as the first indulgence of illegality. Already the two readings of every act were available, and already the choice between them was being made by audiences rather than by facts. The municipal elections of April delivered the honeymoon's verdict: the coalition advanced, the right retreated, and the Christian Democrats held enough to remain the indispensable partner. In the palace the result was read as the road widening. In the opposition's headquarters it was read as the last warning to organise before the road became irreversible. Both readings were rational. The honeymoon ended not with a scandal or a defeat but with the slow arrival of the expansion's bill, and by the time the empty pots marched in December the period's grammar was set: every fact would arrive with its two interpretations attached, and the country's centre would spend three years deciding which interpretation to live in, before the decision was taken out of its hands.

## Women, households and the war of the shopping basket

The Chilean conflict was gendered in its daily conduct to a degree the classic accounts, organised around parties and barracks, long underplayed. The household was the terrain on which the economic sequence was experienced, and women managed the household. The government's distribution committees drew working class women into the organisation of supply, where they became the transformation's local face and its local target. The opposition's mobilisation of middle class women, from the empty pots march of the first December through the cacerolazos that sounded from the wealthy districts' windows in the final year, gave the resistance a moral vocabulary that party politics could not supply: the mother defending the family's table against an ideology that had emptied it. The government answered awkwardly, torn between its feminist commitments, which were real and advanced for their setting, and its instinct to dismiss the marchers as the servants of privilege, an instinct that blinded it to the genuine subsistence fear the marches also carried. The left's own women, in the parties, the unions and the poblaciones, lived the period's double burden, queueing for the collective and organising for the cause, and their testimony, gathered by later historians, complicates every camp's mythology: the same woman might defend the distribution committee against the hoarders in the morning and curse the government's prices by night. That daily war matters to the analysis because it was the mechanism by which macroeconomic failure became street politics. Inflation statistics persuade economists. Empty shelves persuade neighbourhoods, and the neighbourhoods of the centre, whose allegiance the road required, were persuaded in the wrong direction by an experience no pamphlet could argue with.

## The road not taken: the centre's own revolution

The Chilean tragedy is sometimes written as though the only alternatives on offer were the government's transformation and the right's restoration, and the record refuses the simplification. The Christian Democracy that Allende defeated in 1970 had itself governed for six years under the banner of a revolution in liberty, had begun the copper Chileanisation, had passed the agrarian reform law the government later wielded, had unionised the peasantry and had promised a communitarian alternative to both capitalism and Marxism. Its candidate in 1970, Radomiro Tomic, campaigned on a programme in several respects as radical as the coalition's, and the votes that separated the two projects were the votes of a country that wanted deep change and divided over its agent and its pace. The centre's subsequent migration to the coup's antechamber was therefore not the unfolding of an essence. It was a political process with authors and dates: the hardening of the party's leadership after its electoral defeats, the influence of its own frightened electorate, the government's errors supplying the evidence, the right's patient courtship, and the flank's provocations arriving like gifts. Eduardo Frei, the former president and the centre's towering figure, travelled the distance in public, from the statesman who had accepted the constitutional succession to the elder whose letter to a European colleague, circulated after the coup, explained the military's action as the necessary answer to an unconstitutional government, a document his admirers and his critics still contest line by line. The centre's journey is the case's most melancholy subplot, because the democratic road's arithmetic always required the centre's tolerance, and the centre was not a fixed reactionary bloc but a moving one, whose movement the government's own camp influenced for the worse at nearly every turn. A successor strategy that treats the centre as lost in advance will repeat the error in its purest form.

## The labour movement: the CUT, the strikes and the double allegiance

The organised working class was the government's foundation and, in the stoppages, part of its opposition, and the duality deserves a section of its own. The Central Única de Trabajadores, the unified labour confederation led by Communists and Socialists, supported the government as its own, supplied its ministers and its street power, and negotiated the wage rounds that drove the redistribution. Yet the period's most damaging strikes were not all the employers' work. The copper miners of El Teniente, the aristocracy of Chilean labour, struck for weeks in the government's final year over a wage formula, in a dispute the government regarded as economically ruinous and politically suicidal and the miners regarded as the defence of conquests no government of theirs had the right to trim. The strike divided the left's conscience: the president appealed to the miners as the transformation's vanguard and was refused, and the refusal demonstrated a truth the coalition's rhetoric preferred to skip, that the organised working class was a federation of interests and traditions, not an instrument awaiting a hand. In the industrial belts the cordones answered to assemblies in which the coalition's parties competed with the MIR and with independents of no party, and their allegiance to the government was conditional on the government's allegiance to their own advance. The government's enemies understood the duality and exploited it, presenting every labour conflict within the camp as the revolution devouring itself. The analytical point survives the polemic. A democratic road rests on a coalition of classes and organisations whose unity is a daily achievement, and the Chilean government's base, for all its loyalty unto death in the final defence of the factories, could not be commanded in the manner of an army, because it had never enlisted as one.

## The provinces and the sea: the geography of the confrontation

Santiago dominates the literature of the case, and the geography of the confrontation repays correction. The copper north, Antofagasta and the great mines of Chuquicamata and El Teniente in the centre, was the government's industrial heartland, where the unions were oldest and the transformation's promise most tangible. The agricultural centre and south were the agrarian reform's theatre, where the seizures ran fastest and the landowners' resistance organised earliest, and where the gremio of the truck owners drew its strength from the haulage of a harvest the state could not move without them. Concepción, the industrial and university city of the south, was the MIR's stronghold and the cordon movement's second capital, a place where the argument between the government's legality and the flank's popular power was conducted at the highest theoretical level in the country and with the least patience on either side. Valparaíso, the port, was where the navy began the coup in the dawn of the final day, and the choice was not incidental: the fleet's officers had purged their own ranks through the winter, and the port city's working class districts were among the first to learn what the new order intended. The geography matters because the pressures of the table were distributed unevenly across it. The economic sequence bit hardest where supply lines were longest. The internal division was fiercest where the flank was strongest. The external action worked through the ports, the credit and the copper price that the north produced. A map of the case is therefore not an illustration but an analysis, and the student who can say where each pressure lived understands the case better than the student who can recite the palace's last day by the hour.

## Exile, solidarity and the case's second life abroad

The coup scattered the Chilean left across the world, and the scattering became one of the case's principal mechanisms of influence. Tens of thousands of Chileans, the phrase must remain general because the commissions' province is the repression's scale and exile's arithmetic belongs to the same contested record, made their lives in Mexico and Venezuela, in France and Sweden, in both German states and in the Soviet Union, and they carried the argument with them. The solidarity movement that received them, the committees, the benefit concerts, the boycott campaigns against the junta's trade, was among the largest international mobilisations of its decade, and it kept the case before the world's left as a living question rather than a closed file. In the exile the argument about the fall continued with an intensity the dictatorship's censorship made impossible at home: the parties reconstituted their leaderships abroad, the recriminations between the coalition's components were published in journals on three continents, and the slow revision that produced the renovated Chilean socialism of the later years, with its acceptance of democracy as a value rather than a route, was drafted in the exile's seminars long before it could be spoken in Santiago. The case's second life abroad thus fed back into its first country. When the dictatorship finally ended, it was ended by a coalition whose senior figures had spent the interval metabolising 1973, and whose famous prudence, derided by some as surrender and praised by others as wisdom, was the Chilean lesson institutionalised. The series' regional survey follows the same exiles into the continent's later history, where the Chilean file was opened at every subsequent crisis of the Latin American left, from the Argentine and Uruguayan catastrophes to the Pink Tide's deliberations, as the standing exhibit in the argument about roads.

## The prehistory: the near misses that taught the road its shape

The election of 1970 was the fourth act of a drama whose earlier acts explain the strategy's design. In 1958 Allende had come within a whisker of the presidency at the head of a narrower left alliance, defeated by Jorge Alessandri in a race where a populist third candidate's votes might otherwise have carried him. In 1964, facing Eduardo Frei and a Christian Democracy armed with the slogan of revolution in liberty, he was defeated heavily, in an election where the United States, as its own later investigations established, spent heavily to ensure the outcome, regarding the Chilean vote as a test of whether a democratic alternative to the Cuban model could be made to win. The lesson the left drew from the two defeats shaped 1970 in every particular. From 1958 came the conviction that the road existed: the electorate for a socialist president was not a fantasy but a near majority awaiting assembly. From 1964 came the conviction that assembly required breadth: the coalition of 1970 was wider than its predecessors, its programme drafted to hold Radicals and Christian leftists as well as Marxists, its candidate's constitutionalism advertised as a guarantee to the centre. And from both came the strategy's deepest assumption, that Chilean institutions were sturdy enough to survive the left's victory and be used by it. The assumption was not naive. It had been tested by the century: Chile had absorbed a brief socialist republic in the 1930s, a Popular Front with Communist ministers, a Communist Party outlawed for a decade and then legal again, military interventions in the republic's youth and a long professional abstinence since. The road's designers believed the institutions had digested every previous shock and would digest theirs. What the prehistory could not show them was the effect of the transformation's scale on the institutions' digestion, nor the willingness of the hemisphere's hegemon to treat the experiment as an infection to be cauterised. The road was built from the best evidence available to its builders. The evidence was genuine and insufficient, which is the condition of most consequential political judgement.

## The doctrine of the barracks: Schneider's tradition and its erosion

The Chilean officer corps that the road depended on had a doctrine of itself, and the doctrine's erosion is a study in how institutions change their minds. The professional military of the middle century prided itself on a constitutionalism that distinguished it, in its own telling, from the praetorian armies of the continent: promotion by seniority and merit, abstention from party, obedience to the elected authority, and a corporate honour invested in that abstention. General Schneider's articulation of the doctrine in 1970, that the army existed to guarantee the constitutional process and would oppose any attempt to divert it, was not an improvisation but the tradition's self description at the moment of its supreme test, and his assassination made him its martyr before the government he protected had taken office. The tradition's erosion proceeded by pressures the doctrine had no vocabulary for. The government's enemies addressed the officers not as politicians but as Chileans: as men whose families queued, whose salaries inflation dissolved, whose clubs and parishes shared the centre's fear, and whose honour was daily insulted, in the opposition's telling, by a government that tolerated the armed posturing of its own flank. The government's courtship, conducted through budgets, postings and the president's personal courtesy, could not compete with that daily address, because the courtship asked the officers to defend a process while the address asked them to defend their world. Within the services, the conspiracy organised itself through the channels the institution trusted: the navy's intelligence moving first, the air force's commander joining late and decisively, the army's constitutionalists isolated one by one until Prats's resignation removed the last obstacle of rank. The doctrine did not collapse because it was insincere. It collapsed because the society whose settlement it expressed had itself collapsed into camps, and an army that mirrors its society cannot hold a line its society has abandoned. The classical objection, restated in this light, acquires its precise Chilean form: the apparatus was never the government's to presume upon, because the apparatus's neutrality was a social fact, and the social fact was among the casualties of the polarisation the government's own camp had helped to deepen.

## The junta's founding fiction and the uses of a fabricated plan

The regime that took power in September 1973 understood that its legitimacy required a story, and the story it manufactured is part of the case's evidence, because it shows what the coup's authors believed they needed to prove. Within weeks the junta published a White Book alleging the existence of a Plan Z, a supposed Popular Unity conspiracy to assassinate the military high command and a list of political and social figures, and to install a dictatorship of the left by violence on a date the coup had supposedly forestalled. The document was presented as the captured archive of the conspiracy. Its standing in the serious literature is not contested in the way the period's other questions are contested: historians across the interpretive spectrum treat the White Book as a fabrication assembled from genuine fragments, lists of names drawn from the government's own contingency papers and inventions, produced to convert a coup against a constitutional government into a preemptive defence of the nation. The fabrication matters beyond its mendacity. It concedes, by its existence, that the coup required the government's illegality to be total and imminent, that the mere record of seizures, speeches and disorder was insufficient warrant for what had been done, and that even the officers who had bombed the palace felt the need of a plot to answer the question the world would ask. The government's defenders sometimes rest too much on the White Book, as though the exposure of the junta's fiction settled the argument about the government's own record. It does not. The flank's rhetoric and the cordones' existence were real, and the junta's propagandists built their fabrication from the fear those realities had genuinely inspired, which is the usual recipe of the effective lie. What the White Book settles is narrower and sufficient: the claim that the coup prevented an imminent left dictatorship was manufactured after the fact by the men who benefited from it, and any account of the case that repeats the claim as history has mistaken the victors' brief for the record.

## The historiography's waves: how the argument has moved

The writing of the case has passed through phases that track the evidence's release and the region's politics, and a map of the phases helps a reader place any given account. The first writing was the participants', produced in exile and in the coup's immediate shadow, in which the government's camp conducted its reckoning in public: the Communists' self criticism of the coalition's indiscipline, the Socialists' counter charge that gradualism had disarmed the revolution, the MIR's vindication of its warnings, and the president's defenders assembling the record of the external campaign as the declassifications began. The second phase was documentary, built on the Senate investigation and the first intelligence releases, and it shifted the centre of gravity toward Washington, sometimes past the point the documents themselves could carry, as though the discovery of the conspiracy dissolved the need to explain the Chileans. The third phase was the economists' return, in which the sequence of expansion and crisis was reconstructed from the data with increasing precision and decreasing charity toward every camp's mythology, establishing the mechanical account this article has drawn on. The fourth phase, the social and cultural histories, recovered the period from below: the poblaciones, the cordones as lived experience, the women of the queues, the provinces, the Mapuche south, and in doing so restored the flank from a faction label to a population with reasons. The phases have not superseded one another so much as accumulated, and the mature literature holds all four records open at once, which is the condition the three pressures table formalises. A reader who meets an account written wholly inside one phase should enjoy its strengths and supply its absences from the others. The case is large enough to have generated a library, and the library's disagreements are now themselves part of the evidence about how democracies and lefts remember a defeat that neither has finished processing.

## The universities, the students and the generation of the experiment

The Chilean universities were the experiment's intellectual engine room and one of its battlefields, and their part in the case illuminates the flank's origins. The university reform movement of the late 1960s, which swept the Catholic University and the University of Chile in the same season that students occupied campuses across the world, had democratised the institutions' government, opened their doors wider and politicised a generation that entered the 1970s expecting history to be made quickly. The MIR was born in that milieu, at Concepción, and the coalition's parties recruited their cadres from the same lecture halls, so that the argument between legality and advance was conducted, at one level, among contemporaries who had shared the reform's barricades and divided over its meaning. The government's education projects, above all the proposal for a unified national school system in its final year, became one of the polarisation's sharpest fronts, because the opposition could present the reform of the classroom as the transformation reaching for the nation's children, and the church, the private schools and the parents' associations mobilised accordingly. The episode generalises a mechanism the case exhibits repeatedly. A transformation that proceeds sector by sector discovers that every sector is somebody's whole world, and that the defence of each world can be organised faster than the transformation can explain itself. The students who filled the government's marches and the flank's assemblies were also, disproportionately, the generation the dictatorship would hunt through the universities in the years after, purging the faculties and remaking the institutions under rectors appointed by the armed forces. The engine room was dismantled with a thoroughness that testified to its importance, and the Chilean university that eventually reemerged carried the case in its institutional memory as both inheritance and warning.

## What a different September would have required

It is worth gathering, in one place, the minimum conditions under which the government's survival past its term's crisis can be imagined, because the exercise converts the three pressures from a narrative into a specification. The government needed an economic course corrected by the middle of its second year at the outside, which required a finance ministry empowered against the coalition's spending constituencies and a president willing to spend his popularity on stabilisation while popularity remained. It needed a coalition settlement, imposed rather than exhorted, under which the seizures stopped, the flank's organisations accepted the programme's boundaries, and the Socialist Party's public voice was reconciled to the government's line, which required a confrontation inside the camp that Allende's whole formation disposed him to postpone. It needed a Christian Democratic interlocutor preserved from the migration to the right, which required the first two conditions to be visible early enough to give the centre's negotiators something to defend in their own party. It needed the officer corps' constitutionalists reinforced rather than isolated, which required that the centre's preservation deny the conspirators their civilian warrant and that the government refrain from every measure, however justified, that the barracks could read as the construction of a rival armed force. And it needed, beneath all of these, time: the electoral calendar's next test survived, the transformation's benefits consolidated among the uncommitted, the precedent of a left government completing its term and handing over power or renewing its mandate becoming the fact that normalised the experiment. Each condition was individually conceivable. Their conjunction was the road's true width, and the conjunction failed because the conditions were not independent: the economic correction required the coalition settlement, the settlement required the centre, the centre required the barracks quiet, and the barracks required everything else. The specification is the case's final analytical gift. Successor strategies can be audited against it, item by item, and a strategy that cannot say how it will meet the conditions jointly, rather than severally, has not yet answered Chile.

## The plurality and the flank: the case's lesson restated

The argument of this article can now be gathered into the claim its title promises. The Chilean road failed under three pressures, an economic sequence that turned a redistribution into a siege, an external campaign that the archives have documented instruction by instruction, and an internal division that set the government's own camp against its strategy. The pressures were real in unequal measure and they compounded, and the compounding is the finding. But the compounding had a geometry, and the geometry is the plurality and the flank. A government elected by the largest minority, in a system whose other powers it did not control, undertook a transformation whose completion required it to become a majority while governing. Becoming a majority required the centre's tolerance, and the centre's tolerance was destroyed less by the government's programme than by its flank's demonstration that the programme's boundaries would not hold. The flank acted as it did because the transformation's promises, to people who had waited generations, could not be scheduled like statutes, and because a movement outside the coalition stood ready to organise every impatience into a strategy. The external campaign and the economic crisis supplied the pressure; the plurality and the flank supplied the shape of the collapse, the reason the pressure found no majority to break against and no discipline to absorb it. The lesson is therefore not that legal transformation is impossible, which the copper vote and the growing electoral support refute, nor that it is safe, which the burning palace refutes. The lesson is a specification of its preconditions: a majority, assembled before the confrontation or during it, and a coalition whose discipline is a fact rather than a hope. A road attempted without those preconditions is not thereby dishonourable, and Chile's attempt retains, fifty years of polemic notwithstanding, the dignity of its evidence. But the preconditions are the difference between a strategy and a wager, and the case's service to every successor is that it priced the wager exactly.

## The battle of production: participation and the social area's discontents

The social property area was not only an ownership category. It was a promise about how production would be governed, and the promise generated its own politics. The government's agreement with the labour confederation established norms of worker participation in the nationalised enterprises: councils in which elected workers sat beside the state appointed interventor, production committees in the factories, and a rhetoric of the enterprise as a collective conquest. The reality varied by plant. In some, participation gave the transformation a daily substance that no decree could have supplied, and production rose on the strength of workers defending what they regarded as theirs. In others, the interventor and the council fought over appointments, discipline and the pace of work, and the social area acquired a reputation for disorder that the opposition press harvested assiduously. The deeper difficulty was structural. The social area's enterprises were expected simultaneously to raise wages, hold prices, increase output, absorb employment and generate the surplus for the transformation's next stage, a set of instructions no management on earth could have executed jointly, and their mounting losses were covered by the state bank's credit, which is to say by the emission that fed the inflation. The battle of production, the government's campaign of 1972 to raise output by voluntary effort, Sunday work and patriotic exhortation, was the recognition of the difficulty in campaign form. It produced genuine heroism in individual plants and no solution in aggregate, because the aggregate problem was not effort but the price and credit system within which effort was expended. The flank drew one conclusion, that participation should become power and the councils should command. The government's economists drew another, that the social area had to be consolidated at its existing boundary and made to balance. The argument between those conclusions, conducted in the language of production statistics, was the coalition's central argument wearing overalls, and it was never settled before the barracks settled it.

## The assassination of Pérez Zujovic and the price of the fringe

In June 1971 Edmundo Pérez Zujovic, who had been interior minister under the previous Christian Democratic government and was held responsible by the left for a massacre of squatters in Puerto Montt during his tenure, was assassinated in Santiago by members of a small armed group of the far left, the Organised Vanguard of the People. The killing was the young government's first great crisis of order, and Allende's response defined his method at its most characteristic: he condemned the assassination without qualification, pursued the killers with the state's full apparatus, and refused the opposition's demand that the crime be laid at the door of the transformation itself. The episode nonetheless cost the government dearly, and the costs itemise the flank problem in its violent register. The Christian Democrats, burying their former minister, moved measurably away from the government the assassins claimed to be hurrying forward. The right acquired its enduring exhibit for the claim that the government's legality was a facade behind which murder gathered. And the government was forced to spend authority on policing a fringe whose members believed they were its truest servants, while the fringe's survivors denounced the police work as proof of betrayal. A democratic road must monopolise not only legitimacy but the appearance of order, because its majority building depends on the uncommitted believing that the state's protection extends to them. Every act of fringe violence transferred that belief to the opposition at no cost to the opposition, which is why the classical strategists of the government's own camp regarded the armed fringe, for all its smallness, as objectively an auxiliary of the coup long before the coup had a date.

## Lo Curro and the argument that would not close

In June 1972 the coalition's leadership withdrew to a residence at Lo Curro, in the foothills above Santiago, to settle the argument over consolidation and advance, and the meeting's failure to settle it is the internal history of the government in miniature. The consolidation case was argued by the Communists and by the government's economic pragmatists: the transformation had reached the limit of what the country's tolerance and the economy's capacity could bear, the social area's boundary should be fixed and published, the seizures should end, the private sector should be given guarantees it could bank on, and the government's remaining strength should be spent on supply, on the centre and on reaching the congressional elections in governable condition. The advance case was argued by the Socialist leadership's dominant current and echoed by the MIR outside: consolidation was the name the retreat would wear, the enemy was preparing regardless, the workers' organisations were the only real conquest, and a government that fixed its boundary in the middle of a revolution would be overthrown inside the boundary it had drawn. Allende, presiding, produced the synthesis that postponed the decision: a speech that endorsed consolidation in its economics and advance in its rhetoric, a new economy minister of the pragmatic school, and no enforcement machinery for either. The participants left believing their line had prevailed, which is the definition of a failed summit. Lo Curro matters because it demonstrates that the coalition's indiscipline was not a failure of information. The government's leaders understood the trap this article has named, stated it to each other with complete clarity in a closed room, and could not act on the understanding, because acting on it required one component to defeat another in public. The plurality and the flank was not a fate imposed on innocents. It was a diagnosis the patients shared and a treatment none would take.

## The arms control law and the raids on the factories

Among the mechanisms by which the polarisation armed itself, none did more damage than the arms control law, a statute the congress had passed, with the government's own votes in an earlier season of order keeping, empowering the armed forces to search for illegally held weapons. In the government's final year the services began to execute the law in the industrial belts, raiding factories and working class districts in searches whose ostensible object was the left's supposed arsenals and whose practical effect was to demonstrate to the workers of the cordones that the state they had defended in October now entered their plants as an occupying power. The searches found little, because the Chilean left, for all its rhetoric, was lightly armed, a fact the coup's later propaganda laboured to obscure. What the searches found instead was the government's authority, which drained away with each raid it could neither order nor prevent. The president protested the manner of the searches to commanders who answered to the law and to their own institution's judgement. The workers drew the conclusion the flank had always urged, that the apparatus was preparing the coup in rehearsal and that only their own organisation stood between them and the rehearsals' final performance. The opposition drew the conclusion that the factories were fortresses the army was right to inspect. A law intended to keep the republic's monopoly of force had become the instrument by which each side confirmed the other's worst account, and the episode deserves its place in the case's anatomy because it shows the coercive question arriving not in September but month by month, in legal form, under a statute the government's own coalition had helped to pass in the belief that order was its friend.

## The world stage: the United Nations address and the diplomacy of a besieged experiment

In December 1972 Allende addressed the General Assembly of the United Nations in New York and delivered the experiment's case to the world in its own terms. The speech denounced the aggression of the great corporations and the financial blockade, named the copper companies' litigation as an attempt to strangle a nation's lawful decision, and presented Chile as a country conducting, in conditions of siege, the experiment of a pluralist and legal transition that the charter's principles obliged the world to respect. It was received with an ovation by the assembly's majority and with cold fury in Washington, and it belongs in the record because it shows the government conducting its defence on the terrain where it was strongest, the terrain of law and legitimacy, before the audience least able to help it. The diplomacy of the period ran on parallel tracks. Chile renegotiated its foreign debt with the creditor governments at Paris, obtaining rescheduling from most and refusal from the United States. It diversified its trade toward Argentina, Mexico, the socialist states and Western Europe, signed agreements whose tonnages never replaced the American market and the American spare parts, and learned the material meaning of dependence: an economy built for half a century around one supplier of machinery and one buyer of copper cannot reorient itself at the speed of a diplomatic communiqué. The world stage gave the government prestige, solidarity and a hearing. It could not give it credit, parts or time, and the distance between the ovation in New York and the queue in Santiago is the measure of the experiment's international position, admired as a symbol and unsupported as a state.

## The culture of the experiment: Quimantú, the new song and the printed word

The transformation had a cultural front whose achievements outlasted it and whose history complicates any account of the period as pure crisis. The state publishing house Quimantú, built on the purchased plant of a private publisher, issued books at prices set for working class buyers, classic literature, Chilean history, political education and children's stories, in print runs that reached readers the book trade had never served, and its kiosks became a fixture of the period's streetscape. The new song movement, whose musicians had sung the coalition into office, carried the experiment's hopes into a music that fused folk instruments with the continent's politics, and its leading figures became the period's international emissaries and, after the coup, among its most famous victims and exiles. Murals covered the walls of the poblaciones, theatre companies toured the factories, and the national film institute's successors documented the process in footage the dictatorship would spend years trying to destroy and the exile years preserving. The cultural flourishing matters to the analysis for a reason beyond its beauty. It was the transformation as its supporters experienced it from inside: not queues and decrees but a sudden widening of the world, books in houses that had held none, music that treated the poor as history's protagonists, a dignity distributed faster than the goods. The opposition experienced the same culture as propaganda's flood and the occupation of the nation's symbols by a faction. Both experiences were authentic, and their coexistence in a single society is the polarisation's cultural definition. When the coup came it understood the culture's importance perfectly, and its bonfires of books, its banned songs and its murdered singer announced that the war had always been, among other things, about whose story the country would tell.

## The supply apparatus: distribution as a battlefield

The government's attempt to organise supply deserves a closer account, because distribution became the point where the transformation met every citizen daily and where its enemies found their most persuasive evidence. The state inherited a distribution system of private wholesalers and retailers whose cooperation the transformation could not presume, and it built, piece by piece, a parallel apparatus: the state trading company importing food directly, the supply directorate policing prices and pursuing hoarders, and at the base the JAP, the supply and price committees, organised by neighbourhood to receive the state's consignments and distribute them against the ration of need rather than the ration of money. The apparatus worked where it worked by substituting organisation for abundance, and its political meaning reversed itself according to the observer's district. In the poblaciones the JAP were the government's care made visible, the guarantee that the little there was would reach the poor first, and their activists, largely women, became the local state in its most legitimate form. In the middle class districts the same apparatus was denounced as the machinery of a coming rationing by party, the proof that scarcity was not the government's misfortune but its instrument, and the opposition's press catalogued every abuse, real and invented, with which the committees' enemies supplied it. Hoarding prosecutions, in which inspectors discovered warehouses of goods withheld from the controlled market, furnished the government's counter evidence, and each discovery was true and insufficient, because the parallel market was not a conspiracy layered over a functioning economy but the economy's own adjustment to prices that would not clear. The supply war's lesson generalises grimly. A government that controls prices in a scarcity becomes the author of the scarcity in the public's account, whatever the scarcity's true parentage, and it will be judged at the shop counter by citizens who cannot be asked to distinguish the siege's contribution from the ministry's. Distribution is where macroeconomic argument goes to become biography, and the government's biography, in its final year, was written in the queue.

## The navy, the sailors and the purge inside the fleet

The coup's institutional preparation had a chapter inside the navy that the government watched with horror and could not use. Through the winter of 1973 naval intelligence arrested sailors and junior personnel suspected of left sympathies and of contact with the coalition's parties, and subjected many of them to torture in the fleet's own facilities, on the theory that the service was being subverted from below by the government's agents. The episode reached the government through the victims' families and through officers appalled at the methods, and it presented the president with the apparatus question in its purest form. To intervene was to confirm the charge that the government sought to politicise the services. To refrain was to abandon citizens to torture by an institution of the state he headed, and to allow the purge to proceed as the conspiracy's rehearsal. The government protested, investigated within the limits the service permitted, and achieved nothing that slowed the preparation, while the navy's command drew from the episode its own conclusion, that the fleet had barely escaped subversion and that September's action was self defence. The sailors' case matters because it refutes, from inside the institution, the notion that the armed forces were dragged into politics at the last moment by civilian disorder. Parts of the apparatus were preparing the political outcome, by terror inside their own ranks, while the government was still negotiating with the centre and drafting its plebiscite, and the preparation's discovery changed nothing because the government possessed no instrument with which to change it. The coercive apparatus, here, is no abstraction. It has a deck, a brig and a list of names, and the names were being collected while the republic's forms still stood.

## The long apprenticeship: the Chilean left before the victory

The coalition that won in 1970 was the product of the oldest organised left in Latin America, and its habits, strengths and blindnesses were formed across half a century that the case's analysis should not skip. The Chilean labour movement began in the nitrate fields of the northern desert, where the mancomunales and the federation built by Luis Emilio Recabarren, the printer who founded the Socialist Workers Party that became the Communist Party, created a tradition of working class organisation that survived massacres, deportations and the desert's own collapse as an industry. The Socialist Party, founded in 1933 by a generation that included the young Allende, gathered the traditions the Communists' discipline excluded: anti imperialism, a Latin Americanist imagination, an insurrectionary rhetoric coexisting with parliamentary practice. The Popular Front of the late 1930s brought both parties into government as junior partners of the Radicals and taught them the arts and the frustrations of coalition from inside. The Cold War then drove the Communists underground for a decade under a law their former allies had passed, an experience that burned into the party the conviction that legality was a treasure to be defended rather than a bourgeois trick, and that helps explain the discipline with which the Communists later defended Allende's road against the flank's impatience. The FRAP alliance of Socialists and Communists contested the elections of 1958 and 1964 on the programme the seventies would inherit, and the near victory of the first and the heavy defeat of the second supplied the strategy's empirical education. By 1970 the Chilean left was no improvisation but a veteran movement with deep unions, experienced cadres, a parliamentary culture and a theory of its own road, and it carried into government the veteran's characteristic assets and defects together: patience, organisation and an underestimation, bred by decades of survival, of how completely the ground could be cut away when the stakes rose past the point survival had rehearsed.

The tradition also explains the coalition's internal geography better than any organigram. The Communists' legality was the legality of a party that had been outlawed within living memory and had rebuilt itself by demonstrating its reliability to the republic's institutions. The Socialists' radicalism was the radicalism of a party that had never been outlawed as a whole, had governed in fronts and oppositions by turns, and had absorbed, in the sixties, a generation for whom the Cuban example outshone the parliamentary one. The Radicals' presence recalled the older centre left coalitions and supplied ministers the bureaucracy knew. The MIR's founders had been expelled from, or had resigned out of, precisely these traditions, and their critique, that the veterans' patience was the revolution's anaesthetic, was an internal family argument conducted with the intimacy of people who had shared its households. When the article speaks of the flank, it speaks of this family argument at the moment when the family's house was on fire, and the participants' inability to settle it was not a failure of intelligence but the weight of fifty years of genuinely different conclusions drawn from genuinely shared experience.

## The other apprenticeship: the right, the gremios' ancestry and Patria y Libertad

The government's opponents had traditions as deep as its own, and symmetry of treatment requires their reconstruction. The Chilean right descended from the landed and commercial oligarchy that had built the republic in the nineteenth century and had learned, across the twentieth, to rule through a combination of property, press and the congressional veto, retreating from the presidency when necessary while retaining the economy and the courts of opinion. Its modernisation in the sixties produced the National Party, a harder and more ideological formation than the old Conservative and Liberal parties it merged, committed to the defence of property as a system and increasingly contemptuous of the Christian Democratic centre's reformism, which it regarded as communism's antechamber. The gremialista movement, born in the Catholic University's student politics under the theorist Jaime Guzmán, supplied the right with its most original doctrine of the period: the claim that the intermediate bodies of society, the guilds, the professions, the universities and the municipalities, possessed an autonomy that both the Marxist state and the party political machine threatened, and that their defence was the nation's true constitution. Gremialismo gave the truck owners and the professional associations a philosophy grander than their grievances, and it would later supply the dictatorship with its constitutional architect. Patria y Libertad stood at the tradition's violent edge: founded in the inauguration's aftermath by the lawyer Pablo Rodríguez, financed in part, as the declassified record later showed, through channels the intelligence services kept at arm's length, and devoted to making the country ungovernable by sabotage of railways, power lines and bridges, its young militants bombing and strafing in the conviction that a crisis would summon the military. The government dissolved it after the tancazo, in whose preparation its networks were implicated, and its leaders fled abroad or into the conspiracy's protection. Between the National Party's parliamentarians and Patria y Libertad's saboteurs lay the same spectrum the government faced in mirror image: a respectable wing that profited from the disreputable wing's pressure while deploring its methods, and a disreputable wing that counted on the respectable wing's victory to pardon it. The symmetry was lost on neither camp's strategists, and it is one of the case's colder lessons that polarisation manufactures the same structure on both sides of the barricade.

## Washington in depth: the precedent, the corporation and the committee

The external campaign's full shape emerges only when its prehistory is included, because the men who ordered it in 1970 had rehearsed its logic in Chile before. The election of 1964 had been treated in Washington as a Cold War test of the first rank, and the intervention then, conducted through funding of the Christian Democratic campaign on a scale the later investigations described as enormous by the standards of covert electoral action, had established both the machinery and the premise: that a Chilean vote for the left was a hemispheric emergency to be prevented by any instrument short of invasion. The premise survived the decade intact. When the polls of 1970 showed the prevention failing, the instruments escalated through the sequence the Senate record documents, from electoral funding to the two tracks of the inauguration crisis to the long destabilisation. The International Telephone and Telegraph corporation, whose Chilean telephone holdings faced nationalisation, supplied the campaign with a corporate auxiliary: its executives proposed to Washington a programme of economic chaos as an instrument of prevention, offered funds for the effort, and maintained through the period a channel between the company's interests and the agency's plans whose memoranda, leaked and published, became the scandal's most quoted exhibits. The significance of the ITT affair is not the company's wickedness, which is a small subject, but the demonstration it affords of how the campaign's public and private motives interlocked: the state's anti communism and the corporation's balance sheet arrived at the same policy by different roads and thereafter reinforced each other's resolve.

The Church Committee, the Senate select committee whose investigation in the middle seventies dragged the whole record into print, deserves its place in the case's historiography as an actor and not merely a source. Its Chilean volume established the documentary floor beneath every subsequent account: the authorisations, the funding streams, the contacts with plotters, the economic pressure's design, and the careful distinction its evidence supported between destabilisation, which was policy, and the coup's execution, which the committee did not find to have been ordered or run by the United States. The distinction has been attacked from both directions ever since, by those who hold that the committee was deceived or deceiving and by those who hold that destabilisation is a euphemism for a coup conducted by franchise. The article's treatment follows the record's own discipline: what the documents establish is stated as established, what they leave open is stated as open, and the reader is trusted with the difference. It is worth adding that the committee's existence is itself part of the case's meaning. The external power's own legislature investigated and published the campaign against an elected government, and the publication altered the campaign's later conduct and the hemisphere's politics, which is more self correction than the case's other actors managed in the same years, and a fact any balanced account of the external dimension should carry alongside the instructions of September 1970.

## The coup as an operation: the day and the first decrees

The military action of 11 September was executed with a completeness that testified to its preparation. The navy's seizure of Valparaíso in the early hours secured the coast and the congress's seat. The army's regiments occupied the capital's strategic points under a plan whose coordination across the services had been the conspiracy's final achievement, since the services had historically distrusted one another nearly as much as they distrusted the government. The air force's Hawker Hunters, flying from their bases with a precision that suggested rehearsal, struck the palace and the presidential residence. By the afternoon the junta, composed of the four service commanders with the army's Pinochet at its head by seniority of appointment rather than by the conspiracy's original design, was broadcasting its first decrees: the congress dissolved, the parties of the coalition declared illegal and the others in recess, a state of siege proclaimed in the juridical language of a country at war, and the population ordered to its homes under curfew. The decrees' theory, elaborated in the days that followed, was that Chile had been rescued from a Marxist dictatorship in preparation, that the armed forces acted as the nation's reserve of morality, and that the war against the internal enemy would continue until the country's soul was renovated. The national stadium in Santiago became, within days, the emblem of the new order's method, a sports ground converted into a camp where the defeated were processed by category. This article, observing its discipline, leaves the scale of what followed to the commissions it has named. The character of the founding requires no numbers: a regime born in the bombing of the seat of government, governing by decree under a doctrine of internal war, and announcing the extirpation of a political tradition held by a third of its countrymen, had answered the coercive question in the most complete form available, by demonstrating that in the last instance the apparatus was the state, and that every constitution in the case had been a description of the conditions under which the apparatus consented to be bound.

## The counter revolution's economics: what the dictatorship did with its victory

The dictatorship's economic project belongs to the case's record because it reveals, in negative, what the confrontation had been about. The junta's first economic measures were improvised by officers whose doctrine was order rather than economics, but within two years the government had been captured by a team of Chilean economists formed at the University of Chicago under a long standing exchange agreement, whose programme, prepared in opposition and circulated before the coup as a blueprint nicknamed the brick for its weight, proposed the systematic reversal not merely of the Allende years but of the whole developmentalist settlement since the 1930s: prices freed, the social area's enterprises returned or sold, the agrarian reform's beneficiaries stripped of their collective structures, trade opened, the unions crushed as an economic actor and the state's economic role reduced to the guardianship of property and money. The programme was applied through the dictatorship's first decade with a severity its authors described as shock treatment, and its social costs were borne by the same population that had borne the queues, now without the organisations through which it had once answered back. The analytical point for this article is the symmetry of the two transformations' relationship to consent. The democratic road had attempted a structural transformation while asking, at every stage, for the electorate's permission to continue. The counter revolution attempted a structural transformation of comparable depth while abolishing the electorate, and it succeeded in its own terms precisely where the road had failed, in the unimpeded command of the apparatus and the absence of any flank it needed to persuade. The comparison is not an argument that transformation requires dictatorship. It is the case's hardest datum for any democratic transformer: that the depth of change a society will bear is partly a function of the coercion available to the changer, and that a road which renounces coercion must find the equivalent depth in consent, which is slower, shallower per unit of time, and the only currency the road is permitted to mint. The Chilean right learned the lesson in its own idiom and built its later hegemony on the settlement the dictatorship's coercion had made irreversible by the time democracy returned. The left learned it as the price list of its founding defeat.

## The return and the long shadow: the transition's debt to the case

When Chilean democracy returned at the end of the eighties, it returned in the case's shadow and on terms the case had dictated. The plebiscite that ended the dictatorship was won by a coalition whose architecture was the democratic road's corrected draft: the Socialists and Communists' successors allied with the Christian Democrats who had once confirmed Allende and later blessed his overthrow, the programme deliberately bounded, the economic settlement of the dictatorship substantially accepted, the armed forces' prerogatives negotiated rather than abolished, and the whole conducted under a rhetoric of consensus whose every cadence was an answer to 1973. The commissions whose reports this article has cited were the transition's chosen instrument for the past: truth established by mandate, justice pursued within the limits the correlation of forces allowed, and the repression's scale fixed in documents no subsequent polemic could dissolve. The governments of the transition governed well by the standards of stability and growth, and were haunted, in exact proportion to their success, by the accusation from their own tradition's younger generation that the lesson of Chile had been learned too well, that prudence had become the name of a permanent postponement, and that the plurality and the flank had been solved by the simple expedient of never again attempting anything that would require a majority to be brave. The argument is the case's living form inside its own country, and this article takes no side in it beyond noting that both parties to it are quoting the same three years. A case that can arm both the counsel of daring and the counsel of caution, fifty years on, with equal documentary warrant, has not been settled by its historians. It has been inherited, which is a different and more demanding condition, and the inheritance is the reason a series on Marxism gives the Chilean road the longest of its Latin American hearings after the regional survey itself.

## The centre's migration in full: how the Christian Democrats travelled

The Christian Democratic Party's journey from the government's confirmation to its antechamber of the coup is the case's most consequential single process, and it deserves reconstruction at a length its importance commands, because the democratic road's arithmetic always ran through the centre and the centre was never a fixed quantity. The party that confirmed Allende in October 1970 was itself a coalition of temperaments. Its left, formed in the Falange tradition and radicalised by the sixties, had supplied Tomic's programme, which proposed a popular unity of its own kind and regarded the Marxist coalition as a rival for the same historical task rather than as an enemy. Its centre held the institutionalists, for whom the guarantees statute was a genuine covenant and the government's legality a fact to be honoured while it lasted. Its right, gathered around former president Frei's authority and the party's apparatus, regarded the experiment from the first day as a catastrophe to be survived and then reversed. In the government's first year the three temperaments could still share a party, because events had not forced the choice among them: the copper vote united them, the municipal elections warned them, and the government's early courtesies to the centre's sensibilities gave the institutionalists their argument. The migration began when the costs of coexistence rose. Each land seizure in a province where Christian Democratic peasants waited for the legal reform, each factory occupation in a district where the party's unionists competed with the cordones, each speech from the Socialist leadership about the bourgeois state's impending supersession, taxed the institutionalists' defence of the covenant and enriched the right's account of it as a fraud. The party's internal elections became referendums on the government, and the right's candidates won them with increasing ease as the crisis deepened, because the party's electorate was living the crisis in its shops and surgeries and was in no mood to be told that the covenant required its patience.

By the middle of the second year the party's public position had hardened into a doctrine of its own: the government had broken the guarantees in spirit, the transformation was proceeding by administrative usurpation where the congress refused it, and the party's duty was resistance within the constitution until the constitution's own remedies could be applied. The doctrine was sincerely held by many who proclaimed it, and it was also, in its practical effect, the soft road's charter, since it licensed every obstruction the congress and the courts could mount while reserving the party's formal innocence of the hard road's preparations. The negotiations of the final winter exposed the position's inner limit. The party's negotiators, meeting the government under the cardinal's auspices, required as their minimum the surrender of the contested interventions, the disciplining of the cordones and the effective co government of the transformation by the party that had lost the election, terms no government could accept and remain itself. Whether a more generous government offer in an earlier season would have held the centre is the case's most debated counterfactual, and the evidence cuts both ways with unusual evenness. The centre contained men who wanted the covenant to work and said so until late. It also contained a leadership whose deepest conviction, formed in the sixties' rivalry for the same electorate, was that the Marxist left's success would be its own extinction, and who read every concession as the purchase of time for an adversary who would not reciprocate. What can be said without speculation is the sequence. The centre's migration was not a reaction to the coup's approach but one of its preconditions, completed in the party's councils and congresses months before the barracks moved, and the government's camp, which understood the arithmetic perfectly, proved unable to pay the migration's price in the only currencies the centre accepted: the flank's discipline and the transformation's pause. The plurality and the flank, stated in its central application, is exactly this: the majority the road needed was available for purchase, the price was known, and the coalition's composition made the price unpayable by the only government that needed to pay it.

## The economic argument inside the palace: two lines on the crisis

The government's economic failure is often told as a single team's incompetence, and the record shows something more interesting: a sustained argument between two economic lines, each with able exponents, whose unresolved coexistence produced a policy that had the defects of both. The first line, associated with the expansion's architect Pedro Vuskovic and with the coalition's left, held that the transformation's political economy required the redistribution to continue, the social area to grow by absorbing the commanding heights and the contested factories alike, and the resulting monetary disorder to be managed by controls, rationing and the mobilisation of the organised consumers, on the theory that the crisis was the birth pain of a new structure and that retreat would demoralise the base without appeasing the enemy. The second line, held by the Communist economists and by a succession of finance ministers of the pragmatic school, held that the transformation would be decided by supply: that a government which could not feed its own supporters at stable prices would lose the centre, the countryside and finally the barracks' neutrality, and that consolidation, a fixed social area, incentives to the private and peasant sectors, and a negotiated external breathing space were the preconditions of any advance worth the name. Allende arbitrated between the lines as he arbitrated between the parties, by rotation of ministers and by speeches that honoured both, and the economy received the arbitration as incoherence: expansionary budgets under consolidating ministers, price controls announced and selectively enforced, a social area whose boundary expanded by occupation while the ministry published its fixed list. The foreign economists who advised the government, and the Chilean economists who staffed its agencies, have left a literature of unusual candour about the argument, and its lesson generalises beyond the case. A coalition government of transformation contains its economic argument inside itself, between the constituency that experiences the transformation as income and the constituency that experiences it as order, and the argument cannot be abolished by intelligence or goodwill. It can only be decided, by an authority willing to make one line the government's and the other line's holders into its internal opposition. The Chilean government's tragedy in economics is the same as its tragedy in strategy, and for the same reason: the deciding authority existed, in the president's person and prestige, and was spent on postponement, because decision would have broken the coalition that decision was meant to save.

## The south in depth: Cautín, the Mapuche and the reform's sharpest edge

The agrarian reform's most intense theatre lay in the southern provinces, above all in Cautín, where the land question carried a charge older than the republic's class structure. The Mapuche people, whose resistance to the Chilean state's advance had lasted into the late nineteenth century, had been confined to reductions, communal reserves of diminished and fragmented land, from which the surrounding estates, many of them carved from the same territory within living memory, drew labour and legitimacy. The reform law the government inherited addressed the latifundio in the language of size and productivity; it had no category for restitution, for land owed not because an estate was idle but because it had been taken. Into that gap between the law's categories and the territory's memory poured the corridas de cerco, the fence runs, in which Mapuche communities and landless peasants moved the fences of the great estates back toward the boundaries their grandparents remembered, occupying the recovered ground and daring the state to restore the surveyor's line against the historian's. The MIR's rural organisers and the coalition's own peasant federations competed to lead a movement neither had created, and the landowners of the south, organised, armed and connected to the national opposition's hardest currents, answered with a violence that made the province the period's rural cockpit. The government, committed to a reform whose statute could not say the word restitution, found itself expropriating by the law's route the estates the fence runs had already taken in fact, protecting communities it had not authorised against landowners whose property its own reform proposed eventually to abolish, and losing, with each incident, a little more of the centre's belief that the countryside was governed. Cautín matters to the case's analysis because it shows the flank in its most sympathetic and most intractable form. The fence runs were not a faction's adventurism. They were a people's recovery of stolen ground, conducted at the one moment in a century when the state might have been expected to bless it, and a government that suppressed them would have suppressed the most just claim its transformation had encountered. A government that blessed them dissolved its own statute. The democratic road's difficulty is sometimes presented as a failure of nerve or of theory. In Cautín it appears in its true form, as the collision between a legal order whose categories were drawn by the victors of an earlier conquest and a justice that those categories could not pronounce, with the government sworn to the first and elected by the believers in the second.

## The comparative mirror: elected communists elsewhere and the Chilean difference

The Chilean road was not the only attempt by a Marxist movement to govern through elections inside a constitutional order, and the comparisons discipline the case's lessons by showing which of its features were local and which belong to the strategy itself. The Indian state of Kerala, whose communist ministry of the late fifties was dismissed by the central government after two years of land and education reform, offers the closest early parallel: an elected communist government, a reforming programme resisted by the propertied and the churches, a coalition of the displaced appealing over its head to a higher authority, and a removal accomplished, in that instance, by constitutional instrument rather than by tanks. The differences instruct as much as the parallels. The Kerala ministry governed a state within a federation whose centre could dismiss it, and its dismissal, however bitter, occurred inside a democracy that survived the act and later returned the communists to office by the same ballot that had expelled them. The Chilean government governed a unitary state with no higher arbiter, and the authority that removed it abolished the democracy that had elected it. The Finnish and Italian communists of the postwar decades supply the opposite pole of the comparison: parties that accepted the disciplines of coalition and opposition for generations, never held sole power, and were never destroyed, at the price the Chilean flank would have named surrender and the Italian leadership, after Chile, renamed wisdom. San Marino's elected communist government, governing a micro republic for a decade after the war, survived because the stakes its neighbours assigned it were negligible, a reminder that the external pressure in the table is a function of the experiment's perceived importance. Chile sits at the comparison's centre because it combined the maximum of every variable: a sovereign state, a structural programme, a hostile hegemon in its hemisphere, a military with a corporate doctrine to lose, and a coalition that attempted the whole transformation in a single term. Successors who cite the case for any single lesson should be asked which variables of their own situation match Chile's, since the case's authority derives from the conjunction and not from any element alone.

## The social wage: health, housing and the transformation's daily bread

The transformation's claim on the loyalty of the poor rested less on the social area's commanding heights than on a set of provisions that entered daily life directly, and their record belongs in the case's balance sheet. The government inherited and expanded a national health service whose clinics reached into the poblaciones, launched a celebrated programme that delivered a daily ration of milk to every child through the schools and clinics, and treated infant nutrition as the revolution's most measurable promise. Housing starts multiplied on the urban periphery, though never fast enough for the land seizures' generation, and the new settlements, for all their mud and improvisation, carried running water and titles where the campamentos had carried neither. Education expanded at every level, the universities opened their admissions, and the unified school proposal of the final year, intended to dissolve the boundary between the academic and the technical tracks and between the state's schools and the subsidised private ones, became, as earlier sections noted, one of the polarisation's sharpest fronts precisely because it touched the institution through which the middle class reproduced itself. The social wage's politics were double edged in the manner of everything in this case. To its beneficiaries the provisions were the transformation's proof, the reason the government's vote grew under scarcity among those who had least, and the substance of the loyalty that filled the streets in its defence at the end. To the taxpayers of the centre the same provisions appeared as the fiscal deficit's human face, generosity financed by emission and therefore by the inflation that taxed their salaries, and the argument between the two experiences was never joined on common ground because the ground, a shared account of what the state owed and what it could afford, was itself among the casualties. A democratic road lives on the social wage's politics more than on its nationalisations, since the wage is where the uncommitted meet the transformation, and the Chilean record suggests the meeting must be financed honestly or it will be financed by the currency, at an interest rate set by the queue.

## The officer corps as a society: clubs, salaries and the sociology of a coup

The classical objection speaks of the armed forces as an instrument, and the Chilean record supports a more sociological reading that in no way softens the conclusion. The officer corps of 1970 was a society: recruited substantially from the middle strata it would eventually make war on the government in alliance with, educated in academies whose curriculum joined professional technique to a doctrine of the nation and its internal enemies absorbed from the Cold War's hemispheric schools, married into the families of the provincial bourgeoisie, clubbed with its sons, and reading its newspapers. Its corporate grievances were real and were cultivated: salaries eroded by the inflation the government's own policies fed, budgets squeezed, and the humiliation, keenly felt and assiduously reported, of an army asked to guarantee a process whose flank spoke of replacing it with a militia. The government's courtship addressed the institution's head, its commanders and its budget, and neglected, as governments do, the society beneath the rank structure, the majors and captains in the provincial garrisons whose mess talk set the institution's real climate and among whom the conspiracy's organisers, themselves field officers of no great seniority, did their patient work. The navy's purge of its own sailors showed the society policing its lower boundary. The army's tolerance of the tancazo's authors, punished with a leniency that advertised itself, showed it forgiving its own. By the final winter the institution's centre of gravity had moved from the constitutionalist generals at its summit to the conspiratorial colonels at its waist, and Prats's resignation merely registered a transfer of power inside the barracks that preceded the transfer outside it by three weeks. The lesson for the theory of the democratic road is precise and unwelcome. The apparatus is not a switch to be held by appointing its chief. It is a society to be persuaded, divided or overawed, and a government of transformation that cannot do any of the three with the society in uniform has not answered the coercive question, whatever its relations with the society's commanders. Allende's courtesy to generals was genuine and his reading of their institution was the reading of a parliamentarian who believed that form governs substance. September was the institution's reply, delivered in the substance's own language.

## The final fortnight: a chronology of the closing doors

The last two weeks of the government repay a chronological telling, because the sequence shows the pressures arriving not as abstractions but as dates. At the end of August the second military cabinet, the government's final attempt to bind the services to the constitutional calendar, dissolved as the commanders who had joined it withdrew under their institutions' pressure, and the navy announced the discovery of the subversion plot among its sailors that its intelligence had manufactured from the winter's arrests. The chamber's declaration of the government's unconstitutionality, passed days earlier, was carried by a delegation of opposition parliamentarians, in a gesture whose symbolism no one missed, toward the barracks' attention. The truck owners' stoppage entered its second month with the gremios' confederation demanding not redress but resignation. Inside the palace the drafting of the plebiscite appeal proceeded under the president's direction, its announcement fixed for the eleventh of September at a ceremony of the labour confederation, while the Socialist Party's leadership, in a meeting whose minutes the exile later published, resolved against the plebiscite as a surrender and for the preparation of resistance. On the final weekend the government's intelligence reported troop movements it could not interpret with confidence, the president's naval aide de camp, one of the few officers in daily contact with the palace, was already a prisoner of his own service's purge, and the cordones of the industrial belts stood their watches with the arms they had, which were few, against the arms that were coming, which were the state's. On the Monday the president worked at his residence on the plebiscite's text, received the commanders' assurances through the channels that remained, and went to sleep in a republic that had, by the count of its own forms, one more day. The chronology's lesson is the opposite of the coup's later mythology. Nothing about the end was sudden except the shooting. Every door had been watched closing, by men who described the closing accurately to one another, and the government's final tragedy is not that it was surprised but that it had exhausted, one by one and in full view, every instrument its strategy permitted it to use.

## The debate in real time: the world left argues over a living case

Chile was argued about while it lived, in a running international seminar whose record is itself a source for the case's meaning. The Italian party's observers reported home a government whose difficulties confirmed their own evolution toward the historic compromise before the coup supplied the doctrine's founding catastrophe. The French left, negotiating its common programme in the same years, read Chile as the rehearsal of its own possible government, and the argument between its Communist custodians of gradualism and its Socialist advocates of rupture was conducted partly in Chilean vocabulary. The Spanish opposition, conspiring against a dictatorship of its own, studied the Chilean military's behaviour as the negative of its hopes for its own transition. The New Left of the English speaking world divided between those who saw in the cordones the revolution's authentic organ and in the government its brake, and those who saw in the government's survival the only question and in the cordones its epitaph. Even the Soviet bloc's commentary, constrained by Moscow's caution, conducted the argument in code, praising the Chilean Communists' line with an emphasis that indicted the Socialists without naming them. The seminar's existence matters because it falsifies the retrospective illusion that the case's lessons were obvious only after the palace burned. Every position in the later literature was stated, in print, before September 1973, by observers watching the same events, and the positions' disagreement was therefore not about the facts but about the weight of the pressures, which is to say about the table's rows. The reader who masters the contemporary debate acquires the case's true historiographical skill: the ability to see a familiar argument's birth certificate, and to ask of any later account which participant of the seminar it descends from and what that descent obliges it to omit.

## Copper in depth: the metal, the companies and the price

Copper was not one industry among others in the Chilean case. It was the economy's commanding height in the most literal sense, the source of the great bulk of foreign exchange, a principal pillar of the fiscal revenue, and the daily proof, visible in the northern desert's company towns, of the dependence the whole political nation had learned to resent. The two American companies that mined the great deposits, Anaconda at Chuquicamata and Potrerillos, Kennecott at El Teniente, had operated for half a century as states within the state, with their own camps, their own dollar economies and a political weight in Santiago exercised through every channel an oligarchic republic affords. The Christian Democratic government's Chileanisation had bought majority stakes and left management and marketing in the companies' hands, an arrangement whose frustrations, profits remitted under agreements the public could not inspect, prepared the unanimity of 1971 better than any pamphlet. The nationalisation's aftermath supplied the external pressure with its most concrete instruments. The excess profits deduction, by extinguishing the compensation, converted the companies from sellers into litigants and lobbyists: Kennecott pursued Chilean copper cargoes through the courts of Europe, obtaining embargoes that frightened buyers and insurers far beyond the shipments actually seized, while both companies lent their Washington weight to the credit squeeze and to the portrayal of Chile as a confiscatory risk unfit for any lender's book. The copper price completed the siege's material circuit. The metal's world price, high in the government's first year on the back of the previous decade's demand, fell as the world economy slackened, and every cent of the fall was a subtraction from a budget that had committed the metal's earnings several times over to the transformation's promises. The government's economists spoke of the price as weather, and the metaphor was half honest: no Chilean ministry set the London price. The other half belonged to the table's external row, since the market in which the weather formed was one whose credit, insurance and legal systems the government's enemies could work like instruments, and did. Hold the whole circuit in view at once: a nation that owned its mountain at last, and discovered that ownership of the ore was the beginning, not the end, of the question of who commanded the metal's journey from the pit to the price.

## Christians for socialism and the church's own flank

The polarisation ran through the church as it ran through every Chilean institution, and the church's internal division mirrors the case's larger geometry with unusual fidelity. At the hierarchy's summit stood the cardinal's policy of institutional neutrality and mediation, grounded in the conviction that the church's unity across the classes was its treasure and that its identification with any camp would destroy its capacity to shelter the losers of every outcome, a conviction the dictatorship's years would vindicate in the Vicariate's work. Below the summit the currents divided as the country divided. The movement of Christians for Socialism, gathering priests, religious and lay activists radicalised by the poblaciones where many of them lived, declared in the government's second year that the Christian's option for the poor required the socialist option in politics, and carried a current of the church's energy into the transformation's camp, to the hierarchy's visible discomfort. The conservative currents, joined to the gremialista networks of the Catholic University and to the landed parishes of the south, read the same gospel toward the opposite conclusion and supplied the resistance with its theology of order and intermediate bodies. The government, courting the hierarchy and embarrassed by its priestly flank in almost exact proportion as it was courted and embarrassed by its political one, discovered that the church reproduced the plurality problem in sacred form: the institution whose blessing it most needed for the centre's conscience was an institution whose own centre was migrating, whose left wing's enthusiasm frightened the very voters the blessing was meant to reassure, and whose eventual mediation failed for the reasons the mediation section has already given. The parallel is worth a student's attention because it generalises the case's central mechanism. In a polarising society every mediating institution is itself polarised, so that mediation arrives later and weaker than the parties' need for it, and the space in which a negotiated outcome could live is dismantled by the same process that makes negotiation necessary.

## Administering the reform: CORA, the asentamientos and the state's own limits

The agrarian reform's administration is the case's best window onto a variable the polemics neglect, the capacity of the state itself. The reform agency, CORA, inherited from the previous government with its statutes, its surveyors and its legal machinery, was asked to execute in three years a transformation of the countryside that its designers had scheduled for a generation, and to do so while the countryside's own actors, peasants pressing from below and landowners resisting from above, treated the agency's timetable as an opening bid. The asentamiento, the transitional collective into which the expropriated estates were organised, embodied the reform's theory: the estate's workers, now its collective proprietors in formation, would learn management, accumulate capital and emerge, after the transition, as cooperatives or individual proprietors by their own choice. In practice the asentamientos varied as widely as the human material they gathered, from model units whose production and solidarity advertised the reform across the region to estates paralysed by the departure of the patron's credit and knowledge, by internal disputes over work and distribution, and by the state's inability to supply the inputs the patron's network had once commanded. The ministry's extension workers, agronomists and credit officers were too few, the legal processes of expropriation generated files that outran the courts, and the reform's beneficiaries, organised in their own federations, became a political constituency with demands of their own, for titles faster, for credit cheaper, for the neighbouring estate next. The landowners' resistance exploited every administrative failure with professional skill, litigating each expropriation, stripping assets the law had frozen, and presenting each chaotic asentamiento to the centre as the reform's true portrait. The state's limits were not an excuse available to the government in the argument, because the road rested on the premise that the existing state, administered with new purposes, could carry the transformation, and every demonstration that it could not, in the fields as in the factories and the supply system, was a demonstration against the premise by the premise's own test. Capacity is the unglamorous name for the ground on which democratic roads are actually built, and Chile's reformers, who had thought hardest about legitimacy, about majorities and about the apparatus of coercion, discovered in office that the surveyor, the credit officer and the warehouse keeper were also strategic variables, and that theirs were too few.

## Memory under the dictatorship: the regime's story and the resistance's archive

The dictatorship understood itself as a founding regime and conducted a politics of memory as deliberate as its economics. Its story of 1973 was installed in the schools, the ceremonies and the official calendar: the nation rescued at the edge of a Marxist abyss, the armed forces as the reserve of an eternal Chile, the government's three years as a foreign directed chaos whose every document was to be read as conspiracy. The White Book's fabrication was only the founding text of a curation that proceeded by bonfire, by censorship and by the renaming of the country's spaces, and its thoroughness testified to the regime's understanding that the defeated experiment's most dangerous remnant was its memory as a possible future. Against the curation the resistance maintained a counter archive under conditions of clandestinity: the testimonies smuggled to the exile press, the photographs and tapes hidden in the Vicariate's files, the arpilleras, the embroidered scenes of the queues, the arrests and the missing, sewn by the women of the poblaciones and carried abroad as the period's most portable indictment, and the songs, whose performance in a Santiago peña could be an act of war. The two archives confronted each other at the transition, and the commissions were the form the confrontation took when the counter archive acquired a state's mandate: the Rettig process converting testimony into finding, the Valech process extending the conversion to the tortured, and the official story retreating, over the years, from the schools and the ceremonies into the keeping of the regime's unrepentant remnant. The memory war belongs in this article because it is the case's longest front and the one on which the outcome is least complete. The commissions settled the repression's factual floor. They could not settle, and did not pretend to settle, the meaning of the three years that preceded the repression, which remains, in the keeping of the two archives' heirs, the live question with which this article began: whether the Chilean road was a possibility destroyed or an impossibility demonstrated. The article's answer has been given in its analysis and is repeated in its lesson. The memory war's continuation is the evidence that the answer, like every answer the case has received, will have to be rewon by each generation that inherits the question.

## Empanadas and red wine: the experiment's self understanding

The Chilean road had a self image, and the image is evidence. Allende liked to say that Chile's revolution would be made with empanadas and red wine, the phrase promising a socialism in the national idiom, festive rather than funereal, legal rather than barracked, and the phrase travelled the world as the experiment's postcard. Behind the postcard stood a considered claim about the kind of society Chile was: a country of strong parties and stronger habits of legality, of a working class organised for generations, of a middle class attached to its comforts and its rights, in which the transformation would have to be, in the government's own recurring word, pluralist, a socialism with political liberty not as its decoration but as its method and its proof. The claim was aimed at two audiences. To the Chileans of the centre it promised that the road led not to the one party state of the socialist camp but to a deepened democracy in which their liberties were load bearing. To the world left it proposed that the dichotomy of the century, between the ballot that changed nothing and the gun that changed everything at the price of everything, was a false one, and that a third thing existed whose name was the Chilean road. The experiment's enemies understood the claim's power precisely, which is why their propaganda laboured to present every queue as the postcard's refutation and every cordon as the barracks behind the wine. The government's supporters understood it too, and the bitterness of their later reckoning with the flank and with the president's patience was the bitterness of men who had believed the postcard was a strategy. Analysis should be exact about what the self image was. It was not naivety. The men who coined it had survived the century's Chilean chapters and knew the price of illegality in their own biographies. It was a wager that a society's democratic habits could be made stronger than its class fears by a government that fed the habits and starved the fears, and the wager's terms were honestly stated by its makers from the first day. That the fears won is the case's fact. That the wager was available to be made, in a real country with a real electorate, is the case's permanent challenge to the doctrines, of right and left alike, that treat the outcome as having been written before the first vote was counted.

## The intelligence war: blindness, penetration and the uses of ignorance

A government that proposes to transform a society while its enemies conspire requires information, and the Chilean government's information position was among its weakest instruments, a deficiency the case's analysis should record because it interacted with every other pressure. The state's intelligence services belonged, by structure and by loyalty, to the armed forces and the police, institutions whose senior ranks were migrating toward the opposition and whose reporting to the palace was filtered by that migration. The civilian investigative police supplied the government with its most reliable instrument, and its director's fate in the coup's first days, arrested in the palace's defence, marked the instrument's limit. The coalition's parties maintained their own informal networks, in the unions, the ministries and the poblaciones, which reported rumour and atmosphere abundantly and verified fact scarcely, so that the palace lived in a fog of alarming reports among which the genuine conspiracy, proceeding inside the one institution the networks could not penetrate, matured unobserved in its particulars. The opposition's information position was the mirror image: the gremios and the press supplied it with the government's daily condition in granular detail, the external campaign's channels connected it to a professional intelligence service's product, and the conspirators inside the services could read the government's dispositions from within. The asymmetry had operational consequences the narrative has already shown: the government watched the wrong officers in the final weeks, mistook the assurances it received for the institution's mind, and learned of the sailors' purge from the victims rather than from its own apparatus. It also had a subtler consequence for the government's judgement. A palace that cannot distinguish the plot from the rumour must either act on all reports, and govern as a besieged camp, contradicting the road's every premise, or discount them in the name of the road's normality, and be surprised. Allende's government chose the second, as its strategy required, and the choice deserves to be recorded as a structural cost of the democratic road rather than as a personal failure of vigilance: legality disarms its holder's intelligence before it disarms anything else, and the conspirator's first advantage is that he is not required to believe in the normality he exploits.

## The electorate's anatomy: the Chile that voted

Every claim in this article about majorities rests on the structure of the society that cast the votes, and a compact anatomy of that structure will ground the abstractions. Chile at the decade's opening held under ten million people, concentrated overwhelmingly in the central valley's cities, above all in a Santiago that gathered the ministries, the universities, the press and the industrial belts within a single metropolitan society whose classes encountered one another daily. The working class of the Marxist tradition, miners, railwaymen, factory hands, dockers and the great copper camps, was the best organised in Latin America and the coalition's spine, but it was a minority of the labour force, outnumbered by the informal and service workers of the cities, by the public employees of an extensive state, and by the rural population of the central and southern valleys, where the inquilino system of tied labour had only begun to loosen under the unionisation the previous government's laws had permitted. The middle strata, professionals, teachers, shopkeepers, clerks and small proprietors, were numerous, literate, organised in their gremios and associations, and divided in their politics between the centre's reformism and the right's defence of property, with the coalition drawing a real but minority share. The electorate had been enlarged across the century in steps that each altered the system's balance: women's suffrage at mid century, the secret ballot's enforcement and the peasant vote's liberation from the patron's supervision in the reforms of the late fifties, and the lowering of the voting age and the enfranchisement of the illiterate in the reform enacted on the eve of the 1970 contest itself, which admitted to the polls precisely the citizens the coalition's programme addressed most directly. The plurality of 1970 was cast by this enlarged electorate, and the government's subsequent growth in the municipal and congressional contests was growth among the poor of the cities and the countryside, while its losses were concentrated in the strata whose property, salaries and status the crisis taxed. The anatomy explains the case's cruel arithmetic in sociological form. The coalition's road required either the conversion of a large part of the middle strata, whose fears its flank kept feeding, or the mobilisation of the poorest citizens, whose turnout the system had only just begun to register and whose numbers, even fully mobilised, could not by themselves outvote the combined centre and right. The majority the road needed existed as a possibility of persuasion. It never existed as a fact of demography waiting to be counted, and the difference between those two majorities is the difference between the road as a strategy and the road as a hope.

## The desert's memory: Iquique and the labour movement's founding wound

The northern desert supplied the Chilean left with its founding martyrology, and the memory's presence in the minds of the men of 1970 is a historical force the analysis should register. In the first decade of the century the nitrate fields drew a polyglot proletariat into company camps of legendary harshness, paid in tokens redeemable at the company store, and in 1907 the great strike that gathered the fields' workers and their families in the port of Iquique was answered by the army with machine guns in the schoolyard where the strikers had assembled, in a massacre whose scale the sources dispute and whose meaning no Chilean worker's organisation ever disputed. The Santa María school became the labour movement's primal scene, sung in the cantata that Luis Advis composed and Quilapayún carried around the world in the government's own years, and its lesson, as the tradition absorbed it, was double and contradictory in the manner of the whole inheritance. The state that massacred at Iquique was the oligarchic state of the parliamentary republic, whose army was the landlords' instrument without mediation, and the left that grew from the massacre concluded both that the apparatus would kill when the propertied required it and that the republic's forms, extended and democratised, could be turned against the apparatus's masters. Recabarren, who organised in the desert in the massacre's aftermath, held both conclusions in a single strategy, and the coalition of 1970 was his strategy's grandchild. When the flank's young militants invoked Iquique against the president's patience, they were quoting the family's own scripture against its elder, and when the president answered with the ballot's record he was quoting the same scripture's other verse. The coup closed the argument by returning the army to the schoolyard's role in an age of stadiums, and the cantata, banned and sung anyway, became the resistance's portable memorial. A case study that omits the desert's memory will misunderstand the emotional arithmetic of both camps in 1973: the government side's refusal to believe the institution would do again what it had done within the tradition's living transmission, and the institution's own officers, some of whom had sung the cantata's anger in their youth, discovering which verse they had been raised to fulfil.

## Doctrine in the barracks: from Schneider to the national security state

The armed forces' political doctrine had its own history inside the period, and its transformation is the military row of the table stated in the institution's own terms. The doctrine associated with General René Schneider, the commander in chief murdered in the inauguration crisis, held that the army's constitutionalism was not passivity but its defining professionalism: the institution served the constitutional order whoever headed it, and its intervention in politics was the one act that would destroy it as an institution. Schneider's doctrine was not a private opinion. It was the official teaching of a generation of officers formed in the aftermath of the military's last political adventures of the early century, and its authority survived its author's murder long enough to structure the institution's conduct through the government's first two years, during which the army guarded the elections, accepted the military cabinets' logic in its own terms and kept its conspirators at the margin of its counsels. Against it gathered the doctrine that would replace it, the national security doctrine absorbed through the hemisphere's Cold War schools and staff exchanges, in which the enemy was internal and ideological, the nation's survival outranked its constitution, and the armed forces stood as the permanent institution entitled to judge the temporary ones. The two doctrines contested the institution through the period's every crisis, and the contest's resolution was empirical rather than argumentative: each month of disorder enlarged the security doctrine's constituency among the field officers who would have to execute either doctrine, while the constitutionalists' seniority system promoted, by the ordinary calendar, men whose formation the security doctrine had captured. Prats, Schneider's heir and the doctrine's embodiment, discovered in the final winter that he commanded the institution's summit and not its assent. His successor's name is the doctrine's victory certificate. The theoretical point for the series is the one the state section approached from political science and the barracks confirm from within. Constitutions restrain institutions only while the institutions' own doctrine tells them to be restrained, and a transformation that threatens an armed institution's corporate world must reckon with the doctrine inside the institution as a material force, capable of being out argued only by officers, in uniform, whose authority the institution itself recognises. The democratic road had such officers. It lost them, one by assassination, one by resignation, to the sequence this article has traced, and no civilian eloquence could replace them in the only forum where their argument counted.

## The theory after: Carrillo, Poulantzas and the state question's second life

The case's longest intellectual afterlife ran through the theory of the capitalist state, where Chile became the experiment the theorists had not known they were waiting for. Santiago Carrillo, leading the Spanish party out of clandestinity as its dictatorship ended, built his party's new doctrine in explicit Chilean light: his book on Eurocommunism and the state argued that the democratic road required not merely an electoral majority but the transformation of the state's apparatuses from within, the winning of the administration, the judiciary's culture and the armed forces' allegiance by a hegemony established before and beyond the conquest of government, and he drew the Chilean conclusion with a Spaniard's urgency, since his own country held an unreformed Francoist apparatus in reserve. Poulantzas, whose exchange with Miliband this article has already used, spent his final work on the problem Chile had posed his theory: a state that was neither an instrument to be seized nor a subject to be persuaded but a condensation of forces, whose internal fractures the left must work as a terrain, and his late argument for a democratic socialism that would transform the state by combining representative institutions with the direct democracy of the movements reads, page by page, as the Chilean file reopened by a theorist who had watched the cordones and the ministries and refused to choose between them. The posthumous timing of his synthesis gave the case a melancholy distinction: the theory adequate to the experiment arrived, in its fullest statement, only after the experiment and its theorist were both gone. The British and American debates absorbed the case more slowly, through the state derivation controversies and the strategic literature of the eighties, in which Chile figured as the standing refutation of any transition theory that omitted the coercive apparatus from its model. The series' own position can be stated briefly. The case did not refute the democratic road, because a single experiment cannot refute a strategy whose conditions it did not fulfil, the majority above all. It refuted, definitively and at terrible cost, the belief that the road's conditions would assemble themselves in office, under pressure, out of the materials of a plurality and a programme. What Carrillo and Poulantzas each supplied, from their different positions, was the missing category: preparation, the long construction of the majority and of the state's transformation as a single work undertaken before the palace is won. Successors are free to find the category impractical. The case entitles them to no cheaper conclusion.

## Pluralism abroad: the government's foreign policy and its doctrine of respect

The government's foreign policy deserves a section because it applied the road's philosophy internationally and met, in the chancelleries, the same geometry it met at home. Allende's Chile practised an ideological pluralism in its external relations that scandalised both blocs' custodians: it restored and maintained relations with Cuba and extended recognition across the socialist world, and it simultaneously cultivated correct, even warm, relations with the military governments of its own region, receiving their emissaries, settling border business with Argentina's generals and Peru's revolutionary junta by negotiation, and proclaiming that Chile respected every people's chosen path as it asked respect for its own. The doctrine was the domestic wager translated: that forms and courtesies, honoured universally, could build a shelter in which a small country's experiment might live, and that the alternative, enrolment in one camp's discipline, would purchase protection at the price of the experiment's distinctiveness and its domestic centre alike. The policy won the government genuine assets: a near universal recognition of its legitimacy in the hemisphere's forums, the solidarity of governments as different as Mexico's and Sweden's, and the standing to address the United Nations as a plaintiff of the whole developing world rather than as a faction's client. It could not win the one asset the crisis required, a patron whose deterrent weight matched the adversary's, because the doctrine's premise was that the experiment would have no patron, and the socialist camp, respecting the premise from its own calculations, supplied sympathy and credits at a scale the siege's arithmetic rendered symbolic. The foreign policy's record thus closes the case's system of wagers with a perfect consistency. At home the road bet on legality against the apparatus, on persuasion against the centre's fear, on organisation against scarcity. Abroad it bet on universal respect against the hegemon's hostility. Each bet was coherent, each was honourably placed, and the house, in every room, was owned by the other side. A strategy is not refuted by the coherence of its defeats, but a strategy that requires every one of its wagers to win simultaneously has stated, in the language of probability, the concession this article has already made in the language of politics.

## Concepción and Lota: the industrial south and the coal miners' republic

The case's geography is usually told from Santiago, and a section from the industrial south corrects the capital's monopoly of the narrative. Concepción, the country's third city, anchored a regional economy of steel at Huachipato, textiles, glass and the coal field of Lota and Coronel, where the miners descended daily into seams that ran beneath the sea in workings of legendary danger and antiquity. The coal miners were the Chilean labour movement's aristocracy of suffering, organised since the century's early decades, struck repeatedly and repressed memorably, and their company towns, with the paternalism of the mining house on one side and the union hall on the other, formed a society in which the coalition's parties, and increasingly the MIR with its strength in the region's university, contested an allegiance the miners' own traditions made discriminating. The university of Concepción was the MIR's birthplace and remained its intellectual fortress, so that the region held, in concentrated form, the whole coalition and flank geometry: a militant student movement, a veteran labour movement, a reforming regional administration and an industrial bourgeoisie whose plants entered the social area or the cordones' orbit one by one. When the final crisis came, Concepción's cordones and its garrison faced each other across a city whose military commander would declare, on the coup's morning, a local war of unusual severity, and the region's resistance, in the shipyards, the university and the coal towns, was among the most stubborn in the country and paid accordingly. The region's lesson for the case is about the unevenness of the transformation's social base. The government's Chile was not one country but several, a mining north of old loyalties, a capital of every class in friction, an industrial south where the flank was strong and the unions were stronger, and an agrarian south where the land war ran hottest, and its strategy, framed in the capital's categories, engaged each of these countries with the same instruments and the same timetable. The pressures table is a national instrument, and its rows weighed differently in Lota than in Las Condes, which is why the case resists every capital centred telling, including, in its better moments, this article's own.

## The women's war: Poder Femenino, the mothers' centres and the politicisation of the household

No Chilean institution was transformed by the period more thoroughly, and more ambiguously, than the political role of women, and the case's account is incomplete without the two mobilisations that fought over the household. On the opposition's side, the organisation known as Poder Femenino, Feminine Power, gathered the women of the middle and upper classes into the resistance's most theatrical instrument: the empty pots marches, the cacerolazos that drummed the government's evenings from the balconies of the eastern districts, and the delegations that carried the housewives' indictment to the barracks' gates, demanding that the soldiers act as the nation's sons. The organisation's genius was to weaponise the very domesticity its class's ideology assigned to women, converting the kitchen's scarcity into the coup's moral brief and the mother's authority into a summons no general could comfortably refuse. On the government's side the mobilisation ran through the mothers' centres, the neighbourhood organisations the state had fostered since the previous decade, through the JAP's distribution committees whose activists were largely the poblaciones' women, and through the coalition's own women's departments, and its content was the mirror image: the household as the transformation's frontier, scarcity as the siege's work, and the organised mother as the republic's defender. Between the two mobilisations stood the period's small feminist beginnings, women of the left beginning to argue that both camps conscripted women as mothers and neither addressed them as citizens with claims of their own, an argument the dictatorship's years would carry into exile and return with the democracy movement, whose women's protests became the restored politics' conscience. The women's war matters analytically because it shows the polarisation reaching the one constituency both strategies had assumed to be pre political. Once the household was a battlefield, no space remained from which the conflict could be viewed as a spectator, and the centre's last shelter, the private life into which the uncommitted might withdraw, was abolished by both sides' recruiters. A democratic road depends on the uncommitted having somewhere to stand while they decide. Chile's tragedy includes the thoroughness with which everyone, the government included, ensured that they did not.

## The historians' Chile: the scholarly verdicts and their disagreements

The scholarly literature on the case is large enough to have its own history, and a survey of its main verdicts will equip the reader to place any new account, this one included. The first systematic school, writing in the coup's aftermath, treated the breakdown as the Chilean political system's failure: Juan Linz's framework of democratic breakdown, applied to Chile by Arturo Valenzuela in the period's most influential monograph, located the cause in the centre's collapse, the polarisation of the party system and the presidential constitution's rigidity, an account in which the actors' ideologies mattered less than the incentives the institutions supplied. A second school, rooted in the political economy of dependence, reversed the emphasis: the breakdown was the predictable defence of a dependent capitalism against a government that threatened its place in the international order, and the domestic actors were the executors of a structural sentence. A third, the historians' school that matured with the archives, resisted both determinisms and rebuilt the case as a sequence of choices, its leading practitioners distributing responsibility among the government's maximalism, the opposition's disloyalty, the flank's provocations and Washington's campaign in proportions that remain the profession's live argument. The memoir literature overlays all three, with the participants' accounts, from the exiled ministers to the coup's own apologists, supplying evidence and advocacy in the same binding. The reader will notice that this article has declined to enrol in any single school. Its table is a device for holding the schools' variables together: the institutional account supplies the constitutional row, the dependence account the external and the social property rows, the historians' account the decisions that moved the weights among them. Where the schools disagree about Chile they usually disagree about the world, about whether structures or choices govern political life, and the case is unlikely to settle that argument, since each school can find in three years of Chilean evidence a complete confirmation of its premises. The mature use of the literature is therefore comparative in a second sense: read the case through two schools at once, and treat the friction between the readings as the closest approach to the event's own complexity that print allows.

## The universities: reform, gremialismo and the generation of the cordones

The universities supplied both camps with cadres and with doctrines, and their internal wars were the period's in miniature. The reform movement that swept the Chilean universities in the late sixties had democratised their governance, opened their admissions and turned their campuses into the left's recruiting ground and debating chamber, so that by the government's years the University of Chile's student federation spoke in national politics with a voice the parties courted. The Catholic University, where the gremialista movement had been born in reaction against the reform's politicisation, supplied the counter doctrine and its cadres to the resistance, and the contest between the two universities' conceptions, the university as the nation's critical conscience against the university as an autonomous corporation above the parties, was the gremial argument conducted at the level of first principles. The technical university and the regional universities, Concepción above all, added their own weights to the scales. Students manned the cordones' guard posts, the JAP's distribution rounds, the opposition's marches and Patria y Libertad's cells, and the generation formed in those years carried the period into every Chilean institution for the rest of the century, as ministers and exiles, as torturers and as the tortured, a fact the transition's personnel lists confirm on every page. For the case's analysis the universities' role closes a loop the article has traced through the church, the barracks and the household. No institution in the society stood outside the polarisation with its authority intact, because every institution's younger generation had been formed inside the argument and imported it, staffed and armed, into the institution's councils. The mediating centre failed, in the last analysis, not because Chile lacked institutions of mediation but because each of them had been colonised in advance by the conflict it was asked to mediate, and the colonisation was the work of the most educated and committed citizens of both camps, acting on the sincere conviction that neutrality, in such a hour, was a form of surrender. They were not wrong about the hour. That is the tragedy, and the article's final word on the mechanism belongs to it.

## A long thin country: geography, the highway and the strike's logistics

Chile's geography is a strategic fact that the case's analysis should state plainly, because it determined the mechanics of the decisive weapon, the transport stoppage. The country is a ribbon between the Andes and the Pacific, thousands of kilometres long and seldom two hundred wide, with its population, its industry and its agriculture strung along a single central valley and a single highway spine, its northern mines and its southern farms at the ends of supply lines that had no alternative routes. The railway, once the spine's rival, had decayed under decades of road competition, and the coastal shipping that might have bypassed the roads was limited by the ports' capacity and the merchant fleet's size. Into this geography the truck owners' confederation held a weapon no union of factory workers could match: the power to sever the country's circulation by parking. A factory occupation seizes a node. A truck stoppage seizes the network, and in a country built as a line, the network is everything: the mines cannot eat, the farms cannot sell, the cities cannot be provisioned, and the government faces, within days, a crisis of physical distribution that no decree can answer, since decrees do not move freight. The government's counter measures, the volunteer drivers, the requisitioned vehicles, the military's transport units, the improvised coastal convoys, were heroic improvisations against a structural fact, and their partial success taught the opposition the weapon's correct dosage, to be applied in the final winter at the moment of the barracks' ripeness rather than wasted on rehearsal. The analytical moral extends beyond Chile. A transformation's vulnerability is partly written in its country's material shape, in the concentration of its infrastructure, the number of its chokepoints and the organisation of the men who staff them, and a strategy that reads only the society's opinions and not its logistics will be surprised by the speed at which a minority with the right chokepoints can convert itself into the arbiter of the whole. The cordones were the government's answer in the factories. It never found the answer on the roads, because the roads belonged, by ownership, by organisation and by the gremial philosophy that armed both, to the other side.

## The statute read closely: what the guarantees actually promised

The Statute of Democratic Guarantees repays a close reading, because the final crisis's legal argument turned on its text. The statute, enacted as a constitutional reform before the inauguration, did not merely reassure. It entrenched, in the constitution's own language, the pluralism of the party system, the independence of the universities and the armed forces' non political character, the freedom of the press with guarantees against its administrative strangulation, and the right of all citizens to organise, to travel and to teach. Its authors on the Christian Democratic side understood it as the price of the transfer and as a standing charter the coalition's electorate could invoke. Its signatories on the coalition's side understood it as a description of the road itself, a socialism that intended to keep every liberty the statute named. The tragedy of the text is that both understandings were sincere and both were, in the event, unusable. The government invoked the statute's pluralism to defend its tolerance of the flank's excesses and the opposition's press. The opposition invoked the statute's guarantees to indict the government for the same tolerance, as a breach of the charter's spirit by the disorder it licensed. A constitutional text that both camps could quote against the other had ceased to arbitrate between them and had become another weapon in the armoury, which is the fate of guarantees in a polarisation: their meaning is supplied by the correlation of forces that interprets them, and when the correlation moved, the statute's articles moved with it, until the chamber's declaration could present the government's overthrow as the statute's own enforcement. The lesson is not that guarantees are worthless. It is that a guarantee is a promise whose keeper is the future balance of the society that made it, and that a democratic road which treats its founding charter as a substitute for that balance, rather than as one instrument among others for building it, has mistaken the map's legend for the road.

## The paper war: El Mercurio, the Papelera and the economics of opinion

The media battle's material base deserves a section, because the fight over newsprint was the fight over the press conducted in the language of property, where the case's central argument could be staged in a single industry. El Mercurio, the Edwards family's paper, was the Chilean establishment's organ in the fullest sense: the oldest Spanish language newspaper in continuous publication, the record in which the republic's propertied classes read themselves each morning, and an institution whose editorial line through the period was the soft road's daily general staff work, framing each event within the narrative of a government illegal in spirit and ruinous in practice. The paper's financing under the credit squeeze became one of the external campaign's documented threads, the Senate investigations recording the covert funds that reached the paper at the moment its advertising and credit collapsed, a fact its defenders answered by noting that governments of the left have rarely needed subsidies explained to them when the recipient is friendly. The government's own press position was the weaker for its scruples and its poverty alike: it disposed of the state channel in television, of party papers with small circulations and large debts, and of a publishing house whose books reached the converted. Its one attempt to alter the material balance, the purchase through the development corporation of a controlling interest in the great paper mill, the Papelera, whose newsprint supplied every paper in the country, detonated the period's purest controversy. The opposition presented the purchase as the strangulation of the free press by the control of its raw material, the guarantees statute's letter made flesh as a warning. The government answered that the mill was a monopoly entering the social area by the programme's ordinary logic, and that the statute's press guarantees bound its conduct whatever it owned. Both arguments were available in good faith, and the country heard only the first, because the first was printed, daily, on the mill's own product in the establishment's paper. The government let the purchase drop, and the episode entered the case's ledger as the media row of the table in miniature: a government that would not seize the means of opinion, an opposition that would not distinguish purchase from seizure, and a public sphere whose commanding height remained, to the last morning, in the hands of the transformation's enemies, reporting the siege as the government's weather.

## The forty measures: the programme's first page and the politics of the immediate

The coalition's programme opened with a list of immediate measures, the famous first measures whose enumeration in the programme's opening pages was itself a political act, and their fate condenses the government's whole trajectory into a single document's biography. The measures were deliberately concrete and deliberately popular: the milk ration for every child, the suppression of the tax on the poor's staples, the end of the tied cottage's humiliations in the countryside, school places and scholarships, the freezing of the rents of the poor, pensions raised, the bureaucrats' privileges trimmed, a constituent process promised for the longer term. They were designed to be deliverable by decree or by early law, to be felt in the household before the structural battles began, and to build, out of gratitude and organisation, the majority the election had not supplied. In the event the measures were substantially delivered, and therein lies their analytical interest, because their delivery did not build the majority at the rate the strategy required. Gratitude, the government discovered, is a wasting asset in politics, spent by the inflation that the same government's expansion generated, while organisation, the durable form of the majority, grew in the cordones and the JAP at a pace the flank's critics and the government's own ministers read in opposite senses. The measures also taught the opposition its most effective lesson: that the government's promises could be turned into the standard of its indictment, every undelivered litre and unbuilt house a count against it in the press it did not control. The programme's first page thus contains the case in embryo. A government that promises the immediate must deliver it continuously, at prices its enemies help to set, through an apparatus it does not command, to an electorate whose gratitude depreciates faster than its organisation appreciates. The arithmetic is not an argument against promising. It is the democratic road's true budget constraint, stated in the only currency, time, of which the Chilean government was given three years and the strategy required a generation.

## Santiago as a world capital: the trade conference and the experiment's showcase season

In the southern autumn of 1972 Santiago hosted the third United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, and the episode deserves its place as the experiment's showcase season, the moment when the government's wager on universal respect achieved its most visible form. The conference brought the developing world's delegations to a capital governed by Marxists, in a building raised for the occasion that became, in the city's memory, the period's architectural emblem, and the Chilean hosts conducted the proceedings with a correctness that impressed even the delegations instructed to deplore them. Allende's addresses to the conference stated the dependency argument in the language of trade law: the terms on which the poor countries sold and borrowed were the polite form of the relation his government was accused of inventing, and the hall's applause measured the argument's constituency. The showcase cut both ways, as spotlights do. The same season that displayed the experiment's respectability displayed its scarcities to the visiting press, and the conference's delegates departed with both images in their luggage. The building survived the coup as the armed forces' cultural centre and the dictatorship's own showcase, a palimpsest in glass and concrete of the century's Chilean argument about who the modern nation belonged to. The episode's weight in the case is modest and genuine: it marks the high point of the foreign policy's doctrine, the season in which the experiment could still present itself to the world as a government among governments, and it dates, by contrast, the speed of the descent, sixteen months from the conference's applause to the palace's bombing, in a country whose forms had seemed, to its distinguished visitors, so durably in place.

## The August offer: the last negotiation inside the camp

In the government's final month the Communist Party made its most consequential intervention, a public call for a dialogue of consolidation that reached beyond the coalition toward every democratic force willing to defend the constitutional order, and the episode deserves record because it was the strategy's logic stated at the moment of its impossibility. The call proposed, in substance, the settlement the Communists had advocated since the crisis began: the social area's boundary fixed and published, the contested interventions reviewed, the supply emergency administered jointly with the centre's representatives, in exchange for the centre's public commitment against the coup that everyone in Santiago could by then hear being assembled. The party's leaders carried the proposal into their last meetings with the president and into print, and its reception measured the camp's condition exactly. The Socialist leadership read the offer as the liquidation of the transformation at the moment of its danger and answered with the language of advance. The centre's leadership, whose own migration this article has traced, read it as an admission that arrived after its price had risen past payment. The president, who agreed with the offer's substance and had spent three years declining to impose it, received it as he received everything in those weeks, with courtesy and without the instrument that imposition required. The August offer matters because it refutes the simplest version of the flank's indictment, in which a monolithic government sleepwalked into the coup. The government's most disciplined component saw the destination clearly, named the price of survival correctly, and could not pay it either, because the price was denominated in the authority over allies that no component of the coalition possessed. The plurality and the flank, in its final application, is a statement about authority as much as about numbers: a coalition in which no one can decide is a coalition in which events decide, and in August of 1973 the events were already wearing uniforms.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This case teaches best when the class is made to inhabit the constraint before it is allowed the verdict. Open with the unanimous copper vote and ask what it does to the assumption that the confrontation was about expropriation; most groups discover within minutes that their inherited account cannot absorb the fact, and the seminar has begun. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Chilean road to socialism?

The Chilean road to socialism named the strategy of the coalition government that took office in 1970 under Salvador Allende: to transform property relations, nationalising copper, banking and the monopoly sector and completing the agrarian reform, entirely through constitutional and legal means, respecting the congress, the courts and the electoral calendar. Its distinct claim was that socialism could be reached by winning and using the institutions of a democratic republic rather than by overthrowing them, and its fate made it the principal test case in the long argument between reform and revolution.

### Q: Did Allende win a majority?

No. In the presidential election of September 1970 Salvador Allende finished first with a plurality rather than a majority, ahead of the conservative Jorge Alessandri and the Christian Democrat Radomiro Tomic without reaching half the votes cast. Under the constitution the choice then fell to congress, which confirmed him once he had accepted the democratic guarantees statute. The distinction governed his whole government: he held the presidency legitimately while never commanding a majority in the legislature his programme had to pass through.

### Q: What did the Popular Unity government nationalise?

The government nationalised the great copper mines by constitutional amendment, brought most of the banking system under state ownership by purchasing its shares, and assembled a social property area from a scheduled list of monopoly enterprises across industry, textiles, distribution and other sectors, using purchase, intervention and requisition where congress would not legislate. It also accelerated the agrarian reform under the existing statute, expropriating the large estates above the legal ceiling and effectively ending the traditional latifundio in the Chilean countryside.

### Q: Was the copper nationalisation supported across parties?

Yes, completely. The constitutional amendment nationalising the large copper mines was approved by the Chilean congress in July 1971 without a dissenting vote, supported by the government's coalition, by the Christian Democratic centre and by the right's National Party. Copper nationalism rested on decades of criticism of the American companies Anaconda and Kennecott, and the Christian Democratic administration that preceded Allende had already begun buying into the mines. The dispute that followed concerned the compensation formula and its deduction for excess profits, not the nationalisation itself.

### Q: What caused the Chilean economic crisis?

Several causes compounded. The government's first year expansion raised wages and controlled prices while spending reserves and running down inventories, so that when capacity limits were reached the policy produced shortages and monetary inflation. Price controls drove goods into parallel markets, the fiscal deficit was financed by emission, private investment collapsed amid expropriation uncertainty, the copper price fell, and foreign credit contracted sharply under United States pressure, starving the economy of imports and spare parts. Each cause worsened the others, and the resulting siege economy dissolved the government's reputation for competence among the middle strata.

### Q: What is documented about United States involvement?

The declassified record establishes that the United States government resolved in September 1970 to prevent Allende's inauguration or unseat him, that its intelligence services funded opposition parties and media including the newspaper El Mercurio, that it pressed international and private lenders to cut Chile's credit, and that it maintained contact with military plotters, including circles connected to the assassination of the constitutionalist commander General Schneider. A Senate investigation published these findings in the 1970s and later declassifications enlarged them. The record establishes destabilisation as policy; it does not show the coup itself being ordered from Washington.

### Q: What divided the left during the Allende period?

The left divided over the strategy itself. The Communist Party and the president insisted on strict legality, gradual advance and consolidation of a majority, including agreements with the Christian Democratic centre. A powerful current in the Socialist Party, the president's own, pressed for faster advance and resisted compromise. Outside the coalition, the Revolutionary Left Movement, the MIR, held the legal road to be an illusion, encouraged seizures of farms and factories beyond the government's programme and promoted the cordones as embryos of popular power. The government thus faced enemies in front and an undisciplined flank behind.

### Q: Could the Chilean road have succeeded?

The honest answer is that the road was narrow rather than imaginary. The unanimous copper vote and the growth of the government's vote in two elections show that consent could be consolidated, and a corrected economic course, a disciplined coalition and a preserved centre might have carried the government through its term. Against that stand the hostile congress, the financed and determined opposition, the documented external siege and, above all, the unanswered question of the armed forces. Success would have required several difficult conditions to hold together, and by the final winter they had ceased to hold.

### Q: How did Allende die?

Salvador Allende died on 11 September 1973 inside La Moneda palace, which the air force had bombed and ground forces were assaulting, having refused the junta's offer of exile and spoken his farewell to the country by radio. The junta announced suicide, and its opponents alleged murder for decades. The forensic review of 2011, conducted with specialists from abroad and accepted by the courts and the family alike, concluded that he had died by his own hand as the assault closed in.

### Q: What lesson did the left draw from Chile?

Different lefts drew different lessons, and the argument continues. Enrico Berlinguer, the Italian Communist leader, drew the conclusion that governing from the left in a polarised country required the broadest democratic consensus, broader than a bare majority, and built the historic compromise doctrine on the Chilean example. Others concluded that no transition survives without the means to defend it against the old order's apparatus. The Chilean left itself, after exile and dictatorship, largely concluded that its own failures of economic management, coalition discipline and alliance building were the causes within its power to correct, and rebuilt its strategy around majority building and democratic consent.

### Q: How does the plurality and the flank grade the government's room to act?

The phrase names the two constraints that graded every decision the government took. The plurality fixed its constitutional room: a minority in congress, dependent on negotiation or contested decree powers, unable to rewrite the rules it governed under. The flank fixed its political room: every seizure or maximalist speech by its own side narrowed the centre's tolerance on which majority building depended. Room to act is the product of the two, and in Chile the product shrank monthly, because widening the constitutional room required reassuring the centre while the flank was busy frightening it.

### Q: What should a student separate between winning an election and holding state power?

A student should separate the government from the state. Winning the election transferred the presidency, the ministries and the initiative to Popular Unity. It did not transfer the congress, where the opposition held the majority, the judiciary and the comptroller's office, which reviewed the government's decrees, the press, which campaigned against it, or the armed forces, whose officer corps retained its own doctrine and social ties. Holding state power would have meant commanding or neutralising those institutions. The Chilean case is the standard demonstration that the two achievements are distinct, and that a strategy which confuses them will be educated by events.

### Q: Why did the copper vote matter to the democratic road's plausibility?

Because it was the road's strongest single piece of evidence. A congress where the opposition commanded the majority voted unanimously to expropriate the country's commanding industry from its foreign owners, which showed that the inherited institutions were not a single instrument of the possessing class and that a legal transformation could, on the right question, assemble a national consensus behind it. The vote also located the conflict precisely: since expropriation as such commanded unanimity, the confrontation that eventually destroyed the government must have concerned the transformation's speed, boundaries and control, which is where analysis should look.

### Q: How does the three pressures table order the explanations of the fall?

The table refuses to rank the explanations as rivals and instead orders them by how far each lay within the government's power to affect. The economic sequence and the internal division were pressures the government could partly mitigate, at prices it judged too high: stabilisation against its promises, discipline against its base. The external action was not within its gift at all; it could only be denied occasions. The table then records the interactions, because each pressure amplified the others: crisis fed destabilisation, division prevented the answer to crisis, and destabilisation made every error costlier. Ordering by interaction, not by blame, is the table's method.

### Q: What separates the classical objection to the democratic road from a verdict on this government alone?

The classical objection is a claim about all democratic roads: that a government which leaves the old order's army, courts and press intact while expropriating its owners will, in the end, be removed by them, whoever leads it and however well it governs. A verdict on this government alone is a claim about Chilean particulars: the economic errors, the coalition's indiscipline, the missed negotiations. The separation matters because the evidence bears differently on each. Chilean particulars can be corrected by a successor; the classical objection, if sound, cannot. The case supports the narrower verdict comfortably and the wider one only in part, since the road demonstrably functioned for three years before the apparatus moved.
