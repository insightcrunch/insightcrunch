The Frankfurt School is the name given, mostly after the fact, to the circle of social theorists who worked at one address: the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt, founded in 1923 and rebuilt there after a long exile. Almost everything commonly said about it compresses that circle into a mood. Four surnames are recited, a gloom about mass culture is assumed, and the school is filed away as philosophy that lost interest in evidence and in politics alike. This guide works against the compression by returning to the institution itself: its founding money, its first director, its change of leadership, its journal, its surveys and questionnaires, its quarrels, and its members, who held their posts in a building rather than in a temperament.

Two documented facts do most of the correcting, and they organise everything that follows. The first is the empirical correction, the claim this guide names and defends: the institute ran one of the most substantial programmes of empirical social research in its period, including a large collaborative study of authority and the family and a series of American studies on prejudice that produced a personality scale used across postwar social psychology. The charge that the school abandoned evidence for cultural speculation describes a selection of its philosophical books, not the school. The second fact is internal disagreement. The members argued with one another in print and in person about the character of the Nazi state, about Freud, about literature, about whether enlightenment reason could survive its own history. A tradition held together by a building, a budget, and a journal, and divided on nearly every substantive question it touched, is badly described as a unified project.

## What the Frankfurt School was

The canonical definition is institutional before it is doctrinal. The Frankfurt School is the tradition of social research and philosophy developed at the Institute for Social Research, founded in Frankfurt in 1923, which from 1930 pursued an interdisciplinary programme combining Marxist social theory, psychoanalysis, and empirical investigation, and which named its approach critical theory. Every element of that sentence carries weight. The tradition belongs to an institute, with a director, an endowment, a staff, and a publication programme, which is why its history can be told through appointments and budgets as reliably as through books. The programme dates from 1930 rather than 1923, because the founding director's institute was a different enterprise, devoted to the documentary history of the labour movement. The combination of Marxist theory, psychoanalysis, and empirical method was announced as a deliberate method, an attempt to repair what its director saw as the separation of philosophy from the special sciences. And the name critical theory, coined in an essay of 1937, marks the moment the school described its own procedure and distinguished it from the theory it opposed.

The word school misleads in one respect and helps in another. It misleads if it suggests disciples gathered around a master's doctrine, because no doctrine survived contact with the membership intact: the economist and the political scientist on the same staff produced incompatible accounts of the regime that had expelled them both, and the philosopher of music quarrelled for years with the associate whose essay on mechanical reproduction he was simultaneously editing for the institute's journal. It helps if it suggests a training ground and a shared set of problems, because that is what the institute provided. Younger scholars were hired, funded, published, and argued with; a second generation formed after the return to Frankfurt; and the problems recurred across generations even when the answers changed: how domination secures consent, why the oppressed so often defend the order that oppresses them, and what happens to reason when it is organised as an instrument.

Membership, accordingly, is a factual question with factual answers, and this guide treats it as one. Some of the names attached to the school in popular accounts were never members at all. Walter Benjamin, whose work the institute funded and published, remained an outside contributor living in Paris, dependent on a stipend he constantly feared losing. Others central to the record are missing from popular accounts entirely: the economist whose thesis about state capitalism organised the school's political pessimism, and the political scientist whose study of the Nazi state contradicted that thesis from inside the same institute. The members and contributions table later in this guide sets out the roster in full, with each figure's discipline, principal contribution, documented disagreement with colleagues, and whether the association endured. The school's parent current, the wider formation of heterodox Marxist theory between the wars, is mapped in this series in the [western marxism complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/), and the Frankfurt circle belongs inside that map as its most institutionally durable member.

What the school produced, across five decades, resists summary by genre because the genres rotated with the circumstances. The Frankfurt years produced a journal, an archive, and the first version of the interdisciplinary programme. The exile years produced the studies on authority and prejudice, the great philosophical reckoning with enlightenment, and the analyses of the Nazi state written for American readers and American agencies. The years after the return produced group research on the moral afterlife of the dictatorship, public interventions in a restored republic, and the training of a second generation that would carry the name into universities on several continents. A reader who knows only the middle period's philosophy knows the school the way a reader of one act knows a play.

## An institute before it was a school

The institute began as an act of patronage with an academic design. Felix Weil, the son of a wealthy grain merchant whose firm traded from Argentina, had taken a doctorate on problems of socialisation and spent his inheritance's income on Marxist scholarship with unusual seriousness. In 1922 he financed a gathering of Marxist intellectuals in Ilmenau in Thuringia, remembered as the first Marxist work week, where the manuscripts that would become landmarks of the heterodox tradition, Lukács's essays on reification and Korsch's on Marxism and philosophy among them, were read and argued over by men who would recur throughout this guide's story. Weil's conclusion from that week was institutional: Marxist theory needed what theology and medicine had, a permanent home with salaries, a library, and an archive, protected from both the market and the party apparatus. He negotiated with the education ministry and the University of Frankfurt, supplied the capital through a foundation, and in 1923 the Institute for Social Research opened as an institute formally attached to the university and materially independent of it.

### Who paid for the institute, and on what terms?

Felix Weil paid, drawing on a family fortune built in the Argentine grain trade, and he paid through a foundation, so the money arrived as an endowment rather than a subscription. The terms bought independence: the institute answered to its university academically, while its salaries, building, and library were financed privately, beyond the reach of any ministry or party.

The first director set the institute's character, and he was not a philosopher of culture. Carl Grünberg was a Viennese economic historian who held a chair at Frankfurt and edited the leading scholarly journal of labour history, an archive of documents and studies on socialism and the workers' movement that had been appearing since before the First World War. His inaugural programme for the institute was correspondingly archival and empirical in the documentary sense: collect the sources, edit the records, write the history of the labour movement and of political economy from the documents up. The early staff matched the programme. Henryk Grossmann, a Polish economist, worked on the mathematical theory of capitalist breakdown and published his major statement of an accumulation and collapse law in 1929. Karl August Wittfogel studied the agrarian history and hydraulic societies of China. Friedrich Pollock, Weil's friend from student days, handled the institute's finances and its contacts with the young Soviet Union, whose planned economy he reported on with a traveller's care. Nothing in this first phase announced critical theory. It was an institute for the history of socialism run by an orthodox economic historian, and any account of the Frankfurt School that begins with cultural pessimism has skipped its first seven years.

### Who directed the institute before Horkheimer?

Carl Grünberg directed it, a Viennese professor of economic history and editor of the discipline's principal archive journal, and his programme was the documentary history of the labour movement rather than philosophy. Under him the institute collected sources and trained researchers in political economy, until illness forced his retirement and opened the succession that transformed the place.

Grünberg's retirement was compelled by a stroke in the late nineteen twenties, and the succession was arranged with a candidate already inside the building. Max Horkheimer, a young professor of social philosophy at the university whose father owned textile factories and who had written on Kant and on the early bourgeois philosophy of history, took the directorship in 1930. The date matters because the standard story of the school quietly relocates its founding to suit its philosophers: the institute of 1923 belonged to Grünberg's generation and discipline, and the school of the textbooks begins only with Horkheimer's inaugural address of January 1931, in which the new director announced that the questions of social philosophy would henceforth be pursued with the methods of the empirical sciences, in permanent collaboration between disciplines. The address is the true founding document of critical theory, delivered eight years after the institute opened its doors.

Between the founding and the succession, one more precaution of Grünberg's era deserves record, because without it there would be nothing to write a guide about. Weil and Pollock, watching German politics darken, arranged the endowment's affairs so that the capital sat in a Dutch foundation beyond the reach of a German government, and branch offices were prepared in Geneva and elsewhere before any emergency. The institute's survival through 1933, when it was raided and closed within weeks of the Nazi seizure of power, was not luck. It was the founders' distrust of the republic's durability, converted into legal structures years in advance, and it stands as the first example of the pattern this guide keeps finding: the school's reputation for abstraction resting on a base of intensely practical administration.

## The programme Horkheimer set

Horkheimer's inaugural address proposed a method, not a doctrine, and the method was organisational. Social philosophy, he argued, had inherited questions about the relation of the individual to society, the meaning of culture, and the foundations of community that no single discipline could answer, while the special sciences that might have answered them had retreated into collecting facts without a theory of the whole. His remedy was a working community of philosophers, sociologists, economists, historians, and psychologists, united in permanent collaboration, pursuing the large philosophical questions with the most refined methods of empirical research. The phrase attached to the design was interdisciplinary materialism: materialism, because the questions concerned the material life process of society; interdisciplinary, because no existing department owned them. Questionnaires, statistical studies, and expert reports were to serve the same investigations as conceptual analysis. The programme explains the hiring that followed. An economist of Pollock's stamp was already in place. A psychoanalyst was recruited to head a section on social psychology. A literary sociologist took charge of the journal's reviewing operation and of studies in the sociology of literature. A young philosopher of music, Theodor Adorno, moved from association at the margins into the circle's centre over the following decade.

### What changed when Horkheimer took over the directorship?

The institute's object changed. Grünberg's researchers had documented the labour movement's past; Horkheimer's were set to explain the present order's hold on the people living under it, above all why they consented to arrangements that injured them. Philosophy entered a collaboration with the sciences, so that questionnaires and studies became instruments of the programme rather than substitutes for one.

The house journal embodied the design. From 1932 the Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung, the journal for social research, published the staff's essays and studies in a deliberate mixture: programmatic philosophy beside statistical reports, a theory of authority beside the results of fieldwork, long review essays that treated whole literatures as evidence about the state of a discipline. Its contributors were not confined to the staff, and its pages carried work by scholars the institute supported at a distance, including Benjamin's studies of Baudelaire's Paris. The journal's migration tells the institute's own story in miniature: printed in Leipzig, then in Paris after the closure, renamed in English for its final New York volumes, and extinguished by the economics of exile in 1941, while its function survived in the books and study series that replaced it.

The research agenda of the Frankfurt years can be read off the programme's central puzzle. If the materialist tradition predicted that capitalism's crises would radicalise its victims, the observable Europe of the early nineteen thirties showed victims rallying to nationalism and dictatorship instead. Horkheimer's institute set itself to explain the discrepancy at the level where it occurred: in the family that formed the child's character, in the culture that stocked the adult's imagination, in the authority relations of the workplace, and in the psychic economy that made submission feel like strength. The planned instrument was a large collaborative enquiry into authority and the family, drawing economists, analysts, and survey researchers into a single design. Before that design could be completed in Frankfurt, the political ground it stood on was removed, and the study was finished in exile and published in Paris in 1936. Its findings belong to the empirical record set out below; what matters here is the sequence. The school's most famous turn, from political economy toward culture and psyche, began as an empirical research programme with budgets, questionnaires, and field reports, and the philosophy followed the fieldwork at least as often as the fieldwork followed the philosophy.

Recruitment also fixed the school's intellectual centre of gravity for a generation. Erich Fromm, a psychoanalyst of the Berlin institute who had settled in Frankfurt, brought a proposal the programme needed: an analytic social psychology that would mediate between the economic base and the ideological superstructure by studying the socially formed character structure of groups. He directed the institute's survey of German workers and employees described in the empirical sections below, and his contribution to the authority and family volume supplied its psychological mechanism. Herbert Marcuse, a younger philosopher who had studied with Heidegger and been repelled by his teacher's politics, joined at the start of the nineteen thirties and served the institute from its Geneva refuge. Leo Löwenthal brought the sociology of literature and the history of popular reading. Franz Neumann, a labour lawyer who had advised the Social Democratic unions, arrived in exile with the legal and institutional knowledge that would produce the school's great study of the Nazi state. The pattern is consistent: the director hired competences his programme required, and the famous names were acquired the way a laboratory acquires instruments, for work already specified.

## Critical theory as a name and a method

The school named itself late and precisely. In 1937, in the journal's exile volumes, Horkheimer published the essay that fixed the term, setting traditional and critical theory against each other as two ways of knowing society. Traditional theory, modelled on the mathematical natural sciences and on Descartes's ideal of a deductive system, takes its object as given from outside: the facts are assembled, classified, and predicted, and the theorist's own position inside the society being studied is treated as a private matter irrelevant to the results. Such theory serves, whatever its practitioner's intentions, the smooth functioning of the existing order, because it accepts the categories in which that order presents itself. Critical theory refuses the separation. It knows that the facts are socially preformed, that the knowing subject is a product of the same history as the object, and that the concepts with which society is described, exchange, labour, culture, freedom, carry the marks of the arrangements they describe. Its aim is correspondingly different: not prediction and control of a process treated as natural, but the self-clarification of a society capable of judging itself, oriented toward a condition in which human beings organise their common life by reason rather than submitting to it as fate.

### What does critical theory refuse in traditional theory?

It refuses the pretence of standing outside its object. A theory that classifies social facts the way a naturalist classifies specimens forgets that the facts were produced by the society under study, and the classifier by it too, so its knowledge tends to serve the order it declines to judge. Critical theory builds that self-knowledge into its method.

Three features of the 1937 essay guard against the caricature that critical theory licensed its holders to substitute attitude for evidence. The first is its materialism: the critique is anchored in the real contradictions of the social process, in the distance between what the order promises and what it delivers, not in the critic's preferences. The second is its interdisciplinary discipline: the essay's argument was written for an institute whose journal in the same years carried statistical enquiries and documentary studies, and the theory's claims about character, family, and culture were expected to survive empirical examination because the institute was conducting it. The third is its modesty about blueprints. Critical theory declined to picture the emancipated society in detail, on the ground that a future order drawn from within a damaged present would inherit the damage; what it retained was the standard by which the present could be found wanting, the rational organisation of life by those who live it. Adorno later compressed the difficulty into the sentence that wrong life cannot be lived rightly; the compression is famous because the difficulty was structural, and it marks the distance between the school's stance and any programme of personal purity.

The name also performed a protective function in exile, and the guide records this without embarrassment because the members discussed it openly in their correspondence. Marxist terminology attracted the attention of censors, customs officers, and immigration officials across the several countries the institute passed through, and a vocabulary of materialism and critique allowed the work to continue under descriptions its hosts could accept. The disguise had a cost that later readers paid: generations who met the school's writings without its circumstances read the coded terms as the whole position and missed the Marxist architecture inside them, while hostile readers, detecting the code, treated the concealment itself as evidence of conspiracy. Both readings fail the same test. The institute's publications, finances, and personnel were matters of public record in every country it occupied, its journal was sold by subscription, and its most consequential American work was conducted for foundations and universities in the open. The code was a survival practice of refugee scholarship, of a kind every exile institution of the period will recognise.

It remains to say what critical theory was not, since the comparison with its parent tradition is reserved for its own article in this series, [critical theory vs classical marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/). It was not a revision of Marxism by committee, because no committee agreed on the revisions. It was not cultural studies before its time, because its cultural analyses were anchored in a theory of the whole society and its economic structure, and it judged most commercial culture harshly rather than celebrating its consumers' ingenuity. And it was not a political programme: the institute held no party card, issued no manifestos to workers, and directed its criticism as readily at the administered socialism of the Soviet type as at the administered capitalism of the West. What united the staff was a method and a set of unsolved problems, which is why the school's history can be written as a history of arguments without falsifying it.

Rosters settle arguments that adjectives cannot. Two lines of the table deserve prose emphasis. The first is the succession line: the institute had a founder director whose discipline was history, and the school of the textbooks is the product of his replacement, which is why founding dates in reference works mislead readers who assume the institution of 1923 already held the programme of 1931. The second is the Benjamin line. Benjamin's essay on art in the age of its technological reproducibility, Adorno's profile in this series at [theodor adorno intellectual profile](/2015/06/15/theodor-adorno-intellectual-profile/) treats their correspondence, survives in versions shaped by the institute's journal, and his Baudelaire studies were commissioned and paid for by the institute over years. Funding and publication, however, are not membership. He held no post, attended no staff meetings, and lived in Paris on a stipend whose continuance he negotiated letter by letter. Popular accounts promote him to member because his fame now exceeds the members'; the table restores the record, and the record is more interesting, since it shows an institution sustaining its most original contributor precisely by keeping him at the distance his work required.

## The empirical correction

The claim at the centre of this guide can now be stated in its strongest form. Across the nineteen thirties and nineteen forties the institute designed, funded, and published empirical research on a scale that few university departments of the period matched: a multi-method study of authority and the family combining historical analysis, questionnaires, and expert reports; a survey of several thousand German workers and employees whose protocols were coded for the political character behind the political opinion; a five-volume series of studies in prejudice conducted for an American sponsor; within that series, a personality instrument that entered the standard toolkit of social psychology; wartime analyses of enemy propaganda and of the Nazi state's structure written for operational use; and, after the return, a group discussion study of West German attitudes toward the dictatorship's legacy. Set beside that record, the standard charge that the Frankfurt School withdrew from evidence into cultural pessimism is not an exaggeration of a truth. It is a claim about a selection, the school's philosophical books of the nineteen forties, mistaken for a claim about the school.

Three explanations of the error's durability are worth distinguishing, because each teaches a different reading habit. The first is the translation sequence: the philosophical works reached English readers in a wave of translations a generation before much of the empirical corpus was available, so the Anglophone school was assembled from its most speculative shelf. The second is the visibility of genres: a scale used in thousands of questionnaires disappears into other people's footnotes, while a striking phrase about enlightenment or culture circulates under its author's name, so the school's most quotable products are its least empirical ones. The third is polemical convenience on every side. Admirers who prize the philosophy gain a school of pure thinkers unsullied by data; detractors who prize the accusation of an ideological project gain a school that manufactured attitudes without testing them; the archive, which shows researchers arguing over coding decisions and revising instruments, satisfies neither story and is consulted by fewer writers than repeat either.

### Did the Frankfurt School do empirical research?

Yes, extensively. It conducted a large study of authority and the family in the 1930s and, in American exile, a series of studies on prejudice which produced a widely used personality measure, alongside group-discussion research on postwar attitudes. The charge that it abandoned evidence is inaccurate.

The correction matters beyond pedantry, because it changes what the school's famous conclusions were. When Horkheimer and Adorno argued in exile that enlightenment reason carried domination inside itself, they wrote as men whose institute had spent a decade measuring how ordinary people actually related to authority, and whose measurements had returned unwelcome answers: that anti-authoritarian opinions often sat atop authoritarian characters, that the family was manufacturing submission more efficiently than any propaganda ministry, and that prejudice survived education, income, and professed belief. The philosophy's pessimism was an inference from data as well as a response to catastrophe. A reader may still reject the inference, and the sections below record the strongest objections in full, but the rejection has to engage findings, not a legend of armchairs. That engagement is what the empirical correction makes possible, and it is why this guide treats the studies, rather than the slogans, as the school's centre of gravity.

## Authority and the family: the study that set the pattern

The Studies on Authority and Family, published in Paris in 1936 after the institute's expulsion, remain the fullest realisation of Horkheimer's inaugural design. The volume's architecture is the programme made visible. An opening theoretical section by the director treated authority as a historical category, tracing how the bourgeois family's internal structure had trained its members for the authority relations of the workplace and the state. Fromm's social psychological section supplied the mechanism: character, formed in the family under specific economic conditions, binds the individual to the social order by desire rather than by fear alone, so that domination is reproduced inside the dominated. Marcuse contributed a history of the concept of authority from Luther through liberalism, showing how the idea's content changed as the family's economic function changed. Around these essays the volume arranged its empirical reports: surveys of workers, of white-collar employees, of the unemployed, of young people and of experts in several countries, conducted with questionnaires whose answers were interpreted, not merely tabulated.

### What did the authority and family study set out to test?

It tested whether the authority relations of the wider society were reproduced inside the family, and whether that reproduction could be observed rather than merely asserted. Questionnaires and expert reports from several countries were read against a theory of character formation, so the volume could claim, with its evidence in the open, that upbringing was manufacturing obedience.

The study's most consequential instrument was older than the volume. From the end of the nineteen twenties Fromm had directed a questionnaire survey of German manual workers and salaried employees, asking not only the standard questions of party preference and organisation but questions designed to reveal the structure behind the answer: attitudes toward discipline, toward superiors, toward the punishment of children, toward the order of the household. The responses were coded for character type, distinguishing respondents whose answers expressed a consistent rebellion against authority from those whose political radicalism coexisted with submission in every other domain. The coding returned the single most disturbing finding of the institute's German years. Among supporters of the parties of the left, only a minority displayed the anti-authoritarian character their votes promised; a large share scored as ambivalent, and a substantial fraction as authoritarian in structure while radical in opinion. The implication, drawn in the study and vindicated by events within a few years, was that the organised working class's resistance to a dictatorship of the right would be far weaker than its membership figures and vote totals suggested, because character would desert opinion at the moment of trial. The protocols survived the exile in manuscript and were published only decades later, which is one reason the finding's priority went unrecognised; the school knew, from its own data, a truth about Weimar's collapse that political journalism learned from the event.

Two methodological commitments of these years deserve emphasis, because they separate the institute's empiricism from the polling with which it is sometimes confused. The first was interpretation: a questionnaire answer was treated as a symptom to be read against the respondent's whole pattern, not as a transparent report, which brought the survey method under the discipline of psychoanalytic reading and made the coding rules themselves theoretical claims, open to dispute. The second was the refusal to separate the instrument from the enquiry's governing question. The studies were designed to test propositions drawn from the theory of society, about authority, character, and crisis, and their designs were revised when the propositions changed. Critics then and since have pressed the weak points honestly available: the samples were not drawn to modern standards, the character coding depended on interpreters' judgement, and the theory's expectations could steer both. The guide's verdict is narrower than either celebration or dismissal. By the standards of its decade the work was ambitious, systematic, and unusually self-aware about the theory-ladenness of social measurement, and it produced at least one finding, the character and opinion gap among Weimar's left voters, that events confirmed in the most brutal available way.

## Closure, Geneva, and the road to New York

The institute's German existence ended within weeks of the Nazi seizure of power. Its building was raided and its library seized in the spring of 1933, on the stated ground that the institute harboured tendencies hostile to the state, and the formal closure followed. The staff, most of them Jewish and all of them compromised by association with a Marxist foundation, were already dispersing along routes prepared in advance. Because the endowment had been placed abroad under Grünberg and Weil's arrangements, the institute could reconstitute itself legally as an international society for social research, with a working branch in Geneva where Marcuse was among the first installed, a presence in Paris where the journal found a French publisher, and contacts in London. Horkheimer travelled, assessed refuges, and chose the United States, where Columbia University's president offered the institute a building on West 117th Street and the standing of an affiliated research body without demanding control of its programme. The migration was completed in 1934, and the institute resumed work in New York with its library partially replaced, its journal continuing, and its staff reassembled from three countries.

### Where did the institute go when Germany closed to it?

It went where its founders had prepared for it to go. A Geneva office opened under the cover of an international research society, Paris took the journal, and in 1934 it accepted Columbia University's offer of a house and affiliation in New York. Members who stayed in Europe scattered to London and Paris, an archipelago before it was an address.

Exile redistributed the school's personnel across the Allied world's institutions in ways that complicate every simple story about it. Neumann, Kirchheimer, and Marcuse entered American government service during the war, analysing the enemy's structure for the research and analysis branch of the wartime intelligence service, where Neumann's study of the Nazi regime became a working reference and Marcuse drafted assessments of Germany's political condition that carried the institute's categories into official memoranda. Adorno spent a period in Oxford attached to no institution, then joined the radio research project at Princeton directed by the Viennese sociologist Paul Lazarsfeld, an encounter between critical and administrative research whose friction taught both men the boundary of their methods; from there he moved to Los Angeles, joining Horkheimer, who had settled in Pacific Palisades for his health and for the quiet in which the two composed their joint book on enlightenment. Pollock administered, as he had always administered, holding the institute's finances and its American legal existence together. Fromm, whose break with the director is treated below, rebuilt an independent career in New York. The staff's wartime record is a matter of personnel files and published reports: the institute's members served the democracies' war effort with the analytical skills the institute had trained, which is among the plainest facts available to anyone constructing a portrait of the school as a subversive cell, and among the most consistently omitted.

The exile also changed the questions, and the change was driven by evidence rather than by fashion. In Germany the puzzle had been authority: why the subordinate admired the superior. In America the puzzle became prejudice: why a democracy with formal equality sustained organised hatreds, and whether those hatreds marked a personality structure that fascism could mobilise. The institute's answer took the form of the prejudice studies treated in the next section, funded by the American Jewish Committee and executed with American collaborators, sampling, and clinical method. The German question had been why resistance failed; the American question was whether the failure's conditions existed in the victorious democracy too. That the school asked it about its host country, in print, with its host's philanthropic money, is the best short evidence of what the empirical programme was for: the data were gathered where the researchers stood, and the conclusions were not adjusted for the address.

## The studies in prejudice and the authoritarian personality

The institute's American empirical monument is the series published under the title Studies in Prejudice, five volumes produced under Horkheimer's direction for the research department of the American Jewish Committee. The series' design was the Frankfurt programme translated into American conditions: prejudice was to be studied not as an opinion to be counted but as a structure to be explained, with survey instruments, clinical interviews, and content analyses of propaganda arranged to converge on the same underlying formation. Its volumes examined the authoritarian personality, the prejudiced child of Chicago housing studies, emotional disorder and antisemitism in clinical cases, the career of a destroyed German trade union movement, and the techniques of American agitators whose broadcasts and pamphlets Löwenthal and his collaborator anatomised line by line. The volume that carried the series into the permanent literature of social psychology was The Authoritarian Personality, produced at Berkeley by Adorno with the psychologists Else Frenkel-Brunswik, Daniel Levinson, and Nevitt Sanford, and published in 1950.

### What was the F scale built to measure?

The F scale was built to measure a respondent's potential receptivity to fascism without naming fascism or any party in its items. Its statements probed conventionalism, submission to authority, aggression against deviants, hostility to introspection, superstition, and toughness, so that agreement across innocent sounding items could reveal an anti-democratic character that direct questions would have concealed.

The study's reasoning deserves the prominence the scale's fame sometimes steals from it. The authors held that the openly expressed ideology of a person was a surface formation, and that beneath it lay a personality organisation, built in childhood within the family, which determined whether democratic or anti-democratic appeals would find a prepared hearer. The instruments were layered accordingly: attitude scales for antisemitism and ethnocentrism, the F scale for the underlying structure, clinical interviews with high and low scorers, and projective tests whose stories could be scored for the same variables. The portrait that emerged from the high scorers was coherent and has entered the language: rigid conventionalism, submission upward and aggression downward, suspicion of the subjective and the tender minded, a fascination with power and toughness, and a projective readiness to believe that wild and dangerous things were happening in the world. Whatever the verdict on the instruments, the study's central hypothesis, that susceptibility to anti-democratic movements is a measurable property of persons formed in families and cultures rather than a spontaneous product of propaganda, remains one of the most influential conjectures in the social sciences of the period.

The criticism arrived promptly and at full strength, and this guide states it as part of the record rather than as an embarrassment. Methodologists objected that the F scale's items all ran in one direction, so that agreement could reflect a habit of agreeing rather than a structure of prejudice; that the Berkeley sample was not drawn to represent any population; that the clinical interviewers knew the scores they were interpreting; and that the psychoanalytic theory of the high scorer's childhood was asserted where it should have been tested. A substantial critical volume appeared within four years of publication, and later researchers rebuilt the construct with balanced items and a narrower focus on submission, aggression, and conventionalism as a single syndrome of the right rather than of authoritarianism generally. The school's own response was characteristically divided: Adorno conceded points of method in print while defending the design's interpretive core, and the institute's later German work revised its instruments in the light of the quarrel. The guide's assessment follows the record. The scale was a flawed instrument built on a consequential idea, and its afterlife, in which the idea survived the instrument's defects and generated decades of corrective research, is precisely what the empirical correction predicts: this was a research programme, with hypotheses exposed to refutation, revisions, and successors, not a doctrine guarded from contact with evidence.

## Two verdicts on the Nazi state

The school's deepest internal dispute concerned the regime that had destroyed its German existence, and it was never resolved, which is why it anchors this guide's second correcting fact. Friedrich Pollock, the institute's economist and financial manager, had argued since the early nineteen thirties that capitalism was entering a phase in which the market's automatic coordination could be replaced by political command: a state capitalism, he called it, in which a general plan, however brutal its ends, subordinated profit to power and thereby escaped the economic contradictions that classical theory expected to bring the order down. Applied to Germany and to the Soviet Union alike, the thesis removed the consolation that the dictatorships would collapse under their own economic irrationality; a state capitalist order could manage its crises politically, distribute losses, and endure. Its implications for the school's politics were severe. If the economy no longer generated its own gravediggers, no agent of emancipation could be counted on to appear, and theory's task narrowed to preserving the truth about the whole in a period when nothing else could be done with it. Much of the circle's famous pessimism is Pollock's thesis, felt at the level of philosophy.

### What did Pollock and Neumann disagree about?

They disagreed about whether the Nazi regime was a coherent state at all. Pollock's state capitalism thesis described a new order in which political command had displaced the market and a unified apparatus could plan its domination. Neumann's study answered that the regime was a non-state, a shapeless contest of party, bureaucracy, army, and industry held together by plunder.

Neumann's Behemoth, published in 1942 and named for the monster of the book of Job as Hobbes's Leviathan's chaotic twin, argued from the regime's legal and institutional record that Germany was governed by four competing power complexes whose jurisdictions overlapped and whose struggles no sovereign arbitrated. He named the system totalitarian monopoly capitalism, keeping the economic determination that Pollock had dissolved: monopoly capital remained a ruling partner, the profit motive continued to organise production beneath the political terror, and the regime's dynamic incoherence promised not stability but an expansion that would end in ruin. The disagreement was not a nuance. On Pollock's account, the future belonged to administered orders of either flag, and resistance was a moral posture without a mechanism. On Neumann's, the regime carried genuine instabilities that analysis could locate and policy could exploit, which is why his book served the wartime agencies as a working analysis while Pollock's essays served the philosophers as a warning. Kirchheimer's legal studies of punishment and political justice sided methodologically with Neumann's institutional concreteness, while Horkheimer's wartime manuscripts on the authoritarian state and the racket leaned toward Pollock's darker pole. A school whose staff divided over the fundamental character of their common enemy, in print, for a decade, cannot be truthfully represented as a choir, and any account of the Frankfurt School that omits this argument has omitted the school's internal life at its most consequential point.

## Psychoanalysis in the house, and the Fromm break

Psychoanalysis entered the institute as a working discipline rather than an ornament. Frankfurt possessed one of Germany's first psychoanalytic institutes, in whose rooms the young Fromm had trained and practised, and the social research institute's programme required exactly what analysis offered: an account of how social demands get inside the person and acquire the force of desire. Fromm's analytic social psychology proposed that each society produces the character structure it needs, a libidinal cement binding members to their roles, and his survey work operationalised the proposal. For most of the nineteen thirties he was the programme's engine of empirical research, and the authority and family volume bears his stamp on every page. The break, when it came at the end of the decade, was therefore a scientific event inside the school, not a personnel footnote.

### Why did Erich Fromm leave the institute?

He left because his revision of Freud collided with the orthodoxy the director and Adorno defended. Fromm softened the doctrine of innate drives into an account of relatedness shaped by social conditions, which his colleagues judged a dilution that dissolved the theory's critical edge; the quarrel over Freud fused with disputes about his role and income until the association ended.

The substance of the quarrel repays a paragraph, because it prefigures disputes across the whole tradition. Freud's theory, as Horkheimer and Adorno received it, held that civilisation exacts instinctual renunciations whose costs no social rearrangement abolishes, and that this tragic residue was precisely what protected psychoanalysis from becoming a technology of adjustment. Fromm's revision relocated the weight from instinct to interpersonal relations and social conditions, making character more plastic and therapy more hopeful, and opening the path to the humane and popular books that made him famous after the separation. Marcuse later entered the argument from the far side, defending Freud's metapsychology against Fromm in print with a severity that startled readers who assumed the school spoke with one voice about its own departed member. The episode matters to this guide for its evidential value. A coordinated project does not excommunicate its most productive empirical researcher over the theory of drives in the middle of an exile, and then continue the quarrel for twenty years in rival books. A research community with real stakes in its shared premises does exactly that, and the school's cohesion was of that kind throughout: institutional, financial, and argumentative, never doctrinal.

## Benjamin, the funded outsider

Walter Benjamin's relation to the institute tests every loose usage of the word member. He was a Berlin critic of independent means that failed, a collector and essayist whose habilitation the University of Frankfurt had rejected in the nineteen twenties, when Horkheimer, then a young lecturer, had witnessed the faculty politics of the rejection at close quarters. In exile in Paris, without income, he became the institute's most consequential external contributor: a stipend was arranged, his studies were commissioned for the journal, and his great unfinished archaeology of nineteenth century Paris, the arcades project, proceeded under the institute's patronage with its exposés circulated to the staff for comment. The journal published his essay on the work of art in the age of its technological reproducibility in a French version shaped by editorial negotiation, and his studies of Baudelaire, the second of which Adorno's criticism famously forced him to rewrite, with the surviving correspondence showing two formidable intelligences disputing, across the Channel and the Atlantic, whether the flâneur's Paris was a phantasmagoria of the commodity or a field of awakening. Adorno wanted the theory stated; Benjamin wanted the images to convict on their own; the printed record of the argument is one of the treasures of the tradition.

### Was Walter Benjamin a member of the institute?

No. He was its most distinguished external contributor, supported by a stipend, published in its journal, and argued with by its director and by Adorno as an intellectual equal, but he held no post and lived in Paris outside the staff's working life. His death on the Spanish frontier in 1940 ended the association before any appointment could arise.

The correction of the membership error is not pedantry, because the error drives a false inference. If Benjamin was a member, then the school's doctrine must somehow contain both his hope for mass culture's emancipatory shock and Adorno's conviction that the culture industry administered consciousness, and commentators have performed contortions to unify them. Restore the stipend and the distance, and the contradiction resolves into what it was: an institution rich enough, in money and in confidence, to fund a thinker whose conclusions its inner circle expected to dislike, and to publish him while arguing back. The school's central cultural doctrine belongs to the article this guide defers to, the treatment of [the culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), and Benjamin's counterweight belongs to his own profile tradition; here the institutional fact is the point, and the fact is generosity with a budget line.

## California and the book on enlightenment

The war years settled the inner circle in southern California, where Horkheimer's health and the Pacific light drew a small colony of German exile intellect around Los Angeles, and there the two directors of the school's philosophical reputation composed the work on which that reputation chiefly rests. Dialectic of Enlightenment, circulated in mimeograph in 1944 under the title Philosophical Fragments and published in Amsterdam three years later, argued that the reason which had promised liberation from myth had itself become mythic: that enlightenment, from Odysseus binding himself to the mast through the administered pleasures of the film industry, carried domination in its method, reducing nature, including human nature, to material for control. Marcuse's wartime service and Neumann's left the pair in California to write as the institute's public programme paused, and the book's darkness is inseparable from its moment: it was composed while the regime that had expelled its authors industrialised murder, and while the democracy sheltering them perfected the culture industries the book anatomised. Readers who treat its pessimism as a temperament should be directed to its dates.

Adorno's own wartime and postwar production complicates the portrait further. Alongside the enlightenment book he composed the aphorisms of Minima Moralia, reflections from damaged life, dedicated to Horkheimer and written in a form, the fragment that refuses system, which his philosophy of the concept would later justify at length. He worked on the prejudice studies with the Berkeley group, drafted analyses of radio music and of the astrological column of a Los Angeles newspaper with the same empirical care the institute had applied to German workers, and prepared the return to a Germany whose universities were staffed by men whose careers the dictatorship had made. The California years were therefore not a retirement into aphorism. They were the hinge on which the school's two identities, empirical institute and philosophical legend, swung closest together and then apart, as the books travelled further than the studies and the studies' author became, in the public mind, only the books' author.

## The return to Frankfurt

The institute's return was negotiated, not assumed. The city of Frankfurt and the state of Hesse wanted the exiles back as a moral asset for a university town rebuilding its name, the American occupation authorities favoured the return of anti-Nazi scholars, and the institute's recovered funds made reconstruction materially possible. A new building rose on the Senckenberg Anlage, the institute reopened in 1951 with Horkheimer as director and Adorno as his deputy and designated successor, and Horkheimer served a term as rector of the university itself, delivering addresses on the university's responsibility for democracy that were printed and debated across the young Federal Republic. The symbolism was exact: the institution expelled in 1933 as an enemy of the state returned as an instructor of the state's successor, and it accepted the role with a seriousness that its later student critics would find compromising and its defenders would call the point of the exercise.

The research resumed on the German question the exiles had carried with them. The institute's first major postwar study, the Group Experiment published under Pollock's editorship in 1955, adapted American group discussion methods to a delicate object: how ordinary Germans, a decade after the regime's fall, talked about guilt, responsibility, and the recent past when they believed themselves among their own. Discussion groups were convened across the occupation zones' successor society, recorded, and subjected to an interpretive analysis modelled on the questionnaire reading of the Weimar years, attentive to the difference between the official opinion a respondent offered the record and the operative opinion that surfaced in argument, evasion, and joke.

### What did the group discussions after the return set out to hear?

They set out to hear the distance between professed and operative opinion in a society formally committed to democracy and informally saturated with the dictatorship's afterlife. The analysis found respondents who recited the democratic catechism for the record and, in the same session, defended the regime, resented the reckoning, and treated guilt as an Allied imposition.

Adorno's interventions in the republic's public life extended the research into pedagogy. His radio talks and essays on education after Auschwitz, on the meaning of working through the past, and on the authoritarian patterns reproduced in German child rearing addressed the society in its own media with the institute's findings translated into public argument. The same years produced his late philosophical summa, Negative Dialectics, and the beginnings of the aesthetic theory left unfinished at his death. Horkheimer, retired from the directorship and living across the Swiss border, turned toward theology's negative image, the longing for the wholly other, in terms that startled secular readers who had filed him as a materialist. The institute these men led was by then training the generation that would redefine it: Jürgen Habermas arrived as Adorno's assistant in 1956, fresh from a celebrated early essay on Heidegger's politics, and departed three years later when his habilitation project met the director's reservations, completing it instead under a political scientist at Marburg. The friction is instructive and this guide records it without varnish: the school that theorised emancipation ran its succession by the ordinary academic instruments of patronage and veto, and its most consequential heir formed himself partly in reaction against its philosophical pessimism, rebuilding the theory around communication, law, and the public sphere rather than around the philosophy of history that had darkened in exile.

## The school and its students

No episode tests the school's self-understanding like the confrontation of the late nineteen sixties, when the students who filled Adorno's lecture halls concluded that his theory, taken seriously, indicted its author's caution. The German student movement drew real sustenance from the institute's work: its critique of authoritarian character explained the professoriate the students faced, its analyses of administered culture named the world they refused, and Marcuse, from California, supplied the movement internationally with arguments and with a vocabulary of liberation that his Frankfurt colleagues regarded with open unease. When students occupied the institute's rooms in January 1969, it was Adorno who telephoned the police to clear the building, and the photograph of the theorist of non-identity watching his own premises emptied by officers became the movement's favourite emblem of betrayal. His lectures were disrupted that spring, in one notorious instance by demonstrators who bared their breasts and scattered petals in a theatre of accusation, and Adorno, who had written that there is no right life in the wrong one, withdrew from teaching and died that August in Switzerland, weeks before the term his faculty troubles would have resumed.

The guide states the students' charge at its strongest because the school's memory deserves the test. The charge ran: a theory that diagnosed total administration and refused every agent of change had become, whatever its intentions, an alibi for the order it described; its pessimism was self-fulfilling, its institutional comforts were purchased by silence about its host society, and its founder's own wartime manuscripts conceded that the critic who wills nothing becomes the order's licensed mourner. Adorno's answer, given in essays and interviews of those years, was consistent with everything the institute had published since 1937. Theory, he held, is itself a form of practice when the conditions for any other practice have been absorbed by the administered whole; the demand that thought issue immediately in action mistakes the movement's timetable for history's; and a critic who trims his findings to flatter a movement's hopes has surrendered the only instrument he has. The argument was not won, because such arguments are settled by decades rather than by semesters. What the record shows is a school whose concepts armed a revolt against its own institutional caution, and whose leading figure chose the concepts' integrity over the revolt's applause, at real personal cost. Readers will divide over the choice exactly as contemporaries did, and the division is itself a finding about the school: its work generated obligations it could not itself discharge, and it knew this, and published the knowledge.

## The Grand Hotel Abyss: the objection at full strength

The most durable criticism of the school was phrased by a rival Marxist before most of the school's famous books existed in English. Georg Lukács, the Hungarian philosopher whose reification essays the young founders had studied at Weil's work week, looked at the German intelligentsia of the exile generation and described its leading spirits as having taken up residence in a grand hotel on the edge of nothingness. The phrase, usually rendered as the Grand Hotel Abyss, ran in his fuller figure as "a beautiful hotel, equipped with every comfort, on the edge of an abyss", and it has attached itself to the Frankfurt circle ever since, because it condenses into one image the objection that this guide is required to state at its full strength.

### What is the strongest charge against the school?

The strongest charge is that the school converted a political defeat into a philosophy of history. Having concluded that the working class was integrated, that enlightenment reason reproduced domination, and that no agent of emancipation remained, its theory guaranteed its conclusion in advance, criticised everything and organised nothing, and contemplated catastrophe from endowed comfort.

Stated without the metaphor, the objection has four counts. First, the agent count: a Marxism that cannot name the social force that might realise its critique has kept Marxism's vocabulary and surrendered its function, becoming social criticism with a tragic style. Second, the self-sealing count: when every apparent counterexample, working class militancy, reform, cultural vitality, can be reclassified as integration, pseudo-activity, or administered pseudo-individuality, the theory has insured itself against refutation and thereby left science for metaphysics. Third, the institutional count: the criticism of the administered world was financed by a capitalist endowment, housed by universities, and consulted by the very agencies of the American state, and its refusal of parties and programmes conveniently matched the terms on which that comfort was available. Fourth, the count of consequences: a generation taught that the whole is the false, and that action within it is complicity, learned resignation with a sophisticated accent, and the school's own students revolted in 1969 precisely because they had absorbed the diagnosis and refused the resignation.

The answer this guide gives is the school's own, tested against the record assembled above, and it comes with a concession the brief for this article requires. What the school claimed to be doing was preserving the possibility of critique through a period in which the conditions for the practice it theorised had, on its evidence, disappeared: the European working class movements destroyed or integrated, the Soviet alternative discredited as another administration, fascism demonstrated to be a mass possibility rather than a conspiracy of elites. Under those conditions, the members held, the betrayal would have been to manufacture an agent for comfort's sake, to certify some party or movement as history's instrument against the data their own institute had gathered. The questionnaires of the Weimar years had returned their verdict on the revolutionary character of the organised class before Hitler made the verdict undeniable; the prejudice studies found the authoritarian structure distributed through the democracy that had defeated him; Pollock's analysis closed the economic escape hatch. The pessimism was argued from findings, and it kept the findings' standard: it could have been refuted by a different world, and the school's second generation, formed in a different world, duly revised it. The concession stands beside the answer and is not withdrawn. A theory of emancipation without an agent is a real gap in the school's construction, acknowledged by Habermas in rebuilding the theory to fill it, and no defence of the school's integrity converts the gap into a virtue. The hotel had an endowment, and the endowment made the contemplation possible; what the endowment could not purchase was a refutation of the studies, and none has been produced.

## Why faith in the working class failed

The question the school's critics and students alike press hardest, why the expected revolutionary agent never arrived, was answered by the institute in layers, and the layers are worth separating because popular accounts collapse them into a sneer about cultural taste. The first layer was inherited experience: the collapse of working class internationalism in 1914, when the great socialist parties voted their nations' war credits, and the defeat of the German revolution that followed the war, in which a socialist government deployed the old officer corps against the workers' councils. The founders' generation did not need theory to doubt the agent; it had watched the agent's organisations choose order. The second layer was electoral and clinical evidence from the institute's own instruments: the Nazis drew millions of working class votes, and Fromm's coding of the workers' survey showed that even the voters of the left parties were, in character, more obedient than rebellious, a finding that converted a political disappointment into a psychological problem requiring the whole apparatus of analytic social psychology to state. The third layer was the Soviet demonstration: the one successful revolution in the tradition's name had produced an administered society that the school, from Pollock's comparative essays onward, analysed as a variant of the same integration rather than as its opposite, removing the consolation that the agent existed elsewhere under discipline.

The fourth layer was postwar prosperity, and it is the layer Marcuse later theorised for the whole school in his account of [marcuse and one-dimensional man](/2015/08/15/marcuse-one-dimensional-man-guide/): the advanced industrial order learned to deliver goods, security, and managed satisfactions on a scale that absorbed opposition into consumption, so that the working class of the wealthy countries ceased to stand outside the system as its negation and became its most reliable constituency. Horkheimer's wartime manuscripts had reached the same conclusion through the figure of the racket, the protection gang as the elementary form of domination in a world where class rule no longer needed the market's mediation. By the nineteen sixties the school's leading figures disagreed about the consequence, Marcuse searching the margins, students, racial minorities, the Third World, for a substratum the integration had missed, Adorno refusing each candidate in turn as a wish wearing a theory's clothes. The disagreement is the honest residue of the question. The school's data had destroyed the inherited agent; its members never agreed on a successor; and its critics are entitled to the gap, though not to the invention that the loss was a preference rather than a finding.

## A school without a party

The question whether the Frankfurt School was communist dissolves under the institutional record into a more interesting one: what kind of Marxist body takes no party's discipline, no state's money, and no movement's instructions, for fifty years. The institute's founding capital was private, and its statutes protected its independence in both directions, from the university that housed it and from any political organisation that might have claimed it. Its journal published across the spectrum of non-party Marxist scholarship and beyond it, printing psychoanalysts, legal theorists, and economists whose relations to communism ranged from sympathy through indifference to hostility, while the Communist Party's own cultural apparatus denounced the institute's leading figures as renegades with a regularity that continued into the postwar decades. Horkheimer's correspondence shows a director determined that the institute would be nobody's instrument, refusing tactical alignments that would have purchased protection at the price of the research programme, and policing the boundary so strictly that staff complained of caution even as the caution preserved them. In American exile the members' wartime service placed the institute's analytical capital at the disposal of the democracies' intelligence and information agencies, a deployment impossible to reconcile with the picture of a directed cell, and the institute accepted American Jewish philanthropy and Columbia's roof without accepting either sponsor's politics. The school's Marxism was real, structural, and unmistakable in its categories: capital, class, ideology, reification, the critique of political economy as the skeleton beneath the cultural analysis. It was also, by design and by conviction, a Marxism without a card, which is why both the communist movement and its enemies have found it so difficult to file.

## The reading public as evidence: Löwenthal's line of work

One career inside the institute demonstrates the empirical correction at the level of a single bibliography, and it belongs to the member popular accounts omit entirely. Leo Löwenthal came to the institute as a scholar of literature and built its sociology of reading: studies of how the German public had received Dostoevsky across the decades before the First World War, of the historical novel's social function, and, in American exile, of the popular biographies fed to magazine readers, whose heroes he showed had shifted from the producers of an earlier era, industrialists, bankers, and professionals presented as idols of production, to the entertainers and sportsmen of a later one, idols of consumption whose lives taught readers to want rather than to make. The method was content analysis conducted with a historian's patience: large samples of texts, coded for recurring structures, read as evidence about the society that consumed them. His collaboration with Norbert Guterman on the rhetoric of American agitators applied the same discipline to fascist propaganda on American soil, cataloguing the devices, the personalised grievance, the conspiracy that explains everything, by which democratic publics were being rehearsed for authoritarian conclusions. Adorno's impatience with what he took to be Löwenthal's indulgence toward popular forms is recorded in their correspondence and belongs in the table's disagreement column; the work itself proceeded for four decades, in the journal, in the prejudice series, and later at Berkeley, and it refutes by its existence the notion that the institute's interest in culture was a retreat from research into reverie. The culture was the data, and Löwenthal was its fieldworker.

## How the studies sat with the philosophy

Visitors to the school's history often ask how questionnaires and aphorisms could issue from the same address, as if the institute had kept a research department and a philosophy department in an armed truce. The record shows a more deliberate relation, with traffic in both directions and several collisions that the participants themselves described. The philosophy generated the studies' questions: the character and opinion gap was a hypothesis about ideology before it was a coding decision, and the prejudice programme's layered instruments embodied the theoretical claim that attitudes are surfaces over structures. The studies disciplined the philosophy in return: the authoritarian personality research forced the concept of the authoritarian character out of Fromm's clinical idiom into operational definitions that critics could attack, and the group experiment's transcripts confronted the postwar theorists of the unmastered past with the actual sentences in which their countrymen evaded it. Where the traffic jammed, the participants named the obstruction. Adorno's years with Lazarsfeld's radio project produced the period's clearest statement of the difference between administrative research, which serves a client's given purposes, and critical research, which interrogates the purposes themselves; the distinction was Lazarsfeld's formulation as much as Adorno's grievance, offered by the dean of empirical sociology in a spirit of professional truce, and the two men's later accounts of the collaboration agree that the friction was methodological rather than personal, a disagreement about what a finding is for.

The balance sheet can be drawn without romance. The institute's philosophy is read in departments where its studies are unknown, and its studies are cited in literatures that have never opened its philosophy, and this divided afterlife is the accident that made the legend possible. Within the working institution, the two were one enterprise under one director and one journal, and the enterprise's signature products, the authority volume, the prejudice series, the group experiment, are exactly the works in which the union was consummated. A reader who wants the school whole must therefore read across the divide its posterity imposed, which is the practical meaning of the empirical correction for anyone setting a syllabus, writing a history, or checking a claim about what the Frankfurt School was.

## The wartime analyses: the school at work

Between the journal's extinction and the prejudice series' launch, the institute's members produced a body of applied analysis that popular accounts rarely mention because it appeared in memoranda, agency reports, and books published under individual names for operational readers. The research and analysis branch of the American wartime intelligence service recruited Neumann, Marcuse, and Kirchheimer, among other exiled jurists and social scientists, to explain the enemy society to the government fighting it. Neumann's Behemoth circulated inside the agencies as a structural map of the regime; his later memoranda addressed the problems of occupation, the dismantling of cartels, and the political psychology of a defeated Germany. Marcuse's reports for the same branch and then for the State Department analysed the political tendencies of the German population, the prospects of democratisation, and the condition of the parties and unions re-emerging from illegality, carrying into official prose the institute's habits of structural explanation. Kirchheimer wrote on the Nazi legal order's deformations and on the framework within which war criminals might be tried, and published in the same years his studies of punishment and social structure's successor problems. The institute as a corporation also sought war work in its own name, designing studies of antisemitism among American workers for a labour sponsor, whose field reports documented shop floor prejudice with the questionnaires and interpretive protocols of the Frankfurt years.

This record matters to the guide's central claim for a reason beyond completeness. A school that had allegedly abandoned evidence and politics spent the war years supplying both to a state at war with the regime that had expelled it, and its analyses were valued precisely for their empirical discipline: the refusal to treat Nazi Germany as a monolith, the attention to institutional rivalry and economic interest, the insistence that propaganda be studied in its texts rather than denounced in generalities. The distance between that record and the legend of the grand hotel is the distance the empirical correction exists to close. It also explains a feature of the school's postwar standing that outsiders find puzzling: when the members returned to Frankfurt, they returned not as supplicants but as scholars whose wartime service and American publications had made them, in the eyes of the occupation authorities and the German public alike, accredited interpreters of the catastrophe, a standing the institute converted into the rebuilding of its German home.

## Antisemitism as the test case

No single problem runs through the school's work in every genre and every decade the way antisemitism does, and tracing it shows the philosophy and the empirics in their working relation. Horkheimer's 1939 essay on the Jews and Europe, published in the journal's American incarnation, argued in the school's most uncompromising materialist register that fascism's hatred of the Jews belonged to the order of domination itself and could not be argued away by enlightenment about the Jews' actual character. The enlightenment book composed in California contained a chapter on the elements of antisemitism that recast the problem in the philosophy of history's terms: the persecuted minority as the screen onto which a civilisation that had mutilated its own members projected the happiness it had renounced, false projection as the pathology of a reason that could no longer distinguish the world from its fears. Adorno's postwar essay on the Freudian theory and the pattern of fascist propaganda worked the same seam analytically, dissecting the speeches of an American radio agitator to show the standardised devices, the leader's performance of being at once superman and ordinary fellow, by which the technique operated. And beneath all three texts lay the empirical programme: the prejudice studies' scales and interviews, the agitator content analyses, the labour antisemitism field reports, the postwar group discussions in which German respondents' evasions on the Jewish question supplied the return period's most quoted evidence. Theory proposed the mechanism; instruments measured its distribution; content analysis caught it in the act; and the sequence repeated for thirty years. Whatever judgement a reader finally passes on the school's answers, the questions were pursued with every instrument the period afforded, in the open, and the instruments' results were published where other researchers could and did attack them.

## The institute at the microphone: radio, music, and administered taste

The school's cultural research was conducted, characteristically, with budgets and fieldwork, and its first great laboratory was American radio. The Princeton radio research project, financed by a foundation grant and directed by Lazarsfeld, employed Adorno to head its music study, on the theory that the European philosopher might explain what broadcasting was doing to musical culture. Adorno's memoranda from the project remain startling documents: analyses of the symphony broadcast's false intimacy, of the plugging of songs until familiarity masqueraded as liking, of listening habits regressing under repetition to a distracted recognition that consumed music as a trademark rather than as a structure. His empirical colleagues found the memoranda unusable as research designs and magnificent as diagnosis, and the collaboration's published residue, including a joint study of music in radio, shows the two traditions straining at a single object. The episode is usually told as comedy, the metaphysician among the counters. Read against the institute's record it is something else: the empirical programme testing its most speculative member against a commercial medium's data, and the member returning with concepts, plugging, pseudo-individualisation, the culture industry's first drafts, that the data had provoked and that his later work generalised. The institute's other cultural fieldwork of the period ran parallel: the content analysis of a newspaper astrology column, undertaken in Los Angeles in the nineteen fifties, in which Adorno coded the columnist's counsel day by day to expose the authoritarian structure of its address, irrationality administered in a tone of intimate reasonableness. Astrology columns and radio symphonies are humble objects. The school's wager, consistent from the workers' questionnaire onward, was that humble objects, studied without condescension and without illusion, disclose the order that produced them more honestly than its official self-descriptions do.

Music deserves its separate sentence in any account of the institute, because one of its directors was a composer. Adorno had studied composition with Alban Berg in Vienna and piano with Eduard Steuermann, published music criticism through the nineteen twenties, and never treated his musical formation as a biographical ornament: the philosophy of music became his model for how a material could be at once socially determined and irreducibly itself, and his books on Wagner, on Mahler, and on the philosophy of the new music carried the school's categories into a domain where its critics least expected rigour. The institute sheltered this work as it sheltered Neumann's law: as one of the disciplines through which the whole society could be read. When the school's detractors describe a flight from economics into aesthetics, the record of the radio project, the astrology study, and the music division's budget lines gives the accurate reply: the aesthetic was a field site, chosen because the society's truth was stored there, and worked with the same instruments as the factory and the family.

## The positivist dispute: the school defends science against scientism

A generation after the exile, the school's relation to empirical method became a public German controversy in its own right, and its course settles any remaining doubt that the institute's empiricism was a youthful phase outgrown by philosophy. At a Tübingen meeting of the German sociological association in 1961, Karl Popper presented theses on the logic of the social sciences and Adorno answered him, in a confrontation both men conducted with unexpected courtesy and fundamental disagreement: Popper's critical rationalism held that science advances by conjecture and refutation under rules indifferent to the researcher's standpoint, while Adorno insisted that society, unlike nature, is both the object and the product of the knowing subject, so that a method pretending to standpointlessness mistakes the deepest fact about its object. Younger partisans radicalised both positions in print across the decade, Habermas carrying the institute's argument into the philosophy of science with his doctrine of knowledge guiding interests, Hans Albert defending the critical rationalist flank, until the exchange was collected in a volume whose title, the positivist dispute in German sociology, fixed the episode's name. Adorno's own contributions to the quarrel are models of the school's mature position: he defended empirical research against the charge that his philosophy despised it, pointing to the institute's shelves of studies, while attacking the ritual by which method, in the hands of its administrators, became an end that excused the researcher from thinking about the whole. The dispute's importance for this guide is evidentiary. The school's critics had accused it simultaneously of abandoning evidence and of poisoning science with politics; the Tübingen exchange shows its leading figure, at the height of his fame, arguing in public that the social sciences needed more rigour about their concepts and more honesty about their position, and practising, in the institute he directed, the combination he preached.

## The economics underneath: crisis, planning, and the primacy of politics

Beneath the school's cultural and psychological fame runs an economic argument that structured everything else, and recovering it is part of this guide's correction, because the legend of a school that traded economics for aesthetics inverts the actual dependence: the cultural theory was built on the economics, not instead of it. The Grünberg years had installed political economy as the institute's spine, and Grossmann's 1929 book gave the spine a thesis: capitalism's accumulation drives toward breakdown as the mass of capital outgrows the surplus available to valorise it, so that crisis is the system's law of motion rather than its accident. Pollock's travels in the Soviet Union and his studies of planned economy confronted that thesis with an awkward fact already visible before the exile: the crisis of 1929 had not produced breakdown and revolution but state intervention on an unprecedented scale, in Moscow by plan, in Berlin and Washington by emergency management, and Pollock's essays of the early nineteen thirties, including his contribution to the Festschrift honouring Grünberg, drew the inference that the economy had become administrable, that political decisions could override the market's compulsions for long periods, and that Marxist theory had to reckon with a capitalism whose crises would be managed rather than suffered to their revolutionary end.

The inference ripened in exile into the state capitalism thesis and its quarrel with Neumann, treated above, and its afterlife inside the school was long. Horkheimer's authoritarian state manuscript generalised the point into a theory of the racket and of an integrated order in which domination no longer required the market's anonymity. Adorno's late lectures on society returned repeatedly to the question whether exchange still governed beneath the administration, answering, against Pollock's stronger formulations, that the law of value persisted in modified form and that reports of its abolition were premature, a disagreement conducted in lecture halls rather than pamphlets but documented in the transcripts. The school's second generation inherited the problem in altered terms, as the welfare state's legitimation replaced breakdown as the crisis form to be explained. The thread's continuity is the point for a reader meeting the school through its cultural writings: when the culture industry essay argues that entertainment has become a branch of the administered whole, the premise doing the work is Pollock's, that administration had become the economy's dominant form, and the essay's force as analysis, as opposed to lament, depends entirely on whether that economic premise is true. Judging the school therefore begins where the school began, with political economy, and the cultural pessimism that posterity isolated was, in its own architecture, a corollary.

## The Frankfurt field: rivals on the same street

The institute did not work in a vacuum, and its distinctiveness sharpens against the brilliant company its own city provided. Frankfurt's university in the late Weimar years held, alongside the institute, the sociology chair of Karl Mannheim, whose sociology of knowledge proposed that all thought, Marxist thought included, be analysed as the expression of social standpoints, a proposal the institute's materialists received as a solvent that dissolved the question of truth into a census of perspectives. Fromm's published criticism of Mannheim's programme, respectful and fundamental, marks the boundary: the institute insisted that a standpoint could be false, that ideology named a defect in cognition and not merely a location for it, and that a sociology which declined to judge had abandoned the critical inheritance both men claimed. The city's psychoanalytic institute, the legal scholars around the labour law tradition, and the theological and philosophical faculties supplied collaborators, audiences, and antagonists in roughly equal measure, and the young Horkheimer's habilitation had been sponsored by the neo-Kantian Hans Cornelius, whose assistant he briefly was, a reminder that the school's founder of critical theory was formed inside the academic philosophy he later indicted. Exile abolished this ecology at a stroke, and something in the school's later manner, the sense of a complete world carried in a few heads, dates from the loss: the institute in New York and Los Angeles had no Mannheim across the street, and its members became one another's entire intellectual environment, with the gains in coherence and the losses in friction that such enclosure predictably brings. The returned institute of the nineteen fifties rebuilt an ecology deliberately, in a university and a republic that needed its services, and the second generation's greater openness to neighbouring disciplines, to American social science, to law and linguistics, records the lesson the enclosure taught.

## The interpreted questionnaire: a method and its risks

The institute's signature instrument deserves a section of its own, because it embodies the school's whole epistemology in a technique. A conventional survey of the period asked a worker his party and printed the answer as his politics. Fromm's questionnaire asked the party question and then asked what the respondent thought of corporal punishment, of a wife's obedience, of the strong leader, of the causes of his own unemployment, and treated the pattern across the answers as the datum: the manifest opinion was the least reliable item in the protocol, and the character structure inferred from the whole was the finding. This interpretive method purchased its insights at a known price. The coding depended on judges trained in the theory the coding was meant to test, so the design courted circularity; the samples were assembled from workplaces and organisations rather than drawn from populations, so no totals could be projected; and the psychoanalytic vocabulary in which the types were described made the results untranslatable, for many readers, into the plainer currencies of political science. The school knew the prices and paid them deliberately, because the alternative instrument, the honest count of manifest opinions, had demonstrated its own inadequacy in the most painful available way: the manifest opinions of the German working class in 1930 described a republic's electorate, while the characters underneath them described the material of a dictatorship. Method in the social sciences is a wager about where the truth of a society is stored, and the institute wagered, consistently and across five decades of instruments, that it was stored below the surface of what people say, in structures that interpretation, disciplined by as much counting as the object allowed, could bring into the open. The wager's winnings are the findings this guide has catalogued; its losses are the methodological criticisms recorded alongside them; and its coherence across questionnaires, interviews, group discussions, and content analyses is the strongest single evidence that the empirical programme was a programme, with a philosophy of method, and not a sequence of commissions opportunistically accepted.

## The institute's hidden half

A roster drawn from title pages understates the institute's population, because the school's work was carried in part by scholars whose names the period's conventions kept off the spines. Gretel Adorno, trained as a chemist and married to Theodor in 1937, took the dictation of the enlightenment book and of the aphorisms, argued drafts line by line in a collaboration her husband's dedications and letters acknowledge as constitutive, and managed the household in Pacific Palisades that served as the institute's western salon and informal archive; the philosophy's most famous sentences passed through her shorthand before they reached a printer. Hilde Weiss, a sociologist of the institute's early staff, conducted field research for the workers' survey and published studies of the labour movement's sociology under the Grünberg programme, part of the small company of women who held research posts in Weimar social science. Else Frenkel-Brunswik, a psychologist formed in Vienna's great institute of the Bühlers, brought to the Berkeley collaboration the clinical instruments and the interviewing discipline that gave the authoritarian personality its psychological depth, and her subsequent career at Berkeley made her one of the study's principal scientific custodians. Marie Jahoda, whose Viennese study of the unemployed of Marienthal remains a monument of the period's empirical craft, moved in the same exile research world and later subjected the prejudice studies to the professional scrutiny of a fellow practitioner, criticism from inside the craft that the school respected accordingly. The guide names this hidden half not as a gesture but as a correction of the same kind it has practised throughout: the school's legend is a story of a few men's minds, while its record is the work of an institution, and institutions are staffed by more people than their legends remember.

## The other totalitarianism: the school on the Soviet Union

A school's independence is proved by its judgements of its own side's orthodoxies, and the Frankfurt record on the Soviet Union is the exhibit its detractors least often open. Pollock's early reports on the Soviet planned economy, written from travel and study at the end of the nineteen twenties, combined professional respect for the experiment's scale with a banker's eye for its costs, and his mature thesis placed the Soviet order and the fascist order under one comparative concept, state capitalism, not to equate their crimes but to identify a shared structural possibility: the replacement of market coordination by political command, with the party state as the economy's board of directors. Horkheimer's authoritarian state manuscript drew the conclusion in its harshest form, suggesting that the integral authoritarian state was the period's emerging form across the ideological flags, and that whoever wished to speak of fascism's roots while refusing to speak of capitalism's transformation was protecting a phrase. Marcuse, the member with the deepest Russian formation, devoted a book of the nineteen fifties to Soviet Marxism itself, analysing the doctrine as an ideology of rule, in which Marxist categories were preserved as liturgy while their critical content was administered away, and arguing that the Soviet system's legitimation needs drove its theory exactly as the school's method would predict for any established order. Wittfogel, the Grünberg era's sinologist, carried the logic to a personal extreme the others did not follow: a former communist who had been imprisoned by the Nazis, he broke utterly with the movement and ended as a witness against former associates in the American security hearings, a trajectory the institute observed without imitation. The collective record refutes the two standard slanders simultaneously. The school was not a conduit of Moscow's influence, since it theorised the Soviet order as a form of the domination it opposed and said so in print throughout the Cold War; and it was not a asset of the opposing camp either, since its Cold War writings criticised the West's administered society in the same vocabulary and refused the anti-communist crusade's demand for loyalty performances. It kept, at real cost in both directions, the position its statutes had promised Felix Weil: answerable to its evidence.

## The enlightenment book: composition, neglect, and canonisation

The Dialectic of Enlightenment deserves a second pass in this guide, beyond its California composition, because its publication history is the school's reception in miniature. Issued by an exile publishing house in Amsterdam in 1947, in German, by two authors whose institute had just resettled in a country that was not listening, the book sank almost without trace: reviews were few, the German public of the reconstruction years had no appetite for a philosophy that blamed enlightenment itself for the catastrophe, and the American academy that had hosted its authors knew the institute for its studies, not for a speculative treatise it could not read. The text survived the nineteen fifties as a citation in the footnotes of the faithful. Its canonisation arrived with the student generation of the nineteen sixties, who, finding the official edition unavailable, circulated an unauthorised reprint among themselves until the publishers regularised the situation with a reissue at the decade's end; the book thus reached its mass readership as samizdat from below, twenty years after its composition, in a country whose students were using it against the restored order its authors had returned to serve. The argument they found in it, that Odysseus at the mast is the bourgeois individual's founding portrait, that the systematising reason of Kant and the systematised cruelty of Sade are brothers, that anti-semitism is enlightenment's false projection returning as paranoia, is expounded in this series in the reading guide devoted to the book, [dialectic of enlightenment: a guide](/2015/07/01/dialectic-of-enlightenment-guide/). What belongs to this school history is the lesson the trajectory teaches about the whole tradition: the Frankfurt School's influence ran on a delay, its texts arriving when the societies that produced them had changed enough to need them, and any account that dates its importance by its composition years misunderstands the mechanism of its survival, which was institutional patience, the endowment, the collected editions, the students, converting a refugee literature into a permanent possession of the universities.

## Working through the past: the school's German pedagogy

The returned institute's most consequential public work was pedagogical, and its theme was the past its host society was organised to forget. The group experiment had established the finding: a population that recited democratic formulae while privately defending, minimising, or resenting the reckoning with the dictatorship, its guilt converted into grievance against those who mentioned it. Adorno gave the condition its enduring name in a lecture of 1959, the meaning of working through the past, arguing that the past in question had not been worked through but merely repressed, that the survival of the dictatorship's dispositions within the democracy was a greater danger than its survival in organised movements outside it, and that the standard defence, the drawing of a line under the account, was itself a symptom of the structure the prejudice studies had measured. His radio essay on education after Auschwitz drew the practical consequence with unmatched starkness: the premier demand upon all education is that the catastrophe not repeat itself, a demand so prior to every curriculum argument that pedagogy must be rebuilt from it, toward a childhood that does not manufacture the coldness, the hardness, and the indifference to others' suffering that the authoritarian character research had identified as the soil. These interventions were not sermons appended to a research career; they were the research, translated by its senior author into the public media of the society whose questionnaires he had been reading since 1929, and they made the institute, for a decade, the Federal Republic's unofficial ministry of memory. The Auschwitz trial staged in Frankfurt itself in the middle nineteen sixties, in the institute's own city and partly in its moral climate, belongs to the same history: the school did not conduct the prosecution, but the public language in which the trial could be heard as a national examination rather than a victors' ritual was a language the institute had done more than any other body to create.

## The authoritarian personality: the findings behind the scale

Because the F scale's fame has consumed its parent study, the findings themselves deserve restatement, for they are stranger and more durable than the instrument. The Berkeley team's high scorers did not present as political extremists with opinions to match; many held no pronounced ideology at all. What cohered was a way of organising experience: a moral world divided into the clean and the dirty, the strong and the weak; an imagination populated by conspiracies and by fantasies of punishment; a tenderness toward the powerful and a contempt, often cheerful, for whoever could be safely despised; and, beneath a professed conventionality, a projective preoccupation with the sexual and aggressive lives of others. The low scorers, the study's genuine liberals, were not their mirror image but a different organisation: tolerant of ambiguity, capable of inspecting their own motives without panic, erotic and aggressive energies acknowledged rather than projected, and a politics that followed from an ego strong enough to afford reality. The clinical interviews supplied the developmental claim that joined the study to the Frankfurt programme: the high scorer's childhood had typically been governed by a harsh, status anxious discipline in which the parents' authority was worshipped and resented at once, and the resentment, forbidden its natural object, was discharged downward onto outgroups throughout adult life. The family, in short, had manufactured the fascist potential in the nursery, exactly as the authority and family volume had hypothesised from German data fourteen years before, and the Berkeley study's design, scales above interviews above projective tests, was built to exhibit the mechanism at three depths at once. Later science revised the instruments, narrowed the syndrome, and relocated authoritarianism on one side of the political spectrum rather than in personality as such, findings the guide records as the programme's normal fate. The core conjecture, that susceptibility to anti-democratic appeal is formed early, structured deeply, and measurable in persons who believe themselves moderates, survived its critics and remains the study's bequest to every later researcher who has had to explain why propaganda finds the hearers it needs.

## The school's jurists: law under the emergency

Two of the institute's members were lawyers, and their work gave the school a doctrine of law that its philosophical reputation has entirely eclipsed. Neumann, formed in the Weimar labour movement's legal battles, theorised the rule of law as a historical achievement with a material base: the general law, abstract and predictable, had served the competitive capitalism that needed calculable conditions, and monopoly's rise dissolved it into discretionary measures, decrees, and standards whose generality was a façade over command. His studies of the changing function of law, written in English exile, supplied the legal skeleton for Behemoth's institutional anatomy. Kirchheimer, who had begun as a brilliant and alarming student of Carl Schmitt before breaking with his teacher's decisionism, devoted his maturity to the anatomy of political justice: the ways legal procedure, with its forms scrupulously observed, is conscripted to eliminate enemies, legitimate confiscations, and stage the morality plays of regimes. His great book on the subject ranged from the trial of Socrates through the Moscow trials to the procedures of the Federal Republic's early years, and its cold conclusion, that the court's robe has covered every kind of political surgery and that the difference between justice and its political use lies in institutional conditions rather than in forms, is the school's characteristic move applied to law: the form is real, and the form is not enough. Both jurists served the American war and occupation administrations as analysts, and both carried the wartime experience back into scholarship, Neumann dying in a road accident in Switzerland in 1954 with his theory of anxiety and politics unfinished, Kirchheimer teaching in New York until 1965. A reader who meets the school only through its philosophers has missed its most practically consequential faculty: the two men who explained, from inside the century's worst legal catastrophes, how law bends, when it holds, and what its bending costs.

## Theology's shadow: the school and religion

No guide to this school is complete without the thread its secular legend suppresses: the persistent pressure of theology on its materialism. Benjamin's theses on the philosophy of history, written in 1940 in flight and entrusted to friends who carried them across the Atlantic, open with the image of a chess playing automaton whose victories are secretly worked by a hunchbacked dwarf hidden inside it, theology, which the author remarks must keep out of sight while doing the work of historical materialism; the theses' angel of history, blown backward into the future by the storm of progress while the wreckage piles before him, has become the exile generation's most quoted self portrait. Horkheimer's materialism always carried a Schopenhauerian deposit of pity for suffering creatures, and his late work spoke without embarrassment of the longing for the wholly other, for a justice that the world's arrangements cannot deliver and that the thought of God preserved, in negative form, against the administrator's certainty that what exists is all. Adorno formalised the impulse as an inverted theology: metaphysics, after the catastrophe, survives as the discipline of contemplating things from the standpoint of their redemption while knowing the standpoint is unavailable, and the old prohibition on images of God became in his hands a prohibition on picturing the reconciled society, which critical theory had observed from its founding. These are not conversions, and the members would have resisted the label of believers as firmly as that of propagandists. They are the record of a materialism that refused to let the administered world have the last word about the meaning of suffering, and borrowed theology's grammar for the refusal while declining its consolations. The thread explains the school's enduring appeal to religious readers, its equal suspicion among orthodox materialists, and the peculiar tone, mourning conducted as analysis, that no summary of its doctrines ever quite reproduces.

## The director's formation: Horkheimer beneath the programme

The programme had a philosophy before it had a staff, and recovering the director's intellectual formation explains choices the institutional history alone cannot. Horkheimer came to academic philosophy by an unusual road: a manufacturer's son who left school for the factory office, served in the war, and arrived at university with a novelist's ambitions and a settled pessimism learned early from Schopenhauer, whose doctrine that the world's suffering is structural and its consolations fraudulent never left his thought. His doctoral and habilitation works treated Kant's third critique and the beginnings of the bourgeois philosophy of history, the moment when the rising class first narrated its own ascent as reason's biography, and the materialist essays of the early nineteen thirties, on morality, on metaphysics, on egoism and the movement for emancipation, worked out the stance the institute would institutionalise: that moral outrage is a material force with a history, that metaphysical systems are the dreams of social orders, and that a materialism worthy of the name must account for the suffering that idealism at least had the decency to mention. The famous aphorisms he composed in the nineteen thirties, published under the title of dawn and decline, compress the formation into sentences: the young director as moralist of a collapsing class world, before the director as administrator absorbed him. Understanding this layer corrects a persistent misreading of the school's later darkness. The pessimism was not an exile's reaction improvised in California; it was the founder's native metaphysics, present in his earliest notebooks, which the institute's findings then confirmed to him at every stage, and which his colleagues variously shared, moderated, or, in Marcuse's case, spent a career resisting. Schools are arguments among temperaments as well as among theses, and Frankfurt's central argument, between the director's Schopenhauerian floor and his staff's recurrent hopes, is the one its internal history keeps re-enacting.

## Generations: what the heirs rebuilt

The school's survival past its founders was not automatic, and its mechanism was academic reproduction: the returned institute trained doctoral students in the nineteen fifties and sixties who carried its questions into the expanding universities of the Federal Republic and beyond, revising as they went. The pivotal figure of the second generation is Habermas, whose trajectory this guide has already touched at the point of friction. His early book on the structural transformation of the public sphere reconstructed, with a historian's patience rare in the school's first generation, the rise and deformation of the bourgeois public sphere of coffee houses, press, and parliament, converting the elders' lament for administered culture into a researchable institutional history with a normative standard, the public use of reason, built into its design. His subsequent programme replaced the philosophy of history with a theory of communication: where the first generation had located reason's distortion in the exchange relation and its cultural machinery, he located undistorted communication as the counterfactual standard inside ordinary language itself, and rebuilt critical theory as the theory of communicative action, with law and democracy, rather than the proletariat or the refusal of the whole, as emancipation's carriers. The revision was conducted in filial polemic, preserving the school's founding intent, the self-clarification of a society capable of judging itself, while discarding the framework of production and the pessimism that had paralysed it. Around him the generation diversified: Alfred Schmidt, Horkheimer's student and the institute's historian, gave the tradition its scholarly conscience in works on the concept of nature in Marx and on the school's own history; Oskar Negt carried its pedagogy into workers' education; Claus Offe analysed the welfare state's contradictions in its own administrative idiom; Albrecht Wellmer pressed its aesthetics and its theory of modernity. A later generation again, formed after the founders' deaths, restated the theory around recognition, the struggle of persons and groups for the social confirmation of their identity, and its leading figure directed the institute itself through the turn of the century, joining the school's administration to its third reformulation. The pattern across three generations is the one the brief for this guide asks readers to see: continuity of the problem, the self-opacity of a society that injures its members and calls the injury nature, under repeated reconstruction of everything else, method, agent, and hope included. An institution that can be inherited by its critics has solved the problem of survival that its founders' endowment alone could not.

## The name, the editions, and the road to posterity

It is a useful shock to learn that the school's members did not call themselves the Frankfurt School. The label circulated first among outsiders and journalists and hardened into common usage only in the nineteen sixties and seventies, as the returned institute's public fame and the translation of its books created a readership that needed a shelf mark. The members' own names for their enterprise were institutional and methodological, the institute, the materialist theory, critical theory, and the retrospective baptism had consequences this guide has been resisting throughout: a school sounds like a doctrine with professors, where an institute sounds like what it was, an employer with a library and a payroll, and the doctrinal reading of the school's history begins in its name. The textual road to posterity ran through editions whose history a researcher must know. The postwar Frankfurt publisher issued the staff's writings in volumes that mixed periods without apparatus; the collected editions of Horkheimer's and Adorno's works, prepared with philological care from the archive in later decades, restored contexts, drafts, and the correspondence in which the arguments actually occurred; and the English speaking world's school was assembled by a translation movement of the nineteen seventies, when publishing houses in New York and London issued the exile works in uniform editions and a generation of American and British students met, within a few years, books written across four decades as if they were a simultaneous system. The first great narrative history of the school, written by an American scholar with the founders' cooperation and published in 1973, fixed the English language story's shape for a generation, and the later institutional histories, above all the exhaustive German account published in the nineteen eighties, thickened it with the budgets, personnel files, and quarrels that the narrative had smoothed. A researcher entering the field now inherits a school that exists twice, once in the archive and once in the legend, and the discipline of checking the second against the first is exactly the empirical correction practised as scholarship.

One corner of the legend requires routing rather than treatment here. The phrase cultural Marxism, under which the school is denounced in polemical literature as the author of a coordinated campaign against Western institutions, has a documentary history of its own, with usages that must be separated before they can be judged, and that history belongs to its own article in this series, [cultural marxism: the history of a term](/2015/10/01/cultural-marxism-term-history/). What this guide's record contributes to that separate enquiry can be stated in one sentence: an institution whose members publicly disagreed about the state, Freud, literature, and the prospects of reason itself, whose finances and publications were matters of public record on three continents, and whose director spent his exile analysing his hosts' prejudices with his hosts' money, is a poor candidate for the coordinated secret project the polemic requires, and the demonstration is the preceding twenty sections rather than any verdict pronounced here.

## Reading the school: the books in their order

A complete guide owes its readers a road through the shelf, given here as prose because the order is an argument. Begin with the 1937 essay on traditional and critical theory, the school's methodological charter, short enough to read entire and dense enough to reread for years; everything else in the tradition is commentary on its distinction. Proceed to the works of the exile in the order of their evidence: the authority and family volume's theoretical sections for the programme in action, Neumann's Behemoth for the school's institutional intelligence at its peak, and the prejudice series' central volume for the empirical programme's monument and its methods exposed to view. Only then take up the Dialectic of Enlightenment, which reads very differently to a reader who knows what its authors had measured: not as a retreat from the studies but as their philosophical balance sheet, drawn in the darkest year of the century by men whose questionnaires had already told them how the story was going. Adorno's aphoristic book of the same years belongs beside it as the same intelligence in its intimate register. For the second generation, Habermas's history of the public sphere offers the most accessible door, a work of scholarship whose critical standard emerges from its narrative rather than descending on it, and his later systematic books can be approached once the standard is familiar. Marcuse presents a special case and a standing warning against the school's abridgements: the Marcuse of the slogan is the author of one famous book read in excerpts, while the Marcuse of the record wrote a Hegel study, a defence of Freud's metapsychology, and a Sovietology, and is treated whole in his own article in this series at [marcuse and one-dimensional man](/2015/08/15/marcuse-one-dimensional-man-guide/). The reader who follows this order will notice the guide's thesis confirming itself in the experience: the philosophy darkens exactly as the evidence accumulates, and the legend of a school that fled the world for culture cannot survive contact with the shelf in sequence.

## The library, the archive, and the seizure

The institute's material memory suffered the fate its founders had insured the money against. The great library assembled in the Frankfurt years, tens of thousands of volumes on the labour movement, political economy, and the social sciences, among the finest working collections of its kind in Europe, was seized with the building in 1933 and dispersed into the regime's own institutions and the general pillage of Jewish and Marxist property, so that the returning institute of the nineteen fifties began its German life, as it had begun its American one, by rebuilding a library from catalogues and memory. Part of the seized stock was recovered from the depots where the Allies gathered the regime's loot, in the vast sorting operation that returned books to their owners across a ruined continent, and the institute's shelves still hold volumes bearing the stamps of both the institute and its thieves. The archive of manuscripts fared better because the men carried it: correspondence, drafts, the mimeographed first version of the enlightenment book, Benjamin's theses rescued by couriers, the protocols of the workers' survey sleeping in boxes until scholars of a later generation published them. The school's documentary instinct, installed by Grünberg's archival programme at the founding, thus survived even the destruction of the archive, embodied in a staff habit of keeping everything, and the critical editions and institutional histories that rebuilt the school's memory in the nineteen seventies and eighties are that habit's harvest. There is a moral in the sequence for readers of this guide's central claim. A tradition's evidence can be burned, seized, or scattered and still be recovered if an institution survives to remember what it held; the Frankfurt School's legend flourished in the decades when its archive was least accessible, and the correction of the legend began, in the scholarship itself, exactly when the boxes were opened.

## The journal's first volumes: the programme in print

The Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung repays reading as a single work, because its first volumes contain the programme's full range before exile scattered the contributors into specialisations. The opening volume of 1932 carried Fromm's statement of analytic social psychology's method and function, the manifesto of the institute's psychological turn, in which the libidinal structure of a society is proposed as the cement between its economy and its ideology; Horkheimer's essays on materialism and on the poverty of contemporary philosophy; Pollock's report on the crisis and the prospects of planned economy; and the first instalments of the authority research. Marcuse's review essays and his long study of liberalism's transformation, arguing that the authoritarian state grew from liberalism's own premises when monopoly replaced competition, appeared as the German catastrophe unfolded, so that the journal's pages perform the institute's education in real time: a research programme designed for a decaying republic finding its object replaced by a dictatorship. The Paris volumes, printed by a French academic publisher while the staff regrouped, carried the authority and family studies to completion and Benjamin's Baudelaire work, and the English language successor, retitled Studies in Philosophy and Social Science, printed Horkheimer's essay on the Jews and Europe and the first versions of arguments the California book would gather. Journals are usually the small change of scholarship. This one was the school's central bank for a decade, the place where its members' separate competences were converted into a common currency, and its nine volumes, German, French printed, and American, are the most complete single record of what interdisciplinary materialism meant in practice before the phrase became a slogan in other people's mouths.

## Guests who studied their hosts

The school's American years pose a question of intellectual manners that its record answers better than its legend does: what did the refugees make of the country that sheltered them, and what did they do with the making? The simple stories fail in both directions. The exiles were not grateful guests who praised the host and held their peace: the prejudice studies arraigned American society's deepest structure with American money, the radio research arraigned its culture industry from inside its own foundation project, and the enlightenment book, composed in a Los Angeles suburb, placed the republic's entertainments in a genealogy with fascism's spectacles that American readers have never quite forgiven. Neither were they the contemptuous Europeans of the counter legend, sneering at a civilisation they had fled to for safety: Horkheimer's lectures on the eclipse of reason, delivered at Columbia in the middle war years, treated American pragmatism and positivism with a philosophical seriousness he granted few European schools, and argued that the crisis of reason he diagnosed was the common crisis of the West, Germany's barbarism being its most advanced symptom rather than its exception. Adorno's American writings register a genuine double vision, the analyst of the astrology column who also wrote, in his memoir of the exile years, that the emigrant's life had taught him gratitude for the plain decencies of the host society alongside his refusal of its culture's claims. The institute's wartime service, its members' memoranda for the agencies, its studies executed with American collaborators who became lifelong colleagues, all belong to the same record of a working relationship, critical and reciprocal. The school's mature judgement on America was the one its method required: that the freest society of its time was also an administered one, that its citizens' prejudices were measurable in its own instruments, and that saying so, in print, with evidence, was the form its gratitude took. Hosts and guests have rarely understood each other so exactly.

## The American afterlife: Marcuse and the new left

The school's strangest second career belongs to the member who stayed. Marcuse's path ran from the wartime agencies through a decade of State Department analysis to university posts at Brandeis and then at the University of California's new San Diego campus, and along it he composed the books that made the Frankfurt name a household word in a country that had never heard of the institute: the defence of Freud's metapsychology against the revisionists, written partly in polemic with the departed Fromm; the anatomy of one-dimensional society, which argued that advanced industrial civilisation had absorbed its opposition by delivering the goods; and the essays in which he weighed the ethics of tolerance under conditions of administered opinion. When the student movements of the nineteen sixties searched the tradition for a theorist of their refusal, they found the septuagenarian in La Jolla, and the resulting alliance, lecture tours, interviews, honorary kinship with rebels on three continents, made him the school's public face in its American afterlife and its most controversial one. The state of California's governor and the university's regents attempted to end his appointment, veterans' organisations campaigned against him, and threats on his life briefly drove him from his house under guard, while his Frankfurt contemporaries watched the fame with a reserve that was also a disagreement: Adorno, facing his own students in the same years, declined the role Marcuse accepted, and their correspondence across the Atlantic in 1969, each man reporting his university's disorders to the other, is the school's internal argument about practice conducted at the moment of its greatest public consequence. Marcuse died in 1979, on a visit to the Germany he had left forty six years before, having outlived the movement that adopted him and the colleagues who had doubted the adoption. His afterlife inside the school's story is the exception that illuminates the rule: the one member who willed the agent into being by an act of theoretical generosity, and whose reward was to be read, ever since, in a separation from the institution that had formed him and the colleagues whose caution defined its centre.

## Analysis after Fromm: the Frankfurt settlement with Freud

The school's psychoanalytic commitment survived its quarrel with Fromm and took institutional form in the returned city. Adorno's postwar essays restated the orthodox position with a sociologist's twist: in his account the weakening of the father's authority within the changing family did not liberate the ego, as the revisionists hoped, but delivered the child defenceless to the collective agencies, school, gang, screen, that now formed character directly, so that psychoanalysis's tragic anthropology became more accurate as its therapeutic optimism faded. In 1960 Frankfurt acquired a psychoanalytic institute bearing Freud's name, founded by Alexander Mitscherlich, a physician whose wartime documentation of medical crimes had made him the conscience of German medicine and whose work moved in the school's orbit and under its influence; his and his wife Margarete's study of the inability to mourn, published in 1967, applied the analytic instrument to the whole Federal Republic, arguing that the Germans' manic reconstruction had been a defence against mourning for the lost object of their collective love, and it carried the institute's postwar theme, the unmastered past, into the analytic vocabulary the school had kept in repair since Fromm's departure. The settlement's shape is now visible: the institute no longer employed analysts after the break, yet its theory continued to presuppose the analytic account of character, its German pedagogy drew analytic categories into public argument, and its city's analytic institute flourished in intellectual alliance. What Fromm's excommunication had decided was not whether psychoanalysis belonged to the programme, but which psychoanalysis: the unreconciled Freud of the drives, whose pessimism matched the director's own, against every humanist revision that promised adjustment. On that question the school never wavered again, and its consistency, purchased at the price of its best empirical psychologist, is among the clearest demonstrations that its unity, where unity existed, was a unity of premises defended at cost rather than of conclusions imposed by a centre.

## Exile's republic: the school among the emigration

Southern California in the war years held a density of exiled German genius unmatched since the Weimar republic itself, and the institute's directors moved in its centre. Thomas Mann, the emigration's uncrowned head, settled in Pacific Palisades streets away from Horkheimer, and his great novel of the German catastrophe was composed with the school's direct assistance: Adorno supplied the musical theory for the fictional composer Leverkühn, demonstrating at the piano the twelve tone constructions and the aesthetics of late Beethoven that the novel's narrator records, and Mann's published account of the novel's genesis acknowledged the collaboration with a candour that startled readers who thought the philosopher a mere acquaintance. Hanns Eisler, Brecht's composer and the exile's most embattled musician, co-authored with Adorno the period's penetrating study of film music, a collaboration so politically radioactive in the gathering Cold War that the book appeared under Adorno's name alone, its co-author restored only in later editions. Brecht himself, the emigration's other pole, observed the institute's philosophers from a wary distance, his diaries recording an admiration for their intelligence alloyed with suspicion of their endowment and their distance from the movement, a suspicion the institute reciprocated toward his party discipline. Arnold Schoenberg, Adorno's revered and resented master, lived across the same hills, quarrelling with his former publicist's philosophy of the new music even as the philosophy carried his name into the theory of modernism. The colony matters to the school's history because it was the last ecology the founders inhabited as a matter of daily life, a republic of letters in banishment where the argument about Germany's fate was conducted in gardens and at pianos, and because its dissolution, Mann dead in Zurich, Brecht returned to Berlin, Eisler expelled to Vienna and then to the East, Adorno and Horkheimer repatriated to Frankfurt, scattered the school's world a second time, leaving its members to conduct their remaining decades in the diminished company of universities.

## Nature, domination, and the school's ecological afterlife

Running beneath the political argument is a second theme that posterity has promoted from undertone to headline: the domination of nature. The enlightenment book's deepest claim was that the reason which liberates humanity from fear of nature proceeds by nature's subjugation, outer nature reduced to raw material and inner nature, the individual's own impulses, reduced to a workforce under the ego's management, and that the bill for the double conquest is presented in the administered world's characteristic damages: the mutilated environment and the mutilated character are one process read at two scales. Alfred Schmidt, the founders' student and the school's historian, gave the theme its scholarly foundation in his study of the concept of nature in Marx, which recovered from the early manuscripts and the mature critique alike a materialism in which nature is neither a warehouse nor a temple but the medium of a metabolism that society can conduct blindly or knowingly. When the ecological movements of the later twentieth century searched the Marxist tradition for resources against the productivism of both Cold War camps, it was to this strand of the Frankfurt inheritance that they most often came, and the school's analyses of the authoritarian character acquired a second readership among those who connected the domination of women, of colonised peoples, and of the natural world as expressions of a single structure of mastery. The guide records the afterlife without adjudicating it, because the school's own texts sustain both the ecological reading and the objection that a philosophy which treated nature's mastery as reason's fate leaves the ecological critic as agentless as the revolutionary. What cannot be denied is the pattern's recurrence: a theme developed inside the institute's programme, in dialogue with its economics and its psychology, outlived the programme's other elements to become the school's contribution to a movement its founders never addressed, which is how the ideas of institutions escape the intentions of their authors and why the archive, rather than the intention, is the historian's authority.

## The second generation as public conscience: the historians' dispute

The returned school's pedagogy found its largest postwar theatre in the middle nineteen eighties, when the Federal Republic's argument about its past broke into the open in the affair remembered as the historians' dispute. A small number of prominent historians had advanced theses softening the singularity of the Nazi crimes, presenting them as reactive imitations of Bolshevik terror and inviting the republic to a more usable national past; the official mood of the moment, with a chancellor and an American president photographed at a military cemetery where SS men lay, seemed to favour the softening. It was Habermas, the institute's heir, who named the operation for what he judged it to be, an apologetic settling of accounts conducted in the feuilletons and the memorial calendar, and his intervention, answered furiously by the historians and defended by their critics, became the republic's defining intellectual quarrel of the decade. The episode belongs in this guide because it displays the school's complete circuit in operation a generation after the founding: a scholar formed in the institute, wielding its central category of an unmastered past and its pedagogy of memory, intervened in the public sphere his own theory had made his object, and the standard he defended, that the republic's identity could only be constituted in the unflinching remembrance of the catastrophe, was recognisably the standard of Adorno's radio talks carried into a new medium and a new Germany. The founders had preserved critique through the years when it had no addressee; the heir found the addressee, the republic's reading public, and the quarrel's outcome, in which the apologetic theses were beaten back in the universities and the press alike, stands as the school's most consequential public victory, won with its own weapons by the generation that had rebuilt its theory.

## The founders' last decades

The ends of the founders' lives distribute themselves across the map their work had drawn. Pollock, the administrator without whom none of it exists, died in 1970 in the Italian town of his retirement, having edited the group experiment, served as the institute's deputy director, and watched the endowment he had preserved through three countries become the foundation of a German institution again. Horkheimer survived him by three years in Swiss retirement at Montagnola, receiving visitors, conducting the dialogues on religion and politics that his late interviews record, and insisting to the end, against disciples who wanted a doctrine, that the theory he had named was a stance and not a system. Adorno's death in August 1969, on a Swiss holiday weeks after the worst semester of his life, left the aesthetic theory a torso and the lecture hall a legend; the university named its square and its institute rooms for him in the decades of commemoration that followed, an institutional piety he would have annotated without mercy. Fromm died in 1980 in Switzerland, the most widely read of them all and the least read within the school's own seminars, his books a permanent exhibit of the path the institute declined. Löwenthal taught on in California into a honoured old age, the journal's editor become the tradition's living memory for the American scholars who rebuilt its history. Neumann and Kirchheimer were long dead, their legal scholarship quietly absorbed into the disciplines that rarely cite its source. The founding generation's dispersal in death repeats its dispersal in life, across the same countries, and the institution in Frankfurt, which outlived every one of them, remains the only member of the school that never left, never broke, and never published a word: a building, an endowment, an archive, and a library, the material substrate on which a century of argument about domination and enlightenment was, and continues to be, inscribed.

## Art's brief: autonomy against commitment

The school's aesthetics fought a war on two fronts that this guide must record, because the position it produced is the one by which the school's cultural theory is usually, and wrongly, summarised. Against the administered culture industry, whose anatomy belongs to its own article, the school defended the autonomous artwork: the difficult music of the Schoenberg school, the prose of Beckett and Kafka, works whose refusal of easy consumption preserved, in their very hermeticism, the image of a satisfaction not organised by the market. Against the rival doctrine of committed art, preached by Sartre and practised under party direction in the East, Adorno's essay on commitment argued that the work which enlists in a cause betrays both the cause and itself, degenerating into the propaganda it opposes, while the autonomous work, precisely by refusing messages, testifies against the order that makes messages necessary; Kafka's prose and Beckett's stage worlds, he wrote, had the effect on readers that committed literature aimed at and missed, because they presented the administered world's truth without administering it. The position scandalised both camps symmetrically and still does. It is, however, the exact aesthetic corollary of the school's politics: in a period when every collective instrument had been absorbed, the work of art that kept faith with its own material was the last unadministered territory, and its difficulty was not snobbery but the price of the territory's defence. Readers should test the corollary against the school's empirical cultural research before judging it, since the radio project's data on what broadcasting did to listening are the premise of the aesthetics, and the aesthetics without the data is only the legend again.

## The questions the school left open

A guide that has defended its subject against legends owes the reader a closing inventory of the genuine difficulties, the points at which the school's own standards turn against its constructions. Four stand out in the record this guide has assembled. The agent problem is the first and the least answerable: a theory of emancipation that could certify no emancipator preserved its honesty at the price of its function, and the second generation's solution, relocating the emancipatory interest in communication itself and its institutionalisation in law and democracy, purchases an agent at the price of the first generation's radicalism, a trade the tradition continues to renegotiate. The second difficulty is the standard of the critique itself: if reason is as deeply implicated in domination as the enlightenment book argues, the reason conducting the argument stands on the ground it condemns, a paradox Adorno embraced as the method of negative dialectics and his critics cite as the theory's elegant suicide; the school's answer, that critique proceeds immanently, by a society's own promises, is a procedure rather than a refutation, and the procedure's sufficiency remains contested by philosophers who share the school's aims. The third is empirical in the guide's favoured sense: the authoritarian character construct, the school's most influential single hypothesis, has been revised by later research in directions its authors would partly have resisted, its distribution across the political spectrum proving asymmetric in ways the original theory did not predict, and the programme's honour consists in having made claims definite enough for successors to correct. The fourth is the question of the school's own blind spots, which its method obliges the reader to hunt: an institute that studied everyone's ideology was slower to examine some of its own, its near total maleness in the founding generation, its Eurocentric archive, its distance from the colonial world whose thinkers were conducting parallel enquiries it never read. That the hunt can be conducted with the school's own instruments, immanent critique, the interpreted document, the suspicion of the given, is the tradition's final defence of itself: it bequeathed the tools of its own correction. The school's history, told whole, is therefore neither the hagiography of its admirers nor the exposé of its enemies. It is the record of an endowed institute in Frankfurt that asked why human beings submit to orders that injure them, pursued the question with every instrument its century offered, followed its evidence into conclusions its members quarrelled over and sometimes feared, and left the question, better armed, to whoever reads the record next.

## The city that built it: Frankfurt between the wars

The institute's character owes more to its city than its legend allows. Frankfurt was the German city of citizen endowment: its university itself, founded in 1914 on the eve of the war, was a foundation university financed by the city's merchants and bankers rather than by a prince or a state, an anomaly in German higher education that made it the natural home for Weil's similarly endowed institute. The city's wealth was disproportionately Jewish in origin, in banking, metals, chemicals, and the press, and its civic culture expressed itself in foundations: libraries, museums, the great Rothschild free library, institutes of every science. The Weimar municipality added the period's most ambitious municipal modernism, the housing settlements and public buildings of the New Frankfurt programme, so that the institute's first home stood in a city visibly rebuilding itself on rational and social principles. The institute's own building, erected on land beside the university's Senckenberg grounds to designs by Franz Roeckle and opened at the end of the nineteen twenties, embodied the settlement in stone: seminar rooms, a reading room, and stack space for the great collection, in the sober modern idiom the city had made its trademark. The milieu explains the institute's peculiar combination, unavailable in Berlin's party saturated atmosphere or Heidelberg's mandarin reserve: bourgeois money without bourgeois deference, academic standing without state dependence, and a surrounding culture of foundations that treated an endowed Marxist institute as one civic ornament among others. When the milieu was destroyed, first by the regime that seized the building for its own racial research apparatus and then by the bombs that gutted the district, the institute's homelessness acquired its full meaning: it had not merely lost a country but a civic form, the endowed republic of learning, that existed nowhere else in the same concentration, and the American and postwar German chapters of its history are both, in part, attempts to reconstruct that form under other flags.

## The tradition's own historians: the western Marxism verdict

The most serious scholarly classification of the school came from within its own tradition, and it doubles as the objection section's learned form. In the nineteen seventies the English historian Perry Anderson surveyed the heterodox Marxist theory produced between the wars and after, and grouped the Frankfurt thinkers within a formation he named Western Marxism, defined by its birth in defeat: the failure of revolution in the advanced West after 1918 had divorced theory from any mass practice, driven it into the universities, and bent its energies from economics and politics toward philosophy and aesthetics, toward questions of method and culture posed at increasing altitude above the society they described. The classification was diagnostic rather than abusive, and it contained genuine admiration for the work's quality, but its structure was the hotel objection in scholarly dress: a Marxism of the seminar room, pessimistic because its history had given it nothing to be optimistic about, producing theories of ideology and culture whose very brilliance registered the absence of the movement that should have made them unnecessary. The school's record, as this guide has assembled it, supplies the materials for a fair reply, and the reply should be given because Anderson's book remains the map through which many readers first locate the school. The divorce from practice was real but it was imposed by police and exile rather than chosen in a library, and the institute's wartime memoranda, its studies commissioned by agencies and movements of labour, and its postwar pedagogy in the republic constitute a practice record few academic bodies of the century can match. The migration into aesthetics was real but it was conducted as fieldwork, with the radio project's budgets and the astrology column's coding sheets to show for it. And the pessimism, as the Weimar questionnaires testify, was an empirical finding before it was a philosophy. What survives of the verdict is its framing insight, that the school's theory bears the shape of the defeats that produced it, which the school itself, whose whole method was to read thought as the register of its conditions, would have been the last to deny.

## The second insecurity: exile under investigation

The refugees' American safety had a term limit set by American politics, and the school's members learned in the late nineteen forties that the country which had employed their analysis also kept files on their associations. The composer Hanns Eisler, Adorno's collaborator, was hauled before the House committee on un-American activities, denounced by its members as a cultural commissar, and expelled from the United States in 1948 in one of the era's exemplary proceedings; Brecht testified with a performer's cunning and left the country the following day. The security bureaucracy opened or thickened files on several of the institute's figures, Marcuse's wartime service notwithstanding, and the institute's leadership, which had already practised protective vocabulary in three countries, adjusted once more: public statements were weighed, associations audited, and the school's German future was planned with an American insecurity among its premises. The episode completes the record's symmetry and should be stated plainly, because it disciplines two legends at once. The school was not a protected client of American power, since American power investigated it as it investigated the whole emigration; and the school was not a martyred vanguard either, since its members' wartime service had been genuine, its analyses had been used, and its people survived the inquisition with their posts and their institute intact, which is more than Eisler, deported, or the Hollywood witnesses, imprisoned, could say. What the files and hearings chiefly document is the condition the school's theory had already named: the administered democracies' capacity to suspend their own norms in defence of order, a capacity the institute recorded in its hosts with the same instruments, and the same absence of surprise, that it had applied to the republic that expelled it.

## Two languages: what exile did to the prose

A materialist guide should attend to the material of the work itself, and the school's material was language, twice lost. The founding generation wrote a German saturated with the philosophical tradition's technical vocabulary, in which concepts carried their histories audibly; exile forced the staff into English for the agencies, the journals, and the American studies, and the strain shows productively in the record. Adorno composed his American books largely in German and supervised their translation, remarking in his exile writings that the emigrant's mother tongue becomes a possession to be defended precisely because the new language prices every sentence in a currency of plainness; Horkheimer lectured and published in English with a directness his German aphorisms never attempted, and more than one reader has found the English lectures the clearest door into his thought. The journal's own migration, German to French imprint to English title, tracks the staff's reassembly in print. Translation then became the school's posthumous medium: the English editions of the nineteen seventies rendered the exile German into the uniform prose of American academic publishing, smoothing dialectical constructions into statements and occasionally mistaking the argument in ways the later critical editions and retranslations have had to repair, while concepts like reification, constellation, and the administered world entered English as loanwords carrying their German freight. The point is not antiquarian. A tradition whose theory held that thought is formed by its material conditions was itself formed, sentence by sentence, by the loss of its language and the acquisition of another, and the reader who meets the school in translation is meeting the second formation, which the philological turn in Frankfurt scholarship exists to keep honest against the first.

## What the institute taught

Institutions survive by teaching, and the institute's classrooms deserve their paragraph in a history too often confined to its books. The returned institute ran seminars in which the founders' generation instructed the students who would staff the republic's universities: Adorno's lectures, delivered to halls of several hundred in a university where sociology had to be rebuilt from nothing, introduced his listeners to the discipline as a critical practice, reading the classics against the present and the present against its data, and his published lecture courses preserve the pedagogical voice, patient, digressive, and incapable of a platitude, that formed a generation's intellectual conscience. Horkheimer's rectoral addresses and his seminars on the history of philosophy carried the programme's historical wing. The doctoral students of those years, Schmidt and Habermas among them, were formed by the combination this guide has described throughout: archival discipline, theoretical ambition, and the expectation that a dissertation would be an argument with the whole society and not a report on a corner of it. The student revolt of the late sixties was therefore also an inheritance dispute, conducted by pupils who had learned their standards, among other places, in the institute's own rooms, and the bitterness of 1969 on both sides is unintelligible without the intimacy the teaching had created. A school, in the end, is what its name promised and its legend forgot: not a doctrine but a succession of classrooms, in Frankfurt, in exile's improvised seminars, and in the lecture halls of the republic, where the questions were handed on with the instruction, implicit in every syllabus the institute ever issued, that the questions were to be answered against the evidence and not against the teacher.

## Weber's shadow: rationalisation and the quarrel over value freedom

Every German social theory of the institute's century worked in a shadow cast from Heidelberg, and the school's distinctiveness is partly its way of standing in it. Max Weber had given the tradition its master diagnosis before the institute existed: the rationalisation of the Western world, the disenchantment that empties the cosmos of meaning as formal rationality, calculable and purposeless, organises economy, law, and administration into an iron cage of bondage to mechanism. Lukács, reading Weber through Marx, had converted the diagnosis into the theory of reification, in which the commodity form performs the rationalisation and stamps it on consciousness itself. The Frankfurt programme inherited the whole construction and quarrelled with its founder at the decisive point. Weber's methodology demanded that science hold its values in abeyance, judging only the adequacy of means to ends it declined to judge; the institute's founding claim was that this abstinence was itself a social act with consequences, that a science which will not judge the ends will be used by whoever sets them, and that the cage's bars included the doctrine that described them so neutrally. The quarrel was conducted with respect, and the inheritance was never disowned: Horkheimer's eclipse of reason is Weber's rationalisation driven to its conclusion, the moment when formal reason, having consumed every substantive end, becomes an instrument available to any master, and Adorno's sociology lectures treated Weber as the indispensable predecessor whose limits defined the school's task. The guide marks the relation because so much confusion dissolves in it. The school was not a Marxist sect sealed against the bourgeois classics; it was the heir of German sociology's greatest figure, radicalising his diagnosis with Marx's economics and Freud's psychology, and its quarrel with value freedom is the same quarrel, conducted on methodological ground, that its questionnaires conducted on empirical ground: whether the observer of a damaged society may honourably pretend to be a camera.

## Instrumental reason: the career of a concept

The school's most portable concept deserves its genealogy, since it now circulates in debates its authors never imagined. Instrumental reason names a deformation: reason reduced to the calculation of means, superb at achieving ends and constitutionally silent about whether the ends deserve achieving. Horkheimer's wartime lectures on the eclipse of reason gave the concept its classic statement, distinguishing the objective reason of the older philosophical tradition, which judged orders and lives against a rational whole, from the subjective reason of the administered age, which is a faculty of coordination, classification, and control, as available to the advertiser as to the liberator. The genealogy runs backward through the reification essay and Weber's rationalisation to the enlightenment book's founding scene, Odysseus at the mast, the self preserved by self mutilation, cunning as the first form of the instrumental stance toward a nature that includes the listener's own ears, stopped with wax for the rowers and bound for the master who may hear the song only as a prisoner. It runs forward into the school's every later department: into the culture analysis, where the industry's reason is instrumental reason applied to consciousness; into the prejudice research, where the authoritarian character is instrumental reason's personality, efficient, adapted, and empty of ends beyond advancement and discharge; into the second generation's reconstruction, where Habermas's whole project can be read as an attempt to recover, inside language, the objective reason whose eclipse the founders had mourned, locating in undistorted communication the standard that instrumental reason cannot supply from within itself. A concept with that span, from a Homeric excursus to a theory of democracy, is either a key or a solvent, and the school's critics have charged it with dissolving every distinction it touches, since a reason indicted wholesale can no longer state the indictment. The founders accepted the paradox as their method's price and named the acceptance: the critique of reason by reason, conducted immanently, with no tribunal outside the accused. Whether the price buys insight or vertigo remains, as this guide has said of the school's other wagers, a question the reader must settle against the texts, which is precisely the settlement the concept was designed to force.

## The essay as the school's form

The school's theory of its own writing is contained in a single text that unlocks its shelves, Adorno's meditation on the essay as form, composed in the nineteen fifties as the introduction to a volume of his literary notes. The essay, as he describes it, is the anti-systematic genre: it begins not with first principles but with a cultural object, a phrase, a work, a custom, and it thinks in constellations rather than deductions, allowing the object's own tensions to dictate the order of the argument; it refuses the treatise's fiction that thought proceeds from the simple to the complex in a world that presents itself in neither order; and it preserves, in its heretical disregard for the rules of method, the freedom that the administered sciences had surrendered to their procedures. The description is a self portrait of the whole tradition's practice. The enlightenment book proceeds by excursus, from Odysseus to Kant to the film studio, because its subject, reason's self destruction, is a constellation and not a syllogism. The aphorism books think in fragments because their subject, damaged life, would be falsified by a system's composure. Even the empirical studies obey the essayistic discipline in their interpretive layer, reading a questionnaire protocol as an essayist reads a text, for the structure beneath the statements. Academic posterity, which prefers its theory in treatises, has treated the school's forms as literary indulgence, and the school's answer is written across its methodological essays: in a society whose official knowledge is organised as administration, the form of a thought is part of its content, and the essay's refusal to conclude is not indecision but the record of an object that has not concluded either. Readers who find the school's books difficult are meeting the difficulty the form intends, and the guide's advice is the school's own: read them as essays, in constellations, against the evidence their companions in the archive supply.

## The family thesis and its feminist readers

The authority and family volume's central hypothesis, that the bourgeois family was the factory in which the authoritarian character was assembled, had a second career a generation later in hands its authors did not foresee. The feminist scholarship of the nineteen seventies, reconstructing the patriarchal family's economics and psychology, found in the institute's old study a predecessor that had treated the family neither as nature nor as refuge but as a historical institution with a politics, its internal authority relations legible as the training ground of the state's. The recovery was critical in both senses: the feminist readers took the mechanism and indicted the blindness, noting that the Frankfurt analysis, for all its attention to the father's decline, had little to say about the mother's labour or the daughter's formation, and that its family was the bourgeois household of its questionnaires, generalised past its evidence. The episode belongs to this guide's pattern of afterlives, and it is recorded for the correction it performs on the school's self image. The institute that prided itself on hunting ideology in every given found its own givens, the gendered assumptions of its founding generation, waiting inside its most empirical text, and the discovery was made by readers armed with the institute's own method, the immanent reading that takes a work's claims more seriously than its silences. A research programme is vindicated less by the loyalty of its heirs than by the quality of the corrections they can extract from it, and by that measure the family thesis, amended by the feminists who quarried it, stands among the school's most productive constructions.

## Felix Weil: the founder the legend forgot

Every account of the school names its philosophers, and almost none names the man whose money made it, an omission that falsifies the institution's nature at its root. Felix Weil deserves his section. He was not a passive Maecenas signing cheques for other men's ideas: the Ilmenau work week was his conception, the institute's statutes bore his negotiating hand, and his own scholarship, a doctorate on the practical problems of socialisation and later work on the economic history of his native Argentina, placed him inside the tradition he funded as a minor but genuine participant. His fortune's origin mattered to the design: grain trading profits, made in South America beyond the reach of German inflation and German politics, converted into an endowment deliberately structured so that neither the donor's family firm nor the donor's own later opinions could steer the research. The steering problem solved itself in the hardest way, by distance. Weil removed to California in the nineteen thirties, his relations with Horkheimer cooled into formality as the director's programme diverged from the founder's documentary Marxism, and the institute he had created conducted its most famous decades under the management of his student friend Pollock with the founder an ocean away, reading its journal as a subscriber. He died in 1975 in Dover, Delaware, having outlived the republic, the regime, and most of the men his money had gathered. The guide restores him to the centre of the founding story for a reason beyond justice. The school's independence, its refusal of parties, states, and movements, was not an attitude its members adopted; it was a legal and financial architecture its founder built before the first director was appointed, and every later exercise of that independence, the coded vocabulary, the American agencies, the refusal of both German states' embraces, was spent from the capital, in every sense, that Felix Weil deposited in 1923.

## The school's silences: what the programme did not see

The immanent method this guide has praised obliges it to end the survey with the programme's blind spots, and they are neither few nor trivial. The institute that anatomised domination in the family, the factory, and the culture industry had remarkably little to say about the domination of the colonial world: its archive of oppression is European, its fascism a European pathology, and the great anti-colonial movements of its century enter its texts at the margins, in Marcuse's late hopes for the wretched of the earth rather than in any study the institute designed. Its economics, for all its subtlety about administration, never produced the sustained analysis of the postwar boom's labour process that its own categories invited, and its sociology of women, as the feminist readers showed, lagged its sociology of everything else by a full generation. Its judgement of mass culture, defended above as fieldwork based, hardened in some texts into a refusal to examine what audiences actually did with the products sold to them, a question the institute's own Löwenthal had pioneered and its philosophers sometimes forgot. And its theory of the subject, formed in the consulting rooms of Vienna and Berlin, universalised a bourgeois interiority that its questionnaires occasionally contradicted without the contradiction being theorised. The inventory matters because the school's detractors use the silences to dismiss the findings, and its admirers omit them to preserve the monument. The guide's position follows the school's own rule for handling its heroes: a research programme is honoured by the precision of its failure map, and the Frankfurt failures are instructive precisely because they are the failures of its strengths, the European archive of its empiricism, the clinical formation of its psychology, the generational standpoint of its founders, each silence the shadow of a corresponding sight.

## Between the two German states

The returned institute took its rooms in a divided country, and its conduct toward the other Germany is a small, complete exhibit of the independence its statutes promised. The eastern state's official scholarship classified the school as it classified all unorthodox Marxism: as bourgeois decadence, a revisionism that had traded revolution for cultural complaint, and its publishing houses left the school's central works unprinted for most of the state's existence. The institute returned the compliment analytically rather than polemically: its comparative framework, descending from Pollock, treated the eastern order as an administered society of the state capitalist type, its doctrine as a legitimation formula, and its cultural apparatus as an apparatus, judgements the school's members published without trimming them for the sake of any anti-fascist solidarity the eastern state claimed to inherit. At the same time the institute refused enlistment in the western state's Cold War: Horkheimer declined to convert the school's anti-totalitarianism into a loyalty programme, resisted the demand that criticism of the West be suspended for the duration of the emergency, and kept the institute's German pedagogy aimed at its own republic's unmastered past rather than at the convenient enemy across the border. Readers in the East nonetheless found the texts, as readers under censorship do, and the school's analysis of administered society acquired there the clandestine second readership that its analysis of fascism had acquired in the Reich. The double refusal, of the eastern state's embrace and of the western state's enlistment, cost the school friends in both capitals and is among the strongest practical evidence for the claim this guide made at the outset: the tradition was held together by an institution designed to be unbought, and the design held under the century's most systematic attempts to buy or to ban it.

## Why the legend wins: the mechanics of compression

It remains to explain, in the school's own spirit, why the legend this guide has spent its length correcting is so robust, since an error's persistence is a social fact with causes. The first cause is curricular. A syllabus has hours, the school's corpus has tens of thousands of pages in two languages across five decades, and the compression into four names, two concepts, and a mood is the rational response of every teacher who must fit the tradition into a week; the compressed version then reproduces itself, because the students it forms become the teachers, journalists, and pamphleteers who compress again. The second cause is the name itself: a school invites a doctrine, a doctrine invites a summary, and a summary of a tradition whose essence was internal argument must delete the arguments to exist, leaving the mood as the residue. The third cause is the school's difficulty, which is real and was partly deliberate: texts written against the grain of their century's common sense, in a prose that refuses the reader every shortcut, select for readers willing to simplify them, since simplification is the tribute difficulty collects from posterity. The fourth cause is polemical utility, which this guide has met on both flanks: the legend of pure contemplatives serves the activist's reproach, the legend of coordinated ideologues serves the conspiracy's recruitment, and both legends are cheaper to maintain than the archive is to consult. Against these mechanics the guide sets the only counterweight that has ever worked on this material, the one the institute itself embodied: an institution of memory with the patience to keep the whole record in print, the editions, the correspondence, the protocols, so that any reader, in any decade, can perform the correction performed here. The legend will win most weeks. The archive wins the years, which is the temporal scale on which the school always preferred to be judged.

## The administered world: the school's master image

Adorno's phrase for the school's object, the administered world, condenses the whole research programme into two words, and a guide owes it an unpacking. Administration, in the school's usage, is not bureaucracy in the narrow sense of offices and forms; it is the form of domination characteristic of the developed order, in which human relations are mediated by apparatuses that plan, allocate, measure, and pacify, and in which the apparatus's maintenance becomes the purpose its human materials serve. The concept descends in a straight line from Pollock's economics: as the market's anonymous coordination yields to conscious management, domination loses its anonymity and gains an address, and yet grows harder to resist, because the administered experience their administration as service, their dependence as security, and their integration as participation. The culture industry is the administered world's ministry of consciousness; the authoritarian character is its ideal citizen, adapted, efficient, and incapable of the experience that would indict the whole; and the state's planning organs and the corporation's personnel departments are the same form in different liveries, which is why the school's analyses travelled so disquietingly between the Cold War camps, declined by both. The concept has the strengths and the defects of its span. It names something real that narrower terms, bureaucracy, capitalism, totalitarianism, each miss: the convergence of advanced orders on management as the universal solvent of opposition. It also courts the totalisation its critics charged, since a world wholly administered leaves no standpoint for the diagnosis and no crack for the alternative, a difficulty the school met, as this guide has recorded, not by softening the concept but by treating its totality as a tendency to be fought at the level of the individual experience it failed to digest. The phrase survives in scholarly and journalistic prose long after its coinage, usually thinned into a synonym for red tape, and restoring its weight is part of the correction this guide exists to perform: the administered world was not a complaint about paperwork but the name of a social form, argued from economics, verified in questionnaires, and indicted in philosophy, by an institute that had lived under three of its variants.

## The question of progress

No question divided the school from its parent tradition more deeply, or organised more of its work, than the question whether history progresses. The Marxism of the parties had inherited the nineteenth century's confidence in a strong form: the development of the productive forces guaranteed, through crisis and class struggle, an ascent toward a society without domination, and the theorist's task was to accelerate a birth that history had already scheduled. The Frankfurt record is the record of that confidence's destruction by its century. Benjamin's theses, written as the Wehrmacht entered Paris, gave the destruction its image: the angel of history, face turned to the mounting wreckage of the past, blown backward into a future he cannot see by the storm that others call progress. The enlightenment book supplied the argument beneath the image: enlightenment's progress in mastering nature is simultaneously a regress in the humanity of the masters, so that the graph of civilisation and the graph of barbarism are the same graph read in two inks. Horkheimer's philosophy of history had prepared the position from the beginning, reading the bourgeoisie's own ascent as a narrative told by the victors, and his materialism retained from the wreckage only a negative standard: not the guaranteed ascent but the documented possibility, preserved in the tradition's unfulfilled promises, that things could be otherwise. The second generation rebuilt the question rather than abandoning it. Habermas reconceived progress as a pair of learning processes, in technical knowledge and in moral and legal consciousness, whose advances are real, reversible, and institutional rather than metaphysical, and the reconstruction allowed the school's heir to defend modernity's unfinished project against both the founders' despair and the postmodern ironies that succeeded it. The arc across three generations, from Benjamin's angel through the enlightenment book's dialectic to the theory of learning processes, is the tradition's biography in a single problem, and it explains the school's enduring attraction for readers who can neither believe the old guarantee nor accept that the wreckage is the last word: the school is the place where the European left's faith in progress was lost honestly, in public, with the evidence published, and where its reconstruction was begun on foundations meant to survive the same scrutiny.

## The institute as a form: endowment inside the university

The school's least discussed innovation was organisational, and it may be its most consequential. German scholarship of the period knew two homes for research: the university chair, with its solitary professor, his assistants, and his seminar, dependent on the state's budgets and the faculty's politics; and the independent academy or institute, endowed for a science, on the model the Kaiser Wilhelm Society had built for the natural sciences. Weil's foundation fused the two in a new proportion: an institute with its own capital, building, and staff, formally incorporated into a university, whose director held a university chair and whose researchers taught, while the endowment guaranteed that no ministry could starve the programme and no faculty could vote it away. The form solved, in advance, the problems that destroyed other heterodox enterprises of the Weimar years: the party schools lived and died by their parties, the independent journals by their subscribers, the private scholars by their fortunes. The institute survived its republic, its exile, the investigation of its members, the death of its founder, and the transformation of its host university into a mass institution, because its form distributed its dependencies so that no single patron, state, or generation could terminate it. The postwar Federal Republic, rebuilding its research landscape, generalised the pattern the Frankfurt foundation had pioneered, and the modern German ecology of endowed institutes attached to universities, of which the returned institute became an honoured elder, descends in part from Weil's statutes. The guide insists on the point because it completes the correction of the school's legend at the level where legends are weakest. Ideas do not survive by their truth; they survive by their institutions, and the Frankfurt School's ideas survived the century's most determined efforts to exterminate them because a grain merchant's son, in 1923, understood that a heresy with an endowment, a library, and a university charter is harder to kill than a heresy with a manifesto. The school's theory taught that material conditions decide what thought can do. Its own history is the theory's most elegant confirmation.

## Contract research in the republic: the institute at the factory gate

The returned institute paid its way in part as the Weimar institute never had to, by contract research for the republic's institutions, and the commissions it accepted extend the empirical correction into the postwar decades where the legend assumes pure philosophy reigned. The most instructive was the study of industrial climate undertaken in the middle nineteen fifties inside a great steel concern's works, where the institute's researchers interviewed and surveyed the workforce of a Mannesmann plant about supervision, security, works councils, and the meaning of the job itself. The design was the Frankfurt signature in overalls: manifest answers about satisfaction were read against the structure beneath them, and the report's finding, that the workers' operative relation to the firm was one of resigned distance beneath a surface of reasonable contentment, applied the character and opinion method of the Weimar survey to the economic miracle's own shop floor. Trade unions, public authorities, and broadcasters appear in the institute's postwar accounts as sponsors and clients, and the arrangement cut both ways in the school's reputation: critics of the founders' caution pointed to the client list as the price of the return, while the institute regarded applied work as the programme's founding bargain renewed, the special sciences' commissions purchasing the independence of the theory they served. What the record will not support is the picture of a seminary of pure thought above the republic's practical life. The institute that returned to Frankfurt studied that life at its benches and in its ministries, on contract, with instruments, and published what it found.

## The geography of the endowment

The school's survival had a geography, and tracing it corrects the impression that ideas floated free of jurisdictions. The endowment's legal home from the early nineteen thirties was the Netherlands, where a foundation held the capital beyond the reach of German decree; its working refuge was Switzerland, where the Geneva society gave the scattered staff a corporate shell and where Horkheimer would end his days; its publishing lifeline ran through Amsterdam, where an exile house printed the enlightenment book for a readership that did not yet exist; its salaries were paid for a decade in American dollars from a New York office that Columbia's hospitality sheltered. Each address was a decision, taken by Pollock and the founders under pressure, about which legal order could be trusted with which function of an institution that had already lost one country. The map explains a feature of the school's thought that purely intellectual histories miss: its lifelong attention to institutions as the bearers of ideas, to foundations, statutes, and budgets as the real conditions of intellectual freedom, was learned from the logistics of its own persistence. When the second generation theorised the public sphere and the third the institutional conditions of recognition, they were generalising, among other things, the family knowledge of a school that existed because a foundation in Amsterdam and a society in Geneva had kept its name legally alive through the years when its building flew a swastika and its library was being stamped into other men's collections.

## The school in the republic of letters: the feuilleton years

The returned school's widest classroom was not a lecture hall but the cultural pages of the Federal Republic's newspapers and the lists of its paperback publishers, and its occupation of that territory was a historical novelty with consequences for how the tradition was read. The Frankfurt publisher Suhrkamp built its postwar programme substantially on the school's authors, issuing their works in the inexpensive editions through which a student generation could own them, until contemporaries spoke of a Suhrkamp culture as the republic's unofficial curriculum: the grey paperbacks on the seminar table were, to a remarkable degree, the institute's extended bibliography. Adorno wrote for the feuilletons and broadcast on the radio with a regularity that made him, by the nineteen sixties, the most recognisable intellectual voice in the country, his essays on music, literature, and the moral condition of the republic reaching readers who would never open Negative Dialectics. Habermas inherited the position and enlarged it, his interventions in the republic's quarrels conducted in the same pages his theory analysed as the public sphere's surviving organ. The medium shaped the message in ways scholars should register. The feuilleton essay rewarded the school's native forms, the constellation, the aphorism, the immanent reading of a cultural object, and it punished the programme's other half: no questionnaire ever made the cultural pages, and the reading public of the republic therefore met the school, once again, as philosophy and criticism, while the studies that grounded both sat in the institute's reports. The legend's third act was thus written by its heroes' own success. A guide that restores the empirical record is, among other things, a protest against the feuilleton's editorial judgement, delivered in the knowledge that without the feuilleton there would be no readership left to protest to.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk on this topic is not ignorance but the legend: the four names, the mood of pessimism, the culture industry as a slogan, delivered in an answer that the examiner has read four hundred times. The corrections in this guide are mark bearing. Separate the institute's phases, Grünberg's documentary foundation from Horkheimer's programme, before you attribute any doctrine to a date. Treat membership as a factual question and keep Benjamin in his correct position, the funded associate, since questions are set to catch exactly that error. Learn the empirical record as a list you can deploy, the authority and family study, the workers' survey and its character coding, the prejudice series, the F scale and its critics, the group experiment, because the standard criticism question, that the school abandoned Marxism for culture, is answered by it and the answer distinguishes your script. Keep the internal disputes ready as your evaluation material: Pollock against Neumann on the Nazi state shows a school arguing, and an argued evaluation outscores a recited verdict. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

This topic teaches best as an institution rather than as a philosophy, because the institution gives students evidence to handle. Open with the founding documents' world: Weil's endowment, Grünberg's archive programme, the 1931 inaugural address, and let the class discover that the school of the textbooks begins with a change of management. Set the workers' survey as the seminar's centrepiece: distribute Fromm's character types without his conclusions, have students code sample responses themselves, and then reveal the finding about the left electorate's authoritarian structure, which lands with the force of a result rather than an opinion. The Pollock and Neumann dispute stages cleanly as a two text debate on the Nazi state, with Behemoth's institutional chapters against the state capitalism essays, and the authoritarian personality study supports a methods session in which students attack the F scale's design before reading its critics, so that the methodological literature arrives as confirmation of their own findings. Close the sequence where the students' own questions will already have taken them, to [the culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), the school's examinable core, now legible as the cultural wing of an empirical programme rather than as a free standing lament. The Grand Hotel Abyss jibe makes an ideal final discussion: students who have handled the record can judge for themselves what the hotel's comforts were and what they purchased.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this school is archival, and the guide's corrections indicate where the unworked material lies. The protocols and coding frames of the empirical studies, several published only decades after their fieldwork, still await the systematic secondary analysis that modern methods could give them, and the institute's administrative archive, finances, personnel files, correspondence with sponsors, remains less exploited than the famous correspondence between the philosophers. Historians of the social sciences will find in the institute a complete case of a research programme's transplantation between three national systems, with its methods mutating at each border, and historians of the Federal Republic a case of an institution functioning as a state's unofficial memory before the state acquired one. Journalists encountering the school in polemical contexts should begin from the documentary separation of usages in [the history of the term cultural marxism](/2015/10/01/cultural-marxism-term-history/) and check any claim about the school against the membership and research record set out above, since the polemical literature's factual base rate is low. The collected editions and the two great institutional histories supply the verification apparatus; the primary rule of the field, learned from the school's own fate, is that no claim about the Frankfurt School should be trusted that cannot say which edition, which archive, or which study it rests on.

## The inheritance in pieces: the school inside the disciplines

Posterity did not inherit the school whole, because posterity is organised in departments, and each department carried away the fragment its questions could use. Sociology took the authoritarian personality and the survey tradition, absorbed the instruments into its methods courses, and largely discarded the theory of society that had designed them, so that generations of students met the F scale as a cautionary tale in measurement while never learning whose questions it was built to answer. Literary studies took Benjamin, the most portable of the associates, and built from the reproduction essay and the theses a criticism of images, cities, and memory whose sophistication its author, starving in Paris on the institute's stipend, could not have imagined, while often omitting the institute that had paid for the work and the colleagues who had argued with it. Media studies took the culture industry as its founding provocation and has been arguing with it, productively, ever since, the Frankfurt pessimism serving as the discipline's permanent prosecution in its trial of popular culture. Philosophy divided the estate between the generations: the first generation's negative dialectics entered the continental curriculum as a method of reading, the second generation's communicative theory rebuilt the normative foundations of political philosophy in dialogue with the Anglo American tradition, and the recognition theory of the third supplied social philosophy with its working concept of justice's experiential root. Education took the German pedagogy, the demand that schooling organise itself against the repetition of catastrophe, and installed it in the memorial culture of the republic and in the curricula of its schools. Political science took the jurists, Neumann on the state's structure and Kirchheimer on political justice, into its institutional memory of the century's emergencies. The fragmentation is not a betrayal but the normal fate of a programme too large for any successor discipline, and it has one systematic cost that this guide exists to repay: each fragment, detached from the whole, reverts toward the legend, the scale without the theory, the aura without the stipend, the pessimism without the questionnaires. The complete guide's wager is that the whole can still be reassembled in a single reading, which is what the institute, in its building on the Senckenberg Anlage, managed to hold together in a single staff for the better part of five decades.

## The quarrel with authenticity

One postwar front of the school's public philosophy deserves record because it shows the tradition defending its standards against the intellectual fashion of its own country. The Germany of the reconstruction years fell under the spell of existentialism, and in particular of a vocabulary descended from Heidegger, in which authenticity, resoluteness, care, and being toward death circulated as the small change of sermons, editorials, and management seminars. Adorno's short book on the jargon of authenticity subjected the vocabulary to the school's characteristic test: he treated the words as social evidence, asking what work their vagueness performed, and answered that the jargon allowed its speakers to borrow the pathos of metaphysical depth while evading every determinate claim, sanctifying the given order by draping it in the language of inward rebellion. The attack was not a literary feud. Marcuse had begun his career as Heidegger's student and assistant, and his break with the master, completed when Heidegger embraced the regime, was the school's founding parable of philosophy's political test; the postwar quarrel extended the verdict, arguing that a philosophy which had failed the century's examination in its founder's person could not be rehabilitated as a style. The episode completes the portrait of the returned institute's public role: the school policed the republic's intellectual language as it had once policed its own questionnaires, on the principle, which runs from the workers' survey to the jargon book without interruption, that the words a society repeats are among the most reliable documents of what it is doing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Frankfurt School?

The name covers the circle of researchers who worked at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt, a privately endowed research body founded in 1923 and attached to the city's university. After a change of director in 1930, the institute pursued a single programme under several disciplines at once: Marxist social theory, psychoanalysis, and empirical fieldwork, bound together by a journal and a shared set of questions about authority, character, and culture. The tradition took its name from its city only in retrospect, and it survived exile in America and a return to Germany after the war, training further generations who rebuilt its theory more than once. Its unity was institutional rather than doctrinal: a building, an endowment, a journal, and a payroll held together men who agreed on the questions and disputed nearly every answer, which is why the school's history reads as a history of arguments conducted inside one address.

### Q: Who were the main Frankfurt School thinkers?

The director was Max Horkheimer, philosopher and programme builder. Theodor W. Adorno, philosopher and trained musician, became his closest collaborator. Herbert Marcuse wrote the school's studies of Hegel and of advanced industrial society. Friedrich Pollock, the economist, managed the endowment and framed the state capitalism thesis, which Franz Neumann, the political scientist, contested in his study of the Nazi regime. Erich Fromm directed the social psychology work before his departure, and Leo Löwenthal built the sociology of literature and reading. Walter Benjamin, often listed first in popular accounts, was never a member: the institute funded and published him from outside. Jürgen Habermas led the second generation.

### Q: When and where was the Institute for Social Research founded?

The institute was founded in Frankfurt am Main in 1923, in the Weimar Republic's fifth year, as an endowed body formally attached to the University of Frankfurt while remaining financially independent of it. Its capital came from Felix Weil, the son of a grain merchant whose family firm traded from Argentina, and its first home stood beside the university's Senckenberg grounds. The founding director, the economic historian Carl Grünberg, set it to work on the documentary history of the labour movement. The Nazis closed and plundered the building in 1933; the institute reopened on the same street after its return from exile, in 1951. The double date matters for examinations and for accuracy alike: accounts that give the school a single founding moment in the critical theory programme erase the seven years in which the institute was an archive of labour history, and with them the evidence that its famous turn was a change of direction inside a going concern.

### Q: What is critical theory?

Critical theory is the name the school gave its own method in an essay of 1937, where Horkheimer set it against traditional theory. Traditional theory, on his account, imitates the natural sciences: it gathers facts, classifies them, and predicts, while treating the theorist's own place in society as irrelevant. Critical theory refuses that separation. It holds that the facts are socially produced, that the concepts used to describe society carry the marks of the order they describe, and that knowledge should serve a society's self-clarification, orienting itself toward emancipation rather than toward prediction and control alone. The term named a research programme, not a mood. Its descendants in the school's later generations kept the refusal while changing its grounds, rebuilding the standard of judgement first in the theory of communication and then in the theory of recognition, so that critical theory names a lineage of reconstructions as well as a single essay's distinction.

### Q: Why did the Frankfurt School leave Germany?

It left because the Nazi regime closed it. Within weeks of taking power in 1933, the new government raided the institute's building, seized its library, and shut it down as a body hostile to the state; most of its staff were Jewish, and all were compromised by association with a Marxist foundation. The departure succeeded because it had been prepared: the founders had placed the endowment in a Dutch foundation and opened foreign branches before the emergency, so the institute reconstituted itself in Geneva and Paris and then, in 1934, accepted Columbia University's offer of a house in New York, where it worked through the war years.

### Q: Did the Frankfurt School do empirical research?

Empirical research was the institute's daily business for most of its existence. Its German teams surveyed workers and employees with questionnaires designed to expose the character behind the opinion. Its exile programme produced a five volume series on prejudice for an American sponsor, including a personality study from Berkeley whose scales entered the standard literature, and a content analysis of fascist agitators' propaganda. After the return to Frankfurt it ran discussion groups across West Germany to test how the dictatorship was being remembered. The contrary impression survives because the philosophical books were translated and read long before the studies were. The institute's own contemporaries had no such impression: American foundations, agencies, and sponsors employed it precisely as a research organisation, and its German hosts in both centuries treated its questionnaires and reports as its public identity.

### Q: Why did they lose faith in the working class?

The loss was argued from evidence rather than adopted as a pose. The organised working class of 1914 had voted for its nations' war credits; the German revolution after the war was defeated with socialist ministers deploying the old officer corps; the Nazi movement drew millions of working class votes; and the institute's own survey of German workers found that even left voters were, in character structure, far more obedient than rebellious. Stalinism removed the consolation that the agent survived elsewhere, and postwar prosperity integrated the Western working class into the order it was expected to negate. The school's members never agreed on a successor agent, and said so.

### Q: Was the Frankfurt School communist?

No, in the only sense the question usually intends: it belonged to no communist party, took no party's instructions or money, and was denounced by the communist movement's own cultural apparatus as renegade for most of its life. Its founding capital was a private merchant fortune, its statutes protected its independence from every political organisation, and in American exile its members analysed the enemy for the democracies' intelligence services while its leading theorist of the Soviet Union anatomised that state's doctrine as an ideology of rule. The school's Marxism was genuine, structural, and unmistakable in its categories. It was also, by founding design, a Marxism without a card. The distinction is worth keeping in public argument, where the school's undoubted Marxist formation is routinely converted into an organisational allegiance it never had, and where its documented services to the democracies at war are as routinely omitted from the indictment.

### Q: What is the Grand Hotel Abyss criticism?

The phrase is Georg Lukács's, aimed at the German exile intelligentsia and fixed permanently to this school. Lukács pictured its leading spirits lodged in a beautiful hotel, equipped with every comfort, on the edge of an abyss of nothingness: close enough to catastrophe to contemplate it in depth, comfortable enough never to act. The criticism condenses a serious charge, that a theory which finds domination everywhere and an agent of change nowhere becomes contemplation dressed as critique, and that the endowment's comfort made the contemplation easy. This guide states the charge at full strength and answers it from the research record, while conceding the gap it names.

### Q: Does the Frankfurt School still exist?

The institute exists: the building on Frankfurt's Senckenberg Anlage houses a working research body that has been directed by scholars of the school's later generations and continues the programme of empirical social research joined to social theory. The school as a tradition exists in a wider sense, in the universities where its second and third generations rebuilt its arguments, around communication and law in one reconstruction and around recognition in another, and in the disciplines that absorbed its fragments, from the authoritarianism literature in psychology to the founding debates of media studies. What ended with the founders was the improbable unity of the original staff, not the questions they endowed. A reader should therefore treat the name as denoting a tradition with phases and heirs rather than a doctrine with a membership roll, and should date any claim about the school to the generation that produced it before judging the claim.

### Q: How does the empirical correction separate the school from its legend?

The legend is a selection effect. Posterity met the school through its philosophical books, translated in a wave a generation after they were written, and mistook that shelf for the institution. The empirical correction restores the rest of the record: the surveys, the prejudice studies, the group discussions, the wartime memoranda, the instruments that other researchers attacked, revised, and rebuilt. Once the record is whole, the standard charges change their character. The pessimism becomes an inference that can be checked against findings, the flight into culture becomes a set of field sites with budgets, and the school becomes what it was, a research programme whose conclusions can be disputed because its evidence was published. The correction also cuts against the school's pious admirers, since a programme with findings can be wrong in ways a legend cannot, and several of its findings were in fact corrected by the successor research its own instruments provoked.

### Q: What should a student separate between the institute's phases?

Three separations earn their keep in any examination. First, separate the founding phase from the famous one: the institute of 1923 was an archive of labour history under an economic historian, and the critical theory programme dates from the directorship change of 1930 and the inaugural address that followed. Second, separate the exile's products: the empirical studies and the philosophical reckoning were written simultaneously by the same staff, and neither is the other's footnote. Third, separate the generations after the return: the second generation rebuilt the theory around communication and law, and the third around recognition, so that doctrines attributed to the school must always be dated to their phase before they are compared or combined.

### Q: Why did exile change the school's questions?

Exile changed the school's evidence, its audience, and its dangers, and the questions followed all three. In Germany the puzzle had been authority: why subordinates consented, measured through families and workplaces. In America the same method met a democracy that had defeated fascism while harbouring organised prejudice, so the research turned to the prejudiced personality and the agitator's technique, funded by the host society's own philanthropy. The audience changed too, from German readers of a journal to American agencies, foundations, and universities, which rewarded institutional analysis and operational findings. And the catastrophe at the school's back, a Europe murdering its Jews, darkened the philosophy that the same staff wrote after hours, into the reckoning with enlightenment itself.

### Q: How did the empirical studies sit with the philosophy?

They were designed as one enterprise, with traffic in both directions. The philosophy supplied the studies' hypotheses: that opinion sits on character, that character is formed in the family, that prejudice is a structure rather than a mistake. The studies returned the discipline of results: the workers' survey corrected the movement's optimism before the dictatorship did, the prejudice research forced the authoritarian character into definitions that critics could test, and the postwar group discussions gave the theory of the unmastered past its documentary base. Frictions were real and recorded, above all where the philosopher's concepts met the survey profession's standards, but the institute's signature works are exactly those in which the union was consummated.

### Q: What did the members disagree about among themselves?

Nearly everything substantive, in print. The economist Pollock held that the Nazi order was a state capitalism in which political command had mastered the market; the political scientist Neumann answered that it was a chaotic non-state of competing empires, and the school's pessimism follows Pollock's side of an argument it never settled. Fromm's softening of Freud was fought by the director and by Adorno until the association broke. Adorno pressed Benjamin to cut the optimism from the essay on art and reproduction that the institute was paying to publish. Marcuse kept faith with a possible agent of change that Adorno declined, one by one, to certify. The disagreements are the strongest evidence against every portrait of the school as a unified project.
