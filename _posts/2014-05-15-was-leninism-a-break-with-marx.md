Leninism is the name given to a body of theory and a style of organisation associated with Vladimir Lenin, and few questions in the history of political ideas have been argued with more confidence and less precision than whether that body of work continued the thought of Karl Marx or broke with it. The confidence is understandable. The question carries the weight of a century: revolutions were made under the banner of continuity, and crimes were excused or condemned under the banner of betrayal. The imprecision is less excusable, because the disputants frequently argue past one another. One side produces a pamphlet and demands to know where Marx ever wrote such a thing. The other side produces a prison, a dispersed parliament, a banned faction, and demands to know where Marx ever licensed such a deed. Both exhibits are real. They are simply exhibits in different cases, and a reader who cannot tell the cases apart will be persuaded by whichever advocate speaks last.

This article slows the argument down until its moving parts can be seen. It states the proposition exactly, separates the kinds of evidence the rival camps treat as decisive, and then examines that evidence in the order the camps themselves would choose: the texts first, then the method, then the dated record of decisions taken in power. Its governing claim can be named at the outset, because everything that follows is organised around testing it. Call it the practice location. The strongest case for a break between Lenin and Marx rests not on any doctrine Lenin stated in print but on a set of decisions taken between the seizure of power and the early nineteen twenties, decisions that no passage in Marx instructs and several passages in Marx appear to forbid. If that claim survives examination, then the famous question changes shape. It becomes largely a question about the years 1918 to 1921 rather than about the pamphlet of 1902 around which the textbook quarrel revolves.

Four broad positions organise the scholarly and political argument, and a reader will meet all four in the literature, in examination questions, and in the polemics of parties that claim descent from one side or the other. The first position holds that the break was real, early, and doctrinal: Lenin substituted a theory of the party and of seizure for Marx's theory of the self-emancipation of a class. The second holds that talk of a break rests on a misunderstanding of what orthodox Marxism actually said in Lenin's formative years, and that Lenin applied, sometimes brilliantly and sometimes ruthlessly, a doctrine he had received rather than invented. A third position grants the continuity of the theory and locates any rupture later, in the governing practice adopted under the pressure of civil war. The fourth reverses the usual direction of the charge: on its telling, the decisive departure from Marx happened inside the Second International, whose leading parties had quietly domesticated a revolutionary doctrine into a creed of patient organisation, and Lenin's work was a restoration rather than an innovation. These positions disagree about many facts. More fundamentally, they disagree about which facts are allowed to settle the question, and that disagreement about evidence has to be mapped before the evidence itself can be weighed.

## The proposition, stated precisely

Precision begins with the proposition under dispute. In its strongest form it runs as follows. Leninism is a legitimate development of the theory Marx left behind, an application of its method to conditions Marx did not live to see, rather than a substitution in which a doctrine about a disciplined party seizing and holding power replaced a doctrine about a class emancipating itself through its own activity. Every word of that formulation does work. Development implies that change is expected and even required; a theory of history that could not be developed by its inheritors would contradict its own account of how ideas relate to changing conditions. Substitution implies something stronger than development: not the extension of a framework to new material, but the quiet replacement of its centre, so that the vocabulary survives while the load-bearing claims are swapped. Much of the quarrel dissolves once the parties are forced to say which of these they mean, because a great deal of what is denounced as substitution turns out, on inspection, to be development that the denouncer dislikes, and a great deal of what is praised as development turns out to involve at least one claim Marx stated plainly and Lenin's practice set aside.

Three distinctions do most of the clarifying work, and they recur throughout this article. The first separates text from method. A break in text would mean that Lenin asserted propositions Marx denied, or denied propositions Marx asserted, on matters both men addressed. A break in method would mean something subtler: that Lenin reached his conclusions by a route Marx's own procedure would not have licensed, even where the conclusions themselves sound familiar. The second distinction separates theory from practice. A thinker's books and a government's decrees are different kinds of evidence, produced under different constraints, and the inferential path from one to the other is exactly what is contested in this dispute. The third distinction separates Lenin from what was later codified under his name. The doctrine called Marxism-Leninism was assembled after his death, by other hands, for the purposes of a state and a world movement; treating it as identical to the writings of a man who died in 1924 is an error the literature on the subject warns against repeatedly, and this article keeps the two apart throughout, returning to the codification only where the separate treatment of Stalin and the codified doctrine belongs, in [the account of Stalin and Marxism-Leninism](/2019/03/15/stalin-and-marxism-leninism/), which handles that later construction on its own terms.

### Was Leninism a break with Marx?

The textual case for a break is weaker than usually assumed, because the argument for which Lenin is most often criticised was quoted from Karl Kautsky, the acknowledged orthodox authority of that period. The stronger case rests on decisions taken after the seizure of power, which follow from no text in Marx.

That answer is the article in miniature, and the sections that follow unpack both halves of it. The textual half requires a reader to look carefully at the sentence Marx placed at the head of the rules of the International Workingmen's Association, at the argument about consciousness that Lenin borrowed, at what Marx actually said about dictatorship and about the Paris Commune, and at the door Marx himself left open for a Russian revolution that skipped the expected sequence. The practical half requires an equally careful look at a shorter and grimmer list: an elected assembly dispersed after a single sitting, rival socialist parties suppressed in stages, factions inside the ruling party itself forbidden by resolution, and a political police whose wartime emergency powers were never surrendered. Between the two halves sits the question of evidence, because a camp that begins with the pamphlets and a camp that begins with the decrees will rank those lists in opposite orders, and neither ranking is self-evidently the right one.

One further discipline governs everything below, and it is worth stating before the evidence starts to accumulate. Theory and the record of a state are kept in separate accounts in this article. The documented record of the Soviet regime in its first years, including repression, is reported accurately and attributed to the historians who established it. It is never minimised, and it is never waved as a rhetorical trump that ends discussion of the theoretical question, because the theoretical question is a question about the relationship between two bodies of thought, and the record bears on that relationship only through specified arguments that have to be made, not assumed. Equally, the theoretical defence is never allowed to launder the record. A demonstration that Lenin's pamphlet of 1902 was orthodox by the standards of its day says nothing, by itself, about whether dispersing an elected assembly in 1918 was justified, necessary, or continuous with anything Marx wrote. Keeping those accounts separate is not evasion. It is the only way either account can be audited.

## Self-emancipation, the sentence at the centre of the quarrel

Every serious treatment of the break question eventually arrives at a single sentence, and the argument cannot be understood until the sentence has been read slowly. Marx drafted the provisional rules of the International Workingmen's Association in 1864, and the rules open with a consideration clause rather than a programme: "the emancipation of the working classes must be conquered by the working classes themselves". Fourteen words, and the whole dispute is latent inside them. The clause was not decorative. It was aimed, in part, at the conspiratorial traditions of European revolution, at secret societies of committed men who planned to liberate the workers by a stroke of organisation and then present them with their freedom as a gift. Against that tradition the clause insists that emancipation is not transferable. A class freed by somebody else's action, however benevolent, has been ruled by new hands rather than emancipated, because the capacity to rule itself is precisely the thing emancipation consists in.

The break camp treats this sentence as the fixed point against which Leninism fails. Its argument, in the strongest form given by the historian Leszek Kolakowski in the concluding movement of his history of Marxist thought, is that Lenin's doctrine of the party reverses the direction of the clause. On that doctrine, the working class left to itself arrives only at trade union consciousness, at demands for better terms within the existing order, while socialist consciousness is elaborated by intellectuals and carried into the class by an organisation of professional revolutionaries. If the decisive knowledge comes from outside the class, and the decisive organisation stands apart from the class, disciplined and centralised and entitled by its knowledge to lead, then the class is the object of emancipation rather than its subject, whatever the banners say. The party acts for the class, decides for the class, and in the limiting case substitutes itself for the class while continuing to speak in the class's name. That, on this account, is not a development of the sentence of 1864. It is its negation, dressed in its vocabulary.

The continuity camp answers the sentence with the sentence's own history. The clause, its scholars point out, was a rule for an international association of workers' organisations, a statement about who must win emancipation, not a treatise on how revolutionary knowledge is formed. Marx himself spent the International's life fighting for centralised authority against federalists and anarchists, expelled rivals with a hard hand, and never suggested that workers spontaneously generate a complete theory of their situation by reflecting on the factory floor. The theory of political economy that bears his name was, after all, written by a university educated journalist in a reading room, and nobody in the Marxist tradition, beginning with Marx, ever pretended otherwise. On this reading, the sentence rules out liberation by a conspiracy that keeps power for itself; it does not rule out leadership, organisation, or the importation of ideas by people trained to elaborate them, provided the organisation remains the instrument of a class in motion rather than its replacement. Whether the Bolshevik party remained such an instrument is then a question about what the party did, which is a different question from what the pamphlet said, and the continuity camp is content to have the dispute moved onto that ground, because ground about practice can be argued with dates and decrees rather than with duelling quotations.

Between those two readings sits a quieter observation that both camps sometimes miss. Lenin knew the tradition the sentence came from, cited the International's heritage with reverence, and never presented himself as revising it. Whatever else is true of the man, he did not understand himself as Marx's corrector. He understood himself, with a consistency that runs from his earliest pamphlets to his final dictated notes, as the executor of an orthodoxy that others had betrayed or allowed to decay. That self-understanding is not evidence that he was right. Thinkers are unreliable witnesses to their own fidelity. But it changes the shape of the historian's task: the question becomes not whether a rebel broke with a master, but whether a self-declared restoration drifted, step by defensible step, into something the master would not have recognised. Drift is harder to date than rebellion, and the hunt for a single dramatic moment of rupture, which the textbook version of the question encourages, may be a hunt for something that never occurred in that form.

### What did Marx mean by insisting that emancipation could not be delegated?

He meant that a class liberated by an outside agency remains a subject population under new management, because the ability to govern itself is the substance of freedom rather than its reward. The clause therefore tests every revolutionary organisation by one standard: whether its leadership enlarges the class's own capacity to act, or replaces that capacity with obedience.

The test embedded in that answer deserves a moment of attention, because it converts a slogan into an instrument. An organisation can be judged, year by year and decision by decision, by asking whether the people in whose name it acts are becoming more capable of acting without it, or less. Schools, presses, unions, soviets, factory committees: each can be read as a device for building capacity or as a device for extracting compliance, and many have been both in sequence. The break camp's deepest argument is that the Bolshevik organisation, whatever its intentions in opposition, became in power a machine of the second kind, and that the theory of the party contained no internal brake against that transformation. The continuity camp's deepest answer is that the brake existed in the theory and was overwhelmed by circumstances no theory could have survived unchanged: invasion, blockade, famine, the collapse of the industrial class itself into the countryside and the grave. Both of those arguments are about practice again. The sentence of 1864, brandished as a purely textual weapon, keeps pointing its users toward the record of 1918 and after, which is one early sign that the practice location will be difficult to dislodge.

## Consciousness from without, and the name on the quotation

No passage in Lenin has been quoted against him more often than the argument, in the pamphlet What Is To Be Done? of 1902, that the working class by its own effort develops only trade union consciousness, and that the theory of socialism grew out of the philosophical, historical, and economic theories elaborated by educated representatives of the propertied classes. Anthologies print the sentences, lecturers underline them, and the conclusion is invited: here, in black and white, is the substitution of party for class, the germ of everything that followed. The textual criticism of the pamphlet itself, its occasion in the Russian disputes of those years, and its place in Lenin's development are treated fully in [the reader's guide to What Is To Be Done?](/2014/03/15/what-is-to-be-done-readers-guide/), and this article deliberately leaves that ground to the guide. What matters here is a fact about the passage that the anthologies rarely print alongside it. Lenin was quoting.

The formulation he relied on, that socialist consciousness is something introduced into the class struggle from outside rather than something that arises spontaneously within it, was drawn from Karl Kautsky, the leading theoretician of the German Social Democratic Party and the man widely regarded in those years as the authoritative interpreter of Marx, sometimes described as the keeper of the orthodoxy itself. Lenin presented the passage as a correct statement of the position his Russian opponents had abandoned, and he presented it with approval precisely because it carried Kautsky's authority. The scholar Lars Lih, whose re-examination of the pamphlet reshaped the modern debate, argues that generations of readers have misunderstood the text by reading it in isolation from the Erfurtian tradition it was defending: the division of labour in which a party of intellectuals and advanced workers merges socialist theory with a workers' movement that generates organisation and militancy, but not political economy, out of its own experience. On Lih's reading, Lenin's anxiety in 1902 was not that workers were incapable; it was that Russian socialists were neglecting the task of becoming worthy carriers of a doctrine the German movement had already codified. The pamphlet's most notorious sentences, in short, were not a Russian heresy. They were a German commonplace, deployed in a Russian faction fight.

That finding, if accepted, does serious damage to the simplest form of the break thesis, and honesty requires saying exactly how much. It does not show that the from-without doctrine is attractive, or true, or faithful to the spirit of the sentence of 1864. Kautsky's orthodoxy might itself stand at a distance from Marx; the fourth position, examined later in this article, argues something close to that. What the attribution shows is narrower and, for the dating of any break, decisive. A doctrine cannot be the point at which Lenin departed from the Marxism of his time if the doctrine was the Marxism of his time, stated by its most respected living expositor and accepted across the movement's mainstream. Locating the rupture in 1902 requires the historian to argue that the entire orthodox centre of the Second International had already broken with Marx, and that Lenin's offence was fidelity to a corrupted inheritance. Some historians are willing to argue exactly that. But it is a different argument from the one usually printed, and it relocates the break from Lenin's study to the International's congresses, which is why the attribution matters far beyond the pamphlet itself.

The argument needs its other side stated at full strength, because the break camp has a reply and it is not a weak one. Attribution settles where a formula came from; it does not settle what Lenin built with it. Kautsky, whatever he wrote about consciousness, led a mass party embedded in unions, municipalities, and a dense workers' culture, a party that contested elections and expected to win power through the growth of that culture. Lenin took the formula into a country without legal parties, without a free press, without unions worth the name, and built around it a small, centralised, clandestine organisation whose members were professionals of revolution. The same sentence can do different work in different structures, and a critic is entitled to argue that the specifically Leninist contribution was not the doctrine of importation but the organisational machine constructed to perform the importing under Russian conditions, a machine whose habits of secrecy, discipline, and central command were bound to outlive the illegality that justified them. That reply concedes the textual point and moves the dispute, once again, from what was said to what was built and then to what was done. The pattern should by now be familiar. Nearly every road in this controversy, followed honestly, bends toward practice.

A word is also owed to Lenin's own later handling of the pamphlet, because both camps raid it. He described the book in retrospect as a polemical correction of economism, bent the stick in one direction to straighten it, in his own image, and warned readers against treating its formulations as a general theory detached from the fight that produced them. The break camp reads the concession as an admission that the doctrine was never sound. The continuity camp reads it as the author's own instruction to interpret the text historically, as an intervention rather than a catechism. The texts support the narrower point on which both readings accidentally agree: whatever the pamphlet was, it was not written as the founding charter of a one-party state, and nobody in 1902, including its author, supposed that it settled the constitutional form of a future revolution. Charters were written later, under different pressures, and it is to the pressures and the charters that the practical half of the argument must go. First, though, the textual ledger has two more entries, because the break camp's case from Marx's own writings does not rest on the sentence of 1864 alone, and the continuity camp's defence does not rest on Kautsky alone.

### Is the from-without doctrine a departure from Marx on its face?

Not on its face, since the division it describes, in which theory is elaborated by trained intellectuals and carried into a movement that generates militancy and organisation from its own conditions, was the settled teaching of the orthodox Marxism of the period, and Marx's own life as a theorist outside the class exemplified it.

## The revolutionary Marx the textbooks soften

A portrait of Marx circulates in which he figures as a patient determinist, a scholar of economic laws who expected socialism to arrive as the ripe fruit of capitalist maturity and who would have recoiled from insurrectionary politics on principle. That portrait cannot survive his bibliography. Marx praised the Paris Commune of 1871 as the political form at last discovered for the emancipation of labour, and he praised it knowing its fate and its violence. He wrote, in the critique of the Gotha programme, of a period of revolutionary transformation whose state could be nothing other than the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat, a phrase his later editors sometimes handled gingerly and his orthodox followers quoted without embarrassment. He conspired, organised, edited insurrectionary newspapers, quarrelled over the timing of uprisings, and endorsed, in the rules he drafted, a centralised international association with authority over its national sections. None of this makes him a putschist. He despised the conspiratorial sects of his day precisely because they wished to substitute their own daring for a mass movement, and his polemics against them are among the most savage he wrote. But the distance between Marx and revolutionary organisation as such is an invention of later, quieter Marxisms. The continuity camp is on firm ground when it insists that Lenin's willingness to plan an insurrection, to treat the state as an instrument to be smashed and refounded, and to speak of dictatorship as a transitional form would have startled Marx far less than it startles readers formed by the softened portrait.

The Gotha critique repays closer attention because it cuts in two directions, and honest treatment requires both cuts to be shown. In one direction, the document proves that Marx contemplated a coercive transitional state and refused to apologise for the concept; the dictatorship phrase is not a marginal note but part of his answer to the question of what stands between capitalist and communist society. In the other direction, the same document is a sustained attack on a party programme for its vagueness about the state, its cult of phrases, and its willingness to treat the existing state as a neutral helper, and it reveals a Marx intensely suspicious of party orthodoxies and of leaders who preferred formulas to analysis. A Leninist citing Gotha for the dictatorship phrase is citing a real text. A critic citing Gotha for the proposition that Marx would have cross-examined any party claiming his mantle, including Lenin's, is also citing a real text. The document licenses an argument; it does not settle one, and the habit of treating single texts as verdicts is one of the misreadings this article is concerned to correct wherever it appears, on either side.

### Why does the critique of the Gotha programme keep appearing in this dispute?

Because it contains, within a few thousand words, both the most uncompromising statement in Marx of a coercive transitional state and a demonstration of his contempt for programme worship, so each camp can quote the half it needs; the document therefore measures the quoter as much as the question.

The Commune carries a similar double charge. For the continuity camp, Marx's defence of the Parisian rising establishes that he could recognise a revolutionary government improvised by workers as his own cause even when it violated every expectation about stages, since France in 1871 was hardly the most advanced capitalist economy ripening toward socialism. For the break camp, the Commune matters for the opposite reason: the features Marx praised were its safeguards against a separate state power, officials elected and recallable, paid workmen's wages, the armed people replacing a standing army, a working body rather than a parliamentary talking shop. Measured against that template, the state constructed in Russia after 1918, with its appointed officials, its privileged apparatus, and its political police, fares badly, and the break camp is entitled to say so. What the template cannot do by itself is assign the cause of the divergence. A form can be abandoned because its builders never believed in it, or because the conditions for it, a functioning industrial society and a state not fighting for its life, were absent. Which of those explanations fits Russia is a historical question about circumstances and choices, arguable with evidence, and it belongs to the practical sections below. The texts, once again, frame the question with great clarity and then decline to answer it.

There is a further textual deposit that the continuity camp draws on, less quoted but harder to dismiss: Marx's conduct inside the International when legality vanished. Under repression he accepted clandestine organisation, centralised direction, and the discipline of a fighting association, and his correspondence shows no scruple that organisation must always take open and democratic forms regardless of the police conditions under which it operates. Lenin, moving through an empire where his party was illegal for most of its existence, could plausibly claim to be applying a distinction Marx himself had observed, between the forms appropriate to a movement that can organise in daylight and the forms forced on a movement hunted by an autocracy. The break camp's answer is that the distinction is real but that Lenin's innovation was to theorise the clandestine form as superior in itself, a model for all seasons, and to carry its centralism into the period of legality and then into the state. Here too the argument turns on weightings and sequences rather than on the existence of texts, and here too a reader does better to follow the sequence than to collect quotations. The sequence in Russia runs from illegality to insurrection to civil war, and the centralised habits hardened at each stage, which is precisely the mechanism the third position describes when it locates the break in practice rather than in doctrine.

## The Russian door Marx left open himself

The charge that Lenin broke with Marx by making a socialist revolution in an agrarian empire rests on a picture of Marx's theory of history as a rigid staircase: feudalism, then capitalism fully developed, then socialism, each stage compulsory and in order, with backward countries required to wait at the bottom until their bourgeoisies had done the preparatory work. That picture owes more to the catechisms of the Second International than to Marx's late writings, and the single most inconvenient document for the staircase picture carries Marx's own signature. In 1882, Marx and Engels wrote a preface to a Russian edition of the Communist Manifesto in which they entertained, in print, the possibility that the Russian peasant commune might serve as a starting point for a communist development, if a revolution in Russia became the signal for a working class revolution in the West that complemented it. The conditional matters. The door was opened a crack, tied to events elsewhere, and neither author spent his remaining strength pushing it wider. But a crack is not a wall, and the existence of the preface refutes the claim that the very idea of a Russian shortcut was, by definition, unMarxist.

Behind the preface stands a still more awkward body of evidence for the staircase orthodoxy: Marx's correspondence with the Russian revolutionary Vera Zasulich, who wrote to ask whether the commune was doomed to dissolve under capitalism or might ground a different path. Marx drafted his reply repeatedly, at unusual length for a polite letter, and the drafts show him refusing to convert his account of Western European development into a universal fate imposed on every people. The analysis in Capital, he indicated, described the expropriation of producers in a specific historical setting; it was not a master key that turned every lock in every country. The finished letter he sent was brief, but the drafts survive, and historians of the late Marx have made much of them, because they show the founder, at the end of his life, resisting the deterministic use of his own theory by his own followers. Neil Harding, the political theorist whose two volume study of Lenin's political thought remains a standard statement of the continuity case, builds much of his reading on this late material: Lenin's Russia, on Harding's account, was not a violation of a timetable Marx never finally endorsed, and the expectation that revolution might begin at the empire's weak link rather than in its most developed economy had warrant in the master's own hesitations.

### What exactly did the preface to the Russian edition concede?

It conceded that the peasant commune, the collectively held land institution of the Russian village, might pass toward a communist form without first enduring a full capitalist dissolution, provided a Russian revolution ignited a corresponding rising of the Western working class; a conditional possibility, stated publicly, in the founders' joint names.

The break camp has an answer to the preface, and fairness requires giving it room. A conditional speculation in a short preface, tied to a Western revolution that did not occur, is a slender foundation on which to rest the seizure of power in a country whose industrial working class was a small minority of the population. Marx's condition was not decorative. It expressed his conviction that an isolated communal development, surrounded by a hostile state and a world market, would be ground down; and one can argue that the subsequent history, a revolution isolated in a ruined country, governed ever more coercively as the expected European rising failed to arrive, confirmed the wisdom of the condition rather than the wisdom of ignoring it. On this reading the preface does not acquit Lenin; it indicts the gamble, because it specified the circumstance under which a Russian beginning might flourish, and that circumstance was absent. The continuity camp replies that every revolutionary act is a gamble taken before its conditions can be verified, that waiting for certainty is itself a political choice with its own body count, and that Marx in 1848 and again in 1871 backed risings whose conditions were, by any sober audit, unripe. Both arguments are live. Neither is a quotation. The preface thus functions in the debate the way the Gotha critique does, as a text that disciplines both sides without crowning either, and as further evidence that the textual ledger, taken whole, is more ambiguous than the pamphlet war suggests.

Underneath the preface quarrel sits the deeper theoretical issue, the account of how societies change that the whole series treats in [the complete guide to historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/). If that account is read as a law of stages, Lenin's strategy offends it at the root. If it is read as a method for analysing the specific balance of forces in a specific society, a method whose founder applied it flexibly to Ireland, to Poland, to the American civil war, and to Russia itself, then Lenin's wartime conclusion that imperialism had fused the stages and made the weakest link the point of fracture can be presented as an application of the method to material Marx never saw. The distinction between law and method is not a scholastic nicety. It is the hinge on which the entire continuity case turns, and it returns in this article's later treatment of what a break in method, rather than merely in findings, would even look like. A reader who grasps the hinge can already predict the shape of the four positions: each is, at bottom, a different way of holding it.

One more deposit belongs in the textual account before the map is drawn, because it is the deposit the break camp deposits last and weighs most heavily in private: Marx's repeated insistence that the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready made state machinery and wield it for its own purposes. That sentence, drawn from his account of the Commune, is often quoted as though it were a pacifist text. It is nothing of the kind. It is an instruction to smash and rebuild, and Lenin's State and Revolution builds its entire argument on it, quoting Marx at length and with an exegete's devotion. The continuity camp therefore claims the sentence for its side, as proof that Lenin's theory of the state in 1917 was a close reading of Marx rather than an improvisation. The break camp counters that the rebuilt state of the book, a commune state of recallable officials and an armed populace, bears so little resemblance to the state actually built after October that the fidelity of the reading only sharpens the infidelity of the building. Both claims can be true together, and their compatibility is the cleanest illustration in the whole dispute of why the practice location keeps asserting itself: a theory can be faithfully expounded in August and departed from in January, and the interesting question is what happened in between.

## The four positions map

The argument so far has assembled the principal exhibits: a sentence about self-emancipation, a borrowed doctrine of consciousness, a revolutionary Marx softer portraits suppress, a conditional Russian door, and a state theory expounded faithfully in a summer of hiding. What the reader needs now is not more exhibits but a map of the positions that arrange them, because the same exhibits appear in every serious account, arranged into incompatible patterns. The four positions map is this article's findable artifact, built to be returned to: one row per position, setting out the claim in its proponents' own terms, the evidence each position relies on, the evidence each must explain away, and what follows from each for the way Lenin should be read. Read the table slowly. The columns matter as much as the rows, because the third column is where each position is strongest and the fourth is where each is most vulnerable, and a reader who learns to ask of any new book on the subject, which row is this, and what is it explaining away, has acquired the skill this article exists to teach.

A map is not a verdict, and this one is deliberately drawn so that no row wins by the drawing. Each position is stated as its serious proponents state it, with named representatives rather than caricatures, because the failure mode of popular treatments is to give three rows to straw and one row to the author's camp. The sections that follow take the positions in turn, granting each its best evidence and pressing each at its weakest joint. Two warnings belong with the map itself. The positions are not hermetically sealed; individual historians blend them, dating a hardening to 1918 while conceding an authoritarian potential in the earlier doctrine, or defending the texts while condemning the practice in terms the first position would endorse. And the positions are not symmetrically distributed through the literature: the doctrinal break dominated the Cold War decades, the continuity case has been rebuilt by textual scholarship since, and the practical location has gained ground as the archives have opened and the decrees have been dated with precision. A reader mapping a new contribution should therefore ask not only which row it occupies but which decade's assumptions it inherited, because this debate has a history of its own, and the history explains why certain arguments arrive wearing the authority of common sense in one generation and of embattled revisionism in the next.

## Position one, the break in the doctrine itself

The strongest statement of the break thesis remains the one assembled by Kolakowski in the third volume of his history of Marxist thought, published in the late nineteen seventies, and its strength lies in its refusal to be distracted by the later record. The regime, on his account, does not corrupt an innocent theory from outside. The theory contains, from its Leninist refoundation, the premises from which the regime's character follows: a class whose spontaneous strivings are declared insufficient, a party whose possession of correct theory entitles it to act in the class's name against the class's expressed preferences, and a conception of truth in politics that treats disagreement not as error to be persuaded but as the reflex of a hostile class position to be overcome. Grant those premises, Kolakowski argues, and the dispersal of an assembly that voted the wrong way is not a betrayal of the doctrine but one of its possible applications, since the assembly's majority, being peasants led by rival socialists, could be reclassified as the voice of a class condemned by history. The reclassification move is the heart of the charge. A theory that supplies its holders with a standing permission to discount actual votes in the name of real interests has built the one-party outcome into its foundations, and the outcome, when it arrives, should surprise nobody.

Stated that way, the break thesis is formidable, and the versions of it that circulate in textbooks, where it shrinks to the observation that Lenin was impatient and ruthless, do it a disservice. Its real claim is structural. Marx's theory, whatever its own authoritarian temptations, located historical agency in a class acting for itself; the theory's predictions were about what workers would do as their situation educated them. Lenin's refoundation, on the break account, relocates agency to the bearers of theory and demotes the class to the status of material to be organised, educated, and, where necessary, coerced for its own good. That relocation is presented as the moment Marxism becomes something else while keeping the name, a doctrine of emancipation administered from above. The break camp then reads the whole subsequent record as confirmation: the party that knew better than the workers in 1902 knew better than the voters in 1918, better than the sailors of Kronstadt in 1921, and better than its own dissenting members in the same year, when factions were banned. One premise, four applications, and the continuity of the applications is itself offered as the proof that the premise was there from the start.

The position's handling of the counter-evidence is instructive, because a serious proponent does not deny the exhibits assembled earlier in this article; he reprices them. The Kautsky attribution is answered by noting that Lenin chose his authorities, and chose the most authoritarian strand available in the inheritance, passing over the more democratic currents in the same movement; selection, the critic says, is itself an authorial act, and a man is responsible for the tradition he assembles. The 1882 preface is answered by stressing its condition, as this article has already shown. Marx's dictatorship language is answered by observing that Marx's dictatorship was imagined as the rule of an overwhelming majority over a dispossessed minority, whereas the Russian case inverted the proportions, a minority class's party ruling over a peasant majority, so that the same word covered opposite social contents. Each answer has force. Each also has a cost, which the map's fourth column records: the position must treat an increasing amount of the textual record as inessential, must read the pamphlet of 1902 as a regime in embryo while the pamphlet's author insisted it was a factional intervention, and must explain why, if the doctrine dictated the outcome, the outcome required a civil war, an invasion, and a famine to arrive. Determinism about ideas sits awkwardly in a materialist tradition, and the break camp's critics are quick to note the irony of explaining seventy years of history by the content of a pamphlet, as though ideas ruled the world unmediated. That is the joint where position two presses.

## Position two, the orthodox who restored nothing because nothing was lost

The continuity case, in its modern scholarly form, is less a defence of Lenin's reputation than a reconstruction of his intellectual world, and its method is deliberately unglamorous: read the texts in the order and the company in which they were written. Harding's study of Lenin's political thought argued that the concepts were formed inside the Marxism of the Second International and changed as that Marxism changed, through the war's collapse of the old certainties and the discovery, in Hegel's Logic read in a Swiss library during the war's first winter, of a more dialectical Marx than the catechism had supplied. Lih's work on the pamphlet of 1902 extended the method to the most contested text, demonstrating how thoroughly its vocabulary and its hero, the German party, belonged to the mainstream of the movement. On this account the figure of Lenin the innovator, whether celebrated or denounced, is a later construction. The historical Lenin was a faction fighter inside a shared doctrine, distinguished from his rivals less by heresy than by energy, by an exegete's memory, and by a gambler's willingness, at the decisive moments, to act on conclusions his fellow orthodox drew and then declined to act upon.

That last distinction does heavy work in the continuity case, and it deserves to be drawn out, because it converts the most famous episode of 1917 into evidence. When Lenin returned to Russia in April and demanded that his party break with the staged strategy it had been following, the scandal inside the party was not that he cited foreign texts or invented new ones. The scandal was that he insisted the existing doctrine be applied to a situation the doctrine's other custodians preferred to describe as not yet ripe. The separate treatment of that year's insurrection in [the Marxist analysis of the October Revolution](/2014/05/01/october-revolution-marxist-analysis/) examines the seizure in its own terms; here the April episode matters as a specimen of the continuity mechanism. A doctrine that everyone holds and one man acts on will produce outcomes that look like innovations, and the historian who works from outcomes back to doctrines will manufacture breaks that the texts do not contain. The continuity camp generalises the warning: much of what is called Leninism, it argues, is Marxism under the condition of being acted upon by a man who expected the European revolution to relieve Russia's isolation within months, and who made a series of wagers on that expectation. The wagers failed. The doctrine's other custodians, safe in opposition or exile, never had their fidelity tested by the same stakes, which is why their Marxism looks purer in retrospect. Purity, on this account, is partly an artefact of never having held power.

The position is not without its own explaining away to do, and the map records the debt honestly. Continuity of doctrine, even if granted in full for the period to 1917, leaves the decrees of the following years standing in the room, and the continuity scholar must either defend them as applications of the doctrine, which few attempt, or assign them to circumstance, which concedes the third position's central move, or assign them to Lenin personally, which dissolves Leninism as a doctrine into the biography of a chairman. Harding's own work, to its credit, tracks Lenin's thought into the final years and registers the growing weight of the apparatus upon it; the continuity case at its best does not end in 1917 but follows its subject into the machinery he built and watched escape his intentions. There is also a limit internal to the method. Demonstrating that a text was orthodox in 1902 establishes its pedigree, not its truth and not its innocence; the fourth position exists precisely to argue that the pedigree itself is the problem, that the orthodoxy of the Second International had already travelled a long way from the sentence of 1864. Before that argument is heard, however, the position that this article's governing claim favours must be given its full hearing, because it is the position that reorganises the evidence most radically and the one against which the others increasingly define themselves.

## Position three, the break in the practice

The practical position begins by refusing the question's usual invitation to start in 1902. It starts instead with a calendar. In the first weeks of January 1918, the Constituent Assembly, elected two months earlier in the freest vote Russia had yet held, met in Petrograd for a single session. The Bolsheviks had won roughly a quarter of the national vote, strong in the cities and the garrisons, weak in the peasant provinces where most Russians lived; the Socialist Revolutionaries, heirs of the peasant movement, had won the plurality. The assembly declined to ratify the soviet government's decrees on the government's terms, and in the small hours it was dispersed by armed sailors acting on the government's authority, the guard commander explaining, in a phrase the historians have preserved, that the guard was tired. It never met again. No text of Marx discusses the dispersal of an elected assembly by a workers' government. The dictatorship phrase in the Gotha critique contemplates the coercive rule of a class; it does not contemplate the annulment of a national election whose result the ruling party had itself demanded be held, and had accepted as legitimate in advance. The third position rests its case on the gap between those two things, and it asks the reader to notice the date. Whatever happened between Marx and Leninism, on this account, happened here, in a decision, defended at the time not as doctrine but as necessity: the assembly, it was said, represented an order that the soviets had superseded, and a civil conflict was coming in which dual legitimacy would be a weapon for the government's enemies.

The calendar continues, and the position's method is to let the dates accumulate without commentary doing the work that sequence can do alone. Through 1918 the other socialist parties, the Mensheviks and the Socialist Revolutionaries, who had shared the soviets and in some regions the local governments, were progressively excluded from the institutions, expelled from the soviet executive, driven from the press, and, as the civil war deepened, subjected to arrest by the political police established in December 1917, the Cheka, whose powers grew with the war and whose campaign of terror after the assassination attempts of the summer of 1918 is documented in the historical literature in terms this article does not soften: hostages taken, executions carried out without trial in numbers the historians continue to dispute within a range that is terrible at every point of it. In March 1921, as the civil war wound down, the sailors of the Kronstadt garrison, celebrated in 1917 as the revolution's pride, rose with demands for soviet democracy and were suppressed by assault across the ice. In the same month, the Tenth Congress of the ruling party adopted the resolution on party unity that banned organised factions within the party itself, a measure its mover described as exceptional and temporary. The measure outlived the emergency by generations. The third position arranges these entries and then states its claim in one sentence: a break with the Marx of the self-emancipation clause, of the Commune's recallable officials, and of the majority dictatorship can be located in this sequence of dated decisions, each defended as a response to circumstances, none derived from any text, and the sum of them constituting, by 1921, a political order its founder's writings do not describe and in several respects forbid.

The full political indictment constructed from this record belongs to another article in this series, [the case against Leninism](/2014/06/15/case-against-leninism/), which assembles the prosecution on its own terms; the purpose here is narrower, to show what the third position does to the debate. Its first effect is chronological discipline. Arguments about the pamphlet of 1902 lose their status as the main event and become evidence about dispositions at most, since a disposition is not a deed and the same disposition, in the German party, coexisted with electoral politics for decades. Its second effect is to reassign the burden of proof. The defenders of continuity are no longer asked to vindicate a set of decrees; they are asked to explain why a sequence of decisions, each intelligible under the pressure of the month it was taken in, should not be aggregated into a departure, and the question of aggregation, of when emergency improvisations harden into a system, becomes the real subject. Its third effect is to give the break camp a better argument than the one it started with, stripped of the pamphlet determinism that made its case vulnerable: the break, on this showing, does not need Lenin to have been a heretic in his study, because the heresy, if the word is wanted, was committed by a government in a war. Even historians who resist the third position's conclusion concede its reframing, which is why the practice location has become the ground on which the modern debate is increasingly fought.

### What happened when the Constituent Assembly refused the government's terms?

The assembly sat for one session, declined to endorse the soviet decrees as the government required, and was dispersed by armed force before dawn, never to reconvene; its plurality party was thereby excluded from the legitimate succession its election was supposed to provide, and the soviet form was declared the higher legitimacy.

### Why does the ban on factions carry such weight in this argument?

Because it closed the last internal channel through which the ruling party's own members could organise to reverse the direction events had taken, converting a wartime expedient, presented as temporary, into a permanent feature of the system; after it, correction could arrive only from above.

The defenders of the government of those years have their case, and the discipline this article observes requires it to be stated as strongly as the record allows, in the government's own terms rather than in a paraphrase designed to fail. Russia in 1918 was invaded, blockaded, and dismembered; the White armies, backed by foreign powers, aimed explicitly at the restoration of the old order and conducted their own terror in the territories they held; the industrial working class in whose name the government ruled was dissolving into the army, the villages, and the typhus wards; the railway network and the food supply of the cities were in collapse. Under those conditions, the defence runs, every government in Europe had suspended liberties it regarded as sacred, and the relevant comparison is not between the soviet government and an ideal commune but between the soviet government and the military dictatorship that would have replaced it had it fallen. The dispersal of the assembly is defended as the refusal to hand power, mid-war, to a body whose majority party was already splitting into factions arming against the government. The faction ban is defended as the discipline of a party holding a front together. Each defence invokes the same master concept: necessity, understood not as an excuse but as the claim that the alternatives were worse and that history judges governments against their alternatives rather than against their programmes.

The third position's reply is not to deny the circumstances, which are documented beyond serious dispute, but to deny that necessity dissolves the question this article is asking. The question is not whether the decisions were justified; reasonable historians argue that both ways, and the argument belongs to political judgment. The question is whether the decisions were continuous with Marx's theory, and a decision can be necessary and discontinuous at once. Indeed the category of the tragically necessary departure, taken by men who believed in the doctrine they were departing from, is precisely the category the third position offers, and it is a more damning category in some ways than hypocrisy, because it suggests that the doctrine, exposed to the conditions of ruling a ruined country, had no resources with which to govern except ones it condemned. Whether that reflects on the doctrine, on the country, or on the men is the argument the position leaves open, deliberately. What it closes is the alibi that nothing in the relationship between Marx and the Soviet order requires explanation, that the texts and the decrees form a single seamless fabric. They do not, and the seams are dated.

## Position four, the break inside the International

The fourth position turns the entire inquiry around and aims it at the jury. Suppose, it says, that the reader accepts the case assembled by the continuity scholars: Lenin's doctrines were the common property of the Second International, his pamphlet a defence of its orthodoxy, his party a stricter version of a familiar form. What follows is not the acquittal of Lenin but the indictment of the International, because the International's Marxism had itself travelled far from its source. The German party, the movement's pride, had grown into a vast electoral and trade union apparatus whose daily practice was negotiation within the existing order, whose theorists had begun, in the revisionist controversy of the late eighteen nineties, to propose openly that the movement was everything and the final goal nothing, and whose parliamentary fraction, in August 1914, voted the war credits that buried the International's solemn anti-war pledges in a single afternoon. On this account the domestication of Marx was the work of decades and of institutions, not of one Russian faction fighter, and Lenin's historical role was restorative: the man who, in the war's darkest years, went back to the sources, read Hegel and Marx with a convert's intensity, and rebuilt from the texts a revolutionary doctrine the apparatus had filed down to a creed of gradual accumulation. The polemic between Lenin and Kautsky in the war's aftermath, treated in [the account of the Lenin versus Kautsky debate](/2014/07/01/lenin-vs-kautsky-debate/), is on this reading not a quarrel between a heretic and an orthodox but a struggle over which of them had kept the inheritance and which had pawned it.

The position draws strength from evidence the other positions step around. The collapse of 1914 is a fact of a different order from a disputed pamphlet: the parties of the International, pledged at congress after congress to resist an imperialist war by every means, voted in their national parliaments to finance it, and the doctrine they had all shared proved, at the moment of testing, to contain a nationalism stronger than its internationalism. A tradition that can fail that completely, the fourth position argues, had already broken with the sentence of 1864 in the deeper sense, having converted the self-emancipation of a class into the electoral progress of an apparatus whose preservation had become its real final goal. Against that background, Lenin's insistence on the smashing of the state machine, on the Commune as a model, and on the dictatorship phrase read without embarrassment looks less like an innovation and more like a recovery of the revolutionary Marx whom the International's professors quoted on ceremonial occasions and ignored in committee. The position also explains a biographical datum the others neglect: Lenin's wartime notebooks show a reader surprised by his own tradition, discovering in the Logic and in the early writings a dialectical subtlety the catechism had replaced with laws, and emerging convinced that the official Marxism itself needed rescuing from its custodians.

Its weaknesses are the mirror of its strengths. A restoration thesis flatters its subject's self-image, since Lenin indeed presented himself as a restorer, and a historian should be suspicious of any account that ratifies the protagonist's own publicity. The German movement was also more than its apparatus: it sustained a workers' culture of choirs, libraries, cycling clubs, and party schools that embodied, for hundreds of thousands of members, a real education in collective self-activity, and describing that world as mere domestication requires the same aggregation move, in reverse, that the fourth position criticises in others. Kautsky himself, the alleged chief domesticator, wrote with genuine fury against tsarism, defended the Russian revolution of 1905 in terms Lenin admired, and opposed the war's annexationist aims at real personal cost; the pawnbroker of the inheritance turns out, on inspection of the file, to have kept more of it than the polemic allows. And the position must finally explain why the restorer, once in power, reproduced and then exceeded the apparatus logic he had denounced, building a party state beside which the German apparatus looks like a debating society. The explanation offered, that the restoration was overwhelmed by the same pressures that domesticated the International, is available, but it is the third position's explanation wearing the fourth position's colours, and the convergence is telling. As the four positions are pressed at their weakest joints, they lean toward one another, and the point toward which they lean is the sequence of decisions in power.

## The war years reading room

Lenin spent the first winter of the European war in Bern, in the reading room of the university library, working through Hegel's Science of Logic with a concentration his notebooks preserve in exclamations and underlinings. The episode matters to the break question because it is the one moment where a change in Lenin's thought is documented at the level of method rather than of programme, and both camps claim the notebooks. The continuity reading, developed by scholars who treat the notebooks as the key to the later Lenin, sees a thinker recovering the dialectical core of Marx's method from beneath the mechanical materialism of the popular catechism, and thereafter reading every political question, the state, the nation, the timing of revolution, with a new sensitivity to contradiction, reversal, and the transformation of quantity into quality. On this account, whatever Lenin became after 1914, he became by travelling deeper into Marx, not away from him, and the famous flexibility of his later tactics, the willingness to retreat, compromise, and reverse, reflects a method relearned at the source. The break reading sees the same notebooks and draws the opposite moral: a thinker who concludes that his tradition's official philosophy was a vulgarisation has already broken with the tradition as transmitted, and the Lenin who returned to Russia was armed with a private Marx, reconstructed in solitude, against which the movement's collective understanding was bound to be found wanting. A Marx who speaks through one man's notebooks, the critic adds, is functionally a new authority wearing an old name.

The honest treatment of the episode is to notice that it supports a distinction the rest of the article needs. A thinker can break with the received version of a doctrine precisely in order to be faithful to the doctrine's founder, and the history of every intellectual tradition, religious, legal, and scientific, is full of such restorative ruptures. The question the historian must answer is therefore never the crude one, did he change, but the refined one: change relative to which baseline, the founder's texts, the founder's method, or the custodians' current teaching, since the three baselines can deliver three different verdicts on the same act. Much of the literature's confusion, and nearly all of the pamphlet war's futility, comes from sliding among the baselines mid-argument. The four positions map can be reread as a device for preventing the slide: position one measures Lenin against Marx's texts and pronounces rupture; position two measures him against the custodians' teaching and pronounces fidelity; position four measures the custodians against the texts and pronounces them the ruptured party; position three declines all three baselines and measures the government against the theorist, finding the rupture in the space between a man's books and his decrees. A reader who keeps the baselines labelled will find that many celebrated refutations in this literature refute nothing, having quietly changed the standard between the charge and the verdict.

Out of the reading room came the book that dominates the textual debate's second half. State and Revolution, drafted in hiding in the summer of 1917 while its author was a fugitive from the Provisional Government, is the most sustained piece of Marx commentary Lenin ever wrote, and its method is citation piled on citation, Marx and Engels quoted, glossed, and defended against the distortions their followers had allegedly introduced. Its political content is radically anti-statist by the standards of what followed: the old state machine smashed rather than captured, its functions absorbed by armed workers organised as the ruling class, officials elected, recallable, and paid at workmen's wages, bureaucracy and a standing army alike marked for abolition, the whole apparatus designed to wither as administration of people gives way to administration of things. The break camp reads the book as a utopian interlude its author never expected to implement, a mobilising text for a party approaching power. The continuity camp reads it as the authentic doctrine, implemented for a few months in the soviet forms of late 1917 and then crushed between the civil war and the apparatus the war required. The third position reads it as the single most important document in the entire dispute, for a reason neither camp quite states: it supplies, from Lenin's own hand and from the eve of power itself, the standard against which the practice of 1918 to 1921 can be measured without importing any external yardstick. A government that disperses an elected assembly, builds a political police beyond recall, and staffs itself with appointed officials paid at rates its founder's book condemned has departed from something, and the something is not an invention of hostile historians. It is chapter and verse of the book its chairman wrote in a Finnish hut. That is why the practice location does not depend on romanticising Marx. It depends only on reading Lenin whole.

## April as a test case both camps claim

Every general theory of the break eventually has to survive contact with a single episode, and the episode the literature returns to is the April of 1917, when Lenin, days after arriving in Petrograd, presented his party with theses that repudiated the strategy its leaders in Russia had been pursuing in his absence. No support for the Provisional Government. No parliamentary republic as the goal. Power to the soviets, those improvised councils of workers and soldiers that the moderate socialists treated as a pressure group and Lenin treated as the embryo of a new state. Peace, land, bread, and a party renamed to mark its separation from the social democracy that had voted for war. Senior Bolsheviks protested in print that the theses read like the programme of a man who had spent too long abroad. Within weeks, by argument, organisation, and the accumulating failure of every rival strategy, the party adopted them. The episode is a gift to both camps, which is exactly why it functions as a test: a good interpretation should explain why the same ten theses can look, in one light, like the moment of rupture and, in another, like the moment of restoration.

### How can the April theses be quoted by both sides with equal confidence?

Because they simultaneously violated the staged timetable the orthodox parties treated as doctrine and appealed, for every one of their demands, to texts and precedents within the Marxist inheritance, so the episode separates those who define orthodoxy by the timetable from those who define it by the sources.

The break reading of April fastens on the timetable. Orthodox strategy, in Russia as in Germany, assigned the coming revolution a bourgeois character: the autocracy would fall, a democratic republic would be consolidated, capitalism would develop under political freedom, and the socialist stage would ripen in its turn. Lenin's theses collapsed the sequence. They proposed that the party of the working class aim at power in a country of peasants, in alliance with the poorest of them, under slogans that pointed beyond the bourgeois stage from the first day. If the stage theory was Marxism's skeleton, April broke its spine, and the October seizure merely walked on the broken ground. The continuity reading answers that the skeleton was a classroom diagram, that Marx's own political writings treat stages as tendencies entangled in real history rather than as railway stations, and that the war had in fact entangled them: a bourgeoisie too weak and too frightened to make its own revolution, a peasantry in arms demanding land the republic would not distribute, a state dissolving at the front. To insist, in that conjuncture, on completing a stage whose social agent had abdicated was not fidelity to a method but obedience to a diagram, and Lenin's offence, on this reading, was to apply the method to the conjuncture and arrive where the diagram forbade. The argument between the readings cannot be settled by accumulating quotations, because both quote accurately. It can only be settled by deciding what kind of thing the stage theory is, law or heuristic, which is a philosophical decision about Marx's method dressed as a historical one about Lenin's April.

There is a third way of looking at April, quieter than either camp's, which the practical position cultivates and which this article regards as the most instructive. Notice what the theses did not contain. They proposed a transfer of power to the soviets, bodies in which several socialist parties competed, elections were frequent, and mandates were recallable; they looked forward to a congress of soviets as the sovereign authority of the new order; they said nothing about a single party monopoly, nothing about a political police, nothing about banning factions, nothing about dispersing assemblies elected by universal suffrage. The Russia of the theses is a multiparty soviet republic in which the Bolsheviks expected to win and hold majorities by persuasion, and in which, as Lenin repeated through that summer, a transfer of power could still occur peacefully because the soviets held the armed strength of the capital. The distance between that prospectus and the order of 1921 is the distance the third position asks the reader to measure, and April matters to it as the baseline reading: the theorist at his most radical, weeks from the seizure, still imagining a plural soviet democracy. Something between April's prospectus and March 1921's faction ban did the breaking, and it was not a text. It was a sequence of decisions taken by identifiable men on identifiable dates, under pressures that can be named and weighed, in a war that can be narrated. To locate the break there is not to acquit the theory of every charge. It is to insist that the charges be filed against the right defendant.

## Which evidence counts, and why the camps talk past each other

Step back from the exhibits and a pattern emerges that is worth stating as a finding in its own right, because it explains the peculiar sterility of a debate in which both sides command impressive evidence and neither side ever wins. The camps do not primarily disagree about what the documents say. The break camp does not deny that the from-without formula came from Kautsky; it denies that provenance settles legitimacy. The continuity camp does not deny that the assembly was dispersed and the factions banned; it denies that wartime decrees define a theory. Each camp operates with a hierarchy of evidence, rarely stated, in which one kind of material is treated as the theory's true voice and the rest as noise. For the first position, the voice is the founding text of the doctrine, read for its structural implications, and practice is the text unfolding. For the second, the voice is the whole corpus in its historical company, and practice is biography. For the third, the voice is the dated act, and texts are promises to be audited against performance. For the fourth, the voice is the founder's corpus, the custodians are the noise, and the heretic of the hour may be the tradition's truest reader. Arguments constructed on different hierarchies cannot refute one another, because each treats the other's decisive exhibit as inadmissible or as secondary, and the history of the debate is largely the history of exhibits being ruled out of order by courts that never announced their rules.

Making the hierarchies explicit is this article's first service to a reader, and it suggests a discipline for reading any new contribution. Ask, before weighing a single quotation, what the author counts as the theory: a text, a corpus, a method, a party, a government, a decade. Ask what the author does with the material that does not fit, since every position has its fourth column, its exhibit to be explained away, and the quality of the explaining is the best available measure of the position's honesty. Ask, finally, what the author would accept as defeat, because a position that no evidence could dent is not a historical claim but a loyalty. By those tests the four positions are not equally healthy. The doctrinal break, in its vulgar form, fails the third test completely, since every contrary text can be reclassified as camouflage and every contrary circumstance as unfolding. In its strong Kolakowskian form it does better, since it specifies the premise it attributes to Lenin and can be argued with premise by premise. The continuity case passes the tests for the period it knows best and grows evasive precisely where the decrees begin. The practical position passes most cleanly, because its claims are dated and checkable, though it must be pressed on the aggregation question, on why some improvisations count as departures and others as adaptations. The International thesis passes for 1914 and strains thereafter. None of this is a verdict. It is a calibration of the instruments, the necessary preliminary to the question the reader actually came with.

### Which single exhibit does each camp treat as its trump, and is any trump valid?

The break camp trumps with the from-without doctrine, the continuity camp with its Kautsky source, the practical camp with the dated decrees of the civil war years, and the fourth camp with the war credits vote that destroyed the International; no trump is valid outright, because each exhibit governs only the baseline it presupposes.

## What a break in method would look like

The most refined version of the dispute, and the one serious students eventually reach, abandons conclusions altogether and argues about method. Two thinkers can share every conclusion and differ in the machinery that produced them, and the difference matters because machinery outlives conclusions: it determines what the inheritors will do in situations the founder never faced. So the question becomes whether Lenin's way of arriving at political knowledge was Marx's way. Marx's mature method, as reconstructed from Capital and the drafts around it, moves from the concrete whole of a society to the abstract determinations that govern it and back again, letting the categories be corrected by the material at each pass; its political corollary is a suspicion of deduction from principle and a preference for the analysis of a concrete situation in its concrete detail. Much of Lenin's practice honours that corollary ostentatiously. His wartime and revolutionary writings are dense with the balance of forces, the enumeration of classes and fractions, the insistence that a slogan correct in one month is a crime in another, and his most famous methodological boast was that the concrete analysis of a concrete situation was the living soul of the doctrine. A break in method is hard to find in that record, and the continuity camp rests some of its most confident pages on it.

The refined break argument therefore locates the methodological deviation elsewhere, in the theory of knowledge that the party doctrine quietly assumes. Marx's method treats the theorist as inside the process he studies, his categories as the process becoming conscious of itself through the activity of the class whose theorist he is. The party doctrine, its critics argue, installs the theorist outside the process, in an organisation whose correctness is certified by its doctrine rather than by the class's recognition, and whose errors are therefore undiscoverable by the only test Marx's method ultimately trusts, the self-activity of the class itself. On that reading the deviation is epistemological before it is political: a closed circuit replaces an open one, and the political consequences, the reclassification of dissenting workers as backward, of striking sailors as petit bourgeois, of an elected assembly as a formality, follow from the circuit's closure rather than from any individual's cruelty. This is the strongest form the break argument takes anywhere in the literature, stronger than the pamphlet determinism of the vulgar version, because it survives the Kautsky attribution. Even if the formula was borrowed, the circuit it describes is the thing to be judged, and the judgment can be run on Marx's own criterion: does the organisation's knowledge remain answerable to the class's experience, correctable by it, dismissible by it? Where the answer is yes, the method is continuous whatever the vocabulary. Where the answer is no, the break has occurred whatever the quotations say. And the dates on which the answer changed, in Russia, can be given. The soviets of 1917 could dismiss governments; the party congresses of 1921 could not dismiss factions of their own membership. Between those poles the circuit closed, and it closed in practice, through decisions, which is the practice location stated in its methodological form.

## The objection that will not go away

Against everything argued so far stands an objection of real power, and this article states it at full strength because a verdict that has not faced it is worth nothing. The objection runs as follows. A theory is not a poem. When a theory's author seizes power, governs in its name for six years, and produces a one-party state equipped with a political police, the plea that the practice should be assessed separately from the theory is a plea no other tradition is granted and none should be. We do not separate liberalism from the colonial empires its statesmen administered while quoting its philosophers, nor conservatism from the regimes its admirers built; we treat the record as evidence about the doctrine's content, its silences, and its permissions. To locate the break in practice, the objection continues, is therefore not a finding but a manoeuvre, the standard manoeuvre by which a doctrine is preserved from its consequences: the theory is defined as whatever in the record is attractive, and the remainder is reclassified as circumstance, betrayal, or fate. A method that can acquit any doctrine by relocating its failures to the world is not analysis. It is apologetics with footnotes, and it would convict itself if it were applied, by the same historians, to a tradition they disliked.

### Can a theory fairly be separated from the state that was built in its name?

Only under a discipline applied evenly to every tradition: the separation is a tool for locating where fidelity ended, not a verdict that fidelity is innocent, and it must be surrendered wherever the record shows the theory supplying the specific permission for the specific act in question.

The answer this article defends begins by conceding the objection's central demand, the demand for even-handedness, and then disputes its conclusion. The separation of theory from practice is indeed a methodological convention rather than a proof of innocence, and conventions are legitimate only when they are applied symmetrically. Applied symmetrically, the objection's own examples turn in the hand. Liberalism is in fact studied, by serious historians, in separation from the colonial record for some purposes and in fusion with it for others: a scholar reconstructing the logic of a text on toleration is not refuted by an empire, while a scholar assessing liberalism as a governing tradition ignores the empire at the cost of fraud. The two inquiries are different, and both are lawful, provided nobody smuggles the verdict of one into the other. Exactly that discipline is what this article has attempted. The question it set was a question about a relationship between bodies of thought and a sequence of acts, and its answer, that the rupture is located in dated decisions rather than in the doctrine's founding texts, is a claim about location, not about absolution. A break located in practice is still a break. The decisions of 1918 to 1921 are not acquitted by being distinguished from the pamphlet of 1902; they are indicted more precisely, as choices, where a determinist account would dissolve them into inevitabilities and, in dissolving them, quietly excuse the choosers by making them puppets of a text. Precision about where a tradition failed is the opposite of tenderness toward it.

One clause of the objection, however, must be carried forward as a permanent correction, because it identifies the exact point where the practice location can be abused. Where the record shows a theory supplying the specific permission for a specific act, the separation lapses for that act, and the act is evidence about the theory. The reclassification move examined earlier, by which an inconvenient majority is redescribed as the voice of a doomed class and thereby discounted, is the candidate the literature most plausibly nominates for that status, and honest continuity scholarship has to meet the nomination rather than change the subject: either show that the move is absent from, or marginal in, the doctrine as Lenin held it, or concede that at this one joint the doctrine and the decree are continuous, and the break thesis owns a province inside the practical account. The map's rows, it turns out, are not countries with sealed borders. They are claims about where the weight of the evidence sits, and the weight can sit in different places for different elements of a large inheritance. That concession does not dissolve the article's claim. It disciplines it, and a claim that has been disciplined by its strongest objection is worth more than one that has merely survived its friends.

## The witnesses inside the tradition

The four positions are usually debated as though the witnesses were all later historians, but the earliest and in some ways the most penetrating testimony came from inside the revolutionary movement itself, from socialists who shared the goal and quarrelled over the road while the road was still being built. Rosa Luxemburg, writing in a German prison in 1918 about the Russian revolution she supported, produced the critique that the practical position would later adopt as a founding document. She defended the Bolsheviks against the orthodox scolds, credited them with daring to act when the International's parties had dared nothing, and then warned, in the same manuscript, that the suppression of democracy in the name of the revolution would destroy the revolution's own corrective organs: that freedom reserved for supporters of the government is no freedom, that public life suppressed makes the bureaucracy the only active element, and that a dictatorship exercised by a clique rather than by a class in motion would corrupt the very people conducting it. The manuscript was published after her murder, by comrades who disagreed with parts of it, and its authority in the debate derives from its combination of solidarity and alarm. Luxemburg was not measuring Lenin against a liberal yardstick. She was measuring the government against the self-emancipation standard from within the tradition that proclaimed it, and she filed her report in the first year, before the faction ban, before Kronstadt, when the entries in the practical ledger were still few enough to be called exceptions.

Kautsky's testimony cuts in the opposite direction and is equally awkward for the camps that cite him. The same man whose authority underwrote the from-without doctrine became, after October, the author of the most systematic social democratic case against the Bolshevik government, arguing that a minority dictatorship in a peasant country must degenerate into bureaucratic rule because it lacked the social base Marx's theory required. Lenin's reply, a polemic of sustained contempt, branded him a renegade from the doctrine they had once shared. The exchange matters here for what it reveals about the baselines problem. Kautsky in 1918 judged Lenin by the stage theory and the democratic commitments of the pre-war International; Lenin judged Kautsky by the revolutionary texts the war had allegedly exposed him as abandoning. Each man experienced the other as the apostate, and each could document the charge from the shared inheritance. A historian who enters that exchange looking for the moment of break will find two mirrors facing one another. What the exchange establishes, more modestly and more usefully, is that by 1918 the inheritance no longer had an agreed custodian, so that continuity had ceased to be a fact that could be checked and had become a claim that had to be fought for. Claims of that kind are settled by power as often as by scholarship, which is one reason the subsequent codification of an official continuity, examined below, should be treated as an event in the history of the question rather than as its answer.

A third internal witness belongs in the record because he spent a lifetime refusing the separation this article defends. Leon Trotsky, defending the revolution's record in exile, argued that the degeneration of the Soviet state was real but was the product of isolation and backwardness acting on a party whose doctrine remained Marxist, and that the bureaucracy was a parasitic growth on the workers' state rather than the doctrine's fruit. His critics inside the same tradition answered that a growth so durable, fed by the monopoly the doctrine had established and protected by the faction ban it had imposed, could not be quarantined as an accident of climate. The quarrel between those two sentences is the practice location argument conducted by participants with everything at stake, and its longevity is instructive. Trotsky's version requires the separation of a healthy theory from a diseased practice to hold across decades; his critics require only that a practice sustained for decades by a theory's institutions be read back into the theory as one of its properties. Readers will recognise the methodological objection of the previous section wearing historical dress. What the internal witnesses collectively prove, whatever their individual verdicts, is that the question this article addresses is not an invention of hostile outsiders or of examination boards. It was posed, in its modern form, by the revolution's own protagonists, in real time, with the texts open on the table.

## The late Lenin audits his own machine

There is a final body of evidence that no position can afford to skip, because it comes from the one witness whose authority all four camps, in their different ways, invoke: Lenin himself, in the last active months of his life, dictating notes and articles between strokes, increasingly preoccupied with the character of the state he headed. The late writings return obsessively to a handful of themes. The apparatus of government is described as deplorable, borrowed from the old regime and only superficially repainted. The bureaucracy is treated not as a sabotage by enemies but as a condition of the ruling party's own house. The workers' and peasants' inspection, an institution designed to police the apparatus, is judged a failure in its existing form. A dispute over the treatment of Georgia, in which officials of the new state had ridden over a small nation's communists with great-power arrogance, drew from the dying man some of his harshest language, directed at his own comrades and at the chauvinism he detected rising inside the revolution's state. Readers who know the later history read these texts with a sense of alarm recognised too late. For the break question their significance is different and more precise. They show the founder of the practice auditing the practice against standards the practice was failing, and finding the failure not in the doctrine's enemies but in the machinery the doctrine's party had built and staffed.

Each camp metabolises the late writings in its own way, and the metabolisms are revealing. For the continuity camp, the notes prove that the critical, self-correcting Lenin of the tradition persisted to the end, that the doctrine contained the resources for its own repair, and that the tragedy was biographical and institutional, a repairman struck down with the repair half dictated. For the break camp, the notes are a confession: the machine had escaped its maker, the apparatus the party built to emancipate a class now employed hundreds of thousands to administer it, and a theory adequate to its task would have foreseen that a centralised monopoly, once staffed, develops interests no resolution can vote away. For the practical position, the notes are the strongest internal corroboration it possesses, since they show the break, if break is the word, registering in the consciousness of the man best placed to deny it, and registering as a fact about the state rather than about any text: nobody in those dictated pages worries that the pamphlets were wrong. The worry, everywhere, is the apparatus. Even the fourth position finds material here, in the spectacle of the restorer discovering that the restoration had been captured by the same institutional logic that captured the International, as though apparatuses, once built, converge on self-preservation regardless of the doctrine inscribed over the door. The convergence of all four readings on the factual core of the late writings, the apparatus was out of control and its master knew it, is as close as this dispute comes to common ground, and the practical position is entitled to ask why, if the rupture were located in a text of 1902, the alarm of 1923 sounds on every page like a report about a government.

It remains to say what the late writings cannot do. They cannot settle the counterfactual on which so much rhetoric depends, the question of what Lenin, granted health and another decade, would have done with his own alarm: whether the inspection reforms and the warnings about chauvinism were the first moves of a dismantling or the deathbed anxieties of a man whose system had no dismantling procedure. The sources end where they end, and the discipline this article has observed toward every other body of evidence applies to this one too. What the notes establish is location, not destiny. The problem, as its author saw it at the close, sat in the practice and in the institutions, and any account of the break that cannot accommodate that testimony has to explain away the witness all sides call first.

## A different thing again, the codification after Lenin

Confusion between Lenin's own positions and the doctrine later assembled under the compound name Marxism-Leninism has corrupted more student essays, and more pub arguments, than any other single error in this territory, and the four positions map assumes the separation throughout. The compound doctrine was codified after 1924, in the course of a succession struggle, as the official creed of a state and of the international movement it directed: a fixed canon of texts with authorised interpretations, a catechism of dialectical and historical materialism arranged for memorisation, and a set of verdicts, on philosophy, on economics, on the national question, from which deviation became an organisational offence rather than a scholarly error. Whatever one's judgment of that codification, its genre differs from anything Lenin wrote. His books are interventions, occasional, argumentative, dense with the names of living opponents, and repeatedly revised in prefaces that concede overstatement and redirect the reader. The catechism is none of those things. It is a settlement, designed to end argument by supplying its conclusions in advance, and the transformation of a fighting literature into a settlement is itself one of the largest facts in the history of the tradition, large enough to have its own dedicated treatment in the series article already linked from this one's opening sections.

The codification matters to the break question for two practical reasons. The first is evidentiary hygiene. A large share of the propositions popularly attributed to Lenin, including several that feature in examination answers about the break, belong to the codified layer: formulations polished by editors, selections arranged to demonstrate a continuity the codifiers required, and silences where the record was inconvenient. A reader testing the break thesis against the codified Lenin is testing it against a portrait retouched by the institution with the strongest interest in the verdict, and the retouching is not a conspiracy theory but a documented editorial history. The second reason is that the codification is itself evidence in the practical case. A doctrine whose inheritors required a monopoly of interpretation, enforced by the party whose factions were already banned, illustrates the closing of the circuit that the methodological argument described: the point at which a theory's correctness ceases to be answerable to anything outside the institution that guards it. Historians who defend the separation between Lenin and the codification are therefore not engaged in a rescue. They are observing that the compound name marks a second transformation, distinct from the first, and that conflating the two transformations makes both unintelligible: the wartime hardening of a revolutionary government becomes a philosophy seminar, and the posthumous freezing of a literature becomes a footnote. Keep the layers apart, in chronological order, and each becomes arguable. Fuse them, and nothing is.

## Reading the evidence in the order it was produced

A reader who wants to test this article's claim rather than adopt it should work through the primary material in the sequence in which it entered the world, because the sequence is the argument's skeleton. Begin with the rules of the International and the sentence about emancipation, and sit with its anti-conspiratorial edge until it stops sounding like a slogan. Move to the Gotha critique and mark both of its tempers, the coercive transitional state and the contempt for programme worship, refusing to let either temper swallow the document. Take the Commune writings next, and list, in the founder's own emphases, the institutional features he praised, since that list becomes a measuring rod later. Read the Russian correspondence and the preface of 1882 with attention to their conditions, which are clauses in the argument and not decorations. Only then open the pamphlet of 1902, and read it beside the Kautsky passage it leans on, asking at each notorious sentence whether the offence lies in the sentence or in the structure that later received it. The war writings and the state book of 1917 come next, the notebooks if stamina allows, the April theses certainly, read against the party's own protests to feel the shock contemporaries felt. Then stop reading texts for a while and read the calendar of decrees and congresses from the assembly's dispersal to the faction ban, slowly, with the civil war's chronology open alongside, so that every entry carries its circumstance and its weight at once. Finish with the prison manuscript of Luxemburg and the dictated notes of the founder's last winter, the two internal audits, and ask the only question the whole sequence has been building toward: at which of these stations does the enterprise stop being describable in the vocabulary of the first station, and is that station a page or a date?

That itinerary is demanding, and most readers will sample it rather than complete it, which is legitimate. Sampling, though, should be honest about its own bias, because the samples are not neutral. A reader who samples only the texts will graduate as a continuity scholar without noticing the moment of choice. A reader who samples only the decrees will graduate as a prosecutor. The literature's camps are, to an uncomfortable degree, the deposits of sampling decisions taken early and defended thereafter as method, and the four positions map is best used as a check on one's own itinerary: whichever row a reader finds most congenial, the row's fourth column lists the exhibits that reader is most likely to have skipped. Go and read those. The debate improves in direct proportion as its participants colonise one another's evidence, and the practice location itself is a product of such colonisation, assembled by historians who read the pamphlets carefully enough to acquit them and the decrees carefully enough to convict the years.

## Misreadings that keep the quarrel sterile

Certain errors recur so predictably in treatments of the question that correcting them has become part of the subject, and a reader armed against them will find the genuine dispute more visible underneath. The first error has been dealt with at length: the belief that the doctrine of consciousness imported from outside was Lenin's invention, a Russian deviation from a spontaneous Marx. It was the orthodoxy of the age, and repeating the error after the attribution has been documented is a choice rather than a mistake. The second error is the mirror image, the belief that Marx contemplated revolution only where capitalism had completed its work, a belief the Russian correspondence and the preface of 1882 refute in the founder's hand; the staircase was built by the epigones and then attributed, by friend and foe alike, to the architect. The third error is the fusion of the compound doctrine with its namesakes, the assumption that the catechism of the nineteen thirties is a transparent window onto the arguments of 1902 or of 1917. The fourth error is the belief that the twentieth century's record settles the theoretical question by itself, as though an outcome, however vast, could substitute for the analysis of the relationship between a doctrine and its enactment; outcomes are evidence, and heavy evidence, but they enter the argument through the specifications this article has insisted on, not as a verdict delivered by scale. The fifth error is the subtlest and the most common among the sympathetic: the assumption that accepting continuity settles the moral account, that if the texts are acquitted the record is somehow acquitted with them. Nothing in the continuity case, argued at its scholarly best, claims any such thing, and its best practitioners are explicit that pedigree is not pardon. The sixth error completes the set from the other flank: the assumption that demonstrating a break convicts the texts by itself, as though a government's departures from a doctrine could be entered as the doctrine's own contents. Both of the last two errors confuse the location of a failure with the assignment of its guilt, and the confusion is not innocent, since it serves the polemical needs of camps that prefer verdicts to maps.

Beneath the six errors sits a single habit that generates them, the habit of treating the question as a referendum on a name. Asked whether Leninism is Marxist, the referendum mind hears a request to award or withdraw a trademark, and the evidence is then marshalled as character testimony for or against the applicant. Historians of ideas abandoned that frame for good reason. Doctrines in the wild are not applicants; they are populations of claims, emphases, silences, and institutions, varying across decades and continents, and the interesting questions about them are comparative and anatomical rather than judicial. Which claims survived the journey from the founder's study to the revolutionary's party and then to the ruler's state? Which were amplified, which muted, which inverted? At which station did each change occur, and under what pressure, and with whose signature? Those are the questions the four positions, at their best, are competing to answer, and the famous yes-or-no question is best regarded as the door into that anatomy room rather than as the business conducted inside it. A reader who leaves with the anatomy has something usable. A reader who leaves with only a verdict has a badge.

## The verdict this article defends, and what would change it

An article governed by the steelmanning discipline owes its reader, at the end, the author's own reading stated plainly enough to be disagreed with, together with the evidence that would overturn it. The reading defended here is the third position, with the methodological correction of the objection absorbed into it. On the textual question, the continuity case prevails over the doctrinal break: the notorious doctrines were inherited, the founder's own writings contain the coercive transitional state, the revolutionary organisation under illegality, and the conditional Russian opening, and no founding text of Lenin's asserts what Marx denied on a matter Marx addressed in terms that survive contextual reading. On the question of the inheritance's custodians, the fourth position scores a genuine point about 1914 that any honest account must carry forward. On the practical question, the break is real, dated, and documentable: between the dispersal of the assembly and the banning of factions, a political order was constructed that the founder's criteria, and the state theory its ruler had expounded months before taking power, condemn at point after point, and the construction proceeded by decisions defended as necessities rather than by the unfolding of a text. The practice location is therefore this article's answer, and its corollary should be stated without flinching in either direction. The corollary for the doctrine's defenders is that the acquittal of the texts does not touch the record of the decrees, which must be argued on the terrain of necessity and alternatives, where it remains contestable. The corollary for the doctrine's prosecutors is that the case against the government of those years is a case about men, pressures, and choices, and is weakened, not strengthened, by being dressed as the exegesis of a pamphlet, since exegesis is exactly where it loses.

What would change this verdict can be specified, as the discipline requires. The textual half would move if scholarship produced, from the founder's writings, a treatment of post-revolutionary political form that contemplated and rejected, in terms, the plural soviet democracy the April prospectus imagined, since that would relocate the argument from silence to statement. The practical half would move if the documentary record showed the decisive measures to have been genuinely temporary in operation, reversed as the emergency receded rather than entrenched by it; the record as it stands shows the opposite gradient, measures adopted as exceptions surviving as constitutions. The methodological correction would lapse if the reclassification move, the discounting of expressed majorities in the name of imputed interests, could be shown to be absent from the doctrine in practice as well as from its proof texts, rather than merely unquoted by its defenders. Each of those is a researchable proposition, and the article's verdict is offered as a standing invitation to research them. A conclusion that names its own defeat conditions is not weaker for the naming. It is the only kind of conclusion a dispute of this age and bitterness should be asked to produce, and a reader who finds the evidence moving against any clause of the verdict has been given, in the map and in the itinerary, the tools to say exactly where.

The question itself will outlive every verdict offered here, and it is worth closing on why. The relationship between a doctrine of emancipation and the organisations that claim it is not a closed file from a vanished empire. It is the standing problem of every movement that has ever promised liberation through discipline, and the Russian case remains its most fully documented laboratory: a complete run, from the reading room to the insurrection to the audit dictated from a sickbed, with the texts preserved, the decrees dated, and the witnesses, friendly and hostile and internal, on the record. That completeness is the reason the case repays study long after its polemical uses have expired. Handle it well, with the baselines labelled and the accounts kept separate, and it teaches a transferable craft: how to ask of any tradition, including the ones a reader loves, not whether it kept the faith in the lump, but where, and when, and by whose hand, the keeping stopped.

## The party form, examined on its own

No element of the inheritance has generated more heat than the organisational one, and it deserves a hearing separate from the pamphlet that made it famous, because the party form has a documentary history of its own that runs through congresses and split votes rather than through anyone's philosophy. The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party divided at its second congress in 1903 over a clause in its rules, a dispute so apparently trivial that the names the factions carried away, majorityites and minorityites, commemorated the vote that produced them. Lenin's draft of the membership clause required a member to participate personally in one of the party's organisations; his rival Martov's draft required only regular cooperation under an organisation's guidance. Behind the verbal difference stood two theories of what a revolutionary party under an autocracy could be. One imagined a bounded body of the committed, each member known, placed, and answerable, able to survive police penetration because its edges were hard. The other imagined a broader movement party on the German pattern, shading off into sympathisers and unions, able to grow because its edges were porous. The congress chose Lenin's clause in one vote and Martov's support prevailed in others, and the split that followed, widening over the following decade into separate parties, was narrated by both sides as a quarrel about the soul of Marxism in Russia. It can equally be narrated as a quarrel about police. In a country where the Okhrana ran agents inside every opposition circle, where a single arrest could unroll a network, the bounded form had a functional argument that the porous form lacked, and historians who treat the clause dispute as the birth certificate of totalitarianism have to explain why the functional argument was wrong on its own terrain, in 1903, with the prisons filling.

The continuity defence of the party form runs along exactly that functional line, and it is stronger than its critics usually allow. Democratic centralism, the formula under which the mature party operated, joined free debate before a decision to unity in action after it, and in the years of illegality and then of revolution the formula was not a fiction: the party's internal life before 1917 was a brawl of factions, newspapers, walkouts, and reunifications, in which Lenin lost votes, stormed out of meetings, and at least once found himself in a minority of one among his own central committee, in the crisis over the insurrection itself, when two of his closest lieutenants published their opposition to the seizure in a non-party newspaper and were denounced by him in terms that stopped short of expulsion. A machine for suppressing internal dissent does not usually misplace its off switch that often. The break critique, in its organisational form, therefore aims less at the party of the underground than at the transformation of the party in power, when the habits of a besieged conspiracy were fused with the resources of a state: the membership screening, the apparatus of full-time officials, the subordination of unions and soviets to the party's fractions within them, and finally the ban on the factions themselves, which removed the last mechanism by which the underground's brawling pluralism had renewed itself. On that telling, the organisational break shares the address of every other break this article has located. It sits in the years of power, and its instrument was not a clause drafted in a London hall in 1903 but a resolution carried in Moscow in 1921, in the name of a unity the emergency allegedly required, by a party whose founder's own career had been one long demonstration that factions, however exhausting, are how revolutionary organisations think.

There remains the hardest question in this territory, and honesty requires it to be asked in the form its critics prefer. Grant that the centralised form was a response to illegality, and that the founder's party argued with itself ferociously for two decades. Why should the historian not conclude that the form nevertheless carried a permanent temptation, an inbuilt preference for the centre's knowledge over the periphery's experience, which power merely fed rather than created? The question is fair, and the practical position has no need to dodge it, because a temptation located in an organisational form is still a different finding from a doctrine of substitution located in a theory, and its implications differ. Temptations can be resisted, designed against, and corrected by countervailing institutions, and the history of other centralised movements supplies cases of resistance succeeding as well as failing. What the Russian record shows, on the practical reading, is not a temptation mechanically triumphing but a sequence of moments at which countervailing institutions, the plural soviets, the rival parties, the factions, the recallable mandates, might have been preserved and were instead dismantled, each dismantling defended as temporary and each surviving its defence. That is a more useful finding than the mechanical one, because it identifies the points at which the story could have gone otherwise, and a history with branch points is a history from which something can be learned. The mechanical version teaches only fatalism, and fatalism, in a debate supposedly about a doctrine of human agency, is the one conclusion every camp should refuse.

## The soviet alternative and the argument about legitimacy

The dispersal of the assembly is the practical case's cornerstone, and it cannot be weighed without hearing the argument its authors actually made, which was more sophisticated than the cynical reading allows. The argument was constitutional. The soviets, its proponents said, were not a mob or a faction but a form of state, discovered in 1905 and rediscovered in 1917, in which the working population governed through its own workplaces and regiments, electing delegates who remained workers and soldiers, subject to instant recall, combining legislative and executive functions in the manner Marx had praised in the Commune. Against that form, the Constituent Assembly represented the older legitimacy of the territorial ballot and the parliamentary chamber, a form the revolution had overtaken. To subordinate the soviets to the assembly, on this argument, was not to defend democracy against dictatorship but to prefer one democracy's form to another's, the form of the citizen counted once every few years to the form of the producer organised continuously. Nor was the assembly's mandate as pristine as its mourners suggested: the electoral lists had been drawn before the Socialist Revolutionary party split, so that voters choosing that party's candidates could not distinguish its left wing, which supported the soviet government, from its right, which opposed it, and the plurality the assembly embodied was in part an artefact of a ballot printed in a vanished political world. A government asked to dissolve itself on the strength of such an artefact, in the opening weeks of a civil conflict, had arguments available that a seminar can dismantle only by stages.

The counter-argument, stated with equal care, begins by conceding the soviet form's genuine democratic content in 1917 and then follows its trajectory. The soviets of the revolutionary year were plural, competitive, and volatile; governments rose and fell inside them by resolution. For the constitutional argument to hold across the following years, that character had to persist. Instead, the exclusion of the rival socialist parties from the soviet executive, the arrests of their members, the management of soviet elections, and the conversion of the soviets into transmission institutions of a single party hollowed the form while preserving its name, so that by the time of Kronstadt the sailors' demand for soviets without the ruling party's monopoly could be treated, within the official vocabulary, as counter-revolutionary. The form that was invoked to supersede the assembly was itself superseded in substance, and the legitimacy claimed in January rested on institutions whose independence was spent within the year. Measured against Marx, the double movement is the whole story in miniature. The first movement, soviets against assembly, can cite the Commune and be cited honestly. The second movement, the party's absorption of the soviets, cites nothing in Marx and contradicts the Commune's safeguards at every point, and it is the second movement, not the first, that constitutes the practical break. Critics who expend their force on the dispersal itself therefore often attack the strongest joint of the government's case while its weakest joint, the year that followed, carries the real weight. The four positions map's third row dates the break to the sequence rather than to the single night for exactly that reason, and readers weighing the practical case should weigh the sequence whole, from the dispersed chamber through the emptied soviets to the congress that forbade the party to disagree with itself in organised form.

One more consideration belongs to this section, because it explains why the legitimacy argument retains its grip on the imagination of the tradition's defenders and cannot be waved away as a pretext. The soviet form was not invented in a study. It was thrown up by strikes and mutinies, twice in twelve years, by workers and soldiers who had never read the texts that later claimed it, and its reappearance in 1917, within days of the autocracy's fall, before any party could organise it, is among the best documented instances in modern history of a political form arising from below. A theory whose founder had written that the working class discovers the forms of its emancipation in struggle could hardly fail to recognise the event, and Lenin's recognition of it, his willingness in April to stake his strategy on an institution his party did not control, counts among the strongest exhibits in the entire continuity file. The tragedy the practical position narrates is therefore double edged in a way its summary statements sometimes flatten. The same movement that produced the most striking modern confirmation of the founder's expectation also produced, within four years, the machinery that emptied the confirmation of content, and it did so under the direction of the very party that had recognised the form first. Holding both edges at once is uncomfortable, and the discomfort is the correct response to the evidence. Accounts that resolve it in either direction have purchased their comfort with exhibits, and the map's fourth column is where the receipts are kept.

## The wager on Europe and the agrarian problem

Every account of the break must eventually reckon with the fact that the Russian revolution was not designed to stand alone. Its authors said so at the time, repeatedly and without embarrassment: the seizure in Petrograd was the opening act of a European drama, the signal the preface of 1882 had imagined, and the survival of the Russian beginning depended on the German, Austrian, and Hungarian working classes completing what the Russian start had announced. This was not a retrospective excuse assembled after the isolation became undeniable. It was the strategic premise of the whole enterprise, written into the April programme, repeated through the debates over the peace of Brest-Litovsk, when the government traded vast territories for time in the explicit expectation that the German revolution would annul the treaty, and acted on again in 1919 with the founding of a new International intended to organise the expected risings. The risings came, in Berlin, in Munich, in Budapest, and were defeated, and the Russian revolution was left alone in a ruined agrarian empire, holding a state whose theory had assigned it a supporting role. Any verdict on the relationship between the doctrine and the regime that ignores the failed wager mistakes the enterprise's character. The regime of 1921, besieged, monopolist, and coercive, was the residue of a strategy whose central expectation had collapsed, and the residues of collapsed strategies resemble their parent doctrines the way a garrison resembles an expedition.

The agrarian problem gave the isolation its specifically Russian weight. Marx's theory had been built for societies in which a working class formed by large industry was the growing majority; Russia's population was overwhelmingly peasant, its industry an archipelago in a rural ocean, and the revolution's rural policy lurched accordingly, from the land decree that ratified the peasants' own seizures, through the grain requisitions of the war years that turned the countryside sullen and then rebellious, to the retreat of 1921, when requisitioning was replaced by a tax in kind and a limited restoration of market exchange was proclaimed as a breathing space. The sequence is usually read as economics, and it belongs to the agrarian analysis this series gives the October revolution in its own right; for the break question its significance is structural. A government resting on a minority class, in a country at war with its own food producers, will be driven toward coercion by arithmetic before it is driven there by doctrine, and the arithmetic in Russia was merciless. The break camp replies, correctly, that arithmetic does not dictate the political forms through which coercion is organised, and that other arithmetics, in other revolutions, were answered with different forms. The continuity camp replies, also correctly, that the forms available to a government are constrained by the enemies it faces and the allies it has lost, and that the Menshevik alternative, coalition and assembly, was tried by history in several provinces and drowned in the same war. The practical position mediates without splitting the difference dishonestly. The wager's failure explains the pressure under which the break occurred; it does not relocate the break into the doctrine, because the decisions taken under the pressure were still decisions, several of them contested at the time by socialists who faced the same arithmetic and chose otherwise at the cost of their liberty. Pressure explains. It does not erase the location.

## Two Lenins, or one man in two situations?

Popular writing on the subject often resolves the tension by dividing the subject, offering a good Lenin of the soviets and the state book and a bad Lenin of the decrees, or the reverse portrait in which a sinister pamphleteer of 1902 is briefly masked by the summer's democratic rhetoric. The biographical evidence resists both portraits, and the resistance is itself a finding. The documentary record shows a single, unnervingly consistent intelligence applying a stable set of commitments, the primacy of the class struggle as the motor of politics, the state as an instrument of class rule to be smashed and refounded, the party as the organised memory and will of the movement, to situations that changed with a speed no strategist had foreseen. The April theses and the faction ban were both defended by their author in the same vocabulary of necessity and historical tasks. The state book and the Cheka were both, in his presentation, instruments of the dictatorship of the proletariat as the Gotha critique had named it. A reader searching for the moment the good man was replaced by the bad, or the mask slipped, will search the record in vain, and the vain search matters, because it shows that the break, if it is to be found, cannot be biographical either. It is not a change of personnel inside one skull. It is the divergence, under the pressure of governing, between a set of commitments and the institutional safeguards those commitments were supposed to carry, a divergence its author could register in his final writings precisely because the commitments had not changed and the safeguards were visibly gone.

That framing also disposes of the laziest question in the territory, the parlor question of what Marx would have said. The founder's reactions to his followers were, in his lifetime, reliably disobliging; he disowned disciples in two languages and remarked that whatever he was, he was not a Marxist, when the label was pinned on a French faction whose doctrines he disliked. A thinker who policed his inheritance that jealously would certainly have cross-examined the Russian claimant, and just as certainly would have reserved his sharpest contempt for whichever party in the quarrel quoted him most and read him least. Counterfactual séances of that kind settle nothing, and the serious literature avoids them for the same reason this article has: the evidence consists of texts, methods, and dated acts, and the work consists of arranging them without smuggling in a ventriloquised founder to deliver the verdict. What the record supports is not a séance but a sequence, and the sequence, stated once more in its barest form, is the article's whole case. A doctrine inherited entire from the orthodoxy of its day, applied by its most energetic reader to a revolution he did not schedule, in a country its founder had handled with conditional hope, produced first a plural soviet republic imagined in good faith and then, through three years of decisions taken under siege, a one-party state its own founder's final notes surveyed with dismay. Where the break is located in that sequence is a question with an answer. It is located in the second half of it.

## The novelties everyone concedes, and what they prove

Between the claim that nothing changed and the claim that everything did, there is a list of genuine novelties that all four positions can sign, and the list is worth compiling because it shows how much of the dispute is about the interpretation of agreed facts. Lenin's theory of imperialism, assembled during the war from the liberal and Marxist literature of the preceding decade, extended the founder's analysis to a world system of monopolies, finance, and colonial partition that Marx had not lived to describe, and turned that extension into a strategy: the chain of the system would break at its weakest link, not at its most developed point. The doctrine of the alliance between the working class and the peasantry, theorised for a country where the second class dwarfed the first, systematised a partnership Marx had sketched and distrusted in roughly equal measure. The right of nations to self-determination, defended against revolutionaries who found it a distraction, made national liberation a component of the revolutionary programme rather than an embarrassment to it. The soviet, finally, was recognised, theorised, and generalised as a state form in a way no earlier Marxist had attempted. Development, substitution, restoration: the novelties sit on the table, named and dated, and the four positions are, in the end, four theories of what kind of event a novelty is. The break camp sees the weakest link doctrine and reads a licence to dispense with the founder's conditions. The continuity camp sees the same doctrine and reads the founder's method applied to the founder's unfinished business, the analysis of a capitalism that had changed its form since the founder's death. The practical camp observes that none of the novelties, not even the boldest, entails a one-party monopoly, and that the doctrine of the alliance was in fact a doctrine of coalition, the opposite of monopoly in its original design. The fourth camp asks why novelties were needed at all if the inheritance had been intact, and answers its own question with the history of the International. The list disciplines the debate by shrinking it. Nobody serious denies the novelties. Everybody serious must now say what follows from them, and saying what follows is an argument, with premises that can be examined, rather than a posture that can only be applauded or deplored.

One of the novelties deserves a paragraph of cross-examination because it is the one most often smuggled across the border between development and substitution without inspection: the weakest link itself. In its original form the idea is an application of the uneven development of capitalism, the observation that the system's parts mature at different rates and combine their stages in single countries, so that a backward empire can concentrate the most modern factories, the most explosive class tensions, and the most archaic state in one territory. Read that way, the doctrine is an enrichment of the founder's method and a direct descendant of his own analyses of combined social forms. Read as a permission slip, detached from the analysis and carried as a slogan, it becomes something else: a standing authorisation to attempt the seizure of power wherever an energetic party finds a crisis, with the theory supplying retrospective absolution for the attempt's prospects. The career of the slogan in later movements, where it was invoked by parties in countries whose conditions Lenin himself would have audited line by line before pronouncing, illustrates a general law of doctrinal transmission that the break question keeps encountering. Doctrines break with their founders most often not when a heretic rewrites them but when a formula travels without its analysis, and the travelling is done by disciples in good faith. Whether that law acquits or convicts the founder of the formula is a question the reader now has the equipment to answer in a differentiated way, element by element, which is the only way it can be answered honestly.

## How the textbooks froze the question, and how the archives thawed it

The form in which most readers first meet this dispute, as a two-sided controversy with named champions and a verdict expected at the end, is a product of the middle decades of the twentieth century, when the question was a proxy in a larger war. In the universities of the West, the doctrinal break thesis served as the scholarly wing of a political settlement: if the Soviet order unfolded from a pamphlet, then the order and the doctrine could be condemned in one gesture, and the founder could be variously mourned or indicted as the pamphlet's grandfather. In the universities of the East, continuity was not a thesis but a state doctrine, taught as the premise of the system and therefore never tested as a claim. Between the two establishments, the question froze. Its textbook shape, for and against, with the same six quotations doing duty on both sides, survived long after the scholarship underneath it had begun to move, because frozen questions are pedagogically convenient: they fit a seminar hour, they divide a class into teams, and they spare the teacher the labour of explaining why the evidence refuses to line up. Generations of students met the dispute in that shape, and the shape, more than any argument, explains why the misreadings catalogued earlier have proved so durable. A frozen question generates frozen answers, and frozen answers generate the confidence, noted at this article's opening, with which the subject is usually discussed by people who have read the least in it.

The thaw, when it came, arrived through the archive and through a change in the questions scholars thought worth asking. The opening of the Soviet archives after 1991 did not produce a single sensational document that settled the continuity dispute, and anyone who promised one misunderstood what archives do. What it produced was granularity: the decrees dated and minuted, the votes counted, the internal arguments of the governing party recoverable in their sequence, so that the practical position, which lives on dated decisions, acquired a documentary base of a density the pamphlet debate never needed and never had. In parallel, the textual scholarship turned from anthology to context, rereading the contested works inside the debates that produced them, which is how the Kautsky attribution moved from a footnote to a load-bearing finding. The two thawing currents converge on the same moral, and it is the moral of this article's method from its first section. The dispute improves exactly as fast as its evidence is dated, sourced, and placed, and deteriorates exactly as fast as it is conducted with slogans. A reader who understands that has understood why the practice location is not merely a verdict about Russia. It is a bet about how intellectual history should be done, placed on the side of the calendar against the side of the anthology, and the bet has been winning, in the journals, for thirty years.

## The Commune template laid over the new state

The Paris Commune lasted seventy-two days, and Marx's account of it, written while its survivors were being shot against walls, has an intensity out of proportion to the episode's scale because he treated the Commune as a discovery, the political form in which the emancipation of labour could finally be organised. The features he catalogued were institutional and specific. The Commune was a working body, executive and legislative in one, not a parliamentary talking shop. Its members and officials were elected, answerable, and revocable at short notice. Public functions were paid at workmen's wages, so that office carried no material privilege. The standing army was abolished and replaced by the armed people. The police were stripped of their political functions and made responsible to the Commune. Judges became elective. The whole design can be summarised as a machine for preventing the formation of a separate governing caste: every safeguard aims at the same target, the moment at which the servants of society convert themselves into its masters. Marx had watched that conversion happen under the Second Empire in France and had given it a name, Bonapartism, the state rising above society and feeding on it, and the Commune mattered to him because it was the first attempt to build a state that structurally resisted the conversion.

Lay that template over the state constructed in Russia, not at the seizure but across the first four years, and the practical case acquires its most systematic form, because the comparison can be run feature by feature without rhetoric. Election with recall gave way, in the commanding heights of the new order, to appointment from above, with soviet elections surviving as ritual in institutions whose real decisions had migrated elsewhere. The wage principle for officials was abandoned early and defended as impractical, and a stratum of full-time administrators grew whose material position separated them from the population they administered. The standing army was rebuilt as a professional force with former imperial officers in command positions, a decision its makers defended by the war and never reversed after it. The political police, created within weeks of the seizure, operated outside the soviet structure it nominally served and beyond effective recall by anybody. The judiciary was subordinated in political cases to the emergency. None of these divergences requires the Commune to be treated as a blueprint Marx expected to be followed to the letter; he was describing a discovery made in one city under siege, not issuing a specification. The template's force is diagnostic. It identifies the specific danger, the separate caste, against which the founder's institutional imagination was directed, and it shows the Russian construction travelling, feature by feature, toward precisely that danger, until the founder's own heir was dictating alarmed notes about the apparatus in his final winter. A break measured by a template the tradition itself supplies is harder to dismiss than a break measured by the standards of a liberal seminar, and that is why the practical position, in its mature form, spends less time on the assembly's single night than on the four-year divergence from the Commune's safeguards.

The divergence also has a defender's reading that deserves its paragraph. Every Commune safeguard presupposes a society at peace with itself enough to afford them: recallable officials require elections that are not referendums on survival, an armed populace requires a populace whose arms will not be turned on the government by its enemies, workmen's wages for administrators require an economy in which skilled administrators cannot simply sell their skills to the other side. Paris in 1871 could attempt the safeguards because its war was at the gates and its experiment was measured in weeks; Russia's war was everywhere and its experiment was measured in years. On this reading the template is a fair-weather design, and the divergence records the weather rather than the builders' intentions. The practical position accepts the weather as fact and presses its narrower point in reply: whatever the cause, the safeguards were the content of the founder's form, and a state built without them is not that form continued but another form, adopted under pressure, whose relationship to the founder's theory is one of departure. Departure under pressure may be the most defensible kind of departure there is. It remains departure, and the cartography of this dispute consists of nothing else.

## Necessity, cross-examined

Since the practical case rests on decisions defended as necessary, the quality of the whole argument depends on how a necessity claim is tested, and historians have developed, across this literature, a reasonably consistent cross-examination that a reader can apply to any emergency measure in any revolution. The first question is whether the emergency was real and contemporaneous, documented in the situation the decision faced rather than reconstructed afterward, since governments, like individuals, are skilled at discovering necessities in the rear-view mirror. The second is whether the measure was proportioned to the emergency in scope and in time, and in particular whether it carried a mechanism for its own expiry, a date, a review, a sunset, or whether it was drafted from the first as a permanent acquisition of power. The third is whether alternatives were proposed at the time by people sharing the decision makers' information and commitments, because the existence of a contemporaneous internal alternative, argued in the party's own forums by its own members, is the strongest evidence that the decision was a choice rather than a fate. The fourth is what happened when the emergency receded: a measure that is dismantled as the pressure lifts was an improvisation, while a measure that is entrenched, celebrated, and extended as the pressure lifts has declared its true character in the only way institutions can.

Run the sequence of the practical ledger through that examination and the grades vary in a way that flat verdicts, in either direction, conceal. The creation of the political police in December 1917 addresses a real emergency, sabotage, speculation, and the collapse of the ordinary courts, and several of its founders spoke of it as a temporary organ; its subsequent growth, its exemption from control, and its survival into a permanent institution fail the second and fourth questions comprehensively. The dispersal of the assembly faces a genuine dilemma, a hostile majority in a country sliding into civil war, and its defenders can point to the ballot's defects; but no mechanism of expiry or appeal was contemplated, and the precedent it set, that electoral outcomes bind the government only when acceptable, was available for every later use and was used. The suppression of the rival socialist parties grades worst on the third question, because alternatives were not hypothetical: coalition arrangements between socialist parties existed in regions and institutions, were argued for inside the governing party itself, and were ended by decisions whose minutes survive. The faction ban fails the examination in the most instructive way of all, because its movers passed the second question verbally, describing the measure as exceptional and temporary, and then the institution failed the fourth question for seventy years, the temporary resolution outliving its emergency, its movers, and eventually the state it governed. A reader equipped with the cross-examination no longer needs to choose between the camps' adjectives. The grades are the argument, and they grade a sequence of decisions, taken in a war, by men whose founding texts are not on trial in any of the four questions. That is the practice location expressed as a method rather than as a slogan, and it is portable to every other revolutionary government the reader will ever be asked to judge.

## The verdict's afterlife in parties and movements

Decisions in this dispute were never only academic, because the parties that claimed the inheritance used the verdict on Lenin as an instrument for settling their own successions, and a reader should know the main uses to recognise when an apparently scholarly argument is doing organisational work. Inside the Soviet state, continuity was the regime's title deed: the codified doctrine presented the existing order as the founder's theory unfolded, and any scholarship that located a break, in either direction, threatened the deed and was treated accordingly. In the Trotskyist tradition, the verdict split the difference with surgical intent: continuity for Lenin, rupture for his successor, a distribution that preserved the founding generation while indicting the apparatus that exiled the tradition's own founder. In the social democratic tradition, the doctrinal break served as a border fence, marking the point beyond which the common inheritance of the pre-war movement would not be followed, and its historians sometimes wrote as though the fence had been surveyed by Marx himself. In the new left that emerged in the West in the nineteen sixties, the question was reopened from below, with the practical record read against the tradition by activists who wanted the founder's anti-authoritarian texts without the party that claimed them, and several of the misreadings this article corrects were first corrected there, in movement pamphlets rather than in journals. Each use left deposits in the literature, emphases, silences, favourite quotations, that a reader encounters without labels. The four positions map helps here too, since a position's organisational afterlife often explains its fourth column better than its arguments do: what a camp must explain away is frequently whatever its institutional ancestor could not afford to know.

Knowing the afterlives does not discredit the positions, because a claim's usefulness to a camp is no evidence about its truth, and some of the most interested scholarship in this territory is also some of the most exact, written by participants who checked the texts because their lives depended on the checking. The afterlives do impose a reading discipline. When an account of the break arrives with unusual confidence and an unusually tidy distribution of heroes, the reader should ask whose succession it is settling, in the same spirit in which one asks of a memoir what it is defending. The question this article has pursued is interesting precisely because it survives the asking: after the title deeds, the border fences, and the succession settlements are stripped away, there remains a genuine problem about texts, methods, and dated acts, small enough to be argued with evidence and large enough to matter. Returning the dispute to that problem, and refusing to let it be used as a proxy census of the reader's loyalties, is the last service the map performs. The founder, who spent a lifetime refusing to let his name be used as a trademark by people who had not done the reading, would have recognised the service and demanded it be performed on his own claimants first.

## The Menshevik control case

Arguments about necessity and doctrine share a hidden weakness: both can be spun indefinitely around a single case, because a single case cannot show what would have happened under different choices. Historians therefore hunt for controls, situations in which the inheritance was held by rival hands under comparable pressure, and the Russian revolution supplies an unusually good one. The Mensheviks, the Bolsheviks' sibling faction from the same party, the same congresses, and the same canon, faced the same collapsing empire, the same war, and the same soviets, and chose, at each branch point, the road the Bolsheviks declined: coalition rather than monopoly, the assembly's legitimacy defended rather than annulled, the staged strategy maintained rather than collapsed. Their leading theorist of the founding generation, Georgi Plekhanov, had introduced Marxism to Russia a generation before either faction existed and greeted the October seizure as a violation of the doctrine's conditions, predicting, in print, that a premature socialist experiment in a peasant country would end in a coercive regime ruling in socialism's name. His younger colleague Julius Martov, who had stood beside Lenin in the movement's founding circles, led the Menshevik opposition through the civil war years, defending soviet democracy against the monopoly from inside the revolution's own institutions until exile removed him.

The control case cuts into the debate at an angle none of the four positions can ignore. Against the doctrinal break thesis it testifies that the canon did not dictate a single politics, since the same texts, revered with equal sincerity, sustained a party that refused the seizure and condemned the monopoly as heresy; whatever Leninism was, it was a selection from the inheritance, not the inheritance's only possible reading. Against the strongest form of the necessity defence it testifies that alternatives were not the fantasies of later armchairs, since men and women with the same information, in the same cities, under the same shelling, organised their politics around them and paid for the organising with prison and exile; the decisions were decisions, which is the practical position's central contention, and the Mensheviks are its witnesses rather than its authors. Against the continuity camp the control case murmurs something more uncomfortable, that fidelity to the received doctrine, as the Mensheviks understood fidelity, led in practice to defending a republic that the war was dissolving and to a marginality so complete that within a few years the faction of the founders had been expelled from the country whose movement it had created. Fidelity, the murmur concludes, is not self-evidently the virtue the continuity argument needs it to be, because the circumstances in which fidelity was practised changed the meaning of the practice: to repeat the orthodox strategy of 1905 in the Russia of 1917 was not to preserve a tradition but to misread a conjuncture, by the method's own standard of concrete analysis. Each camp, in other words, can extract a lesson from the control case, and each lesson is a rebuke to a different rival, which is the mark of a genuinely instructive comparison rather than a decorative one.

The control case also has limits, and they should be recorded before the comparison hardens into a fable about the road not taken. The Mensheviks governed nowhere for long, and the moderation of a party without power is cheaper than the moderation of a party holding a capital; where Mensheviks did hold regional power, in Georgia, their government suppressed Bolshevik opposition and shot demonstrators, a record its admirers minimise and its critics file under the same heading as every other government of the civil war years. The comparison therefore cannot show that the alternative road led to a democratic socialism strangled only by Bolshevik intolerance. What it can show, and what the argument of this article needs from it, is narrower and sufficient: the inheritance underdetermined the outcome. Between the canon and the one-party state there intervened a chain of choices, made by identifiable people against identifiable opposition from within their own tradition, and any account that dissolves the chain, whether into the unfolding of a text or into the pressure of events, has explained away the very thing that requires explaining. The practice location is, at bottom, the refusal to dissolve that chain.

## A note on sources and their silences

Every position in this dispute leans on an archive with a shape, and the shape deserves a paragraph before the verdict's afterlife is traced, because readers deserve to know what kind of record they are being asked to trust. The textual record is unusually complete: the founder's manuscripts, drafts, and letters were preserved by institutes devoted to them, and the revolutionary's collected works run to dozens of volumes, down to marginalia and dictated fragments. Against that abundance, the record of decisions is skewed in ways that matter. Congresses were minuted, decrees were published, and the governing party's internal arguments survive in stenographic reports of remarkable frankness for the early years, growing thinner as the monopoly hardened, so that the archive itself documents the closing of the circuit this article describes: the later the decision, the poorer the record of the disagreement it overcame. Memoir evidence fills some gaps and contaminates others, since nearly every memoirist wrote with a succession to settle or a reputation to rebuild, and the diplomatic and police archives of the period add the perspective of observers who understood the regime through the categories of their own governments. None of this makes the record unusable. It makes it a record that must be read with its silences labelled, and it explains a feature of the modern literature that newcomers sometimes find puzzling, the migration of confidence: scholars are most certain where the stenographers were busiest, in the debates of the first years, and most tentative where the record was managed, which is one more reason the practical argument, built from the best documented stratum of the governing years, has strengthened as the archives opened while purely textual certainties have thinned. A reader weighing any claim in this territory should ask a librarian's question before a philosopher's one: what would the paper trail of this claim look like, and does the trail exist?

The same discipline applies to numbers, which this article has deliberately kept out of its argument except where a proportion does conceptual work, as with the assembly's vote shares. The governing years generated statistics the way they generated decrees, abundantly and under supervision, and the figures that circulate in the polemical literature, for arrests, for executions, for party membership, for the social composition of institutions, arrive with pedigrees of very uneven quality. Where a range is honestly disputed among historians working from the records, the honest citation is the range with its disputants named, and where a figure descends from a memoir or a ministry's self-report it should travel with that ancestry attached. The break question, fortunately, does not turn on any contested magnitude: an assembly dispersed once is a constitutional fact at any count of the guard, a faction ban carried once is a structural fact at any estimate of the congress's mood, and the argument built from those facts survives every plausible revision of the surrounding numbers. That robustness is worth noticing as a property of the practical case generally. Arguments built from structures and sequences degrade gracefully when their details are corrected, losing a strut rather than the roof, while arguments built from a single quotation or a single statistic tend to collapse entire when the quotation is recontextualised or the statistic revised, a difference in engineering that readers should weigh alongside the arguments' contents.

## Leninism as a word, and why the word is evidence

A curious fact about the subject of this article is that its central term was not chosen by the man it names. The word Leninism circulated first as a polemical label in the faction fights of Russian social democracy, used by opponents to suggest a personal following, a deviation organised around one man's authority rather than around a shared doctrine. Lenin himself reached for the word rarely and without enthusiasm; his own self-descriptions run in other channels, Bolshevik, orthodox, Marxist, the vocabulary of a man who insisted, sometimes to the point of tedium, that his positions were the common property of a tradition and not the inventions of a founder. The word's career therefore begins with an irony that the whole dispute inherits. To call the doctrine Leninism is already to have taken a step toward the break camp's framing, since the name asserts what the inquiry is supposed to test, that something exists which is distinctively his, separable from the inheritance and available for comparison against it. The continuity camp has occasionally made the point explicitly, complaining that the term rigs the trial before the evidence is heard. The complaint is worth registering and then setting aside, because the word is now the unavoidable name of the question, but a reader should notice how much quiet argument a single noun can smuggle past the door.

The word changed its character at its subject's death. In the memorial season of 1924, Leninism was taken up by the inheritors as a banner and a syllabus at once: lectures were delivered defining it, a foundation text bearing the compound's other half in its title was published within the year, and the term became the name of a teachable system whose contents the teachers enumerated with growing precision. That transformation, from polemical label to official syllabus, is the hinge between the subject of this article and the codification treated in its own section above, and it had a consequence for the evidence that scholars still have to correct for. Once Leninism existed as a defined system, readers began to search the historical Lenin's writings for the system, finding its elements where the search required them and passing over the interventions, hesitations, and reversals that no system can digest. The man who bent sticks, conceded overstatements in prefaces, reversed his party's strategy in a month, and dictated doubts about his own apparatus in his final winter is a poor fit for a syllabus, and the syllabised portrait that resulted, serene, consistent, and complete from the first pamphlet, has distorted the break debate from both directions ever since. The doctrinal break camp inherited a Lenin more systematic than the historical one and convicted the system. The official continuity of the codified tradition inherited the same portrait and acquitted it. Between the two verdicts, the documentary figure, arguing, improvising, and auditing himself, was mislaid, and much of the best scholarship of the past half century can be described as the effort to recover him from under the noun.

The word has a Western career as well, and it explains part of the textbook shape the question still wears. In the political science of the Cold War decades, Leninism named a genus, the disciplined vanguard party as an organisational technology transferable across ideologies, and comparative studies examined fascist and communist and nationalist movements under the heading of their shared Leninist form. That usage did the break thesis a quiet service, because it treated the organisational element as the essence and the doctrine as the variable costume, which is precisely the weighting the doctrinal break requires. Scholars of ideas pushed the opposite weighting in the same decades, treating the organisation as an episode in the doctrine's biography. The two usages coexisted in neighbouring departments, citing different shelves, and the undergraduate question that most readers meet first, was Leninism a break with Marx, is the fossil of their failure to meet: it assumes a single thing called Leninism, asks for its relation to a single thing called Marx, and expects a verdict proportioned to a seminar hour. Everything in this article has been an argument that the fossil should be cracked open and its contents examined one stratum at a time. The word survives the cracking, as it should, since no better short name for the whole deposit exists. But it survives as the name of a deposit, layered, dated, and mixed, rather than as the name of an essence, and a reader who hears it that way has already absorbed the article's method, whichever verdict the reader finally signs.

There is a final reason the word deserves its section, and it returns the inquiry to the founder for the last time. Marx suffered the same nominal fate a generation earlier, protesting that he was not a Marxist when the label was fastened on doctrines he disowned, and the parallel is more than a curiosity. In both cases the eponym was coined or popularised amid faction fights, in both cases the named man understood himself as the servant of a method rather than the proprietor of a system, and in both cases the name outlived the protests and became the handle by which institutions, friendly and hostile, lifted the deposit into their own quarrels. The lesson the parallel teaches is about the custody of ideas. A doctrine that passes into a noun with a man's name on it has already changed its social form, from an argument that must be remade by each reader into a possession that can be inherited, defended, and waved. Arguments break, when they break, at identifiable joints, in texts and in decisions, where this article has looked for them. Possessions break differently, in successions and schisms, and much of what is called the history of Marxism and of Leninism is the history of the possessions rather than of the arguments. Keeping the two histories apart is the last application of the discipline with which this article began, and the reader who performs it will find that the famous question, asked of the arguments, has the answer given here, while asked of the possessions it has a different and longer answer, which is the history of the twentieth century itself.

## For students and exam candidates

This is a standard essay question because it tests a transferable skill rather than a stock of facts: the separation of a dispute into the baselines on which its parties are actually arguing. Structure an answer around the four positions map rather than around a for-and-against seesaw. Open by stating the proposition precisely and naming the three registers, texts, method, and practice, since examiners reward the candidate who shows that the camps are answering different questions before showing which answer is best. Give the continuity camp its strongest exhibits first, the borrowed doctrine of consciousness, the dictatorship passage in the Gotha critique, the conditional Russian preface, because an answer that concedes those exhibits and still locates a break in the dated decisions of the governing years will always outscore an answer that pretends the exhibits do not exist. Then make the break case on the sequence from the assembly's dispersal to the faction ban, and protect the argument with the concession this article builds in: the separation of theory from practice is a locating tool, not an acquittal, and it lapses wherever a specific doctrine supplied the permission for a specific act. Close by naming what evidence would change your verdict, which converts a conclusion into a position. Keep the seeded distinctions separate in revision notes, and hold those notes in a single ordered file you can search under pressure; the study pages at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built for exactly that kind of structured capture. Above all, never write that the record of the century settles the question by itself. It is the sentence examiners are waiting to penalise.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The richest classroom use of this material is to make the baselines problem visible before the content debate begins. Distribute the four positions map with the rows unlabelled and ask the group to assign each row its trump exhibit and its exhibit to be explained away; the exercise takes twenty minutes and permanently changes how the participants read polemical history, because they discover that their disagreements are about admissibility before they are about facts. Follow with paired primary sources, the rules of the International beside the faction ban resolution, the state book's chapter on the Commune beside the decree record of the following year, and require every claim in discussion to carry its station, page or date, in the itinerary sense this article uses. The two internal witnesses, Luxemburg's prison manuscript and the founder's dictated notes, work especially well as a closing session, since students must explain how a critique from inside a tradition differs in kind from a critique mounted against it. Guard the room's balance by the rule used throughout this article: theory and the state record in separate accounts, each camp steelmanned before it is scored, and no verdict on the regime as a whole permitted to substitute for the analysis of the relationship under examination. Assessment should reward the dating of claims more than the warmth of conclusions. A cohort that leaves able to ask, of any inherited doctrine, where the keeping stopped and by whose hand, has learned the transferable craft the topic exists to teach.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The live research fronts in this territory are documentary rather than rhetorical. On the textual side, the contextual rereading associated with Harding's reconstruction of Lenin's political thought and Lih's work on the pamphlet of 1902 has reset the baseline for the early period, and any new contribution must now engage that scholarship rather than the anthology tradition it displaced; the outstanding gaps concern the wartime notebooks' reception inside the party and the editorial history through which the codified canon was assembled after 1924. On the practical side, the archival literature on the governing years has made the sequence of decisions recoverable in fine grain, and the open questions are questions of aggregation and intention: which measures their movers expected to reverse, which they expected to keep, and how the expectation can be evidenced from minutes rather than inferred from outcomes. Kolakowski's framing remains the strongest statement of the doctrinal break and should be cited in that strong form, not in the textbook reduction. Journalists covering parties and movements that claim this inheritance will find the baselines discipline directly portable: ask which baseline a claimant is using, founder's text, founder's method, or custodians' tradition, and the claim's apparent solidity usually resolves into a choice among the three. The four positions map generalises, with care, to any tradition whose founder is safely dead and whose custodians are not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Was Leninism a break with Marx?

The answer depends on where the break is looked for. In the founding texts, the evidence for rupture is surprisingly thin: the doctrines most often quoted as Lenin's departures turn out to be inherited from the orthodox Marxism of his period, and Marx's own writings contain the coercive transitional state and a conditional opening for Russia. In the governing record, the case hardens. An elected assembly dispersed, rival socialist parties excluded, factions inside the ruling party banned: each step belongs to the interval that runs from the taking of power into the opening years of the following decade, and none is instructed by any passage in Marx. The strongest supported answer is therefore double. Continuous in doctrine, broken in practice, with the break located in decisions rather than in pamphlets.

### Q: What does self-emancipation mean in Marx?

The phrase comes from the rules Marx drafted for the International Workingmen's Association, whose opening clause assigns the conquest of emancipation to the working classes in their own person. Its target was the conspiratorial tradition of secret societies plotting to hand workers a liberation prepared for them elsewhere. The clause answers that tradition by denying that freedom can be delegated: a class delivered by somebody else's exertions has exchanged one set of rulers for another, since the capacity to govern itself is not the prize at the end of emancipation but the thing emancipation consists of. In the Lenin debate the clause functions as a measuring rod. Any organisation claiming the inheritance can be tested by asking whether its activity enlarged the class's capacity to act for itself or steadily replaced that capacity with obedience to a centre.

### Q: Is consciousness from without a Marxist idea?

Yes, in the specific sense that the formulation Lenin used in 1902 was not his invention. The argument that socialist consciousness is introduced into the workers' movement from outside its spontaneous economic struggle, rather than generated by that struggle unaided, was stated by Karl Kautsky, the leading theoretician of German social democracy and the period's acknowledged custodian of Marxist orthodoxy, and Lenin quoted it approvingly as the correct position his Russian opponents had abandoned. Later scholarship, notably the contextual rereading by Lars Lih, showed that the formula belonged to a settled division of labour in that orthodoxy: intellectuals elaborate theory, while the movement itself supplies organisation, militancy, and experience. Readers may still judge the doctrine mistaken or dangerous. What the attribution removes is the claim that it marks the point where Lenin departed from the Marxism of his own time.

### Q: Did Marx allow revolution in a backward country?

He allowed more than the textbook staircase suggests. In the preface written with Engels for the Manifesto's Russian edition of 1882, Marx publicly weighed a route in which the collectively held village land of the peasantry might ground a communist development without first passing through a full capitalist dissolution. The offer carried one stated condition: a Russian revolution had to touch off a workers' rising across western Europe, the two movements completing what neither could manage alone. His correspondence with Vera Zasulich points the same way, the drafts showing him decline to promote a study of one region's path into a destiny binding on all peoples. The door was conditional and narrow, and the break camp fairly stresses that the condition, a Western revolution, never arrived. But a conditional door is incompatible with the claim that the very attempt in Russia was, by definition, a violation of the founder's scheme.

### Q: When exactly did the break happen if there was one?

On the practical reading defended in this article, no single day carries the whole weight, but the sequence can be dated with unusual precision. It opens in January 1918 with the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly after one sitting, continues through that year with the exclusion of the rival socialist parties from the soviet institutions and the growth of the political police during the civil war, and closes in March 1921 with two events in a single month: the suppression of the Kronstadt rising and the Tenth Congress resolution banning organised factions inside the ruling party itself. Each measure was defended at the time as an emergency response, and several were described by their movers as temporary. Their combined effect, a one-party order in which correction could arrive only from above, outlived the emergency by generations, which is why the sequence, rather than any text of 1902, is where the break is best located.

### Q: Who argues for continuity between Marx and Lenin?

The modern continuity case is associated above all with two scholarly projects. Neil Harding's study of Lenin's political thought reconstructed the concepts from inside the Marxism of the Second International, arguing that Lenin's categories were formed by that inheritance and changed as it changed, and drawing support from the late Marx's own hesitations about Russia. Lars Lih's rereading of the 1902 pamphlet carried the same approach into the single most argued-over text, showing its phrases, its German exemplar, and its teaching on consciousness to be common currency of the movement it addressed. Around those projects stands a wider exegetical scholarship on the wartime notebooks and the state writings, converging on one finding: the innovator of legend, whether the legend praises or damns him, is mostly a retrospective invention, and the documented figure was a period orthodox marked out by stamina, recall, and readiness to act where colleagues merely agreed.

### Q: Who argues that Leninism broke with Marx?

The classic statement is Leszek Kolakowski's, delivered in the closing volume of his three part history of the tradition, which appeared toward the end of the nineteen seventies. Kolakowski located the break inside the doctrine: a class diagnosed as unable, unaided, to rise beyond trade union demands; a party armed with correct theory and thereby licensed to override that class's stated wishes while speaking for it; and a reflex that reads opposition as the symptom of an alien class standpoint rather than as an argument to be answered. On that account the later record is the doctrine unfolding rather than a fall from it. The liberal historiography that followed his framing developed the argument with variations, while a distinct practical variant, held by many historians of the revolutionary years, keeps the charge but moves its address: the break, on that telling, was committed not by a pamphleteer in 1902 but by a government between 1918 and 1921. Naming which variant an author holds is the first step in arguing with any of them.

### Q: Is Marxism-Leninism a different thing again?

Yes, and keeping it distinct from Lenin's own writings is essential hygiene for this whole subject. The compound doctrine was codified after Lenin's death in 1924, amid the struggle over his succession, becoming the authorised creed of a state and of the international movement that state directed: a fixed canon with authorised interpretations, a catechism arranged for memorisation, and verdicts on disputed questions from which deviation became an organisational offence. Lenin's own books are different in genre, occasional interventions dense with the names of living opponents, revised in prefaces that concede overstatement. Several propositions popularly attributed to Lenin belong to the codified layer, polished or selected by editors with an institutional interest in demonstrating continuity. Judging the break question against the codified portrait means judging a retouched image, which is why the codification has its own separate treatment elsewhere in this series.

### Q: Can you be a Marxist without being a Leninist?

Huge numbers of people have, in every generation since the question could be asked. The Marxist tradition has always contained currents that reject Lenin's party doctrine, his reading of the state, or the Russian revolution's claim on the inheritance: social democrats who kept the founder and refused the seizure, council communists who kept the revolution and refused the party's monopoly, academic Marxists for whom the theory analyses capitalism rather than programmes a government, and revolutionaries who regard Lenin as a betrayer of the self-emancipation clause. The four positions map clarifies what each of those identities is doing. Each accepts some elements of the inheritance and declines others at identifiable stations, in the texts, in the method, or in the practice. The reverse identity is equally populated: many Leninists have held that Marxism without the party doctrine is the theory with its conclusion removed. The labels, in short, mark choices among the stations, and the interesting work lies in specifying the choices rather than in policing the labels.

### Q: Does the twentieth century record settle the question?

No, and the reason is methodological rather than sentimental. The question under examination concerns a relationship, between a body of theory and the doctrines and acts that claimed descent from it, and a relationship cannot be read off an outcome, however vast the outcome is. The record is evidence, and heavy evidence: the one-party state, the political police, and the suppression of dissent are facts that any continuity argument must carry, and this article's practical case is built from them. But evidence enters an argument through specifications. Which decisions enacted the record, under what pressures, defended by which arguments, departing from which texts? The same discipline is applied, by serious historians, to liberalism and its empires and to conservatism and its regimes, where outcomes are likewise refused as substitutes for analysis. Scale tempts the mind to treat a verdict as delivered. Resisting that temptation is not tenderness toward the doctrine. It is the condition of saying anything precise about it.

### Q: How does the practice location move the continuity debate?

It changes the debate's address, its chronology, and its burden of proof. The address moves from the pamphlet of 1902 to the decrees and congresses of the governing years, so arguments about what a text implies give way to arguments about what a government did on identifiable dates. The chronology moves with it: instead of a break present from the first page and unfolding for decades, the historian gets a sequence in which a plural soviet republic was genuinely imaginable in the first months and a one-party order was consolidated by stages, each stage arguable. The burden of proof moves most of all. Defenders of continuity no longer have to vindicate the decrees, only to explain why a sum of emergency decisions should not be aggregated into a departure, while prosecutors lose their weakest weapon, pamphlet determinism, and gain a stronger one, the dated record measured against the founder's criteria and against the ruler's own state theory of the previous summer.

### Q: What should a student separate between Lenin's texts and his government's acts?

Three separations, kept in that order, will organise most essays on the subject. Separate the texts from one another first: the factional pamphlet of 1902, the wartime notebooks, the state theory of 1917, and the dictated notes of the final winter are different genres written for different fights, and a quotation from one cannot be used as the caption of another. Separate the texts from the codification second, since the compound doctrine assembled after 1924 retouched the portrait for institutional reasons. Separate the texts from the acts third, and do the separating in both directions: an orthodox pamphlet does not acquit a decree, and a decree does not rewrite a pamphlet. What connects them is a chain of decisions, and the student's task is to exhibit the chain link by link, naming the pressure on each link and the alternative that was available, rather than leaping from a book to a regime in a single sentence.

### Q: Why do the four positions disagree about which evidence counts?

Because each position silently operates a hierarchy in which one kind of material is granted the status of the theory's authentic voice, with everything else demoted to background noise. The doctrinal break position treats the founding doctrinal text, read for its structural implications, as the voice, and practice as the text unfolding. The continuity position treats the whole corpus in its historical company as the voice, and practice as biography. The practical position reverses the polarity and audits texts as promises against dated performance. The fourth position treats the founder's corpus as the voice and the custodians' tradition as the corruption. Arguments built on different hierarchies cannot refute one another directly, since each rules the other's decisive exhibit secondary, which explains the debate's peculiar longevity. The remedy is not to abandon hierarchy, since some ordering of evidence is unavoidable, but to state it openly, so that a reader can see the rules of the court before the exhibits are called.

### Q: How does the April reversal serve both sides of the argument?

In April 1917 Lenin returned to Russia and persuaded his party to abandon the staged strategy it had been following: no support for the Provisional Government, power to the soviets, and a course pointing beyond the bourgeois stage of the revolution from the first day. The break camp quotes the episode as the moment the spine of the orthodox timetable was snapped by one man's will. The continuity camp quotes the same episode as a method being applied to a conjuncture the timetable could not describe, a weak bourgeoisie, a peasantry in arms, a dissolving front, and points out that every demand was argued from texts and precedents inside the inheritance. Both quotations are accurate, which is what makes April the debate's best test case. The episode separates readers who define orthodoxy by the timetable from readers who define it by the sources, and forces each to declare the definition instead of assuming it.

### Q: What would count as a break with Marx in method rather than conclusion?

Differences of conclusion are the weakest form of break, since applying a method to new conditions is supposed to produce new conclusions. A break in method would be a difference in the machinery by which conclusions are reached and corrected. The candidate the literature most seriously examines is epistemological: Marx's procedure keeps the theorist inside the process, answerable to the self-activity of the class whose movement supplies the test of the theory, while the party doctrine can be read as seating the theorist beyond the process's reach, inside a body whose rightness is vouched for by its own teaching and thus shielded against correction by those it claims to represent. On that test, the telling evidence is institutional. While the soviets in the revolutionary year retained the power to remove governments, the circuit stood open. After the faction ban of 1921 prevented even the party's own members from organising a correction, the circuit can plausibly be described as closed, and the break in method, if it is granted, is dated by the same calendar as the break in practice.
