The incentive problem is the objection most people reach for first when socialism is proposed, and it is usually reached for as a single sentence: why would anyone work hard, take care, or build anything new if the reward for doing so is detached from the doing? Stated that way, the charge feels less like an argument than like a fact about human beings, and arguments that feel like facts are rarely examined. This article examines it. The examination begins with a separation, because the single sentence conceals three distinct claims with three distinct fates. One claim concerns the effort of individuals. A second concerns the behaviour of enterprise managers who cannot be allowed to fail. A third concerns innovation, the funding and spread of what has never existed before. The first claim is weakened, substantially, by an empirical literature its proponents seldom mention. The second was confirmed, about as thoroughly as a social scientific claim can be confirmed, by the record of the state socialist economies. The third stands, and it is the one the popular version of the charge almost never states.

![A cooperative workshop bench with the three versions assessed, piece-rate tallies and an innovation ledger, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-12.webp)

That separation is the article's whole method, and it disciplines everything that follows. A reader who finishes these pages should be able to say which version of the objection a given speaker is making, what evidence bears on that version rather than on the others, and what the honest verdict on each version is. The verdicts differ, and the difference is the finding. Where the charge is usually debated as a referendum on human nature, the evidence supports something narrower and more interesting: effort turns out to be a surprisingly weak ground for the objection, management under soft discipline turns out to be a strong one, and the generation and diffusion of novelty turns out to be the strongest of all. The name this article gives to what survives is the innovation residue. Everything else in the incentive debate, the slogans about laziness and the slogans about greed alike, is commentary on claims that the evidence has already sorted.

## The incentive problem stated plainly

The canonical form of the objection can be stated without caricature. A socialist economy, on the definition the objection assumes, weakens or abolishes three connections that capitalist economies rely on. It detaches a person's material reward from that person's measurable contribution, because distribution follows need, membership, or a plan rather than a bargain struck in a labour market. It removes the threat of loss that disciplines enterprise managers, because an enterprise owned by society is not permitted to disappear when it performs badly. And it abolishes profit as the signal and the prize that tell producers which activities are worth undertaking and reward the ones who guess correctly. From those three detachments the objection derives its three predictions. Effort declines, because the worker who exerts more receives roughly what the worker who exerts less receives. Economy declines, because the manager who wastes inputs is rescued while the manager who saves them gains nothing durable. Novelty declines, because the developer of a new product or process cannot appropriate the gain, and so the risk that development requires is not borne. The objection is therefore not one prediction but a small theory, with a mechanism for each level of the economy: the bench, the office, and the laboratory.

Ludwig von Mises gave the charge one of its earliest systematic forms in his treatise on socialism, arguing that where the link between a person's work and a person's income is severed by common ownership, the psychological basis of sustained effort is removed along with it. Later critics generalised the point into a claim about human nature as such, as though the responsiveness to material reward were a constant of the species rather than a variable shaped by institutions. The Marxist tradition replied in several registers: that the charge mistakes the motivations produced by one set of institutions for the motivations possible under any; that alienated wage labour is itself a poor elicitor of effort, so the comparison is rigged from the start; and that new social relations would cultivate new motives, including the moral incentives that one socialist economy later debated in earnest. Each of those replies engages one version of the charge more than the others, which is precisely why the unseparated debate goes in circles. A reply built for the effort claim does nothing for the innovation claim. A concession owed on the managerial claim is not owed on the effort claim. Sorting the versions is not pedantry; it is the difference between an assessment and an exchange of slogans.

### Why does the one-proposition form of the charge mislead both sides?

It misleads because the bundled claims have opposite evidential fortunes, so any verdict on the bundle is wrong somewhere. The defender who answers the effort claim with cooperative evidence has not touched innovation. The critic who cites managerial failure has established nothing about effort. Each side picks the version it can win and treats the selection as the whole.

One further clarification belongs at the start, because it governs the comparisons throughout. The objection is sometimes stated against socialism as Marx described a higher phase of communism, where distribution by need replaces distribution by contribution, and sometimes against any economy of social ownership, including market socialist designs in which enterprises compete and incomes differ. Those are different targets. The effort version bites hardest against the first target and weakly against the second, since a market socialist economy retains wage differentials and can widen or narrow them by policy. The managerial version bites against any design in which enterprise failure is impossible, whatever the wage structure. The innovation version bites against every design so far proposed, because none has specified a convincing substitute for appropriable gain in funding, selecting, and diffusing novelty. Keeping the target in view matters as much as keeping the version in view, and the sections below state, for each verdict, which target it applies to.

## Three claims inside one charge

The effort version is the oldest and the most intuitive. It holds that individuals calibrate their exertion to their reward, that a system which pays the diligent and the idle alike will get idleness, and that collective ownership generalises the free rider problem from the small group, where it is familiar, to the whole economy, where it would be ruinous. Its psychological premise is that material self-interest is the reliable motive and that other motives, craft pride, solidarity, curiosity, duty, are real but too weak or too unevenly distributed to carry an economy's daily exertion. Its institutional premise is that monitoring cannot substitute for reward, because monitors must themselves be motivated, and the regress ends only where someone has a personal stake in the outcome.

The managerial version is quieter and, in the event, deadlier. It holds that the decisive actor in a planned or socialised economy is not the worker at the bench but the director of the enterprise, and that the director's incentives are set by the rules of survival. Where an enterprise cannot fail, where its losses are covered and its plan is negotiated rather than imposed by a market test, the rational director hoards inputs against future shortage, understates the capacity of the plant so that future targets stay reachable, resists shedding activities that lose resources because shedding brings no reward, and treats innovation as a threat to the plan fulfilment on which bonuses and careers depend. János Kornai named the underlying condition the soft budget constraint, and his analysis of shortage showed that the behaviours were not moral failings of individual directors but the predictable output of the constraint's softness. This version does not depend on any claim about human nature in general. It depends on the much more defensible claim that competent people respond to the incentives their institution actually sets.

The innovation version is the least discussed and the most difficult. It holds that new products and new processes are unlike routine production in ways that matter for institutional design. Development is expensive before it is profitable. Most attempts fail. The benefits, when an attempt succeeds, spill over to imitators and users who bore none of the cost. Under capitalism, the prospect of appropriating a temporary gain, through a patent, a head start, or a brand, induces firms and financiers to bear those costs and risks, and the market then performs the further services of selecting among rival attempts and diffusing the winners, as competitors copy what works or lose ground. Remove appropriation, and each of those three services needs a substitute: something must fund development, something must choose which of a hundred proposals deserves resources, and something must carry the successful result from the enterprise that produced it to every enterprise that could use it. The version's claim is that no socialist design has yet supplied convincing substitutes for all three services at once, and the state socialist record, in which invention in favoured sectors was respectable while diffusion across the economy was dismal, is its chief exhibit.

Holding the three apart also clarifies what would count as evidence for each. The effort version is tested wherever people work under collective or participatory arrangements inside market economies, because those settings isolate the reward structure from the rest of the socialist programme. The managerial version is tested by the behaviour of enterprises that cannot fail, which the state socialist economies supplied at scale and over decades. The innovation version is tested by comparing not the stock of inventions but the flow of adoptions, the speed at which a proven improvement travels from its first site to the whole economy. Much confusion in the literature comes from testing one version with another version's evidence: pointing to a brilliant priority sector as though it answered the diffusion charge, or pointing to a contented cooperative as though it answered the managerial one. The assessment below keeps each body of evidence with its own claim.

## The comparison basis: three contrasts that must not be merged

Every verdict in this territory depends on a choice that is often made silently: what socialism is being compared with. Three contrasts circulate in the debate, and they yield different results. The first compares a proposed socialist economy with capitalism as its own theory describes it, a world of alert entrepreneurs, disciplined firms, and rewards tightly fitted to performance. The second compares it with capitalism as it actually operates, with its principal-agent failures, its executive pay loosely connected to results, its short investment horizons, and its large zones of effort sustained by professionalism rather than by piece rates. The third compares it with the state socialist record, the only full-scale attempt so far, in which the managerial version was tested under a particular design of central planning and one-party rule. A claim can be true under one contrast and false under another. The effort version looks formidable under the first contrast and much weaker under the second, because actual wage labour elicits a great deal of reluctant, minimal, alienated effort, and the discretionary margin, the extra care that no contract can specify, is exactly where actual capitalism also struggles.

The third contrast requires the most care, because it is evidence about a design, not about every possible design. The state socialist economies combined social ownership with centralised planning, administered prices, and a political monopoly that suppressed the feedback channels, consumer complaint, independent auditing, a free press, through which failure might have been registered and corrected. A verdict drawn from that record convicts the combination. Whether it convicts social ownership in other combinations, with markets retained, budgets hardened, and feedback channels open, is a further question that the record alone cannot answer, though it can and does shift the burden of proof. The sections below observe this discipline throughout: each verdict names its contrast, and the innovation verdict, notably, is the one that survives all three, because the diffusion failure appears in the record, the design gap appears in the models, and the contrast with actual capitalism, whose diffusion machinery is imperfect but real, still leaves the charge standing.

### What changes when the contrast is actual capitalism rather than its ideal form?

The effort claim loses most of its force, because actual employment elicits exertion through supervision, professional norms, and fear of dismissal rather than through any fine fitting of reward to contribution, and discretionary effort is scarce there too. The managerial claim survives in altered form. The innovation claim survives nearly intact.

There is a corresponding discipline for the defence. Replies that work by pointing at capitalism's failures establish, at most, that the objection is not a comparative proof; they do not establish that the socialist design under discussion possesses the mechanism the objection finds missing. A tu quoque can clear the ground. It cannot build on it. The strongest replies in the literature are the ones that move from the failings of the contrast case to a positive account of how effort, economy, or novelty would be elicited under the proposed arrangements, and the assessment below grades replies by that standard: does the reply supply a mechanism, or does it only spoil the comparison?

## The three versions assessed

The table below is the article in miniature. It states each version's claim, the evidence that supports it, the evidence that tells against it, the best reply available to the defence, and the verdict this article reaches. The sections that follow the table earn each cell of it, and readers who want the argument rather than the summary should treat the table as a map to be checked against the terrain, not as a substitute for walking it. One feature of the table deserves emphasis before the detail begins. The verdicts are not symmetrical, and their asymmetry is the assessment's central result: a weakened claim about effort, a confirmed claim about management, and a standing claim about innovation. Any honest participant in this debate, on either side, has to start from that uneven landscape rather than from the flat one the slogans describe.

The rows also differ in the kind of certainty available. The effort verdict rests on a living empirical literature with the usual margins of dispute. The managerial verdict rests on a completed experiment of history. The innovation verdict rests on both, and on the absence, in the design literature, of the mechanism whose absence the record displays.

### Which version of the incentive objection is strongest?

the innovation version. Effort claims are weakened by evidence that worker-managed firms are at least as productive as comparable firms. The managerial claim was confirmed in state socialist economies. But no socialist model has a convincing account of funding, selecting, and diffusing innovation.

That ordering, innovation first, management second, effort last, reverses the order in which the charge is usually pressed, and the reversal is worth pausing on. The popular debate leads with effort because effort is the version that can be stated as an insult: people are lazy, socialism ignores human nature, the queue for the dole forms in the imagination before any evidence is consulted. The scholarly debate, when it is honest, leads with innovation, because innovation is where the defence's cupboard is barest. Between the two stands the managerial version, which neither side enjoys: critics understate it because it lacks the moral drama of the laziness charge, and defenders understate it because it was not a prediction but a post-mortem. The sections that follow take the versions in the popular order, effort first, so that the reader can watch the charge strengthen as it proceeds, and end where the argument should now be conducted.

## The effort version: the claim about individuals

The effort version deserves to be stated in its strongest form before it is tested, because its strongest form is not foolish. Begin with team production. Many kinds of work are done by groups whose joint output is easy to observe while each member's contribution is not. Armen Alchian and Harold Demsetz built an influential account of the firm on exactly this observation: where output is joint, the temptation to shirk is structural, and some arrangement for monitoring and for rewarding the monitor is required if effort is to be sustained. Bengt Holmström sharpened the problem for teams whose members share the proceeds: if the group's income is divided among the group, each member bears the full cost of an extra hour of effort and receives only a fraction of its return, and the arithmetic points toward under-provision unless a budget-breaking principal or some equivalent device intervenes. Mancur Olson's logic of collective action supplies the general form: rational members of a large group, each of whose contributions is invisible in the total, will free ride on the contributions of others unless selective incentives attach to contributing. Scale the team up to an economy, replace the sharing rule with distribution according to need, and the effort version follows: exertion becomes a donation to strangers, and donations, however noble, are not a basis for staffing an economy.

The claim also draws strength from a plausible psychology. Human beings do respond to material reward; the evidence for that is not in dispute, and any social theory that required people to be indifferent to their own material circumstances would deserve the ridicule the charge directs at it. The effort version's real premise is comparative and quantitative: that the response to material reward is so dominant, and other motives so much weaker, that detaching reward from contribution must produce a large fall in exertion, large enough to matter at the scale of an economy. That premise is empirical. It can be tested, and over the past half century it has been tested, in settings the charge's proponents rarely visit: firms owned by the people who work in them, operating inside market economies, paying their members from the proceeds, and surviving or failing by the ordinary tests of commercial life. If the strong effort claim were correct, those firms should be conspicuous failures, hives of shirking kept alive by subsidy or sentiment. The literature that studies them finds something else, and the something else is the single most under-reported body of evidence in this entire debate.

### How does the free rider argument work inside a single work team?

A team's joint output can be measured while each member's contribution cannot, so a member who eases off keeps a full share while surrendering only a small part of the total product. Unless members monitor one another, or shares track observed effort, the arithmetic rewards restraint and taxes diligence, and exertion settles below what the members could jointly gain.

Two qualifications belong with the strong form, because they mark where it stops applying. The free rider arithmetic assumes that members cannot observe one another, or cannot act on what they observe. In small and stable teams both assumptions fail: effort is visible, reputations form, and informal sanctions, from coldness to expulsion from the group, are cheap to apply and expensive to receive. The arithmetic also assumes that the only return to effort is the material share. Where members value the regard of their peers, the quality of the product, or the survival of an enterprise they regard as theirs, the return has other components, and the prediction weakens accordingly. None of this refutes the arithmetic. It locates it. The effort version is a claim about large, anonymous, unmonitored settings with purely material motivation, and the question for the assessment is how much of an economy actually resembles that description, and how much of the resemblance is itself a product of institutional choices rather than a constant of nature.

## The cooperative evidence: the firms that should not work

Worker cooperatives and labour-managed firms are the effort version's natural experiment. In such a firm the workers are the owners. They elect the management, they share the surplus according to rules they set, and the connection between any individual's exertion and any individual's income is diluted by exactly the mechanism the charge describes: one member's extra effort raises the common pool, from which that member draws only a fraction. If diluted connection meant collapsed effort, these firms should show it in the crudest measures: lower productivity than comparable conventional firms, higher failure rates, a drift toward hiring wage labour to do the work the members will not. A substantial research literature, built over decades and across several countries, has examined those measures, and its findings run in the opposite direction. The balanced summary of that literature is that worker-managed firms match, on average, the productivity of comparable conventional firms; that their pay structures are markedly more compressed, with smaller gaps between the highest and lowest paid; that their employment is more stable across the business cycle, members absorbing downturns through income rather than through dismissals; and that their survival rates are no worse than those of conventional firms, with several careful studies finding them better.

John Pencavel's studies of the plywood cooperatives of the Pacific Northwest remain the classic site-level evidence, because those cooperatives operated in the same industry, with the same technology, as conventional mills nearby, allowing comparison without the usual fog. The cooperatives performed at least as well as their conventional counterparts on productivity, and they differed in instructive ways: they adjusted members' earnings rather than membership when demand shifted, and they sustained effort through a combination of peer scrutiny and the knowledge that the proceeds stayed in the room. Virginie Pérotin's research on French worker cooperatives, using large samples of firms, found survival and productivity at least equal to conventional firms, with cooperatives if anything more durable. Studies of Italian cooperatives, which exist in large numbers across construction, manufacturing, and services, point the same way. Chris Doucouliagos's meta-analysis of the participation literature, aggregating many studies of worker participation in management and ownership, found positive associations with productivity overall. Meta-analysis cannot manufacture truth from weak studies, and the literature has its disputes, about selection, about capital constraints, about whether successful cooperatives are a surviving minority of a larger founding population. But the direction of the findings is not seriously in doubt, and it is fatal to the strong form of the effort claim. Where workers own the firm and share its proceeds, effort does not collapse. By the ordinary measures, it holds up.

### What exactly did the plywood cooperative studies compare?

They compared mills owned by their workers with conventionally owned mills in the same region and industry, using similar machinery to make the same product for the same markets. The cooperative mills matched or exceeded the conventional ones in output per unit of input, while behaving differently when demand fell: earnings were cut before membership was.

Why the cooperative result surprises only those who have not thought about monitoring is a point worth developing, because it converts the evidence from a curiosity into an explanation. In a conventional firm, monitoring runs downward and is resented as surveillance; the monitored have every reason to conceal effort's true level, and the monitor's own motivation is supplied by a salary that does not vary much with what the monitoring finds. In a cooperative, monitoring runs sideways. Each member's shirking is paid for, visibly and immediately, by the other members, who are present, who know the work, and who cannot be deceived about its pace by anyone who has not done it. Peer monitoring of this kind is information-rich in a way hierarchical monitoring rarely is, because the peers know what a hard hour looks like from inside. It is also legitimised by the sharing rule itself: the member who objects to a colleague's idleness is defending the common pool, not serving a distant owner. Add that members hold a residual claim, a share in whatever surplus their collective effort produces, and the cooperative turns out to be not a refutation of incentive reasoning but a rearrangement of it. The incentives are social and collective rather than individual and contractual, and they work.

The honest limits of this evidence matter as much as its direction, and a later section states them fully. Cooperatives remain a small share of employment in every market economy, and Henry Hansmann's analysis of enterprise ownership explains part of why: worker ownership is costly to govern where the workforce is heterogeneous in skill and interest, and it struggles to raise capital where members cannot pledge an asset they are forbidden to sell. Those are real constraints on generalising the cooperative form. But they are constraints about governance and finance, not about effort. The effort version claimed that people will not work without individual material reward tied to individual output. The cooperative literature shows people working, productively and durably, under arrangements where that tie is deliberately loosened, provided the group is stable, the work is visible to peers, and the proceeds are shared by a rule the members regard as theirs. The strong claim fails. What survives is a narrower claim about scale and anonymity, and narrower claims deserve narrower verdicts.

## Why working members watch each other

The mechanisms inside the cooperative result repay a slower look, because they identify the conditions under which effort survives the loosening of individual reward, and those conditions, rather than the cooperative form itself, are what a serious assessment should carry away. The first mechanism is visibility. Effort in most workplaces is not hidden from the people alongside; it is hidden from the people above. A lathe operator knows whether the neighbouring operator is working; a care worker knows whether a colleague's round was done properly; a programmer knows whose code arrives late and full of apologies. Institutions differ in whether that sideways knowledge has anywhere to go. In the cooperative it goes directly into the distribution of esteem, into decisions about membership, and into the informal economy of favour and obligation that every workplace runs beneath its formal chart. The second mechanism is stake. A residual claim, even a shared one, changes the meaning of waste: materials spoiled are the members' materials, customers lost are the members' customers. The third is voice. Members who can change the rules under which they work experience those rules as theirs, and the psychological literature on procedural fairness finds, consistently, that people exert themselves more willingly under arrangements they regard as legitimate, even when the arrangements are demanding.

None of these mechanisms is magic, and each has a failure mode the critics of cooperatives have correctly identified. Visibility decays with scale; a member of a ten-person workshop is watched, a member of a ten-thousand-person enterprise is a statistic. Stake dilutes with numbers in the same way. Voice becomes representative and then ceremonial as organisations grow. The cooperative evidence therefore supports a conditional conclusion: effort is robust to the loosening of individual reward where groups are small enough for peer knowledge to operate, stable enough for reputation to matter, and self-governing enough for the rules to feel owned. The state socialist economies satisfied none of those conditions for the ordinary worker, whose enterprise was vast, whose rules arrived from above, and whose share in the proceeds was notional. That the effort version failed in the cooperatives does not mean it failed everywhere, and the managerial sections below describe settings where the conditions were absent and the predicted behaviour duly appeared. What the cooperative evidence destroys is the universal form of the claim, the appeal to human nature as such. Human nature, on this evidence, is not the obstacle. Institutional design is the variable, which is a conclusion both sides should find usable.

## The behavioural evidence: when payment corrodes the motive

A second literature, from experimental and behavioural economics and from psychology, cuts into the effort version from a different side. It finds that the relationship between material reward and exertion is not the simple upward slope the charge assumes. Under identifiable conditions, attaching payment to an activity reduces the activity's performance or supply, because the payment changes what the activity means. Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's research programme on intrinsic motivation, and the meta-analysis Deci conducted with Richard Koestner and Ryan, found that expected tangible rewards, offered for engaging in tasks that people find inherently interesting, tend to undermine the intrinsic motivation to perform them, leaving total motivation lower when the rewards stop and sometimes while they run. Bruno Frey's work on crowding theory generalised the point for economics: external interventions, payments and controls alike, can crowd out the intrinsic motivations on which much real-world performance depends, particularly where the intervention is experienced as controlling rather than as acknowledging competence. The effect is not universal, and its boundaries are disputed, as the next paragraphs note. But its existence is established enough that no honest statement of the incentive problem can proceed as though reward and effort were joined by a single, reliable, positive link.

The canonical field case is Richard Titmuss's study of blood donation. Comparing systems that relied on voluntary unpaid donation with systems that paid donors, Titmuss argued that introducing payment reduced both the supply and the quality of blood: donors who had given from civic or altruistic motives withdrew when the act was repriced as a transaction, and the donors attracted by payment had, on average, stronger reasons to conceal disqualifying conditions. The study became a classic, and like most classics it has been argued with. Later experimental work, notably by Carl Mellström and Magnus Johannesson, found crowding out concentrated among women donors and sensitive to whether the payment could be redirected to charity, a result that refines rather than reverses the original claim: the meaning of the payment, not merely its size, determines its effect. The broader lesson survives the disputes. For activities sustained by civic or intrinsic motives, pricing the activity is not a neutral addition of an incentive. It is a transformation of the activity's social meaning, and the transformation can cost more motivation than the price buys.

### Why did paying blood donors reduce the supply of blood?

Payment repriced a civic act as a market transaction. Some donors who had given freely declined to sell what they had been glad to give, while the fee attracted more donors with reasons to hide conditions that should have disqualified them. Supply and quality fell together, even though a price had been added to the act.

The crowding literature matters to the assessment in two distinct ways. First, it breaks the effort version's implicit model of motivation as a single fuel that material reward tops up. Motivation is plural. People work for pay, and also for mastery, for the regard of colleagues, for the sense that the task is worth doing, for fear of shame, for the satisfactions of a job finished properly; and these motives interact, sometimes reinforcing and sometimes displacing one another. A payment scheme that strengthens the first motive while weakening the third and fourth can leave an organisation worse motivated than it found it, a result familiar to any manager who has watched a bonus scheme teach a workforce to ask what everything is worth. Second, crowding out identifies a class of work where pay for performance performs worst: complex work, judgement work, work whose quality is hard to measure and easy to fake. On such work, tying reward to the measurable proxy invites the proxy to be gamed and the unmeasured substance to be neglected. The state socialist economies, as the managerial sections show, ran the largest pay-for-performance experiment in history on exactly these principles, tying bonuses to plan indicators, and harvested indicator gaming on an economy-wide scale. The behavioural literature and the Soviet record, approaching from opposite directions, meet at the same conclusion: incentives are instruments with side effects, not levers with guaranteed outputs.

### Which kinds of task lose most when pay is tied to performance?

Tasks whose quality has many dimensions and resists measurement lose most, because reward fastens on the measurable dimension and effort migrates toward it, away from the unmeasured dimensions where much of the value lives. Teaching to the test and producing to the tonnage indicator are one failure in different uniforms: the metric is served and the purpose is starved.

None of this licenses the opposite slogan, that material incentives do not matter. They plainly do, and the same literature that documents crowding out documents crowding in, where payment acknowledges competence and supports autonomy, and documents large domains, routine, measurable, intrinsically unrewarding work, where piece rates raise output in the straightforward way the charge describes. The assessment's conclusion on the effort version is therefore a grading, not a reversal. As a universal claim about human beings, the effort version fails against the cooperative and behavioural evidence. As a claim about particular settings, anonymous, unmonitored, unrewarding work performed by people with no stake in the outcome and no voice in the rules, it retains force, and a serious socialist design has to answer it for those settings rather than pretend they will not exist. What the evidence does not permit is the move the popular charge depends on: from the truth that incentives matter, to the conclusion that only individual material reward tied to a person's own output, under threat of destitution, can elicit effort. That conclusion is not a finding. It is a habit of thought that the evidence has outgrown.

## The limits of the cooperative evidence

An assessment that reported only the cooperative literature's direction would be doing advocacy, not assessment, because the literature's limits are real and they mark exactly where the effort version retreats to defensible ground. The first limit is selection. Cooperatives are founded by people who choose them, often with unusual commitment to the form, and they are studied, disproportionately, after they have survived. A population of firms chosen for motivation and filtered by survival cannot show what the same rules would do to an unchosen population, which is what an economy is. The second limit is environment. Every cooperative in the literature operates inside a market economy: it buys inputs at prices it did not set, sells to customers who can leave, borrows from banks that can refuse, and hires in a labour market that prices its members' alternatives. The market supplies the cooperative with information, discipline, and an exit test that a whole socialist economy would have to supply by other means or go without. The third limit is scale, already noted: peer monitoring, the mechanism doing much of the work, is a small-group technology, and the literature's firms are mostly small or federated into small units. The fourth limit is capital. Hansmann's account of why worker ownership is rare in capital-intensive industries, where members would have to sink wealth they cannot later withdraw into assets they do not control, identifies a constraint that no amount of motivational evidence dissolves.

### Why can a successful cooperative inside a market not settle the whole question?

Because the cooperative borrows from its surroundings much of what a whole economy must generate internally: prices that report scarcity, customers whose departure punishes failure, banks whose refusal disciplines investment, and a labour market that prices members' alternatives. The evidence is gathered entirely inside that environment, and it is silent about whether the borrowed services can be replaced.

What the limits establish is a division of the question, and divisions of questions are what assessments are for. Whether individuals will work hard under shared ownership and loosened individual reward: the evidence says yes, under conditions of visibility, stability, and voice that institutions can be designed to protect. Whether an entire economy can be organised so that those conditions hold at every workplace, while prices, credit, and exit are simultaneously replaced by planning or by some other coordinating device: that is a different question, belonging partly to the calculation debate and partly to the managerial and innovation versions, and the cooperative literature does not pretend to answer it. The effort version, properly graded, is therefore neither the trump its proponents play nor the nullity its opponents claim. It is a design constraint. It says that arrangements for eliciting effort must be built, from peer structures, stakes, and voice, rather than assumed from human nature in either direction, and it warns that the building gets harder as scale and anonymity grow. A charge that ends as a design constraint has been answered in the only way charges of this kind can be: by being made precise.

## Equality inside the commune: the kibbutz as a hard case

If cooperatives test loosened reward, the Israeli kibbutz tested abolished reward. In the classical kibbutz, members received no wages at all. Consumption was communal or allocated by need, work was assigned by the community, and the connection between individual effort and individual material reward was not diluted but severed, more completely than any socialist state ever attempted for its general population. The effort version predicts collapse, and for most of the twentieth century the prediction failed: the kibbutzim were productive, innovative in agriculture, and durable across generations. Ran Abramitzky's research on the kibbutz explains the survival in terms that should by now be familiar. The communities were small and stable, so effort was visible and reputation binding. Entry was screened, so the population was selected for commitment to the arrangement. Social sanctions, from gossip to the withholding of esteem, were cheap to impose and costly to bear. And the equality was embedded in an ideology that members had chosen, which made the arrangement's demands feel like the price of a valued identity rather than an extraction.

The same research records the arrangement's eventual strains, and they are as instructive as the survival. As outside opportunities rose, the most able members, those with the most valuable alternatives, faced the strongest temptation to leave, and the community's equality was maintained partly by devices that limited the value of leaving: assets held in common could not be taken away, and social ties raised the cost of exit. Shirking existed and was managed rather than abolished, through monitoring by work managers and through the community's intense informal scrutiny. When the surrounding economy's rewards grew too large to ignore and the founding ideology thinned across generations, many kibbutzim moved, by their own votes, toward differential wages and privatised consumption. The kibbutz record thus supports both halves of this article's grading. Complete severance of reward from effort proved workable for generations under small scale, screening, ideology, and high exit costs. It proved fragile when those supports weakened, and the fragility appeared exactly where the effort version says it should: among the members whose outside options were best. The lesson is not that equality fails. It is that equality is an institutional achievement with running costs, and the costs rise with the wealth of the surrounding world.

## The managerial version: the enterprise that cannot fail

Turn from the bench to the office, and the evidential weather changes completely. The managerial version of the charge concerns the director of the enterprise, and its central concept is Kornai's soft budget constraint. A budget constraint is hard when an organisation that persistently spends more than it takes in must contract, be taken over, or close. It is soft when the organisation can expect, from experience, that losses will be covered: by subsidy, by renegotiated taxes, by cheap credit extended to keep it alive, by a plan revised downward to meet its performance. Kornai's insight was that the softness is not a detail of financial technique but a property of the relationship between the state and its enterprises, and that it systematically rewires the director's incentives. A director facing a hard constraint economises because waste threatens survival. A director facing a soft one hoards, because the binding scarcity is not money, which can always be obtained by pleading, but physical inputs, which cannot be conjured when the supplier fails to deliver. The same director understates capacity, because revealed capacity becomes next period's obligation. And the same director avoids shedding any activity, however wasteful, because size brings bargaining weight, allocations, and status, while contraction brings only a smaller plan and a smaller establishment.

Those economies confirmed this analysis at a scale and over a duration that social science rarely obtains. Their enterprises hoarded labour as well as materials, keeping workers on the books against future need in the midst of economies that officially suffered labour shortage, a paradox that dissolves once hoarding is understood as insurance. They concealed reserves of capacity and materials as a matter of routine professional competence; a director who revealed true capacity was understood by peers to have damaged the enterprise. They fulfilled plans in the letter while subverting them in substance, producing the assortment nobody had ordered in the sizes nobody wanted, because the indicator rewarded tonnage, or units, or value of output, and the indicator, not the user, was the customer who mattered. And they experienced chronic shortage not as a series of accidents but as the system's normal state, queues and empty shelves coexisting with warehouses of unwanted goods, because every enterprise's rational insurance against shortage was to demand and hold more than it needed, thereby deepening the shortage it insured against. The mechanics of the planning system that framed these behaviours, the bargaining over targets and the material balances, are set out in the article on [how Soviet planning worked](/2019/04/15/soviet-planning-how-it-worked/); the concern here is the incentive structure those mechanics created, and on that structure the evidence admits little dispute.

### What does a soft budget constraint do to a manager's arithmetic?

It reverses the meaning of prudence. Where failure is impossible and rescue routine, saving inputs earns nothing durable, because the savings are absorbed by the next plan, while surplus inputs insure against the supplier failures that threaten fulfilment. The manager therefore hoards, conceals capacity, and bargains for easy targets, because the institution has made them the content of competence.

Kornai's own framing deserves emphasis, because it determines what the confirmation confirms. He did not argue that socialist managers were worse people, or that the behaviours expressed a national character, or even that they expressed socialism as such. He argued that paternalistic protection by the state, of which soft budgets are the financial expression, generates the behaviours wherever it operates, and he noted that capitalist economies exhibit the same syndrome in miniature wherever enterprises are too connected or too large to be allowed to fail. The managerial version, in its rigorous form, is therefore a claim about a relationship, protection without exit, rather than about an ownership form. Its application to the state socialist economies is devastating because those economies universalised the relationship: every enterprise was protected, exit existed nowhere, and so the syndrome existed everywhere, without the counterweight of a sector where budgets were hard. A proposed socialist design that hardened budgets, by whatever institutional means, would not be refuted by this record. A proposed design that left budgets soft, on the ground that social ownership makes discipline unnecessary, would be refuted by it in advance.

## Hoarding, hidden capacity, and the ratchet

Three mechanisms carry the managerial verdict, and each has its own literature. The first is hoarding, already described: the accumulation of inputs, labour, and unfinished projects as insurance against an unreliable supply system, rational for each enterprise and ruinous in aggregate, since generalised hoarding is itself a cause of the unreliability insured against. The second is the concealment of capacity, the systematic understatement of what a plant can produce, maintained because plans are set by negotiation and capacity revealed in one plan becomes the next plan's quota. Joseph Berliner's studies of Soviet management documented the practice from the inside, in the memoirs and interviews of the managers themselves, for whom hiding reserves was not deviance but the elementary duty of a director who intended to fulfil future plans. The third mechanism is the ratchet effect, formalised by Martin Weitzman and known to every practitioner of the system: performance in one period raises the target for the next, so exceptional effort is punished by exceptional obligation, and the rational enterprise restrains its own performance to protect its future. The ratchet is the managerial version's purest exhibit, because it shows the incentive structure taxing exactly the behaviour, exceeding the plan, that the system's propaganda demanded. An economy that punishes its best performers with harder targets has not merely failed to reward excellence; it has fined it.

### How does the ratchet effect punish a manager who reveals capacity?

The coming period's plan is built from the level achieved in the present one, so a plant that reveals its true capacity receives future targets set at that level, with no allowance for the exceptional effort that reached it. Brilliant overfulfilment therefore purchases a harder obligation for every following year, and able directors learn to hold something back.

The three mechanisms share a feature that distinguishes the managerial version from the effort version philosophically. None of them requires any assumption about motivation beyond ordinary prudence. The hoarding director, the concealing director, and the restraining director are not shirking; they are working hard, often heroically, at the tasks the institution rewards, which are insurance, concealment, and restraint rather than economy, candour, and exertion. This is why the managerial version proved so much stronger than the effort version in practice. The effort version bet against human nature and lost in the settings where humans monitor and esteem one another. The managerial version bet on institutional logic, on the proposition that intelligent people serve the incentives in front of them, and the state socialist economies, staffed by intelligent people in enormous numbers, confirmed it for four decades. There is a corollary that defenders of planning have sometimes been slow to absorb: exhortation cannot fix a mechanism. The Soviet system exhorted constantly, through campaigns, honours, and moral appeals directed precisely at managers, and the mechanisms ground on regardless, because the appeals asked directors to act against the interests the system itself had assigned them.

## Shortage as a property of the system

The aggregate result of the managerial mechanisms was shortage, and Kornai's Economics of Shortage remains the indispensable account of why the shortage was systemic. In a market economy, shortage is a signal that raises price, attracts supply, and extinguishes itself. In the classical planned economy, the signal had nowhere to go. Prices were administered and sticky, so shortage expressed itself in queues, waiting lists, forced substitution, and the quiet degradation of quality, none of which fed back to producers as an instruction they were rewarded for obeying. Enterprises, facing buyers who had to take what was offered, operated in a permanent sellers' market, and a sellers' market abolishes the producer's incentive to please, to economise on the buyer's time, or to attend to the assortment actually wanted. The consumer's experience of the system, the queue and the empty shelf and the favour owed to the shop assistant, was therefore not a blemish on the managerial structure but its most visible surface. Where budgets are soft and buyers are captive, shortage is not a malfunction. It is the equilibrium.

This is also the point at which the managerial version connects back to the effort version and explains their different fortunes in the same economy. The ordinary worker in a state socialist enterprise faced an effort environment stripped of the conditions that made cooperatives work: the enterprise was huge, the proceeds of effort vanished into a plan the worker had no voice in setting, monitoring ran downward and was experienced as alien power, and the link between the enterprise's performance and the worker's reward was muffled by wage funds negotiated at levels far above the plant. The system thus combined the worst of both settings: it loosened individual reward without building the peer structures that make loosened reward work, and it softened enterprise budgets without building the feedback channels that might have substituted for exit. That a wry workplace proverb about pretending to work circulated across the whole bloc is not evidence of a people's character. It is evidence of an incentive structure described, by its own subjects, with perfect accuracy.

## The managerial verdict

The verdict on the managerial version can be stated without hedging, and this article states it: for the state socialist design as implemented, the version is confirmed. Enterprises that could not fail hoarded inputs and labour, concealed their capacity, restrained their performance against the ratchet, and shed nothing, and the resulting shortage economy persisted for the life of the system, surviving every reform that left the underlying softness intact. The confirmation is unusually strong by the standards of social science because the mechanisms were specified in advance by theory, observed in operation by participants and scholars, and reproduced across countries that differed in culture, wealth, and political history while sharing the institutional design. Where the design travelled, the behaviours travelled with it. That is what confirmation looks like.

The boundary of the verdict matters as much as its force. What is confirmed is a conditional: where budgets are soft, exit is absent, and prices do not discipline, managerial behaviour degenerates in the specified ways. The verdict does not show that social ownership as such produces the syndrome, because the record contains no case of social ownership combined with hard budgets, genuine enterprise exit, and open feedback over a long period; the Yugoslav experiment in self-management moved partway toward market discipline and produced a mixed record, better on assortment and consumer responsiveness, worse on investment discipline and macroeconomic stability, that fits the conditional reading better than either side's slogan. Nor does the verdict show that capitalism is immune: the syndrome appears inside capitalist economies wherever protection without exit is granted, in rescued banks and sheltered contractors, which is exactly what a conditional, mechanism-based verdict predicts. The managerial version thus ends in a position neither camp finds comfortable. It is a confirmed law about a relationship, and it convicts any future design, socialist or otherwise, that rebuilds the relationship and expects different behaviour. Designers of socialist economies who take the verdict seriously are driven toward hard budgets, exit, and feedback as non-negotiable components, and the interesting argument, taken up in the series' study of planning against the market, is whether those components can coexist with the social ownership they are meant to serve.

## Piece rates and Stakhanov: the planned economy pays by results

A complication belongs in the record before the innovation version is taken up, because it prevents a misunderstanding of everything said so far. The state socialist economies did not, in practice, detach reward from output in the simple way the effort version imagines. They were, on paper and largely in fact, economies of intense material incentive: piece rates covered much of industrial labour, bonuses were attached to plan fulfilment at every level, and differentials between the best and worst paid were substantial. The Stakhanovite movement of the nineteen thirties, named for a miner whose record shift was celebrated as a model, built an entire cult around the exceptional worker whose output shamed the norm, and rewarded its heroes with goods, housing, and fame. Whatever else the Soviet workplace was, it was not a place where the connection between performance and reward had been forgotten. The system's rulers believed in material incentives with the fervour of converts, and deployed them on a scale no capitalist economy has matched.

The complication deepens the assessment rather than disturbing it, because it shows what the incentive instruments actually did when they were attached to plan indicators inside soft budget constraints. Piece rates elicited output of the measured kind and indifference to everything unmeasured: quality, maintenance, safety, the condition of the machine left for the next shift. Bonuses for plan fulfilment elicited fulfilment of the indicator and manipulation of the indicator's definition, the storming of output into the period's final days, and the quiet renegotiation of the plan itself. Stakhanovite records, achieved with prepared conditions and support crews, taught the workforce not that effort was rewarded but that records were theatre, and the movement's legacy included resentment from ordinary workers whose norms were raised on the strength of staged performances. The lesson generalises beyond the case, and the behavioural literature states its modern form: an incentive is only ever an incentive to do what is measured, in the way it is measured, at the expense of what is not. The state socialist economies are therefore not evidence that material incentives fail. They are evidence that material incentives succeed at exactly the task they are set, and that setting the task, in an economy where prices do not report what users value, is the problem that returns in the measurement objection below. Effort was elicited, abundantly. It was elicited in the wrong directions, by instruments pointed at indicators, inside enterprises that could not fail. The incentive problem, in the event, was never mainly a problem of quantity of motivation. It was a problem of aim.

## The innovation version: the charge changes its object

With innovation, the charge changes character. Effort and economy concern the performance of known tasks: producing the year's steel, staffing the year's wards, with methods that exist and outputs that can be specified. Innovation concerns tasks that cannot be specified in advance, because their content is the discovery that they were possible. A plan can order a quantity of steel. It cannot order a quantity of novelty, because the order would have to describe the thing that has not yet been conceived. This asymmetry drives everything distinctive about the innovation version. Development work is costly, slow, and mostly fruitless; for every process that reaches production, many are abandoned at stages where the spending is already sunk. The returns, when they come, are uncertain in timing and often accrue to parties other than the developer: to imitators who copy without bearing the cost, to users whose gains no contract captures, to successor enterprises in other branches entirely. An economic system that wants innovation must therefore solve three problems that routine production never poses. It must induce someone to bear costs whose returns are speculative. It must choose, among far more proposals than can be funded, which deserve the attempt. And it must move the successes, once proven somewhere, everywhere they would help, against the resistance of producers whose existing arrangements the success disrupts.

Capitalism's solution to the three problems is untidy, wasteful, and real. The prospect of appropriable gain, a patent's temporary monopoly, a pioneer's head start, a brand built on being first, induces firms and the financiers behind them to bear development costs in the expectation of capturing enough of the return. Selection is performed by competition among rival attempts, most of which fail at their backers' expense rather than at the public's. Diffusion is performed by imitation under pressure: a rival's better process is copied because not copying it is to lose ground, and the pioneer's gain is eroded by the same copying that spreads the benefit, a built-in tension the system tolerates because the prospect of the temporary gain is what set the process going. None of this is efficient in the textbook sense. Duplicated effort, secretive research, patent thickets, and innovations designed to capture rent rather than to serve need are standing features of the arrangement. The innovation version does not claim that capitalism innovates beautifully. It claims that capitalism possesses a mechanism, however flawed, for each of the three problems, and that no proposed design has yet specified mechanisms of comparable power for all three at once. That is the claim the remaining sections test.

## Invention in the priority sectors

The first distinction the evidence requires is between invention and innovation, and the state socialist record is unintelligible without it. Invention is the production of something new: a device, a process, a design that did not exist before. Innovation, in the sense economists since Schumpeter have given the word, is the carrying of the new thing into general use, its adoption across the economy whose productivity it might raise. The state socialist economies were not bad at invention. In sectors the state prioritised, above all armaments, space, and heavy industry, their scientists and engineers produced work of the first rank, and even outside the priority sectors the flow of inventions, registered and unregistered, was substantial. A system that can concentrate resources on a defined goal, staff it with talent, and shield it from the need to please a customer is genuinely capable of impressive feats of creation, and the record shows it. Any version of the innovation charge that denies this, that pictures the planned economies as uniformly stagnant backwaters of technique, is false to the record and should be retired.

### What separates an invention from an innovation in this argument?

An invention exists when a new device or process has been demonstrated somewhere, under conditions that may be sheltered and exceptional. An innovation exists when the thing has entered use across the producers who could benefit from it, displacing routines in unsheltered conditions. The first is an event in a laboratory. The second is a change in an economy's habits.

The priority sectors prove the rule by their exceptional structure. A weapons programme or a space programme has a single, powerful customer who knows what he wants, pays without haggling, and tests the product against an unforgiving standard. It has guaranteed resources and a staff insulated from the plan indicators that governed ordinary production. In effect, the priority sector recreated, inside the planned economy, the two features whose absence the innovation version laments: a demanding selector, choosing among rival designs with real consequences for the losers, and a diffuser with authority, able to impose the chosen design across every relevant producer. Where those features existed, innovation, and not merely invention, occurred. The civilian economy had neither feature. Its customers were captive, its producers were rewarded for plan fulfilment rather than for improvement, and no actor in the system held both the knowledge that a better method existed somewhere and the incentive to impose it everywhere. The gap between the priority sectors and the civilian economy is therefore not a paradox to be explained away. It is the experiment that isolates the variable. Given selection and diffusion machinery, the system's people innovated. Without it, they invented and the inventions slept.

## Diffusion: the journey an invention never made

Joseph Berliner's study of the innovation decision in Soviet industry remains the foundational account of the civilian failure, because it reconstructed the choice as it appeared to the enterprise director who had to make it. Consider what adopting a new process meant from that chair. The adoption would disrupt current production while equipment was installed and workers retrained, endangering the fulfilment of the present plan, on which bonuses, standing, and career depended. If the innovation succeeded, the enterprise's reward was modest and temporary: the plan for future periods would be revised upward to absorb the new capacity, by the ratchet already described, converting the gain into a permanent obligation. If the innovation failed, the director bore the blame for the shortfall personally. The gains from adoption, meanwhile, accrued mostly elsewhere: to the ministry's statistics, to downstream users, to the economy in the abstract. The costs accrued at home. A director did not need to be lazy, corrupt, or hostile to progress to decline such a bargain; he needed only to be sane. Berliner documented the predictable results: enterprises preferred minor, safe modifications to substantial ones; they resisted innovations originating outside their own walls, which brought disruption without even the credit of authorship; and they adopted new techniques late, under pressure, or on paper, reporting adoption while production continued by the old method in the old assortment.

The diffusion failure had a second layer, between enterprises rather than within them. In a market economy, a successful innovation advertises itself through the innovator's results and is carried across the industry by imitation, by suppliers who standardise the new equipment, by engineers who change jobs and take their knowledge with them, and by journals, exhibitions, and consultants whose business is the traffic in technique. The planned economy possessed the journals and the exhibitions, and maintained an elaborate apparatus of technical information and model enterprises. What it lacked was the motive force. Imitation brought the imitator the same bargain the original adoption had brought the innovator: disruption now, a harder plan later, blame if anything failed. Suppliers had no reason to push new equipment on reluctant customers whose orders were allocated anyway. Engineers moved within a system that rewarded staying put. The result, documented across the scholarly literature on Soviet and East European technology, and surveyed in the article on [the economic record of the socialist states](/2019/06/15/socialist-states-economic-record/), was a characteristic pattern: long lags between first use and general use, whole branches running generations of technique side by side, and a civilian economy whose average practice drifted steadily behind its own best practice, so that the gap to be explained was not between the planned economy and the frontier abroad but between the planned economy's frontier and its own average. That internal gap is the diffusion failure in its purest form, and no appeal to foreign embargoes or inherited backwardness explains it, because the knowledge was already inside the house.

### Why did a proven improvement threaten the enterprise that adopted it?

Adoption disrupted output while the new method was installed and learned, and output was what the plan measured and the bonus rewarded. Success was absorbed into future targets by the ratchet, so the enterprise kept the disruption and surrendered the gain. Failure was personal. Improvement threatened the adopter because the arithmetic made it a cost and routine a safety.

It is worth pausing on the human meaning of this structure, because it completes the article's reversal of the popular charge. The diffusion failure was not caused by a deficit of ingenuity or of effort in the population. Soviet engineers were ingenious; Soviet directors were energetic; the system's technical intelligentsia produced inventions in profusion and argued for them in the journals with passion. The failure was caused by an incentive structure that made the rational custodian of each enterprise a conservative, and then multiplied that conservatism across every enterprise in the economy. Where the effort version looked for a flaw in human nature and did not find it, the innovation version looks for a flaw in the structure of rewards surrounding novelty and finds it documented at every level: in the director's arithmetic, in the ministry's indicators, in the absence of any actor paid to carry improvements across boundaries. An objection that can name its mechanism, show the mechanism in the testimony of the participants, and predict from it the precise pattern the record displays, the priority sectors excepted exactly where the mechanism was suspended, has earned the standing this article gives it.

## Funding, selecting, diffusing: three gaps, not one

The innovation version is often answered as though it were a single gap, and the answers fail because the gap is triple. Take the three services in turn. Funding is the most tractable. Development must be paid for before it returns anything, and a socialist economy disposes of the social surplus centrally, so it can, in principle, allocate generous resources to research and development, and the state socialist economies did exactly that, maintaining large research establishments and spending heavily by any comparative standard. Whatever their failures, a simple shortage of research money was not among them. A proposed design that funds development from public resources is therefore not fantasising; it is describing something that has existed. The funding gap, considered alone, is closable, and the publicly funded research record discussed below shows it being closed in capitalist economies too.

Selection is harder. At any moment, the proposals competing for development resources vastly outnumber the resources, and most proposals, including most plausible ones, will fail or disappoint. Someone or something must choose, and the choice must be made under genuine uncertainty, where the chooser cannot know which attempt will succeed and must be willing to be wrong repeatedly in public. Markets perform this selection brutally and redundantly: many backers choose independently, so the system does not need any single chooser to be wise, and the failures are liquidated by the backers' losses rather than defended by a ministry's prestige. A planning apparatus selects differently. It selects through committees, institutes, and ministries, whose members bear asymmetric risks of their own: a failed project chosen is a visible error with names attached, while a successful project declined is an invisible one, since nobody can prove what the road not taken would have yielded. Committee selection under those risks drifts toward the safe, the incremental, and the sponsored, toward projects continuous with existing programmes and defended by established institutes. The drift is not corruption. It is the selection mechanism's own incentive problem, one level up, and it reproduces, inside the funding body, the same conservatism the diffusion problem shows inside the enterprise.

### Why is selecting among projects harder than funding them?

Funding requires only that resources be set aside, which a state disposing of the surplus can do by decision. Selection requires judgement under uncertainty by choosers who are punished visibly for backing failures and rewarded invisibly, if at all, for successes they declined to block. Many independent backers can be wrong in parallel. A single hierarchy cannot.

Diffusion is the hardest of the three, for the reasons the previous section documented: it requires action by the adopters, who are numerous, dispersed, and rationally reluctant, rather than by a funder, who is one. A design might solve funding by allocation and selection by some ingeniously insulated jury, and still face an economy of enterprises each of which experiences adoption as cost without gain. The three gaps also interact. Weak diffusion devalues selection, since even a well-chosen project yields little if nobody adopts its results. Weak selection wastes funding on the safe and the sponsored. And the prospect that successes will not diffuse discourages the funders from risking selection on bold proposals in the first place. This is why partial answers to the innovation version, however genuine, do not accumulate into a full answer. The version's claim is about the trio as a system, and it stands until a design addresses the trio as a system. The name this article gives the surviving claim, the innovation residue, marks exactly that: after the effort version has been graded down and the managerial version assigned to its proper conditional place, what remains on the table, unanswered, is the problem of how novelty is to be financed in its risk, chosen in its uncertainty, and carried across an economy whose custodians gain nothing by carrying it.

## Design responses and their record

The design literature has not been idle in the face of the three gaps, and an assessment owes its proposals a hearing before it grades them. One family of responses retains markets for most purposes and socialises ownership, expecting competition among socially owned enterprises to supply selection and diffusion pressure while public or cooperative finance supplies the funds. This is the market socialist family, and its treatment of innovation is its least developed chapter: competition does discipline existing products, but the appropriability problem reappears inside it, since a cooperative or municipal enterprise that develops a costly novelty watches rivals adopt the result while the developer's members bore the cost, and the design's own rules for sharing gains across the movement damp the pioneer's reward that the mechanism requires. A second family leans on the state as innovator of last resort: mission programmes on the model of the priority sectors, extended to civilian goals, with the state as demanding customer. The priority sector record shows this can work where the goal is definable and the customer is competent; it works least well for the diffuse, consumer-facing, taste-driven innovation that constitutes much of economic renewal, where no ministry can specify the target because the target is discovered by the attempts themselves. A third family proposes prizes, honours, and professional glory as the pioneer's reward, supplemented by inventor's certificates of the kind the Soviet system itself issued. Honours motivate, and the record of science shows how powerfully priority and reputation can drive effort; but honours do not capitalise an enterprise, do not compensate the adopter's disruption, and do not select among proposals, and the Soviet experience with inventor's certificates, which granted recognition while the state took the invention, is generally read as a demonstration of their insufficiency rather than their promise.

A fourth family, the most serious, proposes to socialise the risk while pluralising the choosers: multiple public funds, cooperatively owned banks, and regional development bodies, each free to back rival projects, each bearing the consequences of its portfolio, so that selection is performed by competing public backers as markets perform it by competing private ones. This design answers the selection objection in form. Its difficulties are practical and political: public backers whose losses are ultimately the public's drift toward softness, becoming the soft budget constraint in a new costume; and the diffusion gap remains untouched by any arrangement of funders, since adopters still face their own arithmetic. The survey in the companion article, [whether planning can beat the market](/2019/10/15/can-planning-beat-the-market/) follows these models in their contemporary forms. For the assessment here, the summary is enough: every design family closes one or two of the gaps, none closes all three, and the one that comes closest on selection leaves diffusion, the gap with the worst historical record, exactly where it found it. The innovation version survives the design literature as it survives the historical record, and for the same reason: the missing piece is a mechanism, not a sentiment, and mechanisms cannot be gestured at.

## Public money and foundational innovation

The strongest single reply to the innovation version begins from an embarrassment for the charge: a substantial share of the foundational innovation of the modern economy was not funded by appropriable gain at all. Mariana Mazzucato's work on the entrepreneurial state documented, case by case, how the core technologies of the digital age, the internet, satellite navigation, touch interfaces, and much else, originated in publicly funded research programmes whose backers could not appropriate the returns and did not try. Universities, mission agencies, and public laboratories have repeatedly borne the riskiest, longest-horizon development, leaving private firms to commercialise what the public purse de-risked. If funding radical novelty required the prospect of private appropriation, this record should not exist. It exists, and it demonstrates that the funding gap can be closed by public action not as a hope but as a habit of the most innovative capitalist economies themselves.

### What does the public research record leave unanswered?

It answers who pays for foundational research, and it is silent on the questions that follow. Which of a thousand applied proposals deserves development for a particular industry's use, and by what process a proven result travels to every producer who needs it, are questions the public laboratory does not face, because markets performed those services downstream of its work.

The reply's limits are the ones the misreadings section will formalise. Public funding in the cases studied operated upstream of a market economy that performed selection and diffusion on the results: firms chose which public research to commercialise, competed to do it first and best, and spread the successful products because spreading them was profitable. Remove the downstream market, and the public funder inherits the selection and diffusion problems it previously outsourced. Nor does the record show public programmes excelling at the applied and consumer end of innovation, the thousand incremental improvements in products and processes, each too small for a mission programme, whose aggregate is the texture of rising productivity. The honest grading of the reply is therefore partial credit of a high order. It refutes the crude form of the innovation version, in which nothing new can be funded without private gain. It leaves standing the refined form, in which funding is the easiest of the three services to replace and the other two remain unaddressed by any mechanism yet demonstrated. A defence that rested here would have shown that socialism can have laboratories. The charge, refined, was never about laboratories.

## The measurement objection: reward needs a measure planning cannot supply

There remains the strongest objection in the entire territory, which the assessment has been approaching through the piece-rate section and now states at full strength, in the form its ablest critics gave it. Grant, the objection says, everything the defence has won so far. Grant that effort can be elicited by peer structures and intrinsic motives, that budgets can in principle be hardened, that development can be publicly funded. The incentive problem then returns in a deeper form, as a problem of measurement. Differential reward, on which every incentive scheme depends, requires knowledge of relative performance: who contributed more, which enterprise used its resources better, which manager's results reflect skill and which reflect easy targets or rich endowments. In a market economy, prices and profits supply a common measure, imperfect but independent, against which performances can be compared without the evaluator needing to understand each production process from inside. Without market prices, the evaluator must construct the measure, and every constructed measure is a plan indicator, subject to the gaming, distortion, and quiet corruption that the state socialist record displays at every level. The incentive problem, on this objection, was never fundamentally about motivation. It is about the impossibility of knowing, in a priceless economy, what performance is worth rewarding, and it is therefore a cousin of the knowledge problem in the calculation debate, arising inside the theory of incentives rather than the theory of allocation. Hayek's argument that the knowledge relevant to economic decisions is dispersed, local, and largely inarticulate applies here with undiminished force: the evaluator cannot collect from below the information that a reward system would need, because much of that information exists only in the acting, and the rest is falsified in the reporting by the very rewards attached to it. Don Lavoie's development of the Austrian case pressed the same point against every scheme of simulated markets and shadow prices: a measure administered by the authority being measured against is not a measure but a negotiation.

The objection must be answered, not applauded, and the honest answer has two parts. The first part surveys what planned and administered systems actually used in place of prices, because they did not reward blindly; they built proxies, and the proxies deserve a fair description before their costs are counted. Output indicators, tonnage, units, value of output at administered prices, gave a crude common scale within branches. Comparisons among similar enterprises, the yardstick method, allowed ministries to judge a plant against its peers rather than against an absolute standard, and socialist emulation campaigns publicised the leaders to shame the laggards. Tournaments of honour, banners, orders, and titles, rewarded relative standing without money. Norms engineered from time study purported to measure the labour content of tasks directly. Each proxy captured something real. Yardstick comparison among genuinely similar plants does reveal slack. Norms do discipline the grossest idleness. Honours do motivate, as the crowding literature's account of esteem predicts. A system with no better instruments would rationally use all of them, and did.

### Why can an evaluator not simply watch performance and reward it?

Because performance is a judgement, not a visible substance, and it needs a standard that can compare unlike things: the manager with rich deposits against the manager with poor ones, the new plant against the old. Prices perform that comparison impersonally. Without them, every standard is constructed by an authority, and the constructed standard becomes the target of everyone's effort.

The second part of the answer is the concession, and it is owed in full. The proxies are second best by construction, and their costs are not incidental but structural. An indicator, once rewards attach to it, is gamed: output by tonnage yields heaviness, output by unit yields flimsiness, output by value yields assortment drift toward the expensive. The evaluator, knowing the figures are gamed, discounts them, and the discounting is itself a negotiation in which the enterprise's bargaining skill counts for more than its performance. Yardsticks compare the incomparable whenever plants differ in endowment, and the differences are always arguable, so the argument replaces the measurement. Tournaments of honour decay into allocations of honour by rotation and favour. Above all, the measure never achieves the one property that makes prices serviceable for reward: independence from the measured. A price is generated by parties with no stake in flattering a particular enterprise. A plan indicator is generated inside the hierarchy that the enterprise can lobby, deceive, and capture. The measurement problem is therefore real, and this article concedes it as the strongest form of the incentive objection, the form in which the objection survives every reply that the effort and managerial debates have produced. What the concession does not concede is that the problem is unique to socialism in kind. Large capitalist organisations live inside it too, wherever transfer prices are administered within firms, wherever executives are rewarded on metrics their own boards constructed, wherever a division's performance is judged by a headquarters that the division lobbies. The difference is one of scope and of exit: the market economy surrounds its administered islands with real prices and real failure, which discipline the proxies from outside. A wholly planned economy has no outside. The measurement objection, graded properly, thus joins the managerial verdict as a conditional law: administered measures corrupt in proportion to their monopoly, and a design that monopolises measurement across an entire economy will be governed, in the end, by the gaming of its own indicators.

### What did planners use instead of prices to judge performance?

They used constructed proxies: physical output indicators in tonnes, units, or administered value; engineered labour norms; comparisons among similar plants, with leaders publicised and laggards summoned; and tournaments of honour rewarding relative standing with banners and titles. Each proxy captured something genuine, and each bent behaviour toward itself once rewards attached, because the measured learn faster than the measurers.

## The replies: the corporation under the same light

The replies to the incentive charge begin, in most presentations, with the failings of the other side, and the assessment has already warned that such replies clear ground without building on it. They still deserve their full weight, because the charge is comparative in its usual deployment, and a comparison is only as strong as its contrast case. The contrast case, examined closely, is not the economy of the textbooks. The large capitalist corporation is itself an island of administered incentives, in which reward and performance are connected only through hierarchies, budgets, and constructed measures strikingly similar to the ones the measurement objection criticises. Michael Jensen and William Meckling's agency theory formalised the problem: where ownership and control separate, managers pursue their own interests, in quiet life, in empire building, in risk avoided or risk dumped on shareholders, and the owners must spend real resources on monitoring and bonding to narrow the gap. The corporation, on this account, does not solve the incentive problem. It manages it, at a cost, with instruments that fail regularly and sometimes catastrophically.

Executive compensation is the contrast case's most embarrassing exhibit. The charge against socialism assumes that capitalist reward tracks contribution closely enough to elicit effort and economy. At the top of the large corporation, where the rewards are largest, the tracking is notoriously loose. Jensen and Kevin Murphy's well-known study of executive pay found the sensitivity of chief executives' wealth to their firms' performance to be remarkably small, a result replicated in spirit by decades of subsequent observation: pay that rises with the market's tide rather than the manager's hand, options that reward luck, golden parachutes that pay for failure at rates no dismissed planner ever enjoyed. Below the executive floor, the picture is mixed in ways the effort sections have already used: much exertion in capitalist firms is elicited by supervision, professional norms, team pressure, and the desire for promotion or security, with fine-grained pay for performance reserved for the measurable margins and often resented or gamed where it is imposed. None of this shows that capitalist incentives do not work at all; plainly the system elicits enormous effort and economy. It shows that the eliciting is done by a bundle of mechanisms, several of them social and normative rather than material and individual, and that the bundle's failures are not marginal. The comparative charge therefore cannot be run in its simple form, in which a finely tuned capitalist incentive order is set against a socialist void. Both systems are incentive orders with characteristic pathologies. The assessment's question is which pathologies attach to which version of the charge, and on effort and on measurement the pathologies turn out to be shared more widely than the slogans allow, while on innovation the asymmetry survives the sharing.

### What does executive pay reveal about the contrast case?

It reveals that the largest rewards in the capitalist firm are among the least tightly connected to performance, rising with market-wide movements, rewarding luck as readily as judgement, and cushioning failure with negotiated exits. The system sustains effort at its summit, as elsewhere, substantially through status, ambition, and professional pride rather than through the fitting of reward to contribution.

There is a further reply in this family, directed at the managerial version, that deserves statement because it is the strongest form of the tu quoque. Soft budgets are not a socialist invention. Capitalist states rescue banks, airlines, automobile firms, and defence contractors whose failure is judged intolerable, and the rescued exhibit, in the interval of their protection, recognisable features of the syndrome: muted economy, lobbying substituted for performance, survival treated as an entitlement. Kornai himself, in his later work, insisted on the point, treating softness as a syndrome of paternalistic protection wherever it occurs. The reply does not acquit the state socialist design, which universalised the protection; a disease that capitalism catches in patches, socialism as implemented made endemic. But it does convict the lazy form of the managerial charge, in which softness is attributed to social ownership rather than to protection without exit. Precision here is not pedantry. A designer who learns the lazy lesson, that ownership is the variable, will nationalise with hard budgets and be surprised by success, or privatise with soft ones and be surprised by failure. A designer who learns the precise lesson watches the budget constraint, and the precise lesson is the one the evidence teaches.

## The replies: effort under actual wage labour

The second family of replies addresses the effort version at its root, by contesting its picture of the work it uses as a standard. The charge compares socialist effort against the effort of the wage labourer as though the latter were wholehearted. The Marxist tradition's oldest category says otherwise. Under alienated labour, as the article on [alienation at work](/2011/06/15/alienation-at-work-today/) develops the theme, the worker's exertion is external to the worker: the product, the process, and the pace belong to another, and the rational response is the one the record of industrial relations documents across two centuries, the restriction of effort to what the bargain, the supervisor, and the work group jointly enforce. The famous studies of output restriction, from the Hawthorne investigations onward, found work groups in capitalist factories enforcing informal ceilings on production, punishing rate-busters, and treating the official incentive scheme as an opponent to be managed. Piece rates, the purest capitalist effort instrument, generated their own pathology: workers concealed improved methods from time study, because a revealed improvement meant a cut rate, the ratchet effect in its original capitalist dress. The parallel is exact and worth dwelling on. The ratchet that Weitzman formalised for Soviet planning was discovered, decades earlier and an ocean away, on the floors of American machine shops. Wherever a revelation becomes the next period's standard without compensation, revelation stops. The mechanism is not socialist. It is human, institutional, and general.

Discretionary effort is the margin that matters most here, and it is the margin where the wage relation is weakest. Every organisation depends on exertion that no contract specifies and no supervisor can compel: the extra check, the warning passed to a colleague, the problem solved rather than reported, the customer handled with a care the manual cannot describe. This margin is supplied, where it is supplied, by identification with the work and the workplace, by professional pride, by reciprocity toward an employer perceived as fair. It is withheld, where it is withheld, by the perception that the surplus of care flows to parties who neither notice nor reciprocate. The cooperative evidence showed the margin expanding where workers held a stake and a voice. The alienation reply argues that the wage relation, as ordinarily constituted, contracts it, and that a socialist design which gave workers real governance over their enterprises would be competing for the margin on favourable ground. The reply does not answer the managerial or innovation versions, and this article does not pretend otherwise. It answers the effort version's implied contrast, and it answers it well: the standard against which socialist effort is to be measured is not the devoted proprietor of the textbooks but the actual wage labourer, whose devotion is rationed, whose methods are concealed from the rate-setter, and whose discretionary margin is the prize both systems are really competing for.

## The replies: moral incentives and the Cuban debate

The third family of replies is the only one that was ever tried as a governing doctrine, and its trial is the Cuban debate of the nineteen sixties. Revolutionary Cuba, building a socialist economy under siege conditions, argued internally about the place of material reward with a seriousness no other socialist state matched. On one side stood the position associated with Carlos Rafael Rodríguez and the advocates of enterprise self-financing: material incentives, profit-linked funds, and interest charges were necessary instruments, and an economy that refused them would pay in inefficiency for its purity. On the other side stood Ernesto Guevara, whose budgetary finance system proposed to treat the whole economy as a single enterprise, to fund its units from the budget rather than from their own earnings, and to build the new society's motivation on moral incentives: emulation, honour, voluntary labour, and the consciousness of building a different kind of order. Guevara's argument was explicitly about the long run of human motivation. Material incentives, he held, were a lever inherited from capitalism that would reproduce capitalism's motives in the people who used it; a society that paid for every exertion would train its citizens to ask the price of everything, and the training would outlast the transition it was meant to serve. The full record of his economic thought is the subject of the article on [Che Guevara's Marxist theory](/2018/11/15/che-guevara-marxist-theory/); the assessment here needs the debate's structure and its outcome.

### How did the Cuban debate divide over moral and material incentives?

One side held that material reward was an indispensable instrument whose refusal would be paid for in inefficiency, and designed finance to preserve it. The other, Guevara's, held that material reward was a capitalist lever that would train the motives it claimed merely to use, and proposed budgetary finance with moral incentives, honour, emulation, and voluntary labour, in its place.

The outcome is usually summarised too quickly by both camps. Cuba did not sustain the Guevara system in its pure form; within a few years the economy moved back toward material incentives, enterprise funds, and the other instruments of the opposing design, and the later Cuban economy used moral appeals as a supplement rather than a foundation. Critics read the reversal as the effort version's vindication: moral incentives were tried, and they failed. The record supports a more precise conclusion. Moral incentives in Cuba were tried under conditions of scarcity, siege, and administrative improvisation that would have strained any motivational system, and they were tried at the level of the whole economy, where the conditions the cooperative evidence identifies, small scale, visibility, chosen membership, were absent by construction. What the episode shows is that moral motivation does not scale by proclamation: a ministry cannot declare esteem into existence, and honour distributed by a bureaucracy becomes another administered indicator, gamed and discounted like the rest. What the episode does not show is that moral motivation is unreal. The voluntary labour campaigns mobilised genuine exertion, the literacy campaign remains a standing exhibit of what organised idealism can do, and the Cuban health and education services sustained professional commitment through decades of material stringency that the pure effort model cannot explain. The precise verdict matches the pattern of this whole assessment. Motivation is plural, its social forms are powerful at the scale where communities confer them, and they cannot be nationalised by decree. A design that builds small-scale communities of esteem into its workplaces has a real resource. A design that substitutes exhortation for mechanism has a slogan.

## The second economy: effort that the plan could not see

One of the most revealing bodies of evidence about effort under state socialism comes from the sector the plan did not organise: the second economy of private plots, moonlighting, informal repair, and unrecorded exchange that grew up inside every state socialist society. The same workers who were proverbially reluctant on the state's time worked, in their unofficial hours, with an intensity and ingenuity that observers never tired of remarking. The peasant who drifted through the collective farm's day rose before dawn for the household plot. The factory fitter who conserved energy through his shift ran an evening practice of repairs whose quality the state's service enterprises could not approach. The builder moonlighting for private clients worked at a pace his official brigade never saw. Critics of socialism cite the second economy as the effort version's cleanest proof: remove the reward, and effort withers; restore it, even illegally, and effort blooms. The proof is real but it proves something more specific than the slogan. The second economy's effort was elicited not merely by payment but by a whole configuration: the worker chose the task, kept the proceeds entire, dealt directly with a customer whose satisfaction decided repeat business, and worked at a scale where reputation travelled by word of mouth. It was, in structure, a cooperative of one, possessing every condition, visibility, stake, voice, and feedback, whose absence in the official sector this article has been cataloguing.

Read that way, the second economy is less a vindication of the charge than a diagnosis of the official economy's failure to build those conditions into lawful work. It also complicates the moral picture in ways both sides prefer to skip. The second economy fed on the first: its materials were diverted from state stores, its hours were stolen from state shifts, its tools walked out of state workshops, so that the celebrated private exertion was partly a transfer, of energy and resources, from the social sector to the private one, and the official economy's slack was partly the shadow cast by the unofficial economy's brightness. A system that makes honest exertion pointless and dishonest exertion profitable has not discovered a law of human nature. It has built a machine for misallocating effort, and the misallocation, not a deficit of willingness, is the finding. The willingness was there all along, queuing at dawn for the private plot. What was missing was an official order capable of deserving it.

## The household plot: a controlled comparison inside one economy

Within the Soviet-type economies, agriculture preserved something close to a controlled experiment on the effort question, because it ran two incentive regimes side by side on similar land with the same people. The collective and state farms worked the great majority of the land under the standard official arrangements: work points, brigade organisation, rewards weakly connected to the harvest the brigade actually gathered. Alongside them, households held small private plots, farmed in the family's own time, whose produce the family consumed or sold. The plots occupied a minor fraction of the cultivated land. Their share of output, in the products households chose to grow there, vegetables, fruit, potatoes, milk, eggs, meat, was out of all proportion to their size, a disproportion reported consistently across the bloc and across the decades, and all the more striking because the plots received the leavings of the family's energy after the official day was done. Direction is all the protocol of this article permits, and direction is all the argument needs: the same households, on the same soil, in the same climate, produced markedly more per unit of land where the proceeds were theirs entire than where the proceeds dissolved into the collective.

The experiment's interpretation needs the same care as the cooperative evidence, because the two regimes differed in more than the reward rule. The plots grew labour-intensive products suited to hand cultivation, while the socialised sector carried the grain and the heavy field work where machinery mattered and private advantage was small. The family on its plot worked with knowledge no brigade could match, of its own soil's moods and its own animals' conditions, and it worked at the hours the crops required rather than the hours the plan prescribed. Selection ran the other way too: the plot's output was partly a measure of effort diverted from the collective fields, rested workers reserving themselves for their own ground. Even with every qualification entered, the comparison retains its force against the strong effort claim in one direction and against the complacent defence in the other. Against the charge: the households' exertion on the plots was elicited by a bundle in which direct retention of the product was joined to autonomy, knowledge, and family obligation, and the bundle, not the cash, is the replicable lesson. Against the defence: the collective farm's failure to elicit comparable care, over decades, with the same people, is a fact about the design of the collective farm, its divorce of effort from result and of decision from knowledge, and no invocation of the plot's special products dissolves it. Two regimes, one people, opposite exertions: the variable was the institution, and the institution is a choice.

## The Yugoslav middle case: self-management between plan and market

Between the cooperative inside capitalism and the command economy stands the case that partisans of both camps cite and neither can wholly claim: Yugoslav self-management. From the early nineteen fifties, Yugoslav enterprises were formally governed by their workers' councils, operated in markets for most goods, retained a share of their earnings for distribution and investment, and competed, within limits, with one another. The design was, in effect, an attempt to answer the incentive charge by construction: to give workers the stake and voice of the cooperative at national scale, to keep the market's discipline on enterprises while socialising their ownership, and to let income differences among firms reflect performance so that effort and economy would be rewarded collectively. The record, over four decades, was genuinely mixed, and its mixture is diagnostic. On assortment, quality, and responsiveness to consumers, Yugoslav enterprises behaved more like market firms than like their counterparts in the command economies; the sellers' market and the chronic shortage of the Soviet type were largely absent, and visitors remarked on shops that looked like shops. On the managerial side, the pathologies were different from Kornai's catalogue rather than absent: enterprises were reluctant to expand employment, since new members diluted the income per member that the existing workforce was maximising; investment was financed heavily by bank credit on soft terms, importing the soft budget problem through the banking system; and regional and republican politics fragmented the market and sheltered local champions. Effort within the Yugoslav firm appears, from the studies available, to have been unremarkable in either direction, neither the collapse the charge predicts nor a festival of self-exploitation, which is itself a finding: given stake, voice, and market discipline together, ordinary people worked ordinarily, and the drama of the incentive debate drained away.

The case's value for the assessment is that it separates variables the other cases bundle. Yugoslavia socialised ownership while retaining markets, prices, and a measure of enterprise autonomy, and the incentive pathologies that dominated were not the Soviet ones: not hoarding against shortage, since shortage was not systemic; not the suppression of assortment, since consumers could refuse; but the pathologies of income-per-member maximisation and soft credit, which belong to the specific design of the workers' claim and the banking system rather than to social ownership as such. Nothing in the Yugoslav record answers the innovation version: Yugoslav industry licensed foreign technology extensively and generated little frontier novelty of its own, and its diffusion, while better than the command economies', rode on the market mechanisms the design had retained rather than on any socialist substitute for them. The middle case thus confirms the article's grading from a third direction. Where markets and stakes were present, effort and economy were adequately elicited, whatever the ownership form. Where the design reached the innovation frontier, the familiar gap reappeared, unclosed by self-management's genuine achievements elsewhere. Ownership, the variable the popular debate treats as decisive, keeps failing to be the variable that decides.

## Judgement work: where both systems run on unpriced motives

There is a sector of every modern economy where the incentive debate's terms quietly cease to apply, and its size is an embarrassment to the charge's simple form. Teachers, nurses, researchers, judges, carers, clergy, soldiers, and the whole archipelago of professions perform work whose quality cannot be specified in a contract, cannot be monitored without destroying it, and cannot be paid by the piece without corrupting it. These occupations run, in every economic system so far observed, on a compound of salary, professional ethic, peer esteem, and intrinsic interest in the work, with material reward present as a condition of fairness rather than as a meter of output. Capitalism does not pay its judges by the verdict or its surgeons by the stitch, and where it has experimented with paying its teachers by measured results, the results have been a seminar in the crowding and gaming literatures. Socialism did not pay its corresponding professions by the piece either, and the Soviet Union's doctors and teachers, working for salaries that were modest by any standard, sustained services whose failures are generally attributed to underinvestment and shortage rather than to any refusal of exertion. The professions are the standing demonstration that advanced economies already trust unpriced motivation with their most consequential work. They do not refute the incentive problem; the professions are small worlds of selection, training, and peer scrutiny, resembling the cooperative's conditions more than the mass factory's. But they fix the debate's centre of gravity. The question is never whether human beings can work without piece rates, since civilisations run on the fact that they can. The question is which institutional conditions extend the professions' motivational compound to wider fields, and which destroy it, and that is a question about design, not about nature.

The professions also expose a quiet asymmetry in the charge's deployment. When a nurse works a double shift from duty, the act is credited to professionalism, a motive the charge's framework cannot see and therefore does not count. When a collective farm brigade works indifferently, the act is debited to socialism's incentive failure. A bookkeeping that records only the debits will reach its conclusion before the evidence is opened. The assessment has tried to keep both columns. Effort under every system is a compound of material reward, supervision, peer regard, professional ethic, fear, habit, and meaning, in proportions that institutions influence and none abolishes. The incentive problem, properly understood, is the study of those proportions and their institutional conditions. The slogan version, in which one system has incentives and the other has none, is not a contribution to that study.

## The popular form: human nature and the queue

Because the charge circulates in a popular form far cruder than any version assessed so far, and because the popular form is what most readers will meet outside this article, it deserves direct examination. The popular form runs: socialism ignores human nature; people are selfish; therefore socialism fails. Each step is weaker than the one before. The premise about selfishness, in the strong form the argument needs, is not supported by the evidence of any discipline that studies it. Human beings are conditional cooperators in the experimental literature: they contribute to common endeavours when they expect others to contribute, punish free riders at cost to themselves, respond powerfully to fairness and to the judgement of their peers, and calibrate their selfishness to the institutions around them with a sensitivity that makes nonsense of the idea that the institutions do not matter. Societies get the behaviour their arrangements reward, esteem, and make visible, which is why the same species staffs both the blood service and the trading floor without changing its nature at the door. The inference from selfishness to failure is weaker still, because it assumes that a viable socialism requires unselfishness, which no serious design does. The designs assessed in this article require only what the evidence supports: that effort follows stake, visibility, and voice; that managers follow the hardness of their budgets; and that novelty follows mechanisms for bearing risk and spreading results. The first two requirements are satisfiable, as the record shows in both directions. The third is the open problem. A popular argument that cannot distinguish the satisfiable requirements from the open problem is not an argument about socialism. It is a mood about people.

The popular form also survives by a trick of tense: it treats the state socialist record as the whole of the evidence and the whole of the possibility. The assessment has used that record heavily, for the managerial verdict and for diffusion, and has drawn from it the strongest conclusions in the article. But a record of one design family, implemented under one-party rule in conditions of scarcity and siege, is evidence about that family, weighted heavily against any design that shares its mechanisms and lightly against designs that differ in the mechanisms the verdicts identify. That is how evidence works in every other field, and the incentive debate is not exempt. The reader who has followed the grading can now see the popular form's function clearly: it exists to prevent the grading. So long as the charge is one proposition about human nature, nobody has to ask which version, which contrast, which mechanism. The moment those questions are asked, the debate becomes interesting, the verdicts become uneven, and the slogans on both sides lose their employment.

## The small innovations: suggestion schemes and the rationalisation that stalled

Between the heroic invention and the routine plan stood a third category of novelty in the state socialist economies, and its fate refines the diffusion verdict. This was the small innovation: the worker's or engineer's improvement to a tool, a layout, a sequence, the thousand minor rationalisations by which production actually advances in any economy. The Soviet system solicited these with genuine energy. It maintained a vast apparatus of invention and rationalisation societies, received suggestions in enormous numbers, paid small bonuses for adopted proposals, and celebrated its rationalisers in the press. The apparatus was not a sham; the suggestions were real, many were valuable, and the flow of them testifies again against any picture of a passive workforce. The failure lay downstream, in adoption. A suggestion that changed a process threatened the same arithmetic as any innovation: disruption to the current plan, a revised norm absorbing the gain, a bonus for the suggester that was trivial beside the risk borne by the manager who authorised the change. Enterprises therefore adopted suggestions selectively, preferring those that eased plan fulfilment without disturbing it, and shelving those whose benefits were large precisely because they were disruptive. Studies of the system found the characteristic pattern of the whole innovation record in miniature: generation abundant, implementation rationed by the adopter's incentives, and a widening gap between what the economy knew and what it did.

The small innovation case matters because it removes the last shelter of the diffusion verdict's critics. It might be argued that frontier innovation failed for reasons of scale or secrecy or foreign technology denial, reasons external to the incentive structure. No such argument reaches the rationalisation scheme. Here the knowledge was domestic, the improvements were proven at the bench where they were proposed, the costs were minor, and the state's own apparatus existed to spread them. What was missing was only the adopter's reason. When an economy cannot diffuse even the improvements its own workers hand it, the constraint is not knowledge, not resources, and not the frontier. It is the structure of reward around adoption, and the structure is the charge. The innovation version, in its diffusion component, rests on evidence of this intimacy and ordinariness, which is why this article grades it as standing rather than merely plausible.

## The consumer frontier: where the diffusion failure was lived

The diffusion failure was not an abstraction in a ministry's statistics. It was the texture of daily life in the state socialist economies, and the consumer sector is where its costs concentrated. Consumer goods are the domain where innovation is most diffuse and least plannable: thousands of products, shifting tastes, quality that lives in details no specification captures, and producers who must learn what is wanted from the wanting itself. The planned economies handled this domain worst of all, for reasons the assessment's mechanisms predict exactly. The producer faced a captive customer and a tonnage or value indicator, so assortment narrowed to what was easy to count, quality sank to what could not be rejected, and novelty arrived, when it arrived, as a decision from above rather than as a response to unmet desire. The queue and the favour and the imported good hoarded as treasure were the consumer's experience of the innovation problem, and the political consequences were not trivial: a system that promised to surpass capitalism in the satisfaction of needs was judged by its citizens, daily and unforgivingly, at the shop counter, and the judgement accumulated into the cynicism that hollowed the system's legitimacy long before its formal end. Defenders reply, correctly, that the same economies delivered security, housing, and basic provision that markets often fail to deliver, and the reply belongs in any whole assessment of the record, which the article on [the replies to the standard critiques](/2019/12/01/marxist-replies-to-critiques/) pursues across the full range of charges. But on the specific ground of consumer innovation, the reply does not engage the mechanism. Security was delivered by allocation. Novelty required adoption by producers who gained nothing by adopting, for customers who could not leave. The shop counter was the diffusion problem with a queue in front of it.

## Diffusion without appropriation: the extension service and the open standard

Fairness to the defence requires examining the strongest counter-evidence on diffusion itself, because diffusion without appropriation is not unknown in market economies, and its instances identify the conditions under which carrying improvements across producers can be organised as a public service rather than left to competitive pressure. The outstanding case is agricultural extension. The land-grant universities and extension services of the United States, and their counterparts elsewhere, existed precisely to diffuse innovation: to test new varieties and methods, demonstrate them on working farms, and persuade thousands of independent producers to adopt what had been proven elsewhere. The system worked, over generations, and it worked without the extension agent or the experimenting station appropriating the gains. Its conditions deserve attention. The adopters were independent owners who captured the benefit of adoption directly and bore its risk knowingly, so the adopter's arithmetic, the variable that failed in the planned economy, pointed the right way. The diffusers were professionals whose standing depended on the usefulness of what they carried, and whose information came from a research system connected to practice. Diffusion was organised, in short, where adoption was in the adopter's interest and the carrier's reputation rode on the cargo. A second case is the open technical standard: where industries converge on shared specifications, through standards bodies, professional associations, and the published literature of engineering, improvements travel as knowledge rather than as property, and adoption is driven by compatibility and by the adopter's own customers. A third is open science itself, where priority and citation reward disclosure, and the producer of a result gains by its widest use rather than by its restriction.

These cases refine rather than refute the diffusion verdict, and the refinement is worth stating exactly. Diffusion without appropriation succeeds where the adopter gains by adopting. The planned economy's failure was not that it lacked extension agents, it had them in profusion, in its technical information institutes and model farms and exhibitions of achievement. Its failure was that the adopter, the enterprise, lost by adopting, in disrupted plans and ratcheted targets, and no extension service can argue an enterprise out of its own arithmetic. The lesson for design is therefore precise and constructive: a socialist economy that wants diffusion must first repair the adopter's arithmetic, by insulating adopters from the ratchet, rewarding adoption as it rewards output, and letting the gains of a successful adoption rest with the adopter long enough to be worth having. Whether such repairs are compatible with central planning is doubtful, since the ratchet is the plan's own method of setting targets. Whether they are compatible with social ownership in a market setting is a live design question, and the extension cases show the shape a positive answer would take: diffusion as a profession, adoption as a gain, and knowledge carried by people whose standing depends on its usefulness. The innovation residue is not a proof that these repairs are impossible. It is the observation that no design has yet embodied them at scale, together with the record of what happens, over decades, when they are absent.

## The genealogy of the charge: three literatures, not one

The three versions have separate birthplaces, and tracing them explains why the debate's participants so often argue past one another. The effort version descends from the earliest controversies over socialism, when critics of the first socialist programmes asked who would do the unpleasant work and defenders answered with machinery, rotation, and the transformation of motives; Mises gave the question its canonical economic form in the interwar treatise already cited. The managerial version descends from the observation of the Soviet system itself, assembled by emigré scholars, by the managers' own memoirs, and by the Western Sovietology that Berliner and David Granick represented, before Kornai gave it theoretical form in the shortage analysis. The innovation version descends from the comparative study of technological progress, from the Amann and Cooper volumes on Soviet industry and from the broader literature on national innovation systems, which noticed that the planned economies' problem was not the laboratory but the factory floor's distance from it. Each literature has its own standards, its own canonical cases, and its own heroes, and a participant formed in one of them experiences the others as distractions. The popular charge, by contrast, has no birthplace; it is the sediment of all three, compressed into a sentence about human nature that none of the three literatures, read carefully, actually supports in its simple form. There is a lesson in the genealogy for the reader of the wider debate. When a critic says incentives, ask which literature he is standing in. When a defender answers, ask the same. Most of the noise in this controversy is the sound of three different arguments being conducted as one.

## Group size and the economy of esteem: the effort version's last refuge

One refinement of the effort version survives every test applied so far, and honesty requires giving it a section of its own. The mechanisms that rescued effort in the cooperative, the commune, and the professions, peer visibility, reputation, esteem, shared stake, are all technologies of small and stable groups. They weaken as groups grow, as membership churns, and as the connection between a person's conduct and a person's standing is mediated by records, supervisors, and strangers. An economy is not a small group. It is a system of millions, most of whose members will never meet, and the effort question for an economy is whether the small-group mechanisms can be federated, nested inside workplaces small enough to sustain them, with the workplaces themselves disciplined by something at the larger scale. The cooperative federations of the market economies are, in effect, experiments in exactly this nesting: primary cooperatives of workable size, owned in turn by secondary bodies that supply scale without dissolving the primary group's visibility. Their existence shows the nesting is possible. Their modest share of employment shows it is not easy, and Hansmann's governance costs explain part of the difficulty: as the primary group's membership diversifies in skill and interest, the cost of deciding together rises, and the esteem economy frays into faction.

The refined effort claim, then, is a claim about scale and heterogeneity, not about selfishness: the larger and more diverse the unit across which reward is pooled, the weaker the social mechanisms that substitute for individual reward, and the more the system must fall back on supervision, measurement, and material differentiation, with all the pathologies the measurement objection catalogues. This version is true, important, and compatible with a wide range of socialist designs, since it constrains the size and pooling of units rather than forbidding social ownership. It also cuts against a certain romantic strain in the socialist tradition, the strain that imagines the whole economy as a single workshop animated by a single solidarity. The evidence assembled here suggests the opposite architecture: solidarity is a small-group product, and an economy that wants its benefits must manufacture small groups and protect them, rather than proclaim a solidarity of millions that no mechanism sustains. The effort version's last refuge is therefore a design principle in disguise, and the assessment counts that as the best possible outcome for a critical charge: to end, not as a slogan, but as a specification.

## Misreadings this assessment refuses

The brief for an article like this one is a set of corrections, and they are gathered here because each is a live error in the wider discussion. The first misreading treats the objection as a single claim, so that evidence about effort is allowed to settle questions about innovation and the confirmation of the managerial version is treated as a verdict on human nature. The second holds that cooperatives are less productive than conventional firms, a claim the empirical literature does not support and generally contradicts. The third treats the managerial version as speculation, a theorist's conjecture about what might happen under socialism, when it is in fact a description of what did happen, documented by the system's own managers. The fourth asserts that moral incentives were never attempted, when a socialist state debated them at the highest level, built a finance system on them, and retreated from the experiment for reasons this article has examined. The fifth, and the most consequential for the defence, holds that publicly funded research answers the innovation version. It answers the funding component, genuinely and importantly. It does not address selection among applied proposals or diffusion across producers, the two components on which the historical record is worst, and a defence that presents the funding answer as the whole answer has mistaken a third of the problem for the problem.

A sixth misreading belongs to the critics, and it is the mirror of the fifth: that the innovation version condemns every form of social ownership equally. It does not. The version's mechanism runs through the adopter's arithmetic and the selector's incentives, which are features of designs, not of ownership as such, and the extension cases show diffusion organised publicly where the adopter's arithmetic points the right way. What the version condemns is the combination, social ownership with administered targets, soft budgets, and no gain to adoption, which happens to be the combination the twentieth century implemented. Keeping the condemnation addressed to the combination is not a courtesy to the defence. It is the condition of the verdict meaning anything for the future, since a verdict against a variable that was not the operative one teaches designers to change the wrong thing.

## Wage differentials in practice: the oscillation the theory forgets

The effort debate is usually conducted as though socialist economies had actually compressed rewards to equality, and the historical record shows something more interesting: a permanent oscillation between egalitarian principle and differential practice, in which the differentials always returned. The early Soviet state proclaimed wage equalisation and even, for a period, party members' salary caps; within years it was denouncing levelling as a petty-bourgeois error and rebuilding steep differentials for skill, responsibility, and output, complete with the piece rates and Stakhanovite prizes already described. China passed through similar cycles, denouncing material incentives as revisionist in its radical phases and restoring them as indispensable in its reforming ones, while Cuba's debate, examined above, staged the same argument as a matter of high doctrine. The oscillation is itself evidence, and it cuts in two directions. Against the effort version's strong form: the differentials that returned were administered, political, and often resented, yet effort did not track them in any simple way, and the economy's gravest exertion failures, in the collective fields and the consumer services, persisted through periods of wide differentials, because the missing conditions were stake and feedback rather than spread. Against the complacent defence: no governing socialist party, however egalitarian its founding doctrine, was willing to operate for long without material differentiation, and the unanimity of that retreat, across cultures and decades, is a datum about the management of large economies that no theory of motivation should wave away. The mature conclusion is the one the grading has been approaching from several sides. Differentials are an instrument, neither the engine of effort the charge imagines nor the contamination the purists feared. They work where they are attached to real differences in contribution that somebody can observe, and they decay into bargaining counters where they are attached to indicators. The variable is never the spread itself. It is the quality of the observation behind it, which returns the argument, once again, to measurement.

## Selection inside the plan: how ministries chose

The selection gap deserves a closer look at the machinery that filled it, because the machinery was neither stupid nor idle, and its failure mode is instructive for any design that proposes to choose projects by deliberation. Research and development in the planned economies was organised through branch institutes attached to ministries, academies with their own networks, and design bureaus attached to great enterprises. Proposals rose through these channels, were reviewed by expert councils, and were funded according to plans that coordinated, on paper, the whole republic of science. The system's genuine strengths were concentration and patience: it could sustain long programmes, build unique facilities, and hold a technical direction for decades without asking whether the direction would pay. Its pathologies were the ones the selection analysis predicts. The institutes were judged by their own ministries, which defended them as empires; the expert councils were staffed by the directors of the programmes they reviewed; the plan's coordination was achieved by dividing territory, so that each branch developed its own version of a technology behind its own wall, and the walls were the scandal of the system's later reformers. A proposal that threatened an established institute's programme threatened the reviewers' own life's work, and the system's conservatism at the frontier of civilian technique was not a failure of intelligence but a success of self-preservation. Compare the market's selection at its worst: herd behaviour, fashions in funding, bubbles that finance the absurd alongside the promising. The market's saving grace is pluralism of error: its choosers are many, their mistakes are uncorrelated, and the failures are liquidated without a ministry's honour requiring their defence. The plan's selection had one chooser per territory, errors that were systematic, and liquidation that amounted to an admission no career could afford. Any future design for public selection has to engineer the pluralism deliberately, multiple funds with rival mandates and real losses, or it will rebuild the council of incumbents under a new name. That engineering is possible to describe. It has not yet been operated at national scale for a civilian economy, and the selection component of the innovation residue stands on exactly that gap between the describable and the operated.

## The coherence of the shortage system: why the pathologies held together

Kornai's deepest claim was that the state socialist economy was not a collection of separate malfunctions but a coherent system, in which each pathology supported the others and partial reform was therefore unstable. Soft budgets made hoarding rational; hoarding made shortage chronic; shortage made buyers captive; captive buyers removed the pressure to improve assortment and quality; the absence of that pressure removed the enterprise's reason to innovate; and the political structure that guaranteed softness, the party's responsibility for every enterprise's survival, made the whole arrangement self-locking, since hardening a budget meant accepting failures for which the party, as universal guarantor, would be blamed. The coherence thesis explains the record's most frustrating feature for reformers: the repeated failure of well-designed partial reforms, in Hungary, in the Soviet Union of the nineteen sixties, and elsewhere, each of which granted enterprises autonomy or profit retention while leaving the guarantee intact, and each of which watched the enterprises use their new freedoms to bargain more effectively rather than to behave like market firms. Autonomy without the possibility of failure is a louder voice in the negotiation, nothing more. The thesis also disciplines the assessment's verdicts. It warns against reading the managerial confirmation as a verdict on any single institution, since the behaviours flowed from the system's joints rather than from any one part. And it supplies the innovation version with its systemic form: diffusion failed not because one mechanism was missing but because the system's coherence required its absence, the adopter's conservatism being one expression of the same guarantee that produced the hoarding and the shortage. A design that proposes to keep the guarantee and fix the innovation has not understood the thesis. A design that abandons the guarantee has accepted the managerial verdict and owes an account of what then disciplines the enterprise, which is the calculation debate's territory and the market socialists' hardest question. Either way, the argument has been moved to its proper ground, which is what the innovation residue, as a claim, exists to do.

## What markets do badly: the innovation charge's blind side

The innovation version, standing as it does, must not be allowed to harden into a claim it never made: that the market's innovation machinery is good. Its pathologies are structural and familiar. Appropriability rewards the capturable, not the valuable, so ingenuity flows toward innovations whose gains can be fenced, patentable tweaks to existing products, features designed for lock-in, and away from improvements whose benefits are diffuse and unfenceable, however large. Secrecy, the pioneer's protection, taxes the whole system, duplicating work in parallel laboratories and delaying the cumulative exchange on which science itself depends. Selection by private backers is plural but correlated by fashion, so the system's errors arrive in waves, whole sectors overfunded in a bubble and starved in the reaction, and the liquidation that disciplines failure also destroys teams and knowledge that a steadier selector would have preserved. Short horizons, imposed by capital markets that reprice every quarter, bias the portfolio toward the quick and the marginal. And the diffusion that competition performs is diffusion of what sells, which is not the same as diffusion of what is needed, as any survey of the world's unmet technical needs, in sanitation, in neglected disease, in the maintenance of unglamorous infrastructure, will show. The innovation verdict is therefore comparative in a precise sense. It does not say that capitalism innovates well. It says that capitalism possesses machinery covering funding, selection, and diffusion of novelty, machinery with known and serious defects, and that the socialist designs so far proposed and the socialist economies so far operated have lacked machinery of comparable reach, defects and all. A flawed machine and a missing machine are different problems, and conflating them is the last error the assessment has to correct.

## Needs that prices cannot hear

The defence's strongest ground on innovation is not the laboratory but the direction of search. Prices transmit the wants of those with money, weighted by the money they have, and are silent about the wants of those without. An innovation system steered by appropriable gain will therefore search brilliantly in the directions the wealthy demand and feebly in the directions the poor need, and the historical record obliges with its standing exhibits: the diseases of poverty that attract a trickle of research beside the flood devoted to the conditions of affluence, the agricultural problems of poor farmers in dry regions neglected beside the problems of rich farmers in temperate ones, the housing and sanitation technologies of informal settlements left undeveloped while the consumer electronics of the rich world renew themselves annually. This is not an argument that planning directed search well; the planned economies directed search toward the state's priorities, which were armaments and heavy industry, and the consumer's needs fared worse there than anywhere. It is an argument that the direction of innovation is a political question under every system, decided by whoever holds the power to fund and select, and that the market's answer to the question is not neutral but weighted, silently and massively, by the distribution of income. A future socialist design could in principle out-perform the market on direction while matching it on machinery, by funding search toward unmet need through plural public backers of the kind the selection section described. The principle has never been operated, and the assessment files it where it belongs: as the defence's most promising unbuilt argument, the one place where the innovation debate could yet be turned rather than merely survived. Its existence does not weaken the residue. It specifies what a defeater would have to include: not only machinery that funds, selects, and diffuses, but a demonstration that the machinery can be pointed at needs the price system cannot hear, and can keep finding them.

## Mission programmes in market economies: selection by the state, once removed

Between the priority sectors of the planned economies and the ordinary market stands a hybrid that both sides cite: the mission programme of the capitalist state, in which a public agency sets a singular goal, funds rival teams, and procures the result. The great wartime and Cold War programmes are the standard exhibits, and their innovation record is genuinely impressive, not only in the artefacts produced but in the techniques diffused outward into civilian industry afterward. The hybrid's lesson for the assessment is double-edged. For the defence: selection by a public body is not inherently sclerotic, since these programmes chose among competing designs, with failures cancelled and the sky intact, and diffused their results through contractors and publications; the council-of-incumbents pathology is a risk of public selection, not its essence. For the charge: the mission programmes succeeded under conditions that reproduce the priority sectors' exceptional structure, a single competent customer, a definable goal, an external test of success, and a surrounding market economy that absorbed the diffusion burden through the contractors' ordinary commercial life. The goal's definability is the crux. A missile either reaches its target or it does not; a vaccine either protects or it does not. The diffuse innovation of a consumer economy has no such test, and its goals cannot be stated in advance by any customer, however competent. Mission programmes therefore show what public selection can do at the singular end of the spectrum of goals, and the innovation version's claim concerns the spectrum's other end. The two exhibits, the priority sector and the mission programme, face each other across the systemic divide and make the same point from opposite sides: where a goal can be defined and tested, concentrated public effort innovates under any ownership. Where goals must be discovered by the search itself, the machinery of discovery is the question, and it remains open.

## The tradition's own answer: labour as a need

No assessment of the incentive charge is complete without the Marxist tradition's deepest reply, which is not a mechanism but a claim about what work is. In the writings of the young Marx and in the programme sketched in The German Ideology, labour under communism was imagined as ceasing to be merely a means of life and becoming, in the later phrase, life's prime want: activity undertaken as an expression of human capacity, in which the division between work and self-realisation dissolves. The mature Marx was more guarded, distinguishing the realm of necessity, where labour remains compelled by need in any society, from the realm of freedom that begins beyond it and rests on the shortening of the working day. But the tradition carried the early vision forward as its horizon, and the incentive charge is, at bottom, a refusal of that horizon: the critic denies that exertion can ever be its own reward at the scale of a whole economy, and the defender's oldest instinct is to assert that it can, once the conditions that poison exertion, its alienation, its compulsion, its subordination to another's profit, are removed. The evidence assembled in this article allows the quarrel to be graded rather than merely restated. The cooperative, communal, and professional records show activity sustained and even flourishing without piece rates, under conditions of autonomy, visibility, and meaning, which is the horizon's partial, local vindication. The mass economy's record shows the horizon receding exactly where those conditions cannot be manufactured by decree. The honest conclusion is that Marx's vision names a real possibility of human motivation that institutions can approach or destroy, and that neither the critic's denial of the possibility nor the defender's proclamation of its arrival survives contact with the evidence. What survives is a research programme for institutional design: find the conditions under which work engages the motives that the professions and the cooperatives display, and extend those conditions as far as scale permits. That programme is less thrilling than the horizon and more useful, and the incentive debate, properly conducted, is one of its working papers.

## The queue as unpaid labour: the consumer in the incentive system

Incentive analysis usually stops at the factory gate, but the planned economy's incentive structure extended into the household, and its costs there complete the picture of the managerial verdict's reach. Where shortage is chronic and prices do not clear markets, the work of obtaining goods is transferred to the consumer, who pays in time what the system declines to charge in money. Queuing, searching, cultivating the shop assistant, maintaining the network of acquaintances through whom deficit goods move, repairing what cannot be replaced, hoarding against the day of need: this was a second shift of unpaid labour, falling disproportionately on women, that the official statistics never counted as work and the official ideology never counted as a cost. Its incentive significance is diagnostic. A system reveals its real reward structure by where it makes people spend their effort, and the planned economy made its citizens spend enormous effort on acquisition rather than production, on knowing a person rather than on making a thing. The consumer's ingenuity, like the worker's in the second economy, was abundant and misdirected, and the misdirection had the same source: the absence of any mechanism by which the effort of serving the consumer was rewarded in the producer. In a market, the queue is a price signal and an invitation to a competitor. In the shortage economy, the queue was a permanent institution with no addressee. The citizen who waited three hours for shoes was experiencing, in person, the diffusion and assortment failures of the producer economy, translated into the currency of her own time. Any accounting of the incentive problem that omits the queue has left the largest group of the system's workers, its consumers, out of the ledger.

## Reading a new proposal: the assessment applied

The grading reached in this article can be carried to any new design as a set of questions, and stating them plainly is a service to readers who will meet proposals this article has not seen. For the effort component, ask how the design elicits exertion in its largest and most anonymous workplaces: what do peers see, what do members hold, what voice do the rules give those who live under them, and what happens to the worker whose outside option is valuable enough to leave. For the managerial component, ask what happens to an enterprise that fails: who covers the loss, who may close it, what information reaches the public when it performs badly, and whether the answers describe an exit or a negotiation. For the innovation component, ask the three questions separately: who bears the cost of development that fails, who chooses among rival proposals and what happens to choosers who choose badly, and what does an enterprise gain by adopting another enterprise's improvement in the first years after adoption, before the gain is absorbed into its obligations. For the measurement component, ask what standard rewards are attached to, who constructs the standard, and what the measured can do to the standard besides obey it. A design that answers all four sets of questions with mechanisms, rather than with assurances about motivation or denunciations of the questions, has earned the right to be argued with seriously. A design that answers any set with an assurance has located, by its own hand, the point at which the historical record will be waiting for it. This is the use of an assessment: not to close the future, but to make the future's proposals legible at sight.

## The household responsibility reform: the critics' favourite exhibit

The single most cited natural experiment in the entire incentive debate comes from Chinese agriculture at the end of the nineteen seventies. Under the commune system, peasants worked collective land for work points whose connection to the harvest was distant and diluted across the production team, and Chinese agriculture, feeding a vast population, did so at levels of productivity that the leadership itself came to regard as a standing reproach. Beginning locally and then spreading with official blessing, the household responsibility system contracted collective land to individual households, which owed the state a fixed quota and kept or sold the rest. The exertion response was immediate and visible to every observer: fields were worked with an intensity the communes had never elicited, sidelines flourished, and output rose markedly in the reform's first years, transforming the food situation of the country. Critics of socialism present the episode as the effort version's decisive proof, the same peasants on the same land transformed by nothing but the reward rule. The episode deserves its fame, and it also deserves the qualifications that serious scholarship attaches to it. The reform coincided with increases in the prices the state paid for farm output, with growing supplies of fertiliser and other inputs whose earlier scarcity had capped what effort could achieve, and with the restoration of rural markets through which extra output could be sold. Economists who have decomposed the surge assign a large role to the incentive change and a substantial role to the accompanying price and input changes, and the decomposition matters less than the joint lesson: effort responded to a bundle in which keeping the product was the centrepiece, and the bundle was assembled by changing the institutions around the peasant, not the peasant.

For the assessment, the Chinese case and the Soviet household plot are the same exhibit at different scales, and together they fix the effort verdict's exact shape. Where the individual or household retains the product of additional effort, in a setting where the effort's result is visible and the product can be used or sold, exertion rises dramatically and reliably. No reading of the cooperative evidence contradicts this, because the cooperative settings that elicit effort supply visibility, stake, and voice by other means, and the settings that fail, the commune field, the vast anonymous enterprise, supply none of them. The effort version's surviving truth is thus about the bundle: human exertion is exquisitely sensitive to whether the exertion's fruits are visibly connected to the exterter's household, team, or firm, and any large-scale design that dissolves that connection into a general fund, while providing no substitute connection, will harvest indifference. The Chinese reformers, whatever their ideology, had grasped the point in practice. A design for socialism that grasps it in theory, building the connection at the scale of the team and the enterprise and defending it against the centre's appetite for pooling, has answered the effort version in its surviving form. A design that waves at solidarity has not.

## Agriculture: the charge's first battlefield

It is not an accident that the incentive debate's hardest evidence comes from the land. Agriculture was collectivised first, resisted longest, and supplied the grimmest chapters of the state socialist record, and the reasons are written into the activity itself. Farm work is dispersed across space, variable with weather, and resistant to supervision: no overseer can watch a hundred scattered tasks, and the difference between care and neglect shows itself months later, confounded with rain and luck. The peasant's knowledge, of this field's drainage and that animal's temper, is local and inarticulate in exactly Hayek's sense, and it is deployed or withheld at the peasant's discretion in a thousand daily decisions no plan can enumerate. Collectivisation therefore faced the measurement problem in its purest form, and its solutions, work points assessed by team leaders, norms per operation, procurement quotas enforced from above, reproduced every pathology the measurement objection predicts: points bargained rather than earned, operations performed for the record, quotas met by concealing the harvest. The peasantry's response, foot-dragging, diversion to the plot, the quiet slaughter of livestock in the first collectivisation's desperate passage, is the historical bedrock on which the effort version's reputation rests. The assessment's grading holds even here, but it is here that it holds with least comfort for the defence. In the factory, peer structures and professional norms supply substitutes for individual reward that the evidence can point to. On the collective farm, the substitutes were thinnest, the monitoring hardest, and the connection between effort and reward most thoroughly dissolved, and the results were correspondingly the worst in the record. A verdict that ignored agriculture would be grading the charge on its easiest ground. Graded on its hardest, the effort version still fails as a claim about human nature, since the same peasants exerted themselves heroically on their own plots, but it stands as a claim about a specific institutional configuration, large-scale pooled production of unsupervisable work, and that configuration was not an incidental feature of the historical socialism. It was, for a third of humanity, its daily form.

## The analytical replies: designing for the motives people have

The most rigorous recent work on the defence's side accepts the incentive problem's terms and tries to design within them, and its leading figures deserve naming because they mark where the argument now lives. John Roemer's reconstructions of market socialism treated incentive compatibility as a design constraint from the start: his models retain markets, money, and differential reward precisely because he judged that any feasible egalitarianism must elicit effort and allocate capital through mechanisms that respect what people will actually do, and his later work on equality of opportunity extended the same discipline to the theory of justice itself. Samuel Bowles, from the behavioural side, supplied the complementary warning: institutions designed for purely self-interested citizens can destroy the civic and moral motives they presuppose as absent, so that the designer's pessimism becomes self-fulfilling, a result he documented across experiments in which introducing fines or payments for a prosocial act reduced the act's performance. Between Roemer's discipline and Bowles's warning lies the mature form of the whole debate. Design for the motives people have, all of them, the material and the moral together, knowing that the design itself will cultivate some motives and starve others, and that a system which assumes the worst of its citizens will manufacture citizens who confirm the assumption. Neither thinker claims to have answered the innovation version, and their candour on that point is part of their authority. The frontier of the argument, after their work, is exactly where this article's residue locates it: not in the theory of effort, which design has largely domesticated, but in the machinery of novelty, where no one, of any camp, has yet built the thing the charge demands be built.

## The innovator inside the firm: capitalism's internal version of the problem

A fact the innovation debate underuses is that the problem of the reluctant adopter exists inside capitalist firms as well, and the management literature's long struggle with it shows both that the problem is real wherever organisations are large and that markets supply an external corrective internal hierarchies lack. The engineer in a great corporation who champions a process that would cannibalise the division's existing product faces an arithmetic recognisably similar to the Soviet director's: the disruption is his, the gain is the company's, the failure would be his biography. Clayton Christensen's account of disruptive innovation documented how excellent firms, managed by the best rules of listening to customers and funding the highest returns, systematically declined the innovations that later displaced them, because the innovations initially served markets too small to matter to a large firm's growth. The firm's internal selectors, like the ministry's councils, preferred the safe and the sponsored. What rescues the capitalist economy from its firms' conservatism is not the firms but the ecology around them: the entrant with nothing to cannibalise, the venture backer whose portfolio profits from one success among nine failures, the market for corporate control that punishes the incumbent's stagnation in the share price. Diffusion and selection, in the capitalist case, are performed substantially by replacement, the new firm carrying the new method in, rather than by conversion of the old. This has a direct implication for the innovation residue's grading. The residue should not be stated as a claim that socialist enterprises are uniquely conservative; all large organisations are conservative, for reasons internal to organisation itself. It should be stated as a claim about the ecology: a system of social ownership must supply, by design, the functions that entry, exit, and independent finance supply in the market ecology, the challenger unburdened by the installed base, the backer who gains by the incumbent's embarrassment, the liquidation that frees resources from failing uses. Designs that socialise the incumbent while abolishing the ecology have kept the conservatism and dismissed the corrective, and the record shows it. Designs that preserve an ecology of challengers under social ownership are the live frontier, and they are, not coincidentally, the designs that look most like markets with altered ownership, which is why the innovation argument so often ends as an argument about how much market a socialism can contain.

## Who bears the cost of failure: the asymmetry that decides

Underneath the three gaps sits a single asymmetry that organises them, and it deserves isolation because it predicts more of the record than any other variable in this article. Innovation requires failure in bulk: most attempts, honestly pursued, do not succeed. A system's innovative capacity is therefore largely determined by what it does with its failures, who pays for them, what happens to the people associated with them, and how quickly their resources are released for the next attempt. In the market ecology, failure is priced and dispersed: the backers lose money they had allocated to risk, the firm contracts or closes, the people move, carrying their knowledge to the next attempt, and the system's memory of the failure is stored in prices and reputations rather than in files. The costs are real and often brutal for the individuals involved, and the waste of duplicated failure is a standing indictment of the arrangement. In the planned economy, failure was unpriceable and therefore political. A failed project was a ministry's embarrassment, to be concealed, refinanced, or completed at any cost rather than admitted; a failed enterprise could not close, so its resources were never released; a manager associated with failure carried the mark in a system where postings were assignments rather than choices. The predictable result was a system that could not afford to fail and therefore could not afford to try, except where the state consciously concentrated its tolerance, in the priority sectors, whose budgets absorbed failure as a cost of the mission. The asymmetry generalises the soft budget constraint into the innovation domain: softness toward failure is the enemy of novelty, because novelty is failure's frequent companion, and a system that rescues everything learns nothing. Hardness toward failure, conversely, is not sufficient for innovation, since it must be joined to tolerance for the failed, the distinction between punishing a project and punishing a person that innovative institutions strain to maintain. The market ecology maintains the distinction imperfectly, through bankruptcy's separation of the venture from the venturer's future employability. The planned economy collapsed the distinction, making every failure a verdict on a career, and its innovators responded as rational people do to such a tariff: they stopped volunteering.

## Time horizons: patience, myopia, and the funding of the long attempt

The funding gap has a temporal dimension that separates the systems less neatly than the slogans suggest. Development of the deepest kind, the foundational research whose fruits arrive in decades, requires a funder indifferent to quarterly judgement, and on this dimension the state, any state, has an advantage that private capital struggles to match: it can be patient because it need not answer to a market's repricing. The public research record surveyed earlier is the proof. Capitalism's impatience is the mirror pathology: capital markets that discount the distant heavily, managers whose tenure is shorter than their projects, and a takeover discipline that punishes the firm whose earnings dip for investment's sake. The planned economy's patience, however, was compromised from the other side: its plans were annual in their grip even when quinquennial in their titles, and the director's horizon was the current plan's fulfilment, beyond which the ratchet waited. The system was patient with programmes and impatient with enterprises, and innovation needs both patiences at once: patience from the funder, who must wait, and security for the adopter, who must be allowed to harvest an improvement over years rather than surrender it at the next target-setting. Neither system supplies both reliably. The market supplies the adopter's security, since a firm keeps its better process until rivals copy it, and starves the funder's patience. The plan supplied the funder's patience and confiscated the adopter's security. A design that joined public patience in funding to enterprise security in adoption would hold both halves of the temporal problem, and the fact that the halves have historically lived in different systems is a clue to why the innovation residue has been so durable: it is not one missing mechanism but a conjunction, and conjunctions are what institutional design finds hardest to build.

## The sponge that did not leak: priority research and the missing spin-off

The priority sectors raise a final question that the diffusion verdict must answer, because it is the defence's natural rejoinder: if the planned economies generated first-rate innovation inside their military and space programmes, why could the civilian economy not simply receive the results, as the capitalist economies received the spillovers of their own defence research? The internet's migration from a defence network to the civilian world is the standing example of spillover working. The Soviet case shows spillover failing, and the reasons are a compact restatement of the whole assessment. The priority sectors were sealed: by secrecy, which forbade the traffic in knowledge on which diffusion rides; by organisational walls, since the defence ministries and their institutes formed a world apart from the civilian branches, with their own suppliers, standards, and careers; and by the absence of carriers, the firms and engineers who in a market economy move between sectors and take technique with them, since Soviet personnel moved by assignment within sealed hierarchies. Above all, the receiving end was missing: a civilian enterprise had no reason to want the priority sector's methods, which arrived, if they arrived at all, as instructions to disrupt production for gains the ratchet would confiscate. Spillover is not a property of knowledge. It is a property of the channels between knowers and the appetites of receivers, and the planned economy had dammed the channels and extinguished the appetites. The rejoinder therefore fails in an instructive way: it treats innovation as a substance that, once produced anywhere, is available everywhere, when the record shows innovation to be a practice that lives in institutions and travels only where institutions carry it. The priority sectors are not the diffusion verdict's exception. They are its control group, demonstrating that the same society, given a sealed customer with authority to impose adoption, could diffuse; and that absent such a customer, it could not.

## The inventor's certificate: property in ideas, abolished and regretted

The socialist treatment of invention's ownership is a small institutional history with a large moral. The Soviet system offered inventors a choice between a patent, which granted exclusion rights the economy had little use for, and an inventor's certificate, which assigned the invention to the state and rewarded the inventor with a payment scaled to the invention's estimated savings, together with honour and precedence. Most inventors chose the certificate, since a patent on a process no enterprise was free to buy was a deed to nothing. The certificate system was, in intention, the innovation version's direct answer: reward the inventor from the social fund, diffuse the invention freely to all, and let no one fence knowledge for private gain. Its record is the answer's refutation in miniature. The payment depended on the estimated savings, which depended on adoption, which the adopting enterprise resisted for all the reasons this article has catalogued, so the inventor's reward was hostage to the very arithmetic that blocked diffusion. Valuations were bargained, delayed, and discounted; the honour was real but thin; and the inventor, like the rationalising worker, learned the system's lesson, that a successful invention created work and enemies in roughly equal measure. The episode matters because it isolates the appropriability question from the ownership of enterprises. Here was a design that rewarded invention socially and diffused it freely, exactly as theory prescribed, and it failed not at the reward's generosity but at the junction between reward and adoption: nobody downstream gained by making the inventor's success real. Property in ideas was abolished, and the abolition was not the problem. The problem was that diffusion is an act performed by adopters, and the system had arranged for adoption to be an act of self-harm. Any future scheme of social reward for invention will stand or fall at the same junction.

## The geography of the record: reform variants and their limits

Those economies were not identical, and their variation supplies a final test of the assessment's mechanisms, because the variants that softened the system's edges should, on this article's account, show softened versions of its pathologies. Hungary's New Economic Mechanism, which loosened plan compulsion and let prices and profits speak more loudly, produced the bloc's most responsive consumer economy and its most tolerable shops, while leaving enterprise survival guaranteed and investment substantially bargained; its innovation record improved at the margin of assortment and adaptation without producing frontier novelty or rapid diffusion, exactly the profile the conditional verdicts predict for a system that repaired feedback while keeping the guarantee. The German Democratic Republic, the bloc's industrial showcase, concentrated formidable resources on microelectronics in its final decades and fell behind at a rate its own planners documented with despair, a case study in selection by incumbents and diffusion blocked by the adopter's arithmetic, pursued with more money and discipline than any other variant and failing for the same structural reasons. The Soviet Union's own reform episodes, the profit indicators of the nineteen sixties and the enterprise autonomy of the late nineteen eighties, each granted managers new freedoms inside the guarantee and each watched the freedoms convert into bargaining power, as the coherence thesis expects. The geography thus corroborates the mechanisms from three directions: soften feedback and assortment improves; keep the guarantee and economy does not; spend without limit on a chosen frontier and the frontier recedes when selection and adoption are unrepaired. No variant combined hard budgets, open feedback, plural selection, and rewarded adoption, because that combination is, in substance, a different system from the one being varied. The variants therefore cannot acquit the design family. They can and do acquit the mechanisms of the charge of vagueness, since each pathology waxed and waned with its mechanism across the map, which is what a causal account, as opposed to a verdict on a civilisation, is supposed to show.

## The burden of proof, shifted and specified

Assessments of this kind are sometimes accused of splitting differences, and the accusation should be answered by stating where the burden of proof now lies, version by version, since burdens, not verdicts, are what an assessment actually distributes. On effort, the burden lies on the charge. The strong claim, that loosening individual reward collapses exertion, has been tested in cooperatives, communes, professions, and participation studies, and has failed often enough, under conditions that can be specified, that anyone who asserts it owes the conditions: anonymous, unmonitored, stakeless work, and an explanation of why a proposed design must contain such work in large amounts. On management, the burden lies on the defence, and heavily. The conditional law, protection without exit breeds hoarding, concealment, and shortage, has the state socialist record behind it, and anyone proposing a design with soft budgets owes the mechanism by which their design escapes a law that has operated across countries and decades. On innovation, the burden sits with the defence as well, but the specification matters: what is owed is not a proof that novelty is possible under socialism, which the priority sectors supply, nor a fund for research, which public finance supplies, but a mechanism, described in operational detail, for selecting among applied proposals with plural and fallible choosers, and for making adoption a gain rather than a confiscation for the enterprises expected to adopt. On measurement, the burden is shared: the critic owes an acknowledgement that administered metrics corrupt inside capitalist organisations too, and the defender owes a measure for rewarding performance that does not depend on the hierarchy the performance is meant to discipline. Burdens stated this way convert a shouting match into a syllabus. They also explain the article's title for what survives. The residue is not what is left when the weak parts of the charge are discarded. It is the set of points at which the burden sits, unshifted, on the defence's side of the table.

## The algorithmic mirror: piece rates inside the platform economy

The measurement objection's generality can be tested inside capitalism's newest labour forms, where the piece rate has returned wearing an algorithm. Platform work, in transport, delivery, and warehousing, pays by the measured task, monitors continuously, and rewards against standards constructed by the platform's own models, and its short history already reproduces, in miniature and at high speed, the pathologies this article has catalogued in the planned economy. Workers learn the metric and serve it: declining unprofitable tasks in ways the model punishes opaquely, clustering where the algorithm's prices spike, treating the rating, a constructed measure of performance, as the real product to be managed. The platform, like the ministry, discounts the gamed figures and adjusts the standard, and the adjustment is experienced by the measured as an arbitrary tightening, a ratchet administered by software. The parallel is not an argument that platforms are planned economies; their participants can exit, their prices are real, and their failures close. It is an argument that the pathologies of administered measurement follow the measurement, not the ownership, appearing wherever reward is attached to a constructed proxy inside a relationship the measured cannot negotiate. The planned economy universalised that relationship. The platform economy has rebuilt it inside the market's own walls, for a growing class of workers whose experience of capitalism is, precisely, an incentive system of indicators, ratings, and algorithmic targets. Critics who wield the measurement objection against socialism while ignoring its operation at home, and defenders who cite the platform's miseries as though they acquitted the plan, are making the same error in opposite directions. The objection is a law about proxies and power. It convicts arrangements, not flags.

## What the charge gets right about the tradition's self-image

There is a final service the incentive charge performs, which neither its grading down on effort nor its confirmation on management should obscure: it identifies a recurring weakness in the socialist tradition's imagination of itself. The tradition's theory of history taught it to expect motivation to follow structure almost automatically, as superstructure follows base: change the ownership, and the motives appropriate to the new order would grow from the new relations as naturally as capitalist motives had grown from theirs. The record suggests the causation is slower, more conditional, and partly reversed. Motives are cultivated by institutions over generations, they lag behind structural change, they persist from the old order into the new in forms the new order's propaganda found inexplicable, and they can be destroyed faster than they can be built, as the crowding literature's warning about priced civic virtue suggests. The Cuban debate was the tradition arguing with its own expectation in public, Guevara insisting that the new motives had to be built deliberately and could not be assumed, his opponents insisting that the old motives had to be used because the new ones were not yet there. Both were describing the same lag from different ends. A tradition that had theorised the lag, as it theorised so much else, would have designed for it: building the small-group institutions where new motives form, protecting them from the centre's impatience, and treating the motivational order as a construction project of decades rather than a consequence to be awaited. The incentive charge, in its crude form, denies that any such construction is possible. In its graded form, it is the construction's specification sheet, listing the loads the building must bear. That is the most useful way to read it, and the reading this article has tried to earn.

## A note on method: how this assessment weighed its evidence

The verdicts in this article rest on unlike kinds of evidence, and the reader is entitled to know how each kind was weighed. The effort verdict rests on peer-reviewed empirical literatures, cooperative studies, participation meta-analysis, and the experimental literature on motivation, weighed as social science is usually weighed: direction and robustness across settings counted for more than any single study, and the known selection problems discounted the conclusions without reversing them. The managerial verdict rests on a finished historical experiment, interpreted through a theoretical mechanism, Kornai's, that was specified independently of the cases it was then found to fit, and corroborated by the testimony of the system's own participants, whose memoirs describe the mechanisms from inside with no incentive to flatter the theory. The innovation verdict draws on both kinds, the historical record of diffusion and the design literature's inventory of mechanisms, and its strength is the convergence of the two: the gap the record displays is the gap the designs leave. Where the evidence was contested, the article has said so and graded the claim down rather than resolving the contest by preference, as with the disputes over Titmuss and the decomposition of the Chinese agricultural surge. Where a claim could not be tested because the design it concerns has never been operated, the article has assigned the burden of proof rather than a verdict, which is the honest treatment of unrun experiments. The figures protocol observed throughout, findings stated directionally with their literatures named and no invented precision, costs the article some rhetorical force and buys it the only authority an assessment can have: the reader can check the direction of every claim in the literature cited, and will find the article's grading, if anything, cautious against its own conclusions.

## The degeneration thesis: the cooperative's own critics

The cooperative evidence has its own classical critic, and the assessment is incomplete without her. Beatrice Webb, studying the British cooperative movement at the end of the nineteenth century, argued that producer cooperatives faced a degenerative logic: they would either fail commercially or succeed by transforming themselves, hiring wage labour, concentrating control in managers, and becoming capitalist firms in all but name, because the disciplines of the market punish democratic governance and reward the forms that subordinate it. The thesis has been debated for a century, and the modern literature's verdict is mixed in an instructive pattern. Outright degeneration, conversion into conventional firms, is rarer than Webb predicted; cooperatives more often persist in form for generations. But the pressures she identified are real and visible in the record: the temptation to hire non-member labour at the margin rather than share the surplus with new members, the professionalisation of management and the quiet migration of decisions toward it, the conservatism of members protecting an established income against the risks that founded the firm. The great Basque federation of worker cooperatives, the largest and most studied of its kind, exhibits the pattern in its mature form: a genuine and durable cooperative order that nonetheless employs non-member workers in subsidiaries and wrestles openly with the tension between its principles and its scale. For the assessment, the degeneration thesis refines the effort verdict rather than overturning it. The cooperative shows that effort survives shared ownership. Webb's question is whether shared ownership survives success, and her evidence warns that the motivational compound this article has praised, stake, voice, visibility, is itself perishable, eroded by growth and prosperity unless the constitution of the firm defends it deliberately. A design lesson follows that neither camp finds congenial: the institutions that answer the incentive charge are not built once. They are maintained, against entropy, forever, and the maintenance is a cost of the system that belongs in its ledger beside the costs of supervision and gaming in the alternative.

## Automation and the changing ground of the effort question

The effort version was framed for an economy of routine manual and clerical work, in which output was countable and the worker's discretion was narrow. The long movement of automation has been shrinking that economy's share of employment and expanding the share of work that is judgement, care, creation, and coordination, the very categories where the behavioural literature finds piece rates weakest and intrinsic motivation strongest. The shift does not dissolve the incentive problem, but it migrates it. In the routine economy, the problem was eliciting exertion that supervision could verify. In the judgement economy, the problem is eliciting care that supervision cannot verify, and the instruments that fail change accordingly: the metric and the bonus become more dangerous as the work becomes less measurable, and the social mechanisms, professional ethic, peer regard, identification with the task, become more load-bearing. Both systems face the migration, and both are visibly strained by it, capitalism in its epidemic of metric gaming and disengagement in white-collar work, the planned economies, while they lasted, in their hopeless attempts to norm the work of designers and researchers by the indicators of the factory floor. For the assessment, the migration strengthens the article's central grading. The effort version's domain, routine verifiable exertion, is the shrinking one; the innovation version's domain, the generation and spread of the new, is the growing one, since an economy whose routine is automated lives by its rate of renewal. The charge's centre of gravity has been moving toward the residue for reasons that have nothing to do with ideology, which is a further reason to conduct the argument where the residue locates it.

## Innovation and equality: the residue's distributional edge

One implication of the innovation residue is seldom drawn, and it cuts against the charge's usual political deployment. If the binding problem of a socialist economy is not effort but renewal, then the societies best placed to attempt socialism, by the charge's own logic, are the wealthy ones, whose stock of technique is deep, whose margin for failed experiments is wide, and whose citizens' basic security does not depend on next year's productivity growth. The poor society that socialises faces the residue in its cruelest form: it must innovate or stagnate with the fewest resources for failure, under the scrutiny of richer neighbours whose example its citizens can see. The historical attempts were made, overwhelmingly, in poor societies, for reasons the tradition's historians have explained: revolution came where capitalism was weakest, not where socialism's preconditions were ripest. The incentive record should therefore be read with its context restored. The designs that failed at diffusion were operated by societies that could least afford the failure, under siege conditions that taxed every mechanism, and their failure, while genuinely theirs, is not a fair sample of what the residue would do to a rich society's attempt. This does not acquit the designs; the mechanisms identified in this article are not artefacts of poverty, and the ratchet does not ask for a passport. It does mean that the residue's force as a prediction varies with the wealth of the society attempting the design, a variation the popular charge, which treats the record as a timeless verdict on an idea, entirely misses. Precision about scope is not charity. It is what distinguishes an assessment from a sentence.

## The stakes: why this charge is about the future

Of all the standard critiques of Marxism, the incentive charge is the one whose subject is the future rather than the past. The authoritarianism charge judges what socialist states did. The calculation charge judges what planning cannot know. The incentive charge, in its surviving form, judges what a socialist economy cannot yet do: renew itself. That is why it outlasts the others in serious discussion, and why the tradition's ablest defenders have always treated it with a respect they withhold from the cruder objections. A society can survive the admission that its founders were wrong about effort; the evidence acquits them. It can also survive conceding that its enterprises were mismanaged under protection; the remedy, hard budgets and exit, is at least describable. What it cannot survive indefinitely is the admission that its techniques age while its rivals' renew, because stagnation compounds: the gap widens geometrically, the consumer's judgement hardens, the ablest citizens calculate their futures elsewhere, and the system's legitimacy, which rested on a promise of historical advance, is consumed by the evidence of historical arrest. Those economies did not fall because their people would not work. They fell, in the part of the explanation this article owns, because their economies could not learn at the speed of their rivals, and the inability to learn was built into the incentives of every custodian in the system. That is the charge's final form, and it is formidable precisely because it is so much narrower than the slogan from which the article began.

## The innovation residue

The assessment can now gather itself into the claim it exists to state. The incentive objection, examined version by version against the evidence, does not survive in the form in which it is popularly stated. Its effort version is weakened past the point of usefulness as a general charge: people work, durably and productively, under shared ownership, loosened individual reward, and even, under stringent conditions, no individual reward at all, provided the work can be seen by peers, the stake is real, and the rules are felt to be one's own. Its managerial version is confirmed, but as a conditional law about protection without exit, binding on any system that rebuilds that protection, and therefore as much a warning to capitalist governments rescuing their champions as to socialist ones protecting their enterprises. Its measurement form is real and general, a law of proxies and power, which the platform economy illustrates as faithfully as the planned economy did. What remains, when the weakened and the conditional and the general are set aside, is specific: the problem of innovation, of funding development whose returns are speculative, selecting among proposals whose merits are unknowable in advance, and diffusing successes across producers who gain nothing by adopting them. That remainder is the innovation residue. It is not a small remainder. It concerns the process by which economies renew themselves or stagnate, and on the evidence of the record and the designs together, it is unanswered. The versions of the critique that stand are not the ones most often stated, and the version that survives scrutiny is the one the popular form of the critique rarely mentions. Relocating the argument to the residue is this article's whole purpose: defenders who answer the effort charge have answered the weakest version and should stop congratulating themselves; critics who press the effort charge are pressing that same weak version and should stop wasting their ammunition. The argument worth having, about mechanisms for novelty under social ownership, is harder for both sides, and it is the only one whose outcome is still open.

### What remains of the objection once the residue is named?

A specific problem: how novelty is to be paid for in its risk, chosen under uncertainty, and carried to producers with no reason to carry it further. The effort claim has been graded down by cooperative and behavioural evidence, the managerial claim assigned to protection without exit, and the measurement claim generalised to every administered metric. What remains is enough.

## Older orders: monasteries, guilds, and the long memory of unpriced work

The effort version assumes, usually without noticing, that production for individual material reward is the default condition of humanity and everything else an experiment. The historical record runs the other way. For most of recorded history, most production was organised without wage labour and without piece rates: in households, in manors, in guilds, and in religious communities, under motives of obligation, custom, status, and faith. The monastic orders are the extreme case and therefore the most instructive. A Benedictine house held its property in common, paid its members nothing, assigned their labour by rule and by obedience, and sustained that arrangement not for a generation but, in the order's continuous history, for well over a millennium, producing along the way some of the most disciplined agricultural and scholarly labour of its civilisation. The monastery's conditions replicate, at civilisational scale and duration, the conditions this article has repeatedly identified: small and stable communities, total visibility of conduct, a chosen and screened membership, an ideology that made the labour itself a form of the life the members valued, and esteem as the currency of reward. The guilds supply the urban counterpart: production organised by masters and journeymen under rules that restricted competition, fixed standards, and subordinated individual gain to the craft's collective standing, sustaining quality through peer scrutiny and the threat of expulsion from the only community that could practise the trade. None of this romanticises pre-capitalist labour, which was also coerced, customary, and often wretched, and none of it shows that a modern economy could be run as a monastery. It shows something narrower and fatal to the strong effort claim: the connection between exertion and individual material payment is a recent and local arrangement, not a law of the species, and human beings have performed demanding, skilled, sustained work under common ownership and unpriced reward for far longer than they have performed it for wages. The burden of the strong claim was always to explain away this record. It has never been discharged.

## The household: the economy's first and largest commune

The largest unit of unpriced production in every modern economy is the one the incentive debate never mentions: the household. Within families, labour of enormous economic value, cooking, cleaning, building, repairing, nursing, and above all the raising of children, is performed without wages, without piece rates, without monitoring by any principal, and allocated by a mixture of custom, affection, obligation, and negotiation that no economist has ever modelled as an incentive scheme. The work gets done, much of it to standards of care that paid provision struggles to match, and its failures, neglect, resentment, the exploitation of one member's labour by others, are real but are not generally attributed to the absence of a wage. The household is not a model for an economy; its motives are kinship's, its scale is intimate, and its internal injustices are a standing warning against romanticising unpriced labour, since the member whose work is unpaid is usually the member with least power to refuse it. Feminist economics has made exactly this point against both camps: against the charge, that production without wages is not only possible but constitutes a vast share of all production; against the romantic defence, that unpaid labour under the name of love or duty can conceal extraction as effectively as any wage bargain. For the assessment, the household fixes the effort question's true dimensions. The problem was never whether people work without pay. They do, on a scale that dwarfs the formal economy's hours. The problem is which institutions can borrow the household's motivational resources, visibility, obligation, care for identifiable others, without borrowing its intimacies and its injustices, and the cooperative and the profession are best understood as attempts at exactly that borrowing, at scales the household cannot reach.

## War Communism: the first encounter and the first retreat

The incentive problem was not discovered by theorists after the fact. It confronted the first socialist government within months of its taking power, in the emergency system remembered as War Communism, and the encounter set the pattern of oscillation that the wage section described. Between the revolution and the retreat of the New Economic Policy, the Bolshevik government requisitioned grain, nationalised industry down to small workshops, abolished money in stretches of the economy, and attempted to mobilise labour by decree and by ration entitlement. The results in production were catastrophic in the cities and mutinous in the countryside, where peasants responded to requisitioning by contracting their sown area to the measure of their own needs, the effort version's mechanism operating at national scale in its agricultural form. The retreat of 1921 replaced requisitioning with a fixed tax in kind, restored legal trade in the surplus, and readmitted material incentive as the economy's organising principle at the base, a restoration Lenin defended as a necessary step back. The episode is sometimes read as the charge's original vindication and sometimes as a wartime aberration, and the assessment's grading suggests the precise reading. War Communism was not socialism as any theorist had designed it; it was an improvisation under invasion and blockade. But the improvisation's failure mode is the datum: when the connection between the peasant's effort and the peasant's retention was severed by force, the peasant withdrew the effort and the knowledge together, and no quantity of exhortation or coercion recovered them, because the state could requisition a harvest it could see and could not requisition the care that produced a larger one. The first socialist government thus learned, at ruinous cost and within four years, the lesson the whole subsequent record rehearses: effort can be commanded at the point of a bayonet for visible, countable tasks, and it cannot be commanded for the thousand invisible acts of care on which production actually depends. Every later design that forgot the lesson relearned it at its own price.

## The Owenite experiments: the charge in its first laboratory

Before the charge had theorists it had experiments, and the experiments of the early nineteenth century, Robert Owen's communities and their many imitators, are its first laboratory. Owen's New Harmony and its sister settlements attempted common ownership and equal provision with a confidence drawn from his doctrine that character is formed by circumstances: arrange the circumstances rightly, and selfishness would dissolve into cooperation. The communities mostly failed within a few years, and their failures were anatomised by contemporaries with a precision the later debate rarely matches. The recurring causes were not laziness in the simple sense. They were disputes over the distribution of labour's burdens, the industrious resenting the support of the idle in communities too large for reputation to discipline and too ideologically committed to expel; the absence of any accepted authority for assigning unpleasant work; and the exit of the most capable members, whose skills commanded a price outside that the community's equality forbade it to match inside. Abramitzky's kibbutz analysis reads, against this background, like a commentary on Owen: the communities that survived for generations were the ones that solved, by screening, sanction, and exit costs, the problems that dissolved New Harmony in seasons. The Owenite record therefore refines rather than settles. It shows that the effort problem is real at the scale of the intentional community when the community is unscreened, unsanctioned, and open, and it shows the problem being solved, at cost, by communities willing to be selective and demanding. The tradition learned the wrong lesson from Owen when it learned only that utopian colonies are impractical. The right lesson is a list of the conditions under which common provision elicits common effort, and the list, assembled from the failures as much as from the survivals, is the same list this article has derived from the modern literatures: visibility, selection, sanction, stake, and a boundary between members and the world that makes membership worth keeping. Two centuries of evidence, from the phalanstery to the plywood mill, keep returning the same specification.

## The boundary with the calculation debate: two problems, often confused

The incentive problem is persistently confused with its neighbour, the calculation problem, and the assessment should mark the boundary because the verdicts differ across it. The calculation debate, treated in this series' article on the socialist calculation debate, concerns knowledge: whether a planning authority can know what to produce, in what proportions, without the information that prices generate. The incentive problem concerns will: whether the people inside any system, whatever it knows, will exert, economise, and innovate in the directions the system requires. The two problems interact, most deeply at the measurement junction this article has examined, where the knowledge needed to reward performance is itself a species of the knowledge the calculation debate finds missing. But they are separable, and the separations matter for grading. A market socialist design that retains prices answers much of the calculation objection while leaving the managerial and innovation versions substantially intact, since its enterprises may still be protected and its adopters still unrewarded. A hypothetical planning system with perfect information, a fantasy the calculation debate grants for argument's sake, would still face the ratchet, the hoard, and the shelved invention, because its custodians' wills would be set by its incentives and not by its knowledge. Conversely, the incentive verdicts reached here do not depend on the calculation debate's outcome: the managerial version was confirmed in economies whose planners knew a great deal, and the diffusion failure occurred among enterprises that knew exactly which improvements existed, since their own engineers had proposed them. Keeping the boundary clean protects both debates. The reader who meets a critic deploying the calculation argument against a market design, or the incentive argument against a knowledge claim, is watching the confusion do its usual work of making every charge answer every design and therefore none.

## The open frontier: questions the assessment leaves genuinely open

An assessment earns trust by marking its own edges, and four questions at this article's edge are open in the strict sense that the evidence does not close them and honest specialists divide. The first is whether plural public selection can be operated at scale: the design is describable, with rival public funds bearing real losses, and the political economy of keeping public losses real, against the pressure to rescue, is untested at national scale over the long periods that would prove it. The second is whether the adopter's arithmetic can be repaired inside a system of administered targets by insulating adoption from the ratchet, since every historical attempt to grant such insulation was bargained away in the target-setting process itself, and it is possible that the ratchet is not a policy choice but a property of target-based management wherever it rules. The third is whether the esteem economies of small groups can be federated by digital means, reputation carried across distance by records and ratings, without reproducing the measurement pathologies in a finer grain; the platform economy is running this experiment now, under capitalist ownership and with results that should make both camps cautious. The fourth is whether the direction of innovation, toward needs that prices cannot hear, can be governed democratically without the governing body becoming a council of incumbents for the applied arts as the ministries were for the industrial ones. None of these questions is rhetorical. Each names a place where a design could, in principle, be built and tried, and where the residue's verdict would then have to be revised in the light of the trial. The assessment's confidence is confined to what has been operated and observed. Beyond that line, it offers the burden assignments of the earlier section and an invitation, which the verdict section now formalises, to bring the exhibits that would shift them.

## Public service motivation: the state's workforce as standing evidence

Between the cooperative and the ministry stands a body of evidence the debate neglects because it belongs to neither camp's mythology: the study of public service motivation. Across many countries, researchers in public administration, following the framework James Perry and Lois Wise set out, have measured a distinctive motivational profile among public employees, an attraction to public service, commitment to the public interest, and compassion, that predicts effort, persistence, and organisational citizenship beyond what salary and security explain. The findings are correlational and the effects are modest, as honest social science usually is, but their direction is consistent with everything this article has assembled: a large workforce, employed by the state, paid by salary scales unrelated to measured output, performs demanding work to professional standards substantially because the work's purpose is part of its reward. The planned economies squandered this resource in a way the public administration literature helps to specify. Public service motivation depends on the credibility of the purpose: the nurse must believe the hospital heals, the teacher that the school teaches. Where the stated purpose and the lived reality diverge too far, where the plan's triumphs are announced over the queue's evidence, the motivational compound curdles into the cynicism that the late state socialist societies displayed in every survey and every joke. The lesson is symmetrical and worth both camps' attention. Purpose is a real incentive, perhaps the most powerful one available for judgement work, and it is also a depletable one, spent by hypocrisy and replenished only by honesty. A socialist design that treated its citizens' belief in its purposes as a capital stock, to be husbanded rather than mined, would be taking the effort evidence more seriously than either the historical regimes or their critics usually do.

## Prizes and honours: the third currency

Between money and exhortation lies a third currency of incentive that both systems have used and neither has theorised well: the prize. The historical record of inducement prizes, from the Longitude Prize that drew a working marine chronometer from a self-taught clockmaker, through the aviation prizes that filled the skies of the early twentieth century, to the great challenge prizes of the space age, shows a mechanism with distinctive properties. A prize pays only for success, so the funder's risk is capped and the failures are borne by the contestants. It selects without a committee's prior judgement, since anyone may enter and the test, not the pedigree, decides. And it rewards with a compound of money and glory in which the glory often dominates, as contestants routinely spend more than the prize's value in pursuit of it. The planned economies used the currency in debased form, their orders and titles distributed by the hierarchy for conformity as much as for achievement, until the honours inflated away. The market economies use it at the margins. The design question the residue poses is whether prizes can be scaled from the singular challenge, reach this target, treat this disease, to the diffuse work of applied innovation, where the targets defy specification in advance, since specifying them is most of the problem. For specifiable goals, the prize is a genuine mechanism, and a socialist economy could deploy it more generously than any capitalist state has done, since it need not apologise for public money spent on public challenges. For unspecifiable goals, the prize shares the mission programme's limit. The third currency therefore buys the defence real territory, the whole domain of definable challenges, and leaves the residue standing on the domain where definition itself is the scarce good. An honest map of the argument's end should show both territories, with the border drawn where this article has drawn it: not between capitalism and socialism, but between the problems whose solutions can be specified and the problems whose solutions must be discovered.

## The suggestion box that worked: adoption as a rewarded act

The contrast that isolates the diffusion variable most cleanly is not between countries but between two suggestion systems. The Soviet rationalisation apparatus and the Japanese quality improvement system, in the form perfected by the great automobile firms, both solicited small improvements from the workforce on an enormous scale. Both received them in floods. They differed in one variable: what happened next. In the Japanese system, the supervisor's own standing depended on the implementation of subordinates' ideas, suggestions were answered quickly, adoption rates were high, and the worker whose idea was adopted saw it operating on the line within weeks, credited and celebrated in the team's own forum. In the Soviet system, the suggestion entered a bureaucracy whose custodians gained nothing by implementing it, and the suggester watched it vanish into valuation disputes and plan negotiations. The floods of ingenuity were comparable. The adoption machinery was opposite, and the outcomes were opposite: one economy compounded its small improvements into a quality revolution that conquered world markets, the other accumulated unimplemented ingenuity as a standing reproach. The comparison is the diffusion verdict in its most portable form, because it strips away every alibi. Not culture, since the ingenuity was present in both. Not resources, since small improvements are cheap. Not knowledge, since the knowledge was supplied by the workers themselves. The variable was whether adoption was somebody's rewarded job. Where it was, diffusion happened as a by-product of ordinary ambition. Where it was nobody's, diffusion waited for a campaign, and campaigns, as the Soviet record shows, move what they inspect and nothing else. A design for social ownership that made adoption a rewarded office, with the adopter's gain protected against the ratchet and the adopter's standing staked on the traffic of improvements, would be answering the residue at its exact point of failure. The fact that no historical design built that office is the residue's sharpest single statement.

## Exit, voice, and loyalty: the member's sanctions

Albert Hirschman's triad of exit, voice, and loyalty supplies a final organising frame for the whole assessment, because each of the three versions can be restated in its terms. The effort version is about voice's weakness at scale: members exert themselves where their voice in the rules and their visibility to peers make membership a relationship rather than a fate, and they withdraw effort, the quiet exit that stays in place, where it does not. The managerial version is about exit's absence: the enterprise that cannot leave the system and cannot be left by it loses the signal that failure sends, and voice, in the form of the ministry's inspections and the plan's negotiations, degrades into bargaining because exit is not there to discipline it. The innovation version is about loyalty's misdirection: the system's custodians were loyal, often intensely, but their loyalty attached to the plan, the ministry, and the established programme, the objects that adoption would have disturbed, so that the sentiment which in other settings powers improvement powered conservation instead. Hirschman's own moral was that healthy institutions keep all three channels open and in tension: exit to signal, voice to repair, loyalty to hold members in place long enough for voice to work. The state socialist design closed exit, monopolised voice, and conscripted loyalty, and the incentive pathologies followed as the frame predicts. The cooperative opens all three at small scale, which is the deep reason its effort record is good. The market economy opens exit widest and voice least, and its pathologies, the unheard consumer of the monopoly, the worker whose only sanction is to leave, are the frame's predictions in the other direction. An assessment that ends with a frame rather than a flag is offering the reader an instrument, and the instrument's reading is the article's last word on method: judge any proposed economy by asking what its members, its managers, and its innovators can do when something is wrong, and how quickly the doing it changes anything.

## Where motives are made: education and the long formation

Every incentive system presupposes a population already formed, and the formation happens largely in childhood, in families and schools whose own incentive structures are the debate's unexamined foundation. The socialist tradition understood this in principle: its pedagogues, from the early Soviet experimenters to the Cuban literacy campaigners, treated education as the manufacture of the new person, and its critics replied that the manufacture was indoctrination. The evidence of developmental and educational research supports a cooler proposition that both camps can use. Children calibrate their effort to what their environment notices and honours with a sensitivity that puts adult workplaces to shame; they learn whether exertion is esteemed or mocked, whether the group's good is a real currency or a teacher's fiction, and they carry the calibration into every institution they later join. Schools that grade competitively manufacture competitors; schools that organise cooperative achievement manufacture cooperators with competitive edges intact; and no school manufactures a finished citizen for any system, since the calibration continues in every workplace that follows. The practical conclusion for the incentive debate is about time. A change of ownership changes the incentive structure on the day it is decreed. The population's motivational habits change across a generation, as the kibbutz's third generation and the post-socialist transitions both showed, the latter in reverse, when populations formed under one system's esteem economy had to learn another's and the learning was neither instant nor painless. Designs that promise motivational transformation on the timetable of a five-year plan are refuted by the formation evidence in advance. Designs that build the schools, the workplaces, and the esteem economies patiently, and protect them from the centre's demand for quick vindication, are at least pointed at the right variable. The incentive problem, seen from the formation end, is a problem of decades, and the historical regimes' impatience with it, their recourse to campaigns and cults of the exceptional worker, was a confession that the slow work was not being done.

## Scarcity and abundance: the charge's changing weight

The incentive charge's force varies with the material condition of the society it is aimed at, and the variation explains part of its historical pattern. Under scarcity, the effort and managerial versions bite hardest: when the margin between sufficiency and hunger is thin, the diversion of effort to private plots, the hoarding of inputs, and the shelving of improvements are not inefficiencies but wounds, and a population queuing for necessities experiences the system's motivational failures as a daily tax on survival. The historical attempts were made under scarcity, compounded by war and blockade, and their records were written in that ink. Under abundance, or the relative abundance of the wealthy economies, the same failures change their meaning: slack is affordable, hoarding is a nuisance rather than a famine's accomplice, and the consumer's complaint shifts from absence to shoddiness. The innovation version, alone among the three, does not soften with abundance; it sharpens, because a rich society's standing depends on renewal more than on exertion, and the compounding of a diffusion gap is fastest where the frontier moves fastest. This is the analytical form of the point made earlier about the residue's distributional edge. The charge that history actually tested was the scarcity form, in which all three versions pressed at once on societies least able to bear them. The charge that a wealthy society's socialism would face is the abundance form, in which effort and management are manageable embarrassments and innovation is the whole question. Critics who quote the scarcity record against an abundance proposal are committing the comparison error this article flagged at the start, in its temporal form. Defenders who dismiss the scarcity record as unrepresentative owe the abundance form's answer, which is the residue's mechanism, undescribed. Both debts are now on the table, and the verdict can be pronounced.

## The health service as a diffusion machine: guidelines and professional standing

The strongest working model of organised diffusion inside a public system is one the innovation debate rarely visits, because it sits in a sector both camps treat as exceptional: the tax-funded health service. A national health service faces the diffusion problem in its purest clinical form. Proven treatments emerge continuously from research; thousands of autonomous practitioners must adopt them; the adopters gain no profit by adopting, since their incomes do not rise with their patients' outcomes in any direct way; and failure to diffuse is measured in morbidity. The machinery such services built deserves the innovation literature's attention: systematic evidence reviews that grade what works, guideline bodies whose recommendations carry professional authority, formularies that make the approved treatment the path of least resistance, audit that shows each practitioner their own outcomes against their peers', and a professional culture in which currency of practice is a condition of standing. Diffusion in this machinery runs on the esteem economy of a profession, the same currency as the cooperative and the commune, organised at national scale through institutions whose whole function is carriage. It works imperfectly, with lags and resistances the health services research literature documents honestly, and it works incomparably better than the planned economy's industrial diffusion, where no equivalent of the guideline body existed with authority over the enterprise and no professional standing rode on adoption. The comparison specifies the residue's defeater conditions with unusual clarity. Diffusion without appropriation is achievable where a trusted body certifies what is proven, where adoption is made the easy path rather than the disruptive one, and where the adopter's standing among peers depends on currency. The failure, in that economy, was not conceptual; its technical information institutes were the shell of the concept, present without authority. The failure was that the machinery's judgements could not bind the enterprise against the plan, and that no director's standing rose by adopting early. Sector by sector, the question for any future design is whether it can build the health service's triangle, certification, ease, and esteem, for producers whose work has no professional body to supply the third side. That is a hard design problem. It is also, for the first time in this long argument, a visibly soluble one, and the residue's final grading should record the fact.

## What each side must surrender

An assessment that has distributed burdens should end the argumentative part by naming the surrenders, the propositions each camp must give up to occupy the ground the evidence marks out. The critics must surrender the appeal to human nature, which the cooperative, communal, professional, and household records jointly refute, and with it the pleasure of the single sentence. They must surrender the treatment of the state socialist record as a verdict on every design, since their own best theorist, Kornai, built the managerial law as a conditional about protection, and conditionals do not convict what does not meet their conditions. They must surrender the contrast with an idealised capitalism that exists nowhere, and argue against the real one, whose incentive pathologies are documented in its own literature. The defenders must surrender the consolation that answering the effort charge answers the objection, and the habit of citing the priority sectors as though invention were innovation. They must surrender the claim that public funding closes the innovation question, when it closes its easiest third. And they must surrender the assumption that motives follow ownership as shadow follows object, accepting instead that the motivational order is a construction of decades whose blueprints are the small-group institutions this article has catalogued. Neither surrender is a defeat. Each is the price of arguing on ground where the argument can be won or lost on evidence, and the history of this debate suggests that both camps have preferred the cheaper ground of the slogan because the dearer ground demands work. The verdicts follow, with the conditions under which they would be reversed, because a verdict that cannot state its own defeater is only a slogan that has learned to dress well.

## The designer's dilemma: hardness without cruelty

The managerial verdict's remedy conceals a political dilemma that any honest assessment must state, because it explains why the remedy was never applied by the governments that understood the disease. Hardening the budget constraint means letting enterprises fail, and letting enterprises fail means letting their workers bear the cost, in lost livelihoods and broken communities, for failures that were usually the system's rather than theirs. A socialist government came to power, in every historical case, on the promise that the insecurity of the labour market would end; the full employment of the planned economies, however hollow its content became, was the one promise visibly kept, and the queues for goods were endured partly because the queue for work did not exist. To harden budgets was to break the kept promise in order to fix the broken ones, and no governing party volunteered. Kornai's coherence thesis has a political corollary here: the guarantee to enterprises and the guarantee to workers were the same guarantee, the state's assumption of all risk, and it could not be withdrawn from the enterprise while being honoured to the worker without a third institution, insurance and retraining and mobility support generous enough to make enterprise failure survivable for the people inside it. Capitalist economies built fragments of that third institution under the name of the welfare state, and their hard budgets are politically possible partly because of it. A socialist design that wants hard budgets must therefore budget for the cushion as a first charge, not as an afterthought, and must say plainly that security attaches to persons rather than to enterprises, that the job is not guaranteed but the livelihood is. That sentence, easy to write, describes a settlement no historical socialism achieved and several capitalist states approximated, which is the final irony the assessment has to record: the precondition for disciplining socialist enterprises was pioneered, under other banners, by the systems the discipline was meant to surpass.

## The long view: the debate that outlived its century

Step back from the versions, and the incentive debate's shape across two centuries becomes visible as a single movement of refinement. It began as a quarrel about character, in the Owenite settlements and the first socialist programmes, where the question was whether people were good enough for the new order. It became, in the interwar treatises, a quarrel about psychology, whether the motives socialism required existed in the required strength. The Soviet experience moved it, in the hands of Berliner and Kornai, onto the ground of institutions, where the question was no longer what people are but what arrangements make of them, and the managerial law was its first durable result. The behavioural and cooperative literatures then complicated the institutional picture from the other side, showing motivation to be plural, context-made, and sometimes corroded by the very instruments meant to strengthen it. The comparative technology studies, finally, isolated the residue, the one component of the original charge that every refinement left standing and every record confirmed. Each stage discarded a slogan and kept a specification. The debate's history is therefore the best argument for conducting it in the graded form this article has used: the slogans have a half-life, the specifications accumulate. A reader who has followed the movement can predict the debate's future with some confidence. The effort quarrel will continue in the popular press, because it flatters a prejudice, and will continue to be lost there by both sides in turn. The serious argument will be where the residue put it, in the design of selection and diffusion machinery, in the engineering of plural public backers and rewarded adopters, in the construction of esteem economies that survive scale. Whoever builds those things, under whatever banner, will have answered the incentive problem. Whoever merely wins the argument about human nature will have won the part of it that never mattered most.

## Verdicts, and what would reverse them

The verdicts can now be stated in their final form, each with the condition of its own reversal, as this cluster of the series requires of every verdict it prints. On the effort version: weakened to a design constraint. Individuals exert themselves where ownership is shared and individual reward is loosened, wherever peers can watch the work, members hold a genuine stake, and the rules belong to the people governed by them, and they withhold exertion where work is anonymous, pooled, and voiceless. The verdict would be reversed by evidence that cooperative and participatory firms, properly compared and followed over long periods, systematically underperform conventional firms in productivity and survival, which the existing literature, with all its selection caveats entered, does not show. On the managerial version: confirmed as a conditional law. Protection without exit produces hoarding, concealment, restrained performance, and chronic shortage, in socialist economies universally and in capitalist ones wherever the protection is granted. The verdict would fall before a durable case of enterprises under guaranteed survival that nonetheless economised, revealed their capacity honestly, and shed losing activities without market discipline or its equivalent, and no such case is on record. On the measurement objection: sustained in its general form. Administered measures of performance corrupt as their monopoly widens, because the measured adapt to the measure faster than the measurers can repair it, and rewards attached to constructed standards bend work toward the standard's letter. The verdict would be softened by a demonstrated metric regime, in any system, that rewarded complex performance over long periods without gaming becoming its dominant fact.

On the innovation version, the verdict is the article's centre, and its defeater must be stated with the precision the cluster's discipline demands. The verdict that the innovation version stands would be defeated by an extended historical case of a planned economy sustaining frontier innovation across sectors over a generation, or by a demonstrated design that separates the return to innovation from the security of the innovating unit, so that developers gain by developing and adopters gain by adopting while neither holds the other hostage. In the form the brief for this article prescribes, the same defeater reads: the verdict would be defeated by a demonstrated mechanism, implemented at scale, that funds high-risk novel development and diffuses successful results across producers without appropriable gain to the developer, of a kind that publicly funded research alone has not so far provided for applied and consumer innovation. Neither exhibit has been produced. The historical case does not exist; the priority sectors, bounded and sealed, are not it, and the reform variants, Hungary and the rest, improved everything except the variable in question. The demonstrated design does not exist; the describable designs, plural public backers, rewarded adoption, guideline machinery for producers, exist as specifications awaiting an operator. The verdict therefore stands, and it stands in the limited, exact sense this article has given it: not that socialism cannot innovate, which the record refutes, and not that capitalism innovates well, which its own literature refutes, but that the machinery for funding, selecting, and diffusing novelty under social ownership remains unbuilt, and the burden of building it, or of showing it built, lies where it has lain throughout this assessment, on the defence.

### What exhibit would reverse the verdict on the innovation version?

A case, not an argument: a planned economy holding innovation's frontier across many sectors for a generation, or a design operated at scale in which the return to developing a novelty is separated from the security of the unit that developed it, so that adoption enriches the adopter instead of endangering the plan. The exhibit has not been produced.

## The argument in one sitting

For the reader who needs the whole assessment compressed, the compression runs as follows. The incentive charge is three charges. The first, about individual effort, has been tested wherever workers own their firms, share their proceeds, or work in professions and households and communes, and it fails in its strong form: effort follows visibility, stake, and voice more faithfully than it follows the individual pay packet, and payment itself can corrode the motives it buys. The second, about managers, has been tested by the largest institutional experiment of the twentieth century, and it is confirmed wherever enterprises are protected from failure: they hoard, conceal, restrain, and generate shortage, not from vice but from arithmetic. The third, about innovation, has been tested by the same experiment and by forty years of design literature, and it stands: invention flourished in the sealed priority sectors while diffusion starved in the open economy, because adopting a proven improvement endangered the adopter's plan and enriched everyone except the adopter, and no design has yet supplied the machinery, plural selection and rewarded adoption, that would reverse that arithmetic under social ownership. Around the three charges orbit two general laws that this article has treated as part of the assessment: administered measures corrupt as their monopoly grows, in ministries and on platforms alike; and motivation is a cultivated stock, built by institutions over generations and spent by hypocrisy in a season. The residue, the innovation problem in its triple form of funding, selecting, and diffusing, is where the argument now belongs. Everything else is either settled, conditional, or a slogan in search of its evidence.

## Sources and their standing

The assessment rests on literatures of unequal standing, and the reader who follows them up should know the order. At the foundation stand the primary institutional studies: Kornai on the soft budget constraint and the shortage economy, Berliner's reconstructions of Soviet management and of the innovation decision from the participants' own testimony, and the cooperative research built on firm-level data by Pencavel, Pérotin, and their successors, with Doucouliagos's meta-analysis as the map of the participation field. Above those stand the behavioural literatures, Deci and Ryan's programme and its meta-analyses, Frey's crowding theory, Titmuss's classic and its experimental corrections, and Bowles's synthesis of incentives and social preferences, each weighed here with its disputes attached. The historical cases rest on the standard scholarly record: Abramitzky on the kibbutz, the the collaborative volumes on Soviet technology associated with Amann and Cooper, Mazzucato on the public funding of foundational innovation, and the reform literature on Hungary, Yugoslavia, and the Chinese countryside. The design proposals are graded from the market socialist literature and its critics, with Roemer as the defence's most rigorous representative and Hansmann as the ownership literature's necessary cold water. Where these sources conflict, the article has reported the conflict rather than resolving it by preference, and where they are silent, as on the operated design that would defeat the innovation verdict, the article has recorded the silence as itself a finding. That discipline, sources named, directions stated, figures withheld where the protocol requires, is the series' standing practice, and it is the only authority this assessment claims.

## The word itself: a short history of incentive

The concept at the article's centre is younger than the debate assumes, and its youth explains some of the debate's confusions. Incentive entered economics from law and administration as a term for a designed inducement, a payment or penalty attached to a specified act by an authority that wants more or less of the act. In that original sense, an incentive is something a designer installs, and the question of incentives is a question about design: which acts to attach them to, at what strength, with what side effects. The popular debate quietly changed the word's meaning, until incentive came to denote motivation as such, as though effort without an installed inducement were effort without a cause. Much of the charge's rhetorical force lives in that slide. If incentive means any reason for acting, then the claim that socialism lacks incentives is the claim that its people would have no reasons to act, which the household, the monastery, and the profession refute by existing. If incentive means the designed inducement, then the claim is the narrower and more interesting one this article has assessed: that certain designed inducements, individual material reward, the threat of enterprise failure, the appropriable gain from novelty, do work that a socialist design must replace or forgo. On the second meaning, the verdicts divide as they have divided here. The first designed inducement turns out to be replaceable across wide domains by stake, visibility, and esteem. The second turns out to be replaceable only by an equivalent hardness that socialism's promises make politically expensive. The third has not been replaced. A debate conducted in the word's precise sense would have reached these verdicts sooner and shouted less. The article's insistence on versions, contrasts, and mechanisms is, in the end, an insistence on the word's original discipline: incentives are designed, designs can be compared, and comparisons can be graded.

## Where these verdicts travel

The grading reached here constrains the neighbouring debates in this series, and the constraints are worth stating so that the cluster's articles do not contradict one another in the reader's hands. The calculation debate inherits the measurement finding: any planning mechanism that proposes to reward performance must construct its measures inside the hierarchy it measures, and the corruption of administered metrics is a cost that belongs in the calculation argument's ledger alongside the knowledge problem proper. The companion survey of the replies inherits the burden assignments: a reply that answers the effort version has answered the weakest third of the charge, and the scorecard there should grade it accordingly. The economic record article inherits the diffusion finding as the mechanism beneath its central fact, the widening gap between the socialist economies' best practice and their average practice, which no account in terms of investment or education reaches. And the exam guide that closes this cluster inherits the whole structure, since the three versions, their verdicts, and the residue are precisely the distinctions that separate an answer that weighs from an answer that recites. Travel in the other direction matters as much. The authoritarianism verdict reached elsewhere in this series, that the theory lacks an account of institutional constraint on power, has its economic analogue here: the theory, in its classical forms, lacks an account of institutional inducement to novelty, a missing machinery rather than a missing virtue. The parallel is not a coincidence. A tradition that theorised production as the transformation of nature and distribution as the political question left the micro-institutions of motivation, like the micro-institutions of constraint, to be improvised by its governments, and the improvisations are the record this cluster exists to grade.

## The counterfactual: a cooperative economy that was never tried

Every verdict in this article has been disciplined by what was actually operated, and it is worth closing the argumentative part by testing the discipline against the strongest counterfactual the defence can offer, because the counterfactual clarifies what the residue does and does not claim. Imagine the road not taken: an economy socialised not by nationalisation under central plans but by federation, in which enterprises are cooperatives of their workers, markets coordinate most production, banks are publicly or cooperatively owned and lend at interest to enterprises that can fail, and the state's role is confined to the framework, the cushion of personal security, and the funding of research whose returns no one can appropriate. Against the three versions, this design reads well on the first two. Effort is elicited by the cooperative mechanisms the evidence supports, at the scale the evidence supports them. Management is disciplined by real budgets, real exit, and real prices, with the cushion attaching security to persons rather than to firms, so the managerial law's conditions are not met. The measurement problem persists inside the firms and the banks, as it persists inside capitalist corporations, but it is bounded by the same external discipline that bounds it there. The innovation version is where the counterfactual must earn its keep, and its performance is genuinely uncertain in a way the historical designs' performance was not. The cooperative firm has a documented bias the design must answer: members maximising income per member are cautious with risky investment whose returns lie beyond their own tenure, and a federation of such firms may systematically underinvest in the long and the novel, the horizon problem that the cooperative literature has debated for decades. Plural public banks could offset the bias by holding the long portfolios the firms will not, and prizes and mission programmes could cover the specifiable frontier, and the health service triangle could be attempted for diffusion. None of this is fantasy; every component exists somewhere in operation. But the conjunction remains unoperated, and the conjunction is the claim. The residue's final form is therefore a statement about a conjunction: the components of an answer exist, scattered across systems and sectors, and no society has assembled them into a working innovation machinery under social ownership. Assembling them is an engineering project, not a refutation, and the article's verdict, properly understood, is an invitation to the project with the specifications attached. That is a harder ending than either camp's slogan, and it is the only one the evidence supports.

## Schumpeter's ghost: the entrepreneur, bureaucratised

The innovation version has a surprising theoretical ancestor, and his testimony complicates both camps. Joseph Schumpeter, who gave economics its modern account of innovation as the entrepreneur's creative destruction, asked directly whether socialism could work and answered, in his most famous book, that of course it could, as a matter of administrative logic. His reasoning deserves the assessment's respect because it identifies the variable on which everything since has turned. Innovation in the heroic age was the act of an individual who bore risk for gain. But Schumpeter observed that innovation was already, in his lifetime, being bureaucratised inside the great capitalist corporation, routinised in research departments where salaried teams produced novelty as a function rather than as an adventure. If innovation could be bureaucratised under capitalism, he argued, the socialist ministry inherited no special disability: it would run the same departments with the same salaried teams, and the entrepreneur's disappearance, which he thought capitalism itself was accomplishing, would cost socialism nothing it had not already lost. The assessment's record suggests the precise point at which this elegant argument fails. Bureaucratised innovation inside the corporation works because the corporation is surrounded by the ecology this article described: entrants who threaten it, capital markets that reprice its stagnation, customers who can leave, and a diffusion traffic carried by rivals' imitation. The research department's salarymen innovate inside a selection and diffusion machine they did not have to build. Transfer the department to a ministry and the salarymen remain, but the machine does not transfer with them, because the machine was the market around the firm and not the firm itself. Schumpeter saw the bureaucratisation of the act of innovation with perfect clarity and mistook it for the bureaucratisation of the system of innovation, the funding pluralism, the rivalrous selection, the adopter's hunger, that made the act fruitful. The distinction between the act and the system is the innovation residue stated in Schumpeterian terms, and it is fitting that the distinction should be drawn against the thinker who taught economics to separate invention from innovation in the first place. His ghost haunts the debate productively: he is the strongest witness that the laboratory is not the problem, called by a defence that has not noticed his testimony is about the laboratory alone.

## A last distinction: incentives to work and incentives to care

The whole assessment can be folded into a distinction that the coming century will make unavoidable. There are incentives to work, which elicit exertion, and incentives to care, which elicit attention to the quality, the safety, and the human meaning of what is produced, and the two respond to opposite treatments more often than administrators admit. Exertion can be bought, supervised, and metered, within the limits the crowding literature sets. Care resists purchase: the carer who is paid by the visible task performs the visible tasks, and the essence of care, the noticed detail, the unhurried minute, the escalation of a worry nobody asked about, drains out of the transaction precisely because it was never in the contract. Every advanced economy is shifting its centre of gravity from work that needs exertion to work that needs care, in health, in education, in the raising of children and the tending of the old, and in the supervision of the automated systems that now perform the exertion. The incentive problem's next century will therefore be fought on the ground of care, where the effort version's instruments are at their most corrosive and the social mechanisms, peer esteem, professional ethic, identification with those served, are at their most indispensable. On that ground, the systems' positions reverse their popular reputations. The market's instruments are the blunt ones, forever trying to meter the unmeterable and harvesting the gaming this article has catalogued. The traditions of common provision, the professions, the public services, the cooperative and the commune, hold the subtler instruments, provided they can protect the credibility of their purposes from the hypocrisy that spends them. Nothing in that reversal answers the innovation residue, which stands where it stood. But it suggests the debate's final irony: the charge that was framed to prove socialism unworkable may end by identifying the terrain, the economy of care, on which its own instruments work worst and the alternatives work best, while the terrain on which the charge is unanswerable, the machinery of novelty, decides whether either system earns the future in which the caring is to be done.

## The bonus and the norm: a biography of two instruments

The daily texture of the incentive system in a planned enterprise was woven from two instruments, and their biography explains much that the grander categories leave obscure. The first instrument was the norm, the standard quantity of labour or output assigned to a task, from which piece rates and staffing were derived. In the textbooks the norm was an engineering quantity, set by time study and technical calculation. In the life of the enterprise it was a negotiated quantity, and the negotiation never ended: workers and foremen pressed for loose norms, which made the rate attainable and the bonus reachable; ministries pressed for taut norms, which extracted more from the same wage fund; and every revision of a norm after a period of overfulfilment taught the workforce, in the most concrete form imaginable, the ratchet's lesson. The second instrument was the bonus, and by the system's maturity the bonus had quietly become the real wage's steering wheel, the part of earnings that depended on the enterprise's plan fulfilment and therefore the part through which the ministry's priorities entered the worker's calculation. A wage system in which the guaranteed portion covers subsistence and the contingent portion covers comfort is a powerful elicitor of the behaviour the contingency names. It named, in this case, the fulfilment of the indicator, and the workforce's intelligence flowed accordingly: into the arts of the attainable plan, the negotiable norm, the end-of-period storm, and the assortment that counted best. Nothing in this biography requires a theory of human nature. It requires only the observation that two instruments, each defensible in isolation, composed a system in which the rational worker and the rational manager were jointly trained to serve the measure rather than the purpose, and in which the training was renewed with every pay packet. Later reformers who proposed to fix the system by adjusting the bonus percentages were adjusting the steering wheel of a vehicle whose engine, the soft budget and the negotiated plan, they had agreed not to touch.

## Shortage as information: what the queue was saying

It is sometimes said that the planned economy lacked signals, and the claim is too crude for the reality, which was stranger: the economy was full of signals that had no empowered reader. The queue was a signal, reporting with precision which goods were underpriced relative to desire. The waiting list was a signal, measuring in years the gap between housing supply and housing need. The network of connections through which deficit goods actually moved, the economy of favour and reciprocal obligation that every citizen maintained, was a signal system of remarkable sensitivity, pricing scarcity in the currency of obligation where money prices were forbidden to speak. Even the planners' own vocabulary admitted the information: plans were described as taut or slack, ministries tracked the tension of the system, and the chronic overbidding of enterprises for investment was understood by everyone as a report on the softness of the constraint. What the system lacked was not information but a circuit between information and consequence. A price signal in a market economy reaches actors who gain by responding to it: the producer who expands the queued-for good is rewarded, the entrant who serves the waiting list is paid. The queue in the shortage economy reached a producer who gained nothing by shortening it, a ministry whose standing was measured by other indicators, and a shop assistant whose wage was untouched by the line at the counter. The signal was therefore archived rather than answered, filed in the citizen's memory and the satirist's repertoire, until the accumulated archive became the system's political obituary. For the incentive assessment, the episode refines the measurement objection into its final form. The objection is not that a planned economy cannot know. It is that knowledge without a rewarded responder is decoration, and that the incentive problem and the information problem meet exactly at the point where a signal looks for someone whose fortune depends on answering it.

## The foreman's republic: where the plan met the floor

Between the ministry's indicators and the worker's hands stood a figure the incentive literature neglects and the system's memoirs never do: the foreman, and behind the foreman the shop chief, the two people who translated the plan into assignments and the workforce's realities back into reports. Their position was structurally impossible in a way that illuminates the whole design. The foreman was responsible for fulfilment and possessed almost none of the levers fulfilment required: he could not hire or dismiss in any meaningful market sense, could not set wages, could not promise promotion beyond a narrow ladder, and could not obtain materials whose absence was the commonest cause of failure. What he possessed was discretion in the assignment of tasks, in the certification of norms as met, in the distribution of the good and bad jobs through which earnings were actually determined, and in the protection of his people from the storm of the plan's final days. The workforce's real incentive system lived in that discretion. A worker's income and standing depended less on the ministry's percentages than on the foreman's goodwill, and the foreman's goodwill was earned by the currencies the floor itself minted: reliability in a crisis, willingness to storm when storming was needed, silence about the shop's informal arrangements. A parallel constitution thus governed the enterprise beneath the formal one, an economy of favour and obligation mirroring, at bench level, the economy of favour through which the citizen obtained goods. The parallel is not a curiosity. It shows the incentive problem reproducing itself at every scale of the system, as each level, denied the formal instruments its responsibilities required, minted informal ones, and the informal instruments rewarded loyalty to the level that minted them rather than performance for the level above. Assessments of the system that stop at the ministry's design miss the republic on the floor, where the design was daily renegotiated into something survivable, and where the system's real motivational order, personal, local, and reciprocal, quietly contradicted its official one.

## Storming: the calendar of a distorted incentive

The plan's period was the metronome of the enterprise's life, and its rhythm produced the system's most recognisable pathology of effort: storming, the concentration of output into the final days of the month, the quarter, and above all the year, when the plan's fulfilment would be judged. For most of the period, the enterprise waited, for materials that arrived late, for decisions that descended slowly, for the components that another enterprise was equally failing to deliver; then, as the deadline approached, the whole establishment convulsed, overtime multiplied, quality was sacrificed to countability, machines were run without maintenance, and the output of the final days dwarfed the output of the weeks before. The costs were itemised by the system's own economists with weary precision: equipment destroyed by haste, workers exhausted in rhythm with the calendar rather than with the work, assortments skewed to whatever could be finished and counted in time, and a workforce trained to experience exertion as a periodic emergency rather than a steady condition. Storming deserves its place in this assessment because it is the incentive system's signature written in time. The workers were not idle; in the storm they worked to collapse. The managers were not indifferent; they organised the convulsion with great skill. The pathology lay in a reward structure that priced the period's end and ignored the period's course, so that effort, that supposedly scarce quantity whose elicitation the whole debate concerns, was summoned in destructive surges by the calendar of accounting. Any designer proposing to steer an economy by periodic targets, of whatever ideological family, inherits the metronome and its music. The capitalist corporation knows a milder version in the quarter's closing weeks, when sales are pulled forward and expenses deferred to please the reporting date, and the mildness of the version is the measure of the surrounding discipline: where the period's number is one signal among many, storming is a vice; where it is the only signal, storming is the system.

## The inspector's dilemma: auditing a system that reports itself

Every incentive system needs verification, and the planned economy's verification apparatus completes the picture of why its measures corrupted. Output was reported upward by the enterprises whose rewards depended on the reports, and the state maintained inspectorates, statistical administrations, and party control commissions to check them. The checkers faced the measured's dilemma in its purest form. An inspector who exposed falsification made enemies of the enterprise, of the ministry that sponsored it, and of the local party organs whose standing rose with the district's reported success; an inspector who certified the convenient figure was rewarded with a quiet life and a reputation for realism. Falsification therefore acquired a professional structure: additions to reported output, known in the system's slang by a term every memoirist records, were not the deviance of a few directors but a distributed practice with its own etiquette, its own tolerable margins, and its own protection, since a falsified figure, once accepted into the plan's fulfilment, became the base from which the next period's targets were set, implicating every level that had accepted it in its defence. The statistician's art in such a system is not measurement but diplomacy. The general law extends well beyond the case, to every regime in which the reporter and the rewarded are the same party: emissions reported by the manufacturer, results reported by the school, performance reported by the division. The planned economy's distinction is only that it universalised the identity of reporter and rewarded across the whole of production, and then built its plans on the reports. A design that wants honest measures must separate the fortunes of the measurer from the fortunes of the measured, a separation that markets achieve imperfectly through competing reporters and that any socialist design must achieve deliberately, through statistical offices whose careers are built on the embarrassment of the powerful. The historical regimes built the opposite, and their numbers, like their incentives, served the negotiation first and the truth where the negotiation allowed.

## Two workshops: a parable the evidence permits

Imagine two workshops on the same street, making the same furniture with the same tools, staffed by people drawn from the same town. In the first, the workers own the benches together, elect the master, share what remains after costs, and can see, across a room small enough for sight, who is carrying and who is coasting. In the second, the benches belong to a ministry in a distant capital, the master answers to a plan he negotiates each year, the workers are paid by norms they did not set from a fund they cannot see, and the workshop cannot close however its order book decays. The incentive charge, in its popular form, predicts that the first workshop will idle and the second, driven by the ministry's bonuses and inspections, will hum. The evidence assembled in this article predicts nearly the opposite distribution of pathologies: the first workshop will work steadily and argue endlessly about fairness, the second will storm at the period's end, hoard timber against the supplier's failure, hide its true capacity from the negotiator, and let its designs age while its reports improve. The parable is permitted by the evidence because each of its elements corresponds to a literature this article has weighed, and its moral is the assessment in miniature. Ownership alone settles nothing. The questions are always the same four: what do the workers see, what do they hold, what happens when the enterprise fails, and who gains when something improves. Ask those of any workshop, any ministry, any economy, and the incentive problem has been stated correctly at last, in the only form in which it can be answered.

The parable's two streets exist, in fragments, inside every real economy, which is why the argument refuses to settle along the border between systems. A capitalist hospital ward runs on the first workshop's motives more than the second's, and a planned economy's design bureau, in its sealed priority, borrowed the demanding customer that the second workshop never had. The assessment's distinctions travel across the border because the mechanisms do, and a reader who has learned to ask the four questions of a ministry will find them equally awkward to ask of a corporation, a university, or a platform. That portability is the final evidence that the article has been about institutions rather than about flags.

## For students and exam candidates

The examinable asset in this article is the separation itself. Candidates lose marks by debating the incentive charge as one proposition; they gain them by naming the three versions, grading each against its own evidence, and closing with the residue. Practise the moves in order: state the effort version, answer it with the cooperative and crowding evidence, concede its surviving design form; state the managerial version, concede it as Kornai's conditional law; state the innovation version, and show that funding, selecting, and diffusing are three gaps, of which public research funding closes only the first. Keep the defeater ready, since evaluation questions reward the candidate who can say what would change the verdict. The three versions assessed table converts directly into revision material: save it to your [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) as a one-page card, and work the cluster's past-paper questions through the [ReportMedic UPSC PYQ explorer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) to see how often the charge appears in its unseparated, slogan form, and how the separated answer outperforms it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The most productive seminar this article supports begins with the evidence students least expect: the cooperative literature. Set the plywood studies and the French survival research against the free rider model in the same session, and let the class discover that the model's assumptions, invisibility of effort, purely material motivation, are doing the argumentative work. A second session belongs to Kornai, whose soft budget constraint lets students watch a mechanism generate a whole economy's behaviour without a single claim about national character or human selfishness; the ratchet effect, in particular, teaches beautifully, since every student who has ever hidden capacity from a target-setter recognises it at once. Close the sequence with the diffusion material and the Japanese suggestion comparison, which relocates the argument from motivation to machinery. The misconception to hunt throughout is the single-proposition habit: students arrive believing the charge is one thing, and they should leave unable to hear it as one thing again, which is a more durable achievement than any verdict they might memorise.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The open research frontier this article identifies is empirical as much as theoretical. The cooperative literature still lacks long-panel studies that follow firms across the degeneration pressures Webb predicted, with member and non-member labour tracked separately. The diffusion verdict rests heavily on the Soviet case and its East European variants; systematic comparison with diffusion inside large capitalist organisations, where adoption is also somebody's rewarded or unrewarded job, would sharpen the variable the verdict actually names. The public selection question, whether plural public funders can bear real losses over decades without rescue, is an institutional experiment waiting for a jurisdiction willing to run it, and its documentation would be a contribution whichever way it resolved. Journalists covering cooperative ventures, public innovation agencies, or platform labour will find the article's distinctions portable: ask of any new scheme who selects, who adopts, who gains by adoption, and how those measured can bend the measure. Those four questions, applied early, separate the designs that deserve coverage as innovations from the designs that are slogans with budgets.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the incentive objection to socialism?

The objection holds that a socialist economy weakens the connections on which exertion, economy, and renewal depend. It detaches a person's reward from that person's contribution, removes the threat of failure that disciplines enterprise managers, and abolishes the profit that rewards successful innovation. From those detachments it predicts declining effort at the bench, waste and hoarding in the office, and stagnation in the laboratory. The assessment in this article separates the prediction into three claims, about individuals, about managers, and about novelty, because the evidence treats each claim differently, and a verdict pronounced on the bundle, for or against, is wrong about at least two of its parts.

### Q: Which version of the incentive objection is strongest?

The innovation version, by a clear margin. Claims about individual effort run into a substantial body of research on worker-managed firms, communes, and the professions, all showing exertion sustained where reward is shared or even absent. Claims about managers were borne out in the planned economies, but they amount to a conditional law about enterprises protected from failure, not a peculiarity of social ownership. The innovation claim is different in kind: it points to three services, bearing the cost of risky development, choosing among rival proposals, and carrying proven improvements across an economy, for which no design yet proposed has supplied substitutes of proven power.

### Q: Are worker cooperatives less productive?

The research literature says no. Studies of labour-managed firms across several countries, including the classic comparisons of worker-owned plywood mills with conventional mills in the same industry, find productivity at least equal to comparable conventional firms, alongside more compressed pay structures, steadier employment through downturns, and survival records that are no worse and in several careful studies better. The findings carry caveats about selection and about the market environment every studied cooperative borrows discipline from, and they do not settle whether a whole economy could run on the form. They do settle the narrower point the charge depends on: loosening the tie between individual output and individual pay does not, by itself, collapse effort.

### Q: What is crowding out of intrinsic motivation?

Crowding out names the process by which an external inducement, a payment or a control, displaces the internal reasons a person had for doing something, leaving the person less motivated than before the inducement arrived. Research associated with Edward Deci and Richard Ryan showed that tangible rewards, when expected, tend to undermine intrinsic interest in engaging tasks, and Bruno Frey extended the analysis to civic behaviour, where pricing a duty can convert it into a transaction people feel free to refuse. The classic field illustration is blood donation: offering payment drove away givers whose motives had been civic, and drew in others who had cause to hide facts that should have barred them. The effect has boundaries and disputes, but its existence breaks any simple equation between more reward and more effort.

### Q: Was the managerial version of the charge confirmed?

Yes, for the design in which it was tested. In the state socialist economies, enterprises that could not fail behaved as János Kornai's analysis of the soft budget constraint predicts: they hoarded materials and labour against future shortage, understated their true capacity so that future targets would stay reachable, restrained performance because one year's achievement became the next year's obligation, and shed no activity however wasteful. The behaviours were attested by the managers who ran the system and reproduced across countries sharing the design. The confirmation is conditional rather than universal, since the same syndrome turns up inside capitalist economies wherever firms are rescued from failure, which is why the verdict convicts protection without exit rather than social ownership as such.

### Q: Why did socialist economies fail at innovation diffusion?

Because adoption was punished and routine was safe. A director who installed a new process disrupted current output, on which bonuses and standing depended, while the gain, once proven, was folded into higher future targets, leaving the enterprise with the disruption and without the reward. Failure, by contrast, was personal. Between enterprises the same arithmetic held: a rival's success offered the imitator the same poor bargain; suppliers had no reason to press new equipment on captive customers, and carrying improvements across boundaries earned nobody a reward. The knowledge existed inside the economy, much of it proposed by its own engineers. What was missing was an adopter whose arithmetic pointed toward adopting.

### Q: Do capitalist incentive structures work?

Imperfectly, and by a bundle of mechanisms rather than by the single lever of the textbooks. Large corporations suffer the principal-agent problems that agency theory describes, executive rewards at the summit are loosely connected to performance and sometimes reward failure outright, and much exertion at every level is drawn out by supervision, by professional norms, by team pressure, and by ambition rather than by any fine fitting of pay to contribution. Piece rates, where they are used, generate their own gaming, including the concealment of improved methods from the rate-setter. None of this shows the system fails to elicit effort and economy on a vast scale. It shows that the contrast case for the incentive charge is an administered, patchwork order with characteristic pathologies, not the finely tuned mechanism of the slogan.

### Q: What is the free rider problem in collective ownership?

It is the arithmetic of shared proceeds. Where a group's output is divided among its members, a member who exerts less draws an undiminished share while giving up only a sliver of the joint product, so restraint is privately profitable and diligence is privately taxed, unless something corrects the arithmetic. Mancur Olson generalised the logic for large groups, and Holmström formalised it for teams. The corrections that work in practice are visibility, since members who can see one another's effort can sanction its absence; stake, since a share in the surplus makes waste a personal loss; and voice, since members who set the rules experience them as their own. The problem is real where those corrections are absent, in large, anonymous, voiceless settings, and weak where they are present.

### Q: Did any socialist economy try moral incentives?

Cuba did, and debated the choice at the highest level. In the internal economic debate of the nineteen sixties, Ernesto Guevara's budgetary finance system proposed to fund enterprises from the state budget and to rest motivation on emulation, honour, and voluntary labour, arguing that paying for every exertion would teach citizens to price everything, reproducing the motives of the order being replaced. The opposing camp, led by Carlos Rafael Rodríguez, defended enterprise self-financing and treated material reward as indispensable. The pure design did not last; the economy soon restored material incentives, and moral appeals survived as a supplement, not a basis. The episode shows moral motivation to be real but not scalable by proclamation: honour distributed by a ministry becomes another administered indicator.

### Q: What would answer the innovation version?

An exhibit, not an argument. The verdict would fall before an actual case: an economy under central planning that held the frontier of innovation across most sectors for a full generation, or before a demonstrated design, operated at scale, that funds risky development, chooses among proposals through plural and fallible choosers who bear their losses, and makes adoption a gain for the adopting enterprise rather than a confiscation of its success. Publicly funded research, for all its genuine achievements in foundational science, supplies only the first of the three services, and in the economies where it flourished, markets supplied the other two downstream. Until the exhibit appears, the innovation version stands as the residue of the whole incentive debate.

### Q: How does the innovation residue grade what survives of the incentive objection?

The residue is the grading itself. Strip away the effort claim, which the cooperative and behavioural evidence reduces to a design constraint about visibility, stake, and voice; assign the managerial claim to its proper conditional place, as a law about protection without exit that binds any system rebuilding that protection; generalise the measurement claim to every administered metric, in ministries and platforms alike; and what remains is a specific, unanswered problem about novelty. The residue names that remainder and relocates the argument to it. Its practical effect is disciplinary: defenders may no longer claim victory for answering the weakest version, and critics may no longer claim the whole objection for pressing it.

### Q: What should a student separate between motivating effort and selecting which risks to fund?

Almost everything, since the two problems share only a vocabulary. Motivating effort concerns people whose tasks are known: the question is what elicits exertion on work that already exists, and the evidence points to stake, visibility, voice, and professional ethic alongside pay. Selecting which risks to fund concerns tasks that do not yet exist: the question is who chooses among proposals most of which will fail, under uncertainty no expertise dissolves, and what befalls choosers whose choices fail. A ministry can be excellent at the first and hopeless at the second, as the planned economies were, eliciting heroic exertion on priorities while their selection machinery drifted toward the safe and the sponsored. Answers that merge the two problems inherit the confusion of the slogan they came from.

### Q: Why did the state socialist economies diffuse innovation so poorly between producers?

The carriers were missing and the appetite was absent. In a market economy, a proven improvement travels because rivals copy it under competitive pressure, suppliers standardise and promote the new equipment, engineers carry knowledge between employers, and the pioneer's customers advertise the gain. The planned economies had the journals, the exhibitions, and the technical information institutes, but its enterprises faced captive customers and negotiated targets, so a rival's improvement was a threat to the plan rather than a spur; suppliers had allocated orders and no reason to evangelise; and engineers advanced by staying within sealed hierarchies rather than by moving. Diffusion is a traffic, and the system had built the libraries while abolishing the roads, the vehicles, and every reason to travel.

### Q: How does the three versions assessed table order the claims?

It orders them by evidential fortune, weakest first. The effort row records a claim weakened by the cooperative and motivation literatures, surviving only as a constraint on design at scale. The managerial row records a claim confirmed in the state socialist design and stated conditionally, so that it binds any system, of any ownership form, that guarantees enterprise survival. The innovation row records a claim standing on both the historical record and the design literature, with its three components, funding, selection, and diffusion, graded separately so that partial answers cannot masquerade as whole ones. The ordering reverses the popular presentation of the charge, which leads with effort because the effort claim lends itself to insult, and it is the reversal, as much as any single verdict, that the table exists to teach.

### Q: What separates inventing in a prioritised sector from diffusing an invention across an economy?

Everything that made the priority sectors exceptional. A weapons or space programme serves one customer able to specify the requirement, pay without haggling, test against an unforgiving standard, and impose the chosen design on every relevant producer, so selection and diffusion are supplied by command within the enclave. Invention there could flourish, and did. The civilian economy had no such customer, no such test, and no such authority; its producers were rewarded for fulfilling plans in established assortments, and an improvement originating anywhere faced thousands of custodians for whom adoption meant disruption now and harder targets later. The separation is therefore not talent or effort, which the enclave and the economy shared, but machinery: where selection and diffusion existed, innovation followed; where they did not, invention slept.
