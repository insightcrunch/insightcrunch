Mariategui is the point where Marxism in the Americas stops being a translation and starts being a theory. Jose Carlos Mariategui, a self-taught Peruvian journalist who died at thirty-five, produced in the nineteen twenties the first substantial original Marxist analysis written on the continent, and he built it around a single relocation of the question. What his country called the Indian problem, debated for generations as a matter of race, schooling, morals, or administration, he declared to be a problem of land: a question of who held the soil of the Andes and on what terms the people who worked it were allowed to live. From that relocation everything else in his work follows, including the claim for which he is remembered wherever the history of Marxist thought is seriously studied. The communal landholding forms surviving in the indigenous communities of the highlands, he argued, were not a museum piece to be dissolved by progress but an existing basis of collective production on which a Peruvian socialism could be built, so that his country need not pass through a completed capitalist stage before socialism became possible. Readers who know their Marx will feel the ground move under that sentence, because it is structurally the argument Marx himself made about the Russian peasant commune in a set of drafts written near the end of his life. Mariategui made it without access to those drafts. They were unavailable to him in every language he read, and there is no evidence he ever knew they existed. That independent convergence, two thinkers arriving at the same heterodox position from opposite ends of the earth and from opposite ends of the tradition, is the strongest exhibit this profile has to offer, and the article returns to it once the arguments themselves have been set out in full.

![Jose Carlos Mariategui at his desk in Lima with the pages of his journal Amauta, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The profile that follows is organised the way his own work is organised. A fifth of it belongs to the life, because the life explains the formation: a newsboy who educated himself in a newspaper office, a crippled leg, a subsidised exile in Europe that placed him inside the Italian labour movement at its most creative moment, a return to Lima, and seven crowded years of writing, publishing, and organising before an early death. A third belongs to the land argument itself, stated at the strength he gave it, with the evidence he offered and the orthodox positions he departed from kept separate so that the reader can grade each part. A fifth belongs to the parallel with Marx's Russian correspondence, where the convergence is demonstrated rather than asserted. The remainder takes up the dispute with the Communist International that his positions provoked, the strongest objection his communal argument has attracted, the answer that can honestly be made to it and the concession that answer owes, and the contested inheritance that has made him the most claimed thinker in Latin American Marxism. Two disciplines govern the whole. His arguments are reported from his texts and from the record of his short career, not from the movements that later adopted his name. And where the scholarship has damaged one of his claims, the damage is recorded in the same register as the claim, because a profile that protects its subject from his critics teaches the reader nothing about why he still matters.

## Mariategui in one paragraph: the definition and the claim

The canonical definition used throughout this series runs as follows. Jose Carlos Mariategui was a Peruvian Marxist theorist and journalist who argued that the condition of Peru's indigenous population was fundamentally a problem of land rather than of race, education, or administration, and that surviving indigenous communal forms could serve as a basis for socialism. Every clause of that sentence carries weight. Peruvian matters because the theory was built from one country's evidence and refused, on principle, to be generalised before it had been grounded. Theorist and journalist matters because the two trades were fused in him: his theory was written in essays, editorials, and polemics produced at newspaper speed, and its unfinished, occasional character is a fact about its form that any assessment must register. The land clause states his diagnosis. The communal clause states his construction, the positive proposal that distinguishes him from every critic of the Peruvian land system who stopped at denouncing it. A reader who holds the definition firmly can already see the shape of the disputes ahead. The diagnosis was resisted by everyone who preferred the Indian problem to be about anything cheaper than property. The construction was resisted by an orthodoxy for which communal forms belonged to the past that socialism would abolish, not to the material from which it might be built.

### What did Mariategui argue that no Marxist in the Americas had argued before him?

That the indigenous question and the agrarian question were the same question, and that the communal institutions surviving among the highland peasantry could serve as the foundation of a socialist order rather than as an obstacle to be cleared away by capitalist development before socialism could begin.

Earlier Marxists on the continent had applied the doctrine to American conditions, sometimes intelligently, but application is not what he did. His predecessors asked how the received stages, the received party forms, and the received reading of history fitted countries the doctrine's founders had never studied. He asked the prior question: what does this specific social formation, examined on its own evidence, actually contain, and what transformation does its own material make possible? The reversal of the direction of inquiry is his originality at the methodological level, and it is why his work reads differently from the party literature of his decade. Where the functionary began from the programme and looked for a country to fit it, he began from the Peruvian sierra and let the programme be judged by what the sierra showed. The communal argument could only be produced by that reversal. No one scanning Peru for the standard prerequisites of socialism, a large industrial proletariat, a mature bourgeoisie, a completed agrarian capitalism, would have found the ayllu interesting. Someone asking what collective forms of production already existed in the country, and what they might become under changed conditions, found it at once.

Three misreadings attach to his name so reliably that a profile should disarm them at the start. He was not a functionary of the Communist International, transmitting instructions from Moscow to a colonial section; the International's representatives criticised his positions sharply during his lifetime, and the party he founded was renamed and aligned only after his death, against the resistance of his own collaborators. He was not an indigenist who borrowed Marxist vocabulary to decorate a romance of the Inca past; his framework remained historical materialism throughout, his unit of analysis was the mode of production rather than the culture, and he polemicised against purely literary celebrations of the indigenous world with as much energy as he spent on the landlords. And he did not reject Marxism in favour of a home-grown creed. His famous insistence that socialism in America must be a creation rather than a copy was a claim about how a universal doctrine is honestly used, made by a man who read Marx closely and expected his readers to do the same. Each misreading survives because it is convenient to somebody: the first to those who would dismiss him as a conduit, the second to those who would claim him for cultural nationalism, the third to those who would set him against Marxism itself. The record supports none of them, and the sections below supply the evidence in each case.

## The three arguments table

His case rests on three arguments, and they are load-bearing in a specific order. The land argument is the diagnosis: the Indian question is at bottom a question of land. The communal argument is the construction: the surviving communal forms supply an existing basis for socialist production. The gamonalismo argument is the identification of the obstacle: the local landlord power system, rather than the distant state or the foreign company in the first instance, is what blocks every remedy. The table below sets each argument out with the evidence he offered for it, the orthodox position each departed from, and its subsequent fortunes in the scholarship. It is the article's findable artifact, and a reader pressed for time can take the whole profile from it, though the sections that follow exist because each row conceals a dispute that the table can only name.

| The argument | What it claims | The evidence he offered | The orthodox position it departed from | How it has fared in scholarship |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The land argument | The condition of the indigenous population follows from the land tenure system, not from race, schooling, morals, or administration, and only agrarian transformation can alter it | The persistence of servile labour relations on the highland estates, the failure of protective legislation across a century, and the correlation between land concentration and indigenous misery in the regions he surveyed | That the Indian question was a matter of uplift, schooling, and legal protection to be solved within the existing property order, or a backwardness that capitalist development would dissolve in due course | Substantially vindicated as a framing: subsequent agrarian historiography and the region's own reform history treat land tenure as the central variable, though the mechanisms are now described with more precision than he commanded |
| The communal argument | The communal landholding and cooperative labour forms surviving in the indigenous communities are a living foundation of collective production on which socialism can be built, without a completed capitalist stage | The persistence of the ayllu and of communal tenure and cooperative labour practices through the colonial and republican assaults, and their continued functioning as the organising forms of village production in much of the sierra | That communal forms were residues of a superseded mode of production, destined for dissolution, and that socialism presupposed a completed bourgeois stage that Peru had not undergone | The most contested row: the survival he documented is not in dispute, but historians have shown a deeper commercial penetration of the highlands than he allowed, and the argument now stands in the qualified form discussed later in this profile |
| The gamonalismo argument | The effective obstacle to every remedy is the local landlord power system, the gamonal and his network, which controls land, labour, and local authority and nullifies protective law | The fusion of economic and political power in the provincial landlord, the unpaid labour services exacted on the estates, and the demonstrated impotence of legislation and of the communities' legal recognition against local power | That the principal enemy was foreign imperialism acting through a national state, with the landlord treated as a secondary survival rather than as the operative system of domination in the countryside | Broadly sustained: the literature on the Peruvian sierra treats the gamonal order as the real structure of rural power in his period, while refining his account of its links to coastal and foreign capital |

Two features of the table deserve emphasis before the detail begins. The first is that the rows depend on one another. Detach the land argument from the communal argument and he becomes a radical agrarian reformer of a familiar type, demanding redistribution without saying what social order the redistribution serves. Detach the communal argument from the gamonalismo argument and it floats free as ethnography, a claim about village institutions with no account of the power that would have to be broken for those institutions to become a foundation. The three together form a complete political anatomy: what is wrong, what exists to build with, and what stands in the way. The second feature is the asymmetry of the fourth column. The diagnosis has worn best, the anatomy of the obstacle nearly as well, and the construction carries the heaviest subsequent damage. That distribution is common in the history of theory and it is not a refutation of the whole. A thinker is not obliged to be equally right in every part, and the sections on the strongest objection will show exactly where the damage falls and what it leaves standing.

### How does the order of the three arguments shape the way his case must be read?

Because each argument answers the question the previous one raises. The diagnosis of land creates the demand for a social form that could hold land differently; the communal survey supplies that form from the country's own material; and the analysis of the gamonal explains why neither reform nor recognition has ever reached the village unimpeded.

Read in the reverse order, the case collapses into its weakest version. Begin with the communal forms and he sounds like an antiquarian, in love with survivals. Begin with the gamonal and he sounds like a regional pamphleteer, absorbed by provincial tyranny. The order is the argument's logic made visible: he earns the right to speak of communal forms by first proving that the question is land, and he earns the right to propose a transformation by naming the power that has defeated every lesser remedy. Examiners of his reputation, friendly and hostile alike, have often raided the second row while ignoring the first and third, and the resulting Mariategui, a dreamy celebrant of the ayllu, is a figure he would not have recognised. The profile therefore keeps the rows in their order and asks the reader to do the same.


## A newsboy's education: poverty, print and the injured leg

He was born in Moquegua, in the far south of Peru, in June of the year eighteen ninety-four, into a household that poverty was already dismantling. His father, a minor employee of mixed descent who claimed kinship with one of the country's founding families, left the family while the boy was small and died soon after; his mother, Amalia La Chira, of indigenous and African as well as Spanish ancestry, raised her children in straitened circumstances, moving between the southern provinces and the capital. The family's descent from comfort into precarious respectability is not decoration in his biography. It placed him, from childhood, inside the Peru of the unpropertied while giving him the literacy and the manners of the lettered world, and the doubleness of that position, insider to poverty and insider to print, is the social fact his whole career exploits. He never romanticised his origins and never concealed them. They are simply the first instance of the pattern his theory would later formalise: a man formed by two worlds, obliged to translate between them, and unwilling to accept the account either world gave of the other.

His schooling was brief and broken. At about the age of eight, at school in Huacho, he injured his left leg in an accident whose consequences never left him: the damaged limb, the long hospitalisations, the convalescences in which a bedridden boy read whatever came to hand. The injury ankylosed the knee and left him lame for life, subject to pain and to the recurring medical crises that would eventually cost him the leg itself. The biographical tradition is tempted to make the sickroom into the forge of the thinker, and there is a limited truth in the temptation that should be stated without sentiment. The injury ended his formal education and threw him on his own resources at exactly the age when a poorer boy without the accident would have been apprenticed to a trade. It gave him enforced leisure of a peculiar kind, the leisure of the invalid, in which reading was not a school task but the only available enlargement of a narrowed world. What he made of that leisure was his own doing. Thousands of injured children read and forget. He read and built a system of self-instruction that carried him, within a decade, from the newspaper's back room to the front rank of his country's intellectual life.

At fourteen he went to work at the Lima newspaper La Prensa, entering at the bottom of the trade, in the world of the linotype operators and the delivery boys, running copy and learning the material production of a daily paper from the floor up. The detail matters for the same reason the sickroom matters: his Marxism was never the doctrine of a university seminar. He encountered the working class first as workmates, in an industry whose production process he knew with his hands, and he encountered ideas first as things that had to be set in type, fitted to a column, and sold on a street. He rose quickly. By his late teens he was writing for the paper, and under the pseudonym Juan Croniqueur he became one of the most read chroniclers in the capital, writing on theatre, literature, society, and the races at the Hipodromo with a fluency that made him, while still very young, a figure of the literary generation that gathered around the poet Abraham Valdelomar and the short-lived journal Colonida. The young Mariategui of those years was a modernist man of letters, an aesthete of the horse track and the salon, and his contemporaries remembered the elegance before they remembered the politics. The conversion, when it came, was not a repudiation of that formation but a deepening of it: the literary generation's rebellion against the official Peru of the oligarchy found, in him alone among its leading figures, a social theory adequate to the rebellion.

### How did a boy with almost no formal schooling become a theorist?

By treating the newspaper as his university and the sickroom as his library. Excluded from secondary education by poverty and by his injured leg, he educated himself in the production rooms and pages of the Lima press, reading omnivorously through long convalescences and learning theory as a working journalist learns everything, against deadlines and in public.

The self-education was real, and its gaps were real too, and honesty about both is required. He had no languages beyond Spanish, French, and the Italian he acquired in Europe; he read Marx in translation; his economics was that of an exceptionally intelligent autodidact rather than of a trained political economist, and his statistical equipment was thin even by the standards of his decade. The Seven Essays contain no tables of his own construction and little quantitative argument. What the autodidact's formation gave him, in compensation, was a freedom from the scholastic habits of the doctrine's official custodians. He had never been examined on the catechism, and so he never mistook the catechism for the theory. His Marxism was assembled from reading undertaken to solve problems he had met in Peruvian reality, the land, the Indian, the power of the provincial boss, and the assembly shows in the work's structure: every theoretical commitment in the essays is doing local work, earning its place by what it explains about Peru. That is the formation's strength and its limit in one. A reader looking for his treatment of questions that Peru did not pose to him, the theory of the state in the abstract, the economics of the trade cycle, will look in vain, and the unfinished character of the corpus, which his early death froze in place, begins in the circumstances of his education.

## The European years: exile with a scholarship's face

In nineteen nineteen the political ground shifted under him. The populist president Augusto Leguia took power, and the young journalist, whose chronicles had made him conspicuous and whose sympathies were visibly moving leftward, became an embarrassment the new regime preferred to export. The instrument chosen was characteristic of the period's manners: a government scholarship and a nominal official posting abroad, a subsidised exile dressed in the costume of a reward. He understood the transaction perfectly and accepted it on his own terms. Europe, which the regime intended as a shelf on which to park him, became the making of him, and the four years he spent there, most of them in Italy, constitute the second and decisive stage of his formation. He travelled with his formation half made, a literary rebel with socialist sympathies and a head full of the Peruvian questions. He returned with a doctrine, a method, and a set of experiences no other Latin American Marxist of his generation possessed.

Italy in those years was the most instructive country in Europe for a man asking his questions. He arrived as the postwar crisis broke: the biennio rosso, the two red years of strikes, land occupations, and factory councils, culminating in the occupation of the factories in the autumn of nineteen twenty, when hundreds of thousands of metalworkers held the plants of Turin and Milan and the question of who should run industry ceased to be theoretical for several weeks. He witnessed the occupations and their aftermath at close quarters, travelled in the country, wrote chronicles for the Lima press, and read his way into the Italian debate: the idealist philosophy of Benedetto Croce, whose anti-positivism taught a generation that history is made by human action rather than by automatic laws; the liberal socialist Piero Gobetti, whose respect for the Turin workers' movement modelled an intellectual's alliance with it; the French syndicalist Georges Sorel, whose theory of the myth, the mobilising image that moves masses where syllogisms cannot, left a permanent mark on his understanding of how doctrines act in history; and above all the circle of the journal L'Ordine Nuovo in Turin, where Antonio Gramsci and his comrades were working out, in the factory council movement, a Marxism of national specificity and cultural preparation strikingly parallel to the one he would build for Peru. The Italian years gave him his permanent intellectual furniture: a hatred of mechanical materialism, a conviction that will, culture, and organisation are material forces, and a model of the intellectual as a builder of institutions rather than a commentator upon them.

### What did the Italian years give him that Lima could not?

A lived experience of a workers' movement at the point of production, and a Marxism being remade under national conditions in real time. The factory occupations, the council movement, and the debates of the Italian left showed him doctrine being created from a country's own material, which is the operation he later performed for Peru.

He also acquired a family and a language. In Italy he married Anna Chiappe, who became the steadfast partner of his Peruvian years, the manager of his household and after his death the guardian of his papers and the publisher of his complete works. His Italian became fluent, his reading in French deepened, and his chronicles home, later collected in the volume La escena contemporánea, show a reporter of European calibre analysing fascism's rise with a clarity most European journalists did not match. He saw Mussolini's movement early and named it correctly, as the armed reaction of a frightened bourgeoisie, at a time when much of the European press still treated it as a colourful disorder. The European years also fixed his distance from the two temptations that would beset Latin American Marxism for a century: the temptation of the salon, which he had already outgrown, and the temptation of the apparatus, which he now saw from inside a movement that was about to build one. He admired the Italian communists' seriousness. He also absorbed, from the Italians themselves, the lesson that a party is an instrument a nation makes, not a franchise a centre issues, and he carried that lesson home in his luggage like contraband.

## Livorno and the founding of a party not his own

In January of nineteen twenty-one the Italian Socialist Party held its congress at Livorno, and the congress split. The left, refusing the conditions and the discipline demanded by the Communist International's emissaries and impatient with the majority's refusal to expel its reformists, walked out and founded the Communist Party of Italy in a hall across the town. Mariategui was present at Livorno as an observer and reporter, in the company of the Italian movement he had attached himself to, and he watched at arm's length the birth of a communist party out of the crisis of an old one. The experience completed his political education in a way no book could have done. He saw that a party is not founded by resolution but by a real division in a real movement, that the division at Livorno expressed genuine strategic disagreements about the factory occupations' failure and the reformists' paralysis, and that the new party's founders, Gramsci among them, understood their act as the creation of an Italian instrument for an Italian revolution, whatever their loyalty to the International. The congress also showed him the International's manner at close quarters: the emissary's ultimatum, the demand for expulsions as the price of recognition, the treatment of a national movement's internal life as a branch office's compliance problem. He filed the observation away. Eight years later, at a conference table in Buenos Aires, he would meet the same manner directed at his own movement's positions, and his response had been prepared at Livorno.

The Gramsci parallel deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the closest the period offers to a controlled comparison. Two intellectuals of the same generation, both formed outside the universities' main channel, both journalists before they were theorists, both marked by physical affliction, both convinced that their country's revolution required a cultural and moral preparation the orthodox schema ignored, both builders of journals as instruments of formation: the resemblances are not coincidence but convergence, the same doctrine being remade by similar men under similar pressures in different national material. He read the Turin circle's work and moved in its orbit during the Italian years, and the reader who wants the Italian side of that exchange in full should follow the series treatment of [Antonio Gramsci's intellectual biography](/2015/01/01/antonio-gramsci-intellectual-biography/), where the council movement and its theory receive their own profile. What he took from Italy was not Gramsci's system, which did not yet exist in written form, but the Turin example: proof, witnessed at first hand, that Marxism could be thought originally inside a national reality without ceasing to be Marxism. Peru's difference from Italy was obvious to him, an agrarian and indigenous country against an industrialising one, and the difference was the assignment. If the method was to think the doctrine from the national material outward, then Peru's material, land and the Indian, would dictate a theory as different from Turin's as the sierra is from the Po valley, and no less legitimate for that.


## Return to Lima: the journal, the amputation, the work

He came home in nineteen twenty-three to a country whose questions had not waited for him. Leguia's modernising dictatorship was dressing Lima in avenues and monuments while the sierra's order stood untouched, and the labour movement, the university reform movement, and the new indigenista current in literature were all looking for a doctrine. He supplied lectures before he supplied a programme: at the Universidad Popular González Prada, the workers' night university founded by the student movement, he delivered a celebrated series on the crisis of the world, expounding the European catastrophe, the Russian Revolution, and the collapse of the old order to audiences of workers and students who had never heard the material treated whole. The lectures established his authority in the movement that would become his. They also show the method already formed. He did not begin from Peru and apologise outward; he began from the world crisis and located Peru inside it, teaching his audience that their country's landlords and its foreign creditors were parts of a single structure whose crisis was general. The Peruvian argument, when it came, would be built inside that frame: the sierra's feudalism and the coast's dependent capitalism as local forms of a world order, not as national peculiarities.

In nineteen twenty-four the injured leg declared its final crisis. The limb that had been damaged in childhood, operated on and endured for two decades, deteriorated past saving, and in May of that year it was amputated. He was thirty years old. From then until his death he worked from a wheelchair, in a house in Lima that became, by the testimony of everyone who visited it, a place of extraordinary intellectual intensity: the chair drawn up to the desk, the visitors received in the study, the conversation ranging over literature, politics, and the movement's business with a gaiety that his friends recorded with astonishment. The disability is reported here as he would have required it to be reported, factually and without sentiment, because the facts carry the meaning without assistance. He lost a leg in nineteen twenty-four and worked from a wheelchair thereafter. In the six years that remained to him he founded the most important journal in the region's intellectual history, wrote his major book, founded a political party and a trade union confederation, conducted polemics on three continents, and raised a family. The wheelchair was the condition of that production, not its subject, and his correspondence treats it with a practical man's impatience for anything that wasted time. A profile that lingers over it in the key of pathos betrays its subject, who had no use for the register.

The journal came first, and the order was deliberate. Amauta, founded in September of nineteen twenty-six, took its name from the Quechua word for the sage and teacher of the Inca world, a title that announced the whole programme in a word: an indigenous name for a Marxist journal, the doctrine addressed to Peru in Peru's own vocabulary. It ran for thirty-five issues, until his death closed it, and it became what he designed it to be, the organising centre of a generation: literature, art, indigenista scholarship, labour news, translations of European Marxism, and his own editorials and essays, assembled into an instrument for forming the intellectual cadre of a movement that did not yet have a party. Its circulation reached across Latin America; its contributors included the leading writers of the continent; its pages are still the single richest archive of the region's intellectual life in those years. With his brother Julio Cesar he also ran the publishing house Minerva, which printed the journal, his books, and the works of his circle, giving the movement its own material base in print. The party followed in October of nineteen twenty-eight, founded as the Peruvian Socialist Party on an explicitly Marxist programme, and the General Confederation of Peruvian Workers followed in nineteen twenty-nine, uniting the labour movement under a leadership formed in his circle. Journal, party, confederation: the sequence is the Turin lesson applied, formation before organisation, organisation before the bid for power, and it was executed in four years by a man in a wheelchair who knew, though he did not say, how short his time was. He died in Lima in April of nineteen thirty, at thirty-five, of the complications of the old injury. The Seven Interpretive Essays, his major work, had been published in nineteen twenty-eight, two years before, and the bulk of his writing, the journalism in which much of his political argument lives, lay scattered in the periodicals of three countries.

### Why did he build a journal before he built a party?

Because his theory of how movements form put consciousness before apparatus. A party founded before a cadre had been educated in a common analysis would be a label in search of members; the journal was the school in which the analysis was worked out publicly, so that the party, when founded, inherited a doctrine already argued rather than a programme merely announced.

The sequence also answers a question about his Marxism that the Comintern dispute will raise in sharper form. An apparatchik builds the organisation first and receives the doctrine from the centre as the organisation's operating instructions. He built the doctrine first, in the open, in a journal any reader could buy, and founded the organisation afterward to embody it. The order of construction is the biography of his independence. When the International's representatives later demanded that the Peruvian movement revise positions he had developed in print over years, they were not asking a branch manager to update a circular. They were asking the author of a public body of work to unsay it, and the impossibility of that demand, for a man of his formation and his pride, structured everything that followed. He died before the demand could be pressed to a conclusion, and the party he left behind, deprived of the one authority that could have sustained the argument, was renamed and brought into line within the year.

## The Indian problem restated: four framings refused

The Seven Interpretive Essays on Peruvian Reality, published in nineteen twenty-eight, is a book of seven essays and one argument. The essays treat the scheme of Peru's economic evolution, the Indian problem, the land problem, public education, the religious factor, regionalism and centralism, and the process of Peruvian literature, and they are bound together by a single analytical decision taken in the second and third of them: the decision to treat the Indian problem as the land problem. To feel the force of the decision, a reader must first see the field it cleared. The condition of Peru's indigenous majority, impoverished, coerced, excluded from the republic that nominally included them, had been debated for a century under four framings, and he refused all four, not because each contained nothing true but because each located the cause where no remedy could reach it, and because each, in its practical effect, protected the arrangement that produced the misery it deplored.

The racial framing held that the Indian's condition expressed the Indian's nature: a degenerate race, in the vocabulary of the period's scientific racism, or a fallen one, in the vocabulary of its romantic inversion, which mourned the Inca past while agreeing that the living Indian was its ruin. He dismissed the entire register. The problem was not ethnic, and no conclusion about blood could explain why the same population that had administered the most sophisticated agrarian civilisation in the pre-conquest Americas now starved on the edges of estates that occupied its ancestors' land. The educational framing, the most respectable of the four, held that schooling was the remedy: bring the Indian the alphabet and citizenship would follow. He answered that a pedagogy addressed to a population held in servitude by its landlords was a decoration on the structure of its oppression, and that the school, where it existed in the sierra, functioned as an instrument of the very order it claimed to leaven. The moral and religious framing, the missionary's, located the problem in the Indian's soul and its remedy in evangelisation or moral uplift; he treated it as the racial framing in clerical dress. The administrative and legal framing, the framing of the reforming state, proposed protective legislation, official defenders of the race, commissions and codes. Against it he deployed his most quoted analytical move: a century of protective law had left the Indian's condition untouched, because the authority charged with enforcing the law in the provinces was the very power the law was meant to restrain. Legislation addressed to the gamonal's district, enforced by the gamonal's judges, under the gamonal's eye, was not a remedy imperfectly applied. It was a remedy addressed to its own negation.

### Why does Mariategui say the Indian problem is the land problem?

because he held that the condition of Peru's indigenous population followed from dispossession of land rather than from race, education, morality, or administration, so that reforms addressing those things could not alter it and only agrarian transformation could. That is why his socialism begins from the community's surviving communal forms rather than from an imported programme.

The positive statement follows from the refusals with the force of an elimination proof. If the Indian's condition does not follow from his blood, his ignorance, his soul, or the absence of statutes, it follows from his material situation, and his material situation is defined by his relation to the land: dispossessed by the colonial and republican orders, confined to the shrinking territory of the communities, and bound to the great estates by labour relations that exacted his work without wages in forms surviving from the colony. Change that relation and the condition becomes changeable; leave it and every other reform is theatre. He stated the conclusion in its most compressed form by insisting that the problem of the Indian is rooted in the land tenure system of the Peruvian economy, and that every thesis on the subject which evades the socioeconomic core is a sterile exercise, however humane its intentions. The sterility charge is the point. He was not accusing the reformers of bad faith, though he found plenty of it. He was accusing their framings of a structural futility that no sincerity could cure, and relocating the debate onto the only ground where a remedy could be built: property.

One further consequence of the relocation should be drawn out, because it governs his difference from the indigenismo of his literary contemporaries. If the Indian problem is a land problem, then the Indian is not a cultural essence to be celebrated or a racial tragedy to be lamented but a peasant: a producer in a determinate relation to land and lord, whose emancipation is an agrarian revolution and whose culture, language, and communal institutions matter politically because they are the forms of his material life, not because they are picturesque. This is why his writing on indigenous themes is so free of the period's lyricism about the race and so full of tenures, services, and estates. It is also why the indigenous movement's own later traditions have been able to build on him without embarrassment. He offered the Indian not admiration but an analysis in which the Indian's own institutions, the community and its cooperative labour, appear as assets in a struggle rather than as relics in a museum. The offer was made by a Lima intellectual of mixed ancestry who never pretended to speak as an Indian. He spoke as an analyst of the system that held the Indian down, and he assigned the leading role in its overthrow to the Indian peasantry itself, organised and armed with an understanding of its own situation. The distinction between speaking for a people and analysing its enemy runs through the whole work.


## Gamonalismo: the power that owns the district

The third argument names the enemy, and the naming is a piece of analysis in itself. Gamonalismo, from gamonal, the term the sierra used for its local magnates, designates in his work not a class of wicked landlords but a system: the fusion, in the person and network of the provincial landowner, of economic, political, judicial, and social power, exercised over a district as a single undivided dominion. The gamonal held the best land and the water that made land valuable. He exacted from the indigenous tenants and colonos on his estate the labour services of the old regime, the unpaid work in his fields and the domestic service in his house known as pongaje, and he recruited labour beyond his estate through the enganche system, the advance of cash that bound the indebted peasant to seasons of work in the mines and plantations of the coast and the jungle. He was, or he appointed, the local authority: the subprefect, the governor, the justice of the peace were his creatures or his allies; the priest and the schoolteacher depended on his tolerance; the police of the district were his instrument. Against his power the community's legal personality, recognised on paper by the constitution of the republic in the decade he wrote, was a formality he could ignore, delay, or purchase. He was not a survival embarrassed by modernity. He was the state, in the only form in which the sierra's population ever met it.

The analytical yield of the concept is the explanation it gives of a fact that puzzled every reformer: the perfect sterility of protective law. Peru had legislated for the Indian since independence; the republic's founding liberator had decreed him a proprietor; constitutions had proclaimed his equality; a patronato and a bureau of indigenous affairs administered his protection from Lima. Nothing changed, and the gamonalismo analysis says why. A law is not a force. Between the statute in the capital and the village in the highlands stood the entire apparatus of local power, and that apparatus belonged to the man whose interests the statute threatened. He did not need to defy the law. He needed only to administer it, and administration, in his district, was a family business. Mariategui's formulation of the point was absolute: gamonalismo necessarily invalidates every law and ordinance for the protection of the Indian, because the feudal lord is, in the village, the judge, the authority, and the executor in one person. The absoluteness is earned by the analysis. It converts a century of reformist disappointment from a mystery of national character into a theorem about power, and it dictates the conclusion he drew: that the Indian's emancipation required not better laws but the destruction of the gamonal order, which is to say an agrarian revolution carried out against the system that owned the districts.

### Why does the gamonal, rather than the foreign company, stand as the first obstacle?

Because the gamonal is the power the indigenous peasant actually encounters: the holder of his land, the exactor of his labour, the appointer of his judges. Foreign capital dominates the coast and the export enclaves, but in the sierra its rule is mediated, and usually administered, by the landlord system that owns the districts.

The ordering matters and was deliberate. An anti-imperialism that began from the foreign company, the mine, the plantation, the bank, described the coast's reality accurately and the sierra's hardly at all. In the highlands the chain of domination had a local first link, and a politics that could not name it could not organise the population it claimed to serve. His insistence on the priority of the internal obstacle was not a denial of imperialism, which his analysis of the coastal economy states plainly, but a refusal to let the distant enemy excuse the proximate one, a refusal with a polemical edge against nationalists of every party who preferred their enemy foreign. It also carried a strategic implication he drew explicitly: the alliance against the gamonal order had to be built where the order ruled, among the peasantry of the communities and the colonos of the estates, and the party that proposed to lead that alliance had to be a party of that countryside and not only of the capital's workshops. The gamonalismo argument is thus the hinge between his analysis and his organising practice, the point where the Seven Essays touch the party he founded in the same year the book appeared.

A refinement of the concept should be registered, because later scholarship has both confirmed and complicated it. The gamonal was not, in the main, a grandee of the colonial aristocracy surviving into the republic. The research of the decades after his death showed the type renewing itself from below and from the market: merchants, mestizo strongmen, returned migrants, and purchasers of distressed estates all entered the gamonal class, and its power rested as much on control of commerce, credit, and office as on ancient title. The complication strengthens rather than weakens his argument. A system that recruits its personnel from successive generations and multiple sources is a structure, not a residue, and must be analysed as he analysed it, by its function in the organisation of land and labour rather than by the pedigree of its holders. Where the scholarship qualifies him is on the system's integration: the gamonal order was never as sealed against the coastal economy as his sharpest formulations suggest, since the wool trade, the mines' labour drafts, and the state's own fiscal needs bound sierra and coast into one circuit. That qualification, the penetration question, returns in force when the strongest objection is stated, and it is flagged here so that the reader can watch it approach.

## The ayllu and what survived of it

The second argument concerns the material from which a Peruvian socialism might be built, and its object is the ayllu: the kin-based communal organisation of the Andean village, holding land in common, allocating its use among families, and working it through cooperative labour forms whose names, ayni for reciprocal work between households and minka for collective work on common tasks, still structured the agricultural year in his lifetime. He traced the institution to the social organisation of the pre-conquest world, where the ayllu had been the cell of the Inca order, and he described that order itself, in the first of the essays, as an agrarian communism: a civilisation without private property in land, in which the community's labour fed the state, the cult, and itself under a theocratic administration of remarkable efficiency. The description is famous and has been much fought over, and its precise place in his argument must be fixed before the fighting starts. He did not propose the restoration of the Inca system, which he characterised without illusion as an authoritarian theocracy resting on the peasant's obedience, and he said explicitly that the development of the communal principle under modern conditions was a different thing from the resurrection of a dead empire. The Inca past functions in his argument as a demonstration, not as a destination: proof that the Andean population's communal habits were not a utopia imported from books but the sediment of its own history, a way of holding and working land that had been practised on that soil for centuries before a European doctrine named it.

What survived into his century, after the colonial and republican assaults, is the empirical heart of the argument, and he surveyed the assaults with a historian's care. The colony had battered the community without abolishing it: the reducciones that concentrated the population into administered villages, the mita that drafted its labour for the mines of Potosi and Huancavelica, the tribute that monetised its obligations, all pressed on the ayllu while using it, since the colonial state found the community a convenient unit of extraction and left its internal organisation standing the better to tax it. The republic, in the name of liberal principle, struck the harder blow in law: the founding decrees of the eighteen twenties declared the Indians proprietors of the communal lands and ordered their division into individual holdings, a reform whose effect, across the following century, was to expose the community's territory to piecemeal purchase, fraud, and seizure by the expanding estates. The latifundio grew at the comunidad's expense through the whole period, fastest where the export economy reached into the sierra, as the wool boom enriched the southern estates at the communities' cost. And yet the community survived: diminished, encroached upon, legally precarious, but present across the highlands as the actual organisation of indigenous production, holding land in common, working it cooperatively, and governing itself through its own authorities. His claim rested on that survival. The habits of cooperation and collective labour that socialism would require, he argued, already existed in the Peruvian countryside as living practice, not as memory, and a socialist transformation could develop them with modern technique instead of first destroying them in the name of a progress that had shown the Indian only its face as expropriation.

### What survived of the communal order into the Peru he analysed?

Not the Inca state, and not an intact pre-conquest world, but the working institution of the community itself: common holding of land in much of the highlands, cooperative labour in its reciprocal and collective forms, and self-government through the community's own authorities, all persisting under legal disability and territorial loss.

The distinction between the institution's survival and its purity is the load-bearing distinction of the whole argument, and this profile will return to it when the objections are heard. He claimed survival and function. He did not claim that the ayllu of his day stood outside the surrounding economy, untouched by money, markets, or the estate system; his own essays describe the community penetrated by the enganche recruiter, drained by migration, and bound to the hacienda by a hundred threads of debt and seasonal labour. The claim was that within that penetration the communal forms still organised production for a large part of the indigenous population, and that a form which organises production can be developed, where a form that merely survives as folklore cannot. Whether the balance between the communal core and the commercial penetration was as he judged it is an empirical question, and it is precisely the question on which the later historiography has pressed him hardest. For the moment, the argument's structure is what matters: he proposed to build forward from an existing institution, not backward toward a vanished one, and the proposal's audacity lies in its direction. The orthodoxy of his time, in both its liberal and its Marxist dialects, agreed that the community was the past. They disagreed only on whether its disappearance should be hastened by the market or mourned by the poets. He alone, among the systematic thinkers of his continent, proposed that it was the future's raw material.


## Feudal, bourgeois, communal: the three economies of his Peru

Underneath the three arguments sits a classification, set out in the first essay's scheme of Peru's economic evolution, and a reader who grasps the classification holds the key to the rest. He read the Peruvian formation of his day as the coexistence of three economies. In the sierra, a feudal economy: the great estate worked by servile labour, the colono bound to the hacienda by customary obligation rather than by a wage contract, the gamonal's dominion resting on land monopoly and extra-economic coercion. On the coast and in the export enclaves, a bourgeois economy, but a stunted and dependent one: sugar and cotton plantations, mines and guano and oil, organised capitalistically and owned substantially by foreign capital or by a national bourgeoisie entangled with it, importing its machinery and its politics together. And within and beneath both, the surviving economy of the indigenous community, communal in its property form and oriented to subsistence, neither feudal nor capitalist but older than either, the remnant of the pre-conquest order functioning inside the interstices of the republic. The classification did the theoretical work of the whole book. It explained why no single stage label fitted Peru, why the vocabulary of a European sequence, feudalism succeeded by capitalism succeeded in time by socialism, described his country only by violence to its facts, and why a politics adequate to the formation would have to fight on several historical levels at once.

The feudal classification of the sierra was the element his contemporaries contested most, and its function in the argument should be understood before its accuracy is judged. To call the hacienda feudal was to say that its labour relations rested on coercion outside the wage: the colono received his plot in exchange for services, the pongaje took the Indian into the landlord's house as an unpaid servant, and the whole structure reproduced itself through custom, debt, and force rather than through a labour market. It followed that the tasks the bourgeois revolution had performed elsewhere, the abolition of servile tenure, the creation of free labour and of a national market, remained unperformed in Peru, and that whoever performed them would inherit the historical role the Peruvian bourgeoisie had declined. The classification was thus not antiquarian. It assigned the coming revolution its first tasks and, by showing the national bourgeoisie too weak and too compromised to perform them, assigned those tasks to the socialist movement. Readers who want the general theory of such classifications, the criteria by which a [mode of production](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/) is identified and distinguished from its neighbours, will find the series treatment of that concept linked here, since the present profile's business is with his use of the instrument rather than with the instrument itself.

### Why does the classification of the sierra matter to the argument?

Because the classification assigns the revolution its tasks. A feudal sierra means the bourgeois revolution's own work, the destruction of servile tenure and of the landlord power that rests on it, remains undone and falls to whoever will do it; a capitalist sierra would have meant a different strategy, a different alliance, and a different timetable.

Later scholarship has revised the classification's edges without dissolving its centre. The haciendas of the sierra were more entangled with markets than the word feudal suggests to a European ear: they sold wool and food into commercial circuits, they responded to prices, and some of their labour was hired. His defenders reply, with justice, that his category was never the manor of the European middle ages but a relational claim, that the core labour relation on the estate was unfree, and that an estate can sell into a market while organising production through servitude, as slave plantations did on a grander scale. The debate is instructive precisely because both parties are describing the same institution from different ends, the market end and the labour end, and because his choice of end was dictated by his question. He was asking what had to be broken for the Indian to be free, and from that end of the institution the servile relation is the fact that matters. The classification also performed a second service, less often noticed. By naming three coexisting economies he refused the consolation of the single queue, the doctrine that all countries stand in one line of development at different distances from its head. Peru was not behind Europe on the same road. It was a different composition, and its transformation would be its own event. That refusal, generalised, is the multilinear position whose pedigree in Marx's own late writing the parallel sections below will establish, and it is already fully present in the first essay of a twenty-eight-year-old journalist's first book.

## The stage refused: why Peru need not wait

The question the communal argument forces can now be stated in its canonical form. If the sierra is feudal, the community a survival, and the bourgeoisie incapable, must Peru nevertheless live through a completed capitalist stage, enduring the dissolution of the communities and the full development of a bourgeois order, before socialism can be placed on the agenda? The orthodox answer of his decade said yes, and said it with the authority of a schema: history moves through its stages, each prepares the next, and a socialism attempted before its material prerequisites exist will degenerate into voluntarist adventure. His answer was no, and the grounds of the refusal deserve exact statement, because they are the theoretical core of his originality and the point at which his argument converges with the unpublished Marx.

The first ground was the composition of the formation itself. The stages of the schema are abstractions from a European experience in which one mode of production succeeded another across a whole society. Peru presented no such succession to wait for: its capitalism was not an immature version of the European article, growing toward completeness, but a dependent enclave formation whose growth actively preserved the feudal sierra it fed upon, since cheap servile labour and the gamonal's political order were among the conditions of the export economy's profitability. Waiting for that capitalism to complete itself meant waiting for a process whose tendency ran the other way. The second ground was the changed world context. The schema's stages presupposed a country developing in isolation, generating its prerequisites from its own resources across centuries. Peru existed inside a world economy in which socialism was no longer a speculation but an established state and a world movement, and in which technique, organisation, and example could be appropriated from outside rather than slowly secreted at home. A country need not repeat every stage of another's history when the results of those stages exist in the world to be taken up. The third ground was the communal basis itself. The prerequisites argument assumed that socialism requires the prior dissolution of communal forms and the universalisation of private property and wage labour, so that collectivism can then be rebuilt on the far side of individualism. His survey denied the premise for Peru: the collective forms existed, functioning, and the rational course was to develop them under modern conditions rather than to destroy them in obedience to a sequence. The revolution he proposed would therefore be socialist in its agency and its aim while performing, on the way, the unperformed tasks of the bourgeois revolution, the destruction of feudal tenure first of all. Tasks and stage come apart in his hands: the tasks are historical and unavoidable, the stage is a European memory mistaken for a law.

### On what grounds could Peru refuse to wait for a completed capitalism?

On three: because its capitalism was a dependent enclave whose growth preserved the feudal interior rather than dissolving it; because the world context had changed, with socialism an existing power and technique appropriable from outside; and because the communal forms supplied, already functioning, the collective basis the waiting was supposed to create.

The refusal should not be misread as a promise of ease, and he did not so present it. A socialism built on the communities would still require the conquest of power against the gamonal order and its coastal allies, the appropriation of modern technique, the industrialisation the country lacked, and the education of a peasantry the republic had kept in enforced ignorance. What the refusal removed was not difficulty but a sentence: the doctrine that the Indian must first be expropriated by progress, his community dissolved and his labour made free for the market, before history would permit him to be emancipated. That sentence, in its liberal form, had justified a century of land theft as the tuition of development, and in its orthodox Marxist form it threatened to justify another century of waiting on the same terms. His argument struck at both forms with a single move, and the move's ancestry in the founder's own late thought, which he could not have read, is the subject of the parallel to come. Before that parallel is drawn, the programmatic statement and the organisational conclusion must be set down, because the convergence is a claim about his theory, and the theory includes its method and its party as well as its agrarian core.

## Heroic creation: the programme in a sentence

In September of nineteen twenty-eight, in the anniversary editorial of Amauta, he wrote the sentence by which Latin American Marxism has known itself ever since. Socialism in America, he declared, must not be a tracing and a copy of the European article: "It must be a heroic creation." The sentence is quoted wherever his name is honoured, usually as an incitement to originality in the abstract, and the quotation habits of a century have worn it smooth. Restored to its setting, it is a methodological instruction with a precise content. The editorial, taking stock of the journal's two years, argued that a doctrine is not imported the way machinery is imported. The problems of Peruvian socialism were Peruvian: an indigenous peasantry, a feudal sierra, a dependent coast, a communal tradition. A socialism that copied the programmes, the vocabularies, and the stage theories of Europe would fail those problems exactly as the copied liberalism of the independence era had failed them, producing constitutions without citizens and parties without countries. Creation, in his usage, did not mean invention from nothing. It meant the labour of thinking a universal doctrine through a particular reality until the doctrine yielded answers the reality's questions required, answers that would necessarily be new because the questions had never been posed in Europe. Heroic named the effort and the risk of that labour, undertaken without the alibi of obedience.

### What does heroic creation demand of anyone who reads Marx?

That the reading be an act of application rather than of custody: the doctrine's method carried into a reality its author never examined, its categories tested against that reality's evidence, and its conclusions allowed to differ from every European precedent where the evidence differs, without the difference counting as heresy.

The demand cuts in two directions, and both edges were used. Against the dogmatists of the International it asserted the right, indeed the duty, of a national movement to think: the Peruvian party's positions on the Indian and the land were not deviations awaiting correction but the doctrine doing its proper work on new material. Against the nationalists and indigenistas of his own camp it asserted the opposite discipline with equal force: creation was not licence to dissolve Marxism into local colour, to replace the analysis of property and class with celebrations of the national soul. The same editorial that proclaimed heroic creation proclaimed the journal's Marxism without qualification, and his polemics against purely literary indigenismo, against the cult of the race, and against the aprista attempt to substitute a continental nationalism for socialism all enforce the second edge. A heroic creation is a creation within a method, answerable to the method's standards of evidence and to the reality it claims to describe. The phrase has been borrowed since by movements that wanted the licence without the discipline, and the borrowing is one of the misreadings the reception section will have to correct. In his hands it was a working rule, and the Seven Essays, published two months after the editorial, are its demonstration: a Marxist book unlike any written in Europe, because Peru is unlike any country in Europe, and recognisably Marxist in every line of its analysis.


## The party he founded and the front he refused

The third argument of the table concerns the obstacle; the organisational conclusion of the whole case concerns the instrument, and it brought him into the dispute that defined his last years. In the autumn of nineteen twenty-eight he founded the Peruvian Socialist Party on a programme drawn from the analysis: a Marxist party, socialist in aim, recruiting workers and peasants, committed to the agrarian revolution and to the alliance of the coastal proletariat with the indigenous peasantry of the sierra. The founding was also a refusal. Victor Raul Haya de la Torre, the brilliant leader of the university reform generation, had built from exile the American Popular Revolutionary Alliance, APRA, as a continental front of workers, peasants, students, and the middle classes against imperialism, postponing socialism in favour of a broad national liberation movement on the model of the Chinese Kuomintang. The two men had begun as allies, and the alliance broke on the question of the instrument. Haya's front subordinated class organisation to national unity and the socialist aim to an indefinite future. Mariategui judged that a front which included the landlords' and merchants' classes in its ranks could not make the agrarian revolution, since the revolution's first objects sat inside the alliance, and that a movement which postponed its aim indefinitely had in practice abandoned it. He broke with APRA in nineteen twenty-eight, kept the name Indo-America for his own project while rejecting the front's politics, and founded a party of a deliberately narrower and harder kind.

### Why did he refuse the broad nationalist front as his vehicle?

Because the agrarian revolution's targets, the gamonales and the landholding class, stood inside the front's proposed alliance, and a coalition cannot expropriate its own members. A socialist party of workers and peasants, he argued, could make the land revolution that a multi-class national front was structurally forbidden to attempt.

The labour confederation completed the instrument in nineteen twenty-nine, when the confederation of the country's workers, the CGTP, was founded under leaders formed in his circle, uniting the young union movement on a class programme. Journal, party, confederation, and behind them a body of theory in print: the construction was complete, and it was his. Its character explains both his strength against the International and his movement's fragility after him. The positions the International would criticise were not negotiating counters adopted for a season; they were the published doctrine of the party's founder, argued in the party's journal, embodied in the party's programme. And the party's coherence was personal in a way its structure never had time to outgrow. He had been its theorist, its editor, its arbiter, and its connection to the European movement in one person, and a construction so centred on a single intelligence is a bequest as much as an institution. The confederation was dissolved by government repression within a few years, the journal died with him, and the party, as the next sections record, was remade by other hands into something he had refused to build. What survived intact was the work in print, and it is on the work, therefore, that his historical existence now rests, which is the final reason this profile treats the arguments, rather than the organisations, as the substance of the man.

## Marx and the Russian commune: the question put by letter

The parallel at the centre of this profile begins in February of eighteen eighty-one, when Vera Zasulich, a Russian revolutionary then in exile, wrote to Marx in London with a question that her movement could not settle. Russian Marxists and Russian populists disputed the fate of the obshchina, the peasant commune that still held much of Russia's land in common. The Marxists of the capital's school held that the commune was dissolving and that Russia must pass through capitalism as the West had done. The populists held that the commune might be the foundation of a distinct Russian road to socialism. Zasulich asked Marx, in effect, to arbitrate: did his theory require the commune's destruction? The question reached him when his health was already broken, and he took it with a seriousness that the archive preserves in physical form. He drafted his reply four times. The drafts, long, hesitant, and densely argued, work through the commune's structure and Russia's situation; the letter he finally sent, on the eighth of March, was short and cautious. The drafts were not published in his lifetime, nor in Mariategui's: they surfaced only in a Russian archival journal in the middle of the nineteen twenties, in a language Mariategui did not read and a venue that never reached Lima, and no evidence connects him to them. What he could not have known is therefore exactly what must now be compared with what he wrote.

The drafts' argument, in its developed form, runs as follows. The commune is a dual structure: communal ownership of the land coexists with individual cultivation and with private appropriation of the house and its produce, and this dualism makes two futures possible, since either element may prevail over the other. Nothing in the theory of capitalist development, Marx insisted, compels the commune's dissolution: the analysis in Capital of how England's producers were expropriated is a history of Western Europe, and its conversion into a universal path that every people must tread, in defiance of its own material, is a misuse he expressly refused. Russia's peculiar advantage is contemporaneity: the commune exists alongside a developed capitalism in the West, whose achievements, technique above all, a revolutionised Russia could appropriate without repeating the process that produced them. On those conditions the commune could serve, in the drafts' phrase, as "the fulcrum of social regeneration in Russia", the point of departure for a communist development that spared the country the sufferings of capitalist expropriation. The conditions were stated as sternly as the possibility: the deleterious influences assailing the commune, the state's fiscal extraction and the growth of internal differentiation, would have to be removed, and a Russian revolution would have to arrive in time. The published fragments of the position in his lifetime were two: a letter of eighteen seventy-seven refusing the universal path in terms, and the preface to the Russian edition of the Communist Manifesto of eighteen eighty-two, signed with Engels, which allowed that Russian common ownership of land might serve as the starting point for a communist development if a Russian revolution complemented a proletarian revolution in the West. The preface he might conceivably have met in translation. The drafts, with their structural analysis of the commune's dualism and their explicit refusal of the unilinear sequence, he did not meet at all.

## The independent convergence

Set the two arguments side by side and the correspondence is structural, point for point. A communal form of landholding survives inside a formation dominated by other modes of production: the obshchina inside a Russia of advancing capitalism and autocratic extraction, the ayllu inside a Peru of feudal estates and dependent coastal capital. The form is internally dual in both analyses, communal in its property and its cooperative labour, penetrated by individual and commercial elements that threaten it, so that its future is a question of politics rather than of fate. The unilinear schema is refused in both: neither thinker accepts that the country must first complete the capitalist dissolution of the commune as the tuition fee of socialism. The enabling condition is the same in both: contemporaneity with a more developed world, whose technique and whose socialist movement can be appropriated instead of recapitulated. And the political conclusion is the same: the communal form, defended and transformed by a revolution, can serve as the point of departure for a socialist development that does not pass through the full capitalist stage. This profile names the correspondence the independent convergence, and the name is meant to carry the claim's full weight. It is not influence, since the channel of influence did not exist. It is not coincidence, since the correspondence runs through five structural points and not through a shared slogan. It is the independent arrival of two analysts, working from analogous material with the same method, at the same heterodox conclusion, and its interest lies in what it certifies about the method and the conclusion alike.

### What makes the convergence evidence about the framework rather than about either man?

Because independent arrival is the test that separates a position available within a framework from a position imposed on it. Had the argument descended from Marx's drafts by transmission, it would prove only that a disciple agreed; reached without transmission, from Peruvian evidence, it shows the multilinear conclusion can be generated by the method itself.

The grading of his originality follows from the same analysis, and it should be stated carefully because his admirers and his detractors both habitually misstate it. He did not invent the multilinear position; the founder had reached it, in private, before him. He did not receive it; the receipt was impossible. What he did was to reach it first in public, in print, as the foundation of a political programme, in a hemisphere the founder never studied, working from evidence the founder never saw, and to defend it against the orthodoxy that claimed the founder's authority for the opposite position. That is a specific and considerable originality, of the kind the history of science recognises in simultaneous discovery, and it is worth more than the inflated claim of absolute novelty it replaces. The inflated claim makes him a prophet outside his tradition. The true claim makes him something more interesting: the tradition's own method, operating at full strength on new material, reproducing its founder's most heterodox conclusion in ignorance of it. A framework that can do that is not a catechism, and a thinker who can do it is not a copyist, which is what he had demanded, in the anniversary editorial, that American socialism refuse to be.


## The pattern elsewhere: reification and the manuscripts

The series to which this profile belongs has met the pattern before, and the precedent disciplines the claim. In the Western Marxism cluster, the Hungarian philosopher Georg Lukacs, writing in the early nineteen twenties, developed the concept of reification, the deformation of human relations into relations between things, into the centre of a whole reading of capitalist society. He built it from the analysis of the commodity in Capital. What was hidden from him was that Marx, in manuscripts written in eighteen forty-four and unpublished until nineteen thirty-two, had built a parallel account around the concept of alienated labour, starting from the worker's estrangement and arriving at a strikingly similar anatomy of a world in which human powers confront their producers as alien things. Lukacs reached, independently and from the published texts alone, the neighbourhood of a position the founder had occupied in private eighty years earlier. The convergence did not make Lukacs a plagiarist or a prophet. It showed that the published framework contained, in solution, conclusions its author had drawn in manuscripts the world had not seen, and that a sufficiently rigorous reader could precipitate them out again.

### Why is independent discovery stronger evidence than influence would be?

Because influence is compatible with mere obedience, and obedience certifies nothing about the framework. A conclusion transmitted can be repeated without being understood or earned; a conclusion regenerated from new evidence, by a reader who did not know it existed, demonstrates that the framework's method produces it, which is the property under examination.

His case is the Lukacs case with the stakes raised by geography and by politics. Lukacs worked inside the European tradition, in its languages, among its debates, and his convergence concerned a philosophical concept. Mariategui worked from Lima, in Spanish, at the end of every line of communication the movement possessed, and his convergence concerned a strategic conclusion on which a party's programme and a continent's argument turned: whether communal peoples must be dissolved by capitalism before they may be emancipated. The two cases together suggest a general proposition about this tradition that neither the faithful nor the hostile usually entertain. The framework's published core, the analysis of modes of production and of their coexistence and combination, is generative in a way its official codifications were not. The codified stage theory, which the International of his decade enforced as orthodoxy, is precisely the element that neither convergence reproduces: both Lukacs and Mariategui, reading rigorously, arrived at positions the codification excluded. The moral for the reader of this series is not that the founder's unpublished thoughts sanctify the conclusions, which would be a new dogmatism with the polarity reversed. It is that the multilinear position, the refusal of the single queue, is available from within historical materialism to any analyst who lets a real formation's evidence override the schema, and that its two independent discoveries, in Budapest and in Lima, are the experiments that demonstrate the availability.

## Multilinear history from inside the framework

The point deserves one more section, because the convergence can be misread as a curiosity when it is in fact a verdict on a question the whole tradition argued about for a century: whether historical materialism contains one road or several. The unilinear reading, in which every society climbs the same staircase of modes of production in the same order, drew its authority from the famous preface of eighteen fifty-nine, where Marx sketched the Asiatic, ancient, feudal, and bourgeois modes as progressive epochs, and from the first volume of Capital, whose account of England's expropriation of its producers was widely taken as the type of a universal process. The multilinear reading draws on the same author's other hand: the letter of eighteen seventy-seven, written against a Russian critic who would have turned the English sketch into a fate for all peoples, in which Marx protested that his historical account of Western Europe was being converted into a theory of the general path every nation is fated to tread; the Zasulich drafts, with their analysis of a commune permitted two futures; and the ethnological reading of his last years, which filled notebooks with the communal forms of peoples the staircase had no step for. The founder's corpus, in short, contains both the schema and its refusal, and the subsequent history of the doctrine is in large part the history of which hand a given movement chose to read. The codifiers chose the schema, because a schema can be administered. He, without access to the refusal's fullest texts, chose the refusal, because Peru's evidence left him no honest alternative, and the choice aligned him with the founder's late thought against the founder's official church.

The reader who wants the stage question treated in its full generality, with the Russian correspondence examined as a text in its own right, should follow the series guide to [historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/), where the multilinear dossier is assembled. The present profile's concern is narrower: what his independent arrival at the position does to his standing. It removes him, definitively, from the two categories in which lazy classification keeps him. He is not the tradition's Latin American branch manager, applying a finished product to a new territory, since the product he arrived at contradicted the branch's official stock. And he is not a national heretic who bent an alien doctrine to local purposes, since the position he reached was the founder's own, reached by the founder's method. He stands instead as the tradition's independent witness in the Americas, and the convergence is the credential. It also explains, better than any psychology, the confidence with which he faced the International's correction in his last year. A man who has derived his positions from his country's evidence, and printed the derivation for anyone to check, experiences a demand to revise them by instruction not as discipline but as an invitation to intellectual dishonesty. He was capable of compromise on many things. The record shows no sign that he regarded that as one of them.

## The Buenos Aires conference and the International's criticism

The confrontation came in June of nineteen twenty-nine, at the first conference of the communist parties of Latin America, held in Buenos Aires. He did not attend; his health forbade the journey, and the Peruvian positions were carried by delegates of his movement, among them the physician Hugo Pesce, his close collaborator, who defended them on the floor. The positions themselves had been drafted under his direction, in documents prepared for the conference that set out the Peruvian analysis: the Indian problem as a land problem to be solved by the agrarian revolution, the rejection of any separate indigenous republic or separate indigenous party, the socialist character of the coming revolution in countries of Peru's composition, and the refusal of the stage sequence that would have postponed socialism behind a completed national capitalism. The conference's leading figures, the representatives of the International's South American Secretariat, among whom the Argentine Victorio Codovilla was the dominant voice, received the Peruvian theses as a deviation. The criticism, as the record preserves it, ran on several rails at once. The Peruvian analysis was charged with populism, with overestimating the peasantry and its communal forms in the manner of the old Russian narodniki whom Lenin had fought. It was charged with underestimating the national bourgeoisie and the anti-imperialist front the International's line required. And against its treatment of the Indian question the conference's orthodoxy pressed the formula then circulating from the International's colonial theses, the right of oppressed peoples to their own republics, which in the Peruvian case pointed toward an indigenous republic in the sierra: a conclusion the Peruvian delegation rejected as a fantasy that would divide the revolutionary forces, abandon the Indian to a reservation dressed as a state, and mistake a land question for a national one.

### What did the International's representatives object to at the conference?

To the whole architecture of the Peruvian position: its handling of the Indian question as agrarian rather than national, its refusal of a separate indigenous republic, its confidence in the peasantry and the communal forms, which they named populist, and its postponement of nothing, where their line required stages and a broad anti-imperialist front first.

The dispute was never resolved, because he died ten months later with the argument still open, and its sequel belongs to the history of his party rather than to his life. Under new leadership imported from the International's own ranks, the party he had founded took the name Peruvian Communist Party within the year of his death, its programme brought into conformity, and his closest collaborators pressed to recant or marginalised. The renaming is usually reported as an administrative detail. In the light of his founding choice it is the dispute's epitaph: he had named the party Socialist deliberately, to mark its independence of programme and its rooting in the Peruvian analysis, and the first act of the alignment was to strike the name. The conference itself has had a long historiographical afterlife, and the verdict of the scholarship has moved steadily toward the Peruvian delegation on the substantive points: the indigenous republic formula was quietly dropped by the International itself in later years as unworkable, the populism charge has been reassessed by historians who note that the narodnik parallel cuts both ways, since the Russian populists too were right about the commune's existence and wrong, in the event, about its political future in a country whose revolution took another road, and the stage sequence the conference defended is no longer defended by anyone as a reading of Peru. None of that retrospective vindication was available to him, and a profile should record the dispute as it stood in his lifetime: a dying man in Lima, informed that the world movement's regional authority considered his life's work a deviation, declining to recant it. The steel of the man is in that declension, and so is the explanation of his posthumous fate, since a movement that would not bend him living could only claim him dead, on terms the next sections examine.


## The strongest objection: the charge of romanticising the commune

The objection that matters is not the International's, which was an argument about discipline dressed as an argument about doctrine. It is the charge, pressed in his lifetime and developed by scholarship since, that the communal argument romanticises its object: that he recast the indigenous communal institutions of the Andes as already socialist in substance, mistaking their moral qualities, their solidarity, their reciprocity, their dignity under oppression, for a mode of production, and that he did so because the polemic he was conducting, against an oligarchy that despised the Indian and against an orthodoxy that despised the peasant, rewarded a golden counter-image of the communal past and present. The charge has a contemporary form and a scholarly form, and both must be given at full strength. The contemporary form came from Victor Andres Belaunde, the Catholic historian and statesman whose La realidad nacional, published the year after his death, was the most serious reply the Seven Essays received from the Peru they indicted. Belaunde contested the economic reading of the colonial past, defended the civilising work of the colony and the Church, and treated the communal argument as an idealisation: the Inca order and its surviving forms belonged, in his account, to a world whose restoration no modern politics could honestly propose, and the essays' Indian was a literary figure constructed for a polemic. The scholarly form came later and cut deeper, because it came with evidence. Historians of the sierra economy, working the regional archives he never had, Nils Jacobsen's study of the Altiplano prominent among them, demonstrated that the indigenous communities of the southern highlands had been engaged with markets for generations before he wrote: selling wool into the export trade, hiring and hiring out labour, litigating, borrowing, and differentiating internally between richer and poorer households. On that evidence the highlands of his essays, a feudal interior of servile estates and subsistence communities standing outside commercial relations, is a construction that understates, by a wide margin, how far money and markets had already penetrated the world he described.

The two prongs of the charge should be distinguished, because they have different weights. The first prong is about categories: a communal institution's moral qualities, however admirable, do not make it a socialist mode of production, and to argue from the ayllu's solidarity to socialism's basis is to confuse an ethos with an economy. The second prong is about facts: the feudal character he assigned to the sierra, and the communal character he assigned to the ayllu, were both partly constructions of his polemic, formed by generalising the most archaic features of a heterogeneous reality and understating the commercial relations that ran through it. If both prongs hold in full, the second row of the three arguments table collapses, and with it the construction that distinguishes him from a radical agrarian reformer: there would remain the land diagnosis and the gamonal anatomy, both powerful, and a proposal to build socialism on institutions that were neither as communal as he claimed nor as separate from capitalism as his argument required. That is the objection at full strength, and it is the strongest that can be brought against him, stronger than the International's discipline and stronger than the nationalist complaint that he was a foreign doctrine's agent, because it strikes at the evidence of his central construction.

## The answer, and the concession the argument owes

The answer begins by separating the claim he made from the claim his rhetoric sometimes invited. His analytical claim about the communal forms was a claim about their function, not about their purity. He argued that the community and its cooperative labour practices still organised production for much of the indigenous population, that they embodied habits of collective work and common holding which a socialist transformation could take up and develop with modern technique, and that this made them a basis in the strict sense: a starting structure, not a finished socialism awaiting only recognition. He did not argue that the ayllu was socialism already, and the texts resist that reading at the decisive points. He described the Inca order as a theocratic authoritarianism whose restoration nobody sane could propose. He insisted that the new order would have to be built with the technique and the science of the modern world, and that the community's development, not its preservation in amber, was the programme. He treated the communal tradition the way his method treated every survival: as material whose future depended on the forces that took it up. On the categorical prong, then, the answer is that the ethos and the economy were never simply confused in his analysis: the solidarity he praised was praised as the social form of a real organisation of production, of land held in common and worked in common, and it is the organisation, not the warmth of its manners, that the argument builds on.

The concession concerns the second prong, and it must be made without varnish, as the brief of this profile requires. His empirical claim about the extent of commercial penetration of the highlands was not established. He wrote from Lima, from the reports of allies and the literature available to him, without the regional archival research that the question needed, and the research, when it came, showed penetration deeper, older, and more general than his account allowed: communities marketing their wool, peasants moving between communal agriculture and wage labour as a settled pattern rather than an emergency, internal differentiation further advanced. The feudal character he assigned the sierra as a whole was, to that extent, partly a construction of his polemic, the archaic face of the formation emphasised because the polemic's target was the servile relation, and the commercial face understated because it complicated the picture of a communal world awaiting its defender. A profile that has presented his method as the subordination of schema to evidence must apply the standard to its subject: on the empirical balance between the communal core and the commercial penetration, his judgement was asserted beyond his evidence, and later evidence has moved the balance against him.

### What survives of the communal argument after the concession on penetration?

Its core, in a qualified form. The communities existed and organised production; the habits of cooperation were real; the proposal to develop them rather than dissolve them stands. What falls is the image of a communal world substantially outside commercial relations, and with it any version of the argument that needs the ayllu's purity.

What the concession leaves standing is worth enumerating precisely, because obituaries for the whole argument have been written on the strength of it. The land argument is untouched: the Indian's condition followed from tenure, and the subsequent century of Peruvian history, including the agrarian reform that a military government eventually carried out a generation later, proceeded on that premise. The gamonalismo argument is untouched, and indeed the penetration evidence strengthens it in one respect, since the gamonal appears in the newer historiography not as an archaic survival but as the broker of the sierra's integration into markets on terms he controlled, which is his function in the essays with the mechanism made explicit. The stage argument is dented but not destroyed: if the communities were more commercialised than he allowed, the basis he proposed was less pristine, but the conclusion that Peru need not complete a capitalist stage does not rest on the ayllu alone, since the dependent character of its capitalism and the changed world context carry much of the weight independently. And the methodological claim, heroic creation itself, is if anything confirmed by the episode, since the correction of his empirical judgement by later research is the method working as designed: evidence revising a conclusion that evidence had under-supported. The thinker who emerges from the objection and the concession together is smaller than the prophet of the commemorative literature and larger than the romantic of the hostile one: an analyst whose framing of his country's central question was permanently right, whose anatomy of its rural power was substantially right, and whose construction from its communal material was right in direction and overstated in its empirical premise. That is a considerable estate for a man who died at thirty-five with a first book two years old, and it is the estate this profile records.

## Claimed by many currents: an inheritance under dispute

No Latin American thinker of his century has been claimed by more numerous or more incompatible heirs, and the contest over his corpse began before it was buried. The party he founded claimed him, after renaming itself and reversing his positions, as its own founder-saint, printing his portrait on its banners while administering a doctrine he had been criticised for refusing. The aprista movement he broke with claimed his Indo-American vocabulary and, in its later decades, a share of his prestige. The indigenista tradition claimed the defender of the Indian, sometimes at the price of the Marxism that made the defence an analysis rather than a lament. The academic world claimed him, eventually, as the continent's first Marxist theorist, and built a scholarly literature in several languages on the claim. And in a later generation an armed movement arose in his own country claiming his authority in the most consequential appropriation of all, presenting its war as the continuation of his road and its leader's doctrine as the development of his thought. That claim is contested, and the contest must be recorded exactly as the record stands: other readings of his texts, by scholars and by movements that also descend from him, dispute it root and branch, pointing to his insistence on the mass line of patient organisation, his defence of the communities as subjects rather than as fuel, and his death before any of the questions the war posed had been formulated. This profile names no current organisation and adjudicates no living claim. It records the historical fact that the claim was made, that it was and remains disputed by rival readers of the same texts, and that the dispute itself is evidence about the corpus: a finished system with a designated heir does not attract this kind of contest.

### Why does an unfinished corpus attract rival heirs?

Because its author died before he could arbitrate it. The essays and the journalism point in a direction without legislating every turn; the party was founded but its doctrine was never codified by the one man whose authority all factions acknowledge; so each heir can find its own emphasis licensed somewhere in the texts, and none can be silenced by the author's word.

The causes of the contest are worth one more paragraph, because they double as a guide to reading him. He died at thirty-five, leaving a major book of essays, a journal's files, and a mountain of journalism, but no treatise, no testament, and no settled line on the questions his successors would face: the character of the Peruvian state after mid-century, the road of armed struggle, the relation of the indigenous movement to the party form. Every current that claims him is extrapolating, and the honest ones know it. His texts also have the peculiar property of rewarding opposed emphases: the same corpus contains the celebration of the community and the demand for modern technique, the refusal of stages and the acceptance of the bourgeois revolution's tasks, the national creation and the international doctrine. A reader who quotes selectively can build several Mariateguis, and several have been built. The defence against the whole industry is the discipline this profile has practised: read the three arguments together and in their order, hold the analysis apart from the polemical frames, and measure every inheritance claim against the full architecture rather than against the quotable sentence. The man who emerges from that discipline is harder to recruit than the saint of the banners, and considerably more worth reading.


## The untranslated corpus: what an English reader cannot reach

A profile of a thinker whose reputation crosses languages owes its readers an exact statement of what can and cannot be read, because the Mariategui available in English is a selection, and the selection silently shapes the interpretation. The Seven Interpretive Essays are available in English in full, in the translation by Marjorie Urquidi published by the University of Texas Press, and the land argument, the communal argument, and the gamonalismo analysis as this profile has presented them rest on that translated material and can be checked against it by any reader. A substantial anthology of the essays and journalism, edited by Harry Vanden and Marc Becker, has widened the translated corpus in the present century, carrying the anniversary editorial and a range of the political writing into English. On that basis the core of the case, the three arguments, the heroic creation programme, and the main lines of the Comintern dispute, stands on translated ground, and nothing in this profile's central claims depends on material an English reader cannot reach.

The rest of the corpus tells a different story, and the proportions matter. His journalism was voluminous, the daily and weekly production of fifteen years, and much of his political argument, his literary criticism, his European reportage, and his running commentary on Peruvian affairs lives there and nowhere else. The complete works published in Peru run to twenty volumes, assembled after his death by his widow and sons through the family publishing house, and the great bulk of that corpus, the chronicles, the reviews, the polemics, the lectures, remains untranslated. Works an English reader will meet cited in the scholarship, and should know how to place, include the Defensa del marxismo, rendered in English as In Defence of Marxism, his sustained polemic against the revisionist Henri de Man, written in his last years and published after his death; La escena contemporánea, The Contemporary Scene, the collection of his European chronicles of the early nineteen twenties; and El alma matinal, The Morning Soul, the posthumous collection of his essays on art and the spirit of his age. The arguments of this profile that touch his literary criticism, his reading of European fascism at first hand, and his theory of myth and religion lean, where they lean on those works, on the Spanish texts as mediated by the scholarship, and the reader is entitled to know it. The rule the series applies to such cases is the one stated here in the open: cite the untranslated works by their original titles with a translation supplied, state plainly which arguments rest on them, and never allow the inaccessibility of a text to inflate its authority. His reputation does not need the inflation. The translated core is enough to establish it, and the untranslated remainder is a research frontier, not a mystery.

## The tradition he opened: successors and the questions inherited

He founded no school in his lifetime and left no disciples trained by his hand, yet the tradition of Latin American Marxism runs from him in a way it runs from no one else, and the routing of his influence is the profile's final piece of analysis. The regional tradition he opened is treated whole in the series guide to [Marxism in Latin America](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/), and the successor current that took his agrarian and dependency questions furthest, the theory of dependent development that made the continent's economists famous a generation later, receives its own treatment in the account of [dependency theory in Latin America](/2019/01/15/dependency-theory-latin-america/). What belongs here is the inventory of the questions he bequeathed, because a thinker's afterlife is the list of problems his successors could not put down. The land question as the region's central question, prior to and deeper than the question of industry. The indigenous population as a revolutionary subject in its own right, neither a residue to be dissolved nor a folklore to be celebrated, but a peasantry whose institutions were political facts. The refusal of the imported sequence, the insistence that each formation be read from its own composition. The communal and cooperative forms of the countryside as material for socialism rather than as its prehistory. And the methodological bequest that contains all the others, heroic creation itself: the standing instruction to the region's Marxists to think their own reality or stop calling the result Marxism.

### Which of his questions passed to the generation that followed?

All five of the inventory, though under changed names. The land question became the agrarian reform debates; the indigenous subject became the peasant movements' theory; the refused sequence became dependency theory's refusal of the development queue; the communal forms became the cooperative experiments; and heroic creation became the region's permanent quarrel with imported doctrine.

The inventory also explains the pattern of his reputation's growth, which has been the reverse of the usual decay. In the decade after his death he was a party saint in one country, honoured and unread. In the middle of the century the continent's intellectual revival recovered him as its own founder, and the recovery has never since been reversed: each generation of Latin American social thought has found its questions prefigured in his essays, because the questions he posed, land, indigeneity, dependence, the composition of formations, are the questions the continent's reality keeps posing. His standing outside the region has grown more slowly and on the strength of the convergence this profile has documented: the multilinear argument, rediscovered in Marx's late manuscripts when those were finally published and studied, sent readers back to the Peruvian who had reached it alone, and the Gramsci comparison, once the Italian's prison writings conquered the world's universities, sent readers to the contemporary who had built the parallel construction in Lima without the prison. He is read now, increasingly, where he always said he should be read: not as a regional curiosity but as one of the places where the tradition thought hardest about what it was. The assessment this profile leaves is the one the evidence supports. The diagnosis stands. The anatomy of the obstacle stands. The construction stands in its qualified form, its empirical premise corrected, its direction unrefuted. And the method stands entire, the heroic creation that was never a slogan in his hands, the demand that a doctrine earn its conclusions from the reality in front of it. A self-taught journalist in a wheelchair in Lima, dead at thirty-five, set that standard for a continent. The continent has been trying to meet it since.


## The essays one by one: the book beyond its famous argument

The land essays made the book's reputation, but the Seven Interpretive Essays is a survey of an entire society, and its remaining essays show the method at work on material where the land argument does not reach. The essay on public education traces the Peruvian school through its three inheritances: the colonial university and college, scholastic and clerical, formed to serve an empire's administration and a church's doctrine; the French influence of the early republic, which supplied liberal forms without a society to fill them; and the North American influence of his own century, importing the techniques of a commercial civilisation into a country whose economy could not employ them. His verdict followed the master argument exactly. A school system is the superstructure of an economy, and a feudal and dependent economy would produce, and reproduce, an education of clerks and rhetoricians for the estate and the ministry, whatever the reformers inscribed in its programmes. The university reform movement of his generation, in which he had lectured and whose martyrs he honoured, receives in the essay both its due and its limit: the students had stormed the colonial university and won its statutes, and the victory remained a victory inside an institution whose social function the surrounding order continued to dictate. Education, in short, stood where he had placed it in the refusal of the four framings: real, necessary, and downstream of property. The essay is the book's quiet proof that the land argument was not a hobby but a key, cut to open every room in the house.

The essay on the religious factor performs the same operation on the Church, and it surprises readers who expect a Marxist of his decade to reach for the vocabulary of priestcraft and superstition. The colonial Church in his account was a great landowner and a partner in the conquest's economy, its missions and its tithes integrated into the extraction the essays describe, and its clergy in the sierra functioned as an arm of the gamonal order, blessing the arrangement that fed it. That is the materialist half of the analysis, and it is unflinching. The other half is the one that marks him. He refused to treat religion as a fraud practised on the credulous, and he refused the anticlericalism of the liberal tradition as a strategy. The Indian's Catholicism, syncretised with the older religion of the soil, was in his reading a real spiritual world, the form in which a conquered people had preserved its inner life, and a socialism that made war on that inheritance would make war on the people it claimed to emancipate. His own theory of belief, formed under Sorel's influence in the Italian years, held that great movements are moved by myths, by images of a transformed world held with a faith that syllogisms cannot supply, and that revolutionary socialism possessed its own myth in exactly that sense. The essay's practical conclusion followed: the Church as landlord and as ally of the gamonal was an enemy; the faith of the Indian as a fact of his inner life was a reality to be respected and, in the movement's own ranks, answered with a faith of equal depth rather than with mockery. No other Marxist text of the period, in any language, handles the religious question with this combination of materialist severity and anthropological respect, and the combination is pure Mariategui: the refusal, learned in Italy, to confuse the analysis of an institution with contempt for the people who live inside it.

## Regionalism, centralism, and the literature of a nation

The essay on regionalism and centralism takes up the standing Peruvian quarrel between Lima and the provinces, and dissolves it in the manner the book has taught the reader to expect. The federalists of the provinces blamed the capital's centralism for the interior's backwardness; the centralists blamed provincial caudillismo for the republic's disorder. He granted both descriptions and denied both explanations. The real division of Peru was not administrative but economic: a coast integrated into the world market and an interior held in feudal relations, and no redrawing of the constitutional map would alter a division that property had drawn. Regionalism as a doctrine was, in his reading, the ideology of the gamonales themselves when it suited them, a provincial patriotism that defended the local tyrant against the capital's interference while leaving his land and his labour system untouched; centralism, conversely, was the ideology of the coastal bourgeoisie, administering the interior as a colony of the capital. The essay's constructive point follows the pattern: the unity of Peru would be built not by constitutional engineering but by the transformation that dissolved the two economies into one society, and the Indian, the one Peruvian present in every region, was its natural agent. A reader alert to the book's architecture will notice that the essay performs, for the national question, exactly the relocation performed for the Indian question in the second essay: a debate conducted in the vocabulary of sentiment and administration is returned to the terrain of land and labour, where alone it can be settled.

The seventh essay, on the process of Peruvian literature, is the one his first readers found most surprising and the one that now reads as the book's signature. A Marxist treatise that ends with literature is making a claim about what literature is: not an ornament on the national life but a register of it, in which the formation's divisions appear in their most legible form. He periodised the literature as he had periodised the economy: a colonial literature, Spanish in form and subject, the writing of the conquerors' world; a republican literature still colonial in spirit, the costumbrismo and romanticism of a creole class that had changed its flag without changing its place; and the stirrings, in his own generation, of a literature that might at last be Peruvian, whose herald he found in the indigenista writers and, supremely, in the poetry of Cesar Vallejo, whom he championed early and judged, correctly, to be the country's first universal poet. The essay's most quoted judgement concerns the indigenismo of the Lima salons, which he distinguished from the real thing with a surgeon's calm: a literature about the Indian, written for the capital by writers who aestheticised him, was not yet the Indian's literature, and its sentimentality was the cultural form of the same evasion the reformers practised in politics. Against it he set the standard his whole work implies: the indigenous world would enter Peruvian literature as a subject when it entered Peruvian society as a protagonist, and the writer's task in the meantime was analysis, not embroidery. That the author of this essay had begun his career as the most elegant chronicler of the salons he was now dissecting gave the judgements their authority. He was not denouncing a world he had failed to enter. He was reporting, from inside knowledge, on the world he had left.

### How should a reader weigh a book whose famous argument occupies only two of its seven essays?

As a survey, not as a pamphlet. The land essays state the thesis, but the education, religion, regionalism, and literature essays are the thesis at work, each showing the same relocation performed on a new field, and the book's unity, and its claim to be an interpretation of a whole reality, rests on the demonstration.


## The stone age: what the literary generation taught him

His own account of his beginnings is the severest, and a profile should let him deliver it. The young chronicler of the Colonida circle, the aesthete of the racecourse and the theatre stalls, belonged in his later judgement to the stone age of his development, a phrase he coined for the period and applied without sparing himself. The phrase is worth pausing over, because it shows the mature man's relation to his own past, and because his detractors have used the early writings, with their dandyism and their society gossip, as evidence that the Marxist was a costume adopted for advantage. The record supports the opposite reading. The Colonida generation's rebellion was real but unarmed: it mocked the official culture, adored the new European art, and changed nothing, and his retrospective phrase registers the discovery, made in the newspaper office and completed in Italy, that a rebellion of taste is a pastime unless it finds the social force whose interest coincides with it. What the stone age gave him, and what he never discarded, was the equipment of the writer: the prose style, forged in daily journalism, that made the Seven Essays the most readable Marxist book of its continent; the knowledge of the Lima world from inside, which armed his contempt for it; and the friendships of the literary generation, several of whose members followed him into the movement he built. The aesthete did not vanish into the theorist. He survives in the essays' architecture, in their refusal of the party pamphlet's grey manner, and in the conviction, visible on every page, that a doctrine which cannot be written beautifully has not yet been fully thought.

## The man and the myth: his philosophy of action

Running beneath the Peruvian arguments is a philosophy, and it must be stated because without it his divergences from the orthodoxy of his time look like carelessness when they are in fact positions. He had absorbed, in the Italian years, the anti-positivist current that ran from Sorel through the young Gramsci's circle: a suspicion of the doctrine of automatic progress, of history as a mechanism that delivers socialism the way a timetable delivers a train. Against that mechanism he set the will. Great transformations, in his reading of the evidence, are made by minorities possessed by a myth, a total image of the world to be created, held not as a prediction but as a faith that organises sacrifice. The religious essay's tolerance has its root here: the revolutionary's myth and the believer's faith are, as psychological and social forces, of the same species, and a movement that sneers at faith understands neither its enemy nor itself. His celebrated defence of passion in politics, of the heroic and romantic element in socialism, scandalised the catechists then and since, and he returned to it deliberately in the essays of his last years, insisting that Marxism is a method and a faith in history's movement, not a body of conclusions to be memorised, and that its founder's own work was a creation of exactly the kind he demanded from its inheritors. The philosophy explains the tone of the whole corpus, its impatience with scholasticism, its respect for the enemy's serious representatives, and its confidence that a small movement with a true analysis and an absolute conviction outweighs a large one reciting a programme. It also explains his vulnerability to the charge of voluntarism, which the International's critics duly brought. The answer the corpus makes is the one this profile has already given in the agrarian case: the will he celebrated was a will disciplined by analysis, the myth he praised was the myth of a class whose material situation he had spent a book examining, and the faith he demanded was faith in a conclusion the evidence had earned. Whether the balance between the voluntarist philosophy and the materialist analysis is stable in his work is a genuine question, and the scholarship divides on it. That it was a considered balance, and not a confusion, the texts leave in no doubt.

## In defence of Marxism: the last polemic

The philosophy found its systematic statement in the work of his final years, the Defensa del marxismo, In Defence of Marxism, written as a running polemic against the Belgian socialist Henri de Man, whose revision of Marxism into a psychology of contentment had become the fashionable heresy of the European movement. The choice of opponent is diagnostic. He did not spend his last strength on the oligarchy's publicists or on the International's catechists. He spent it on the most intelligent revisionist available, because the defence he wanted to write was a defence of the doctrine's core against its softening from within: against the substitution of ethical aspiration for the analysis of property, of reformist gradualism for the conquest of power, of the psychology of the consumer for the economics of production. The Defence is unfinished and was published after his death, and it remains the least read of his major writings in any language, which is a loss the untranslated corpus section has already registered. Its pages contain his most explicit statements on method: that Marxism is neither a dogma nor a calendar, that its truths are historical and must be re-earned in each formation, that the doctrine's founder revised his own conclusions when the evidence required it and expected the same courage from his school. Read beside the Seven Essays, the Defence supplies the theory of the essays' practice. The essays show a Marxist analysis being created from Peruvian material; the Defence explains, against the revisionist and the catechist in a single argument, why creation of that kind is not a deviation from Marxism but its definition. A reader who masters the two works together possesses his complete position, and possesses also the answer to the question the Buenos Aires conference posed: the positions the International criticised were not a young section's improvisations but the applications of a stated and defended philosophy of the doctrine itself, and their author had written the defence before the criticism arrived.

### What separates his Marxism from the indigenism he is mistaken for?

The unit of analysis. Indigenismo, in its literary and political forms, takes the Indian as its subject: his culture, his race, his wrongs, his glory. His analysis takes the land tenure system as its subject and the Indian as the peasantry that system holds, so that the remedy follows the analysis: not the celebration of a culture but the transformation of a property order.


## The colonial economy: how the land was lost

The first essay's scheme of the economic evolution deserves a fuller walk than the classification section gave it, because the land argument's history is written there, and the history is what converts the argument from a slogan into a demonstration. The conquest, in his account, destroyed the Inca economy's organisation without replacing its productive achievement: a civilisation that had fed its population from terraced mountainsides was succeeded by an economy of extraction, organised for the mine and the treasury, in which agriculture decayed, the population collapsed under labour drafts and disease, and the land passed to a class of encomenderos and hacendados whose wealth was measured in Indians rather than in produce. The colonial regime that followed was feudal in its structure and spirit: land held in great grants, labour exacted as service, production for the local lord and the distant fisc rather than for a market, and a ruling class whose values were those of a military and bureaucratic aristocracy. Independence changed the flag and the personnel and left the structure standing, because the creole class that made the revolution was the landholding class itself, and its liberalism, imported as doctrine, stopped at the boundary of its own estates. The republic's nineteenth century added the episodes his scheme enumerates with an accountant's brevity: the guano age, whose windfall enriched the state and its contractors without touching the sierra; the catastrophe of the Pacific war, which stripped the country of its nitrate provinces and its credit together; the slow construction, under foreign capital's direction, of the coastal export economy of sugar, cotton, minerals, and oil. At the scheme's end stands the formation of his own day, the three economies already described, and the historical point the whole survey exists to make: the feudal sierra was not an inheritance Peru had failed to shake off but a structure each successive order, colonial, republican, and export-capitalist, had found useful and renewed. The gamonal was not behind the times. He was the times, in the form they took in the highlands, and the history is the proof.

Within that history, the community's long resistance is the counter-thread, and its mechanics deserve the detail the ayllu section promised. The colonial state, needing a unit through which to tax and to draft, preserved the community's shell while draining its substance: the reduccion villages gathered the population for administration, the mita took its men for the mines in rotating levies that the Potosi silver mountain consumed for two centuries, and the tribute took its surplus in cash that forced the community into the market on the worst terms. Yet the same community remained the Indian's defence: the holder of his land against total expropriation, the organiser of his labour and his festivals, the keeper of his language and his gods under their Catholic names. The republic's liberal legislation struck at the shell itself, in the name of a freedom whose practical content was the freedom of the strong to buy the weak's land parcel by parcel, and the long erosion of the communal territory across the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries is the material history of the Indian problem as he defined it: not a fall from grace, not a racial decline, but a transfer of land, proceeding by law, debt, fraud, and force, from the communities to the estates. The transfer had not ended in his lifetime; the wool boom of the southern sierra was accomplishing in his own decades, at the communities' expense, what the guano age had accomplished elsewhere, and his essays on the land were written as dispatches from a process still under way. That contemporaneity is the source of the essays' urgency, and of their difference from the indigenista literature of lament. He was not mourning a finished dispossession. He was analysing a going concern and proposing to stop it.

## The community in law: recognition without power

The decade in which he wrote offered a controlled experiment in the administrative framing he had refused, and he used it as one. The constitution under which Peru was governed in the nineteen twenties recognised the legal existence of the indigenous communities and declared their lands protected; the regime maintained a bureau of indigenous affairs and a patronage charged with the race's defence; official commissions toured the sierra and reported on its wrongs in language sometimes as strong as his own. Here, if anywhere, was the legal framing's opportunity, backed by a modernising dictatorship with no love for the provincial oligarchy. The result, as he documented it, was null, and the nullity is the gamonalismo argument's best evidence. The community's legal personality entitled it to defend lands it lacked the money, the lawyers, and the local power to defend; the bureau's inspectors wrote reports the provincial authorities filed; the protective statutes encountered, in each district, the judge who was the gamonal's compadre, the subprefect who was his tenant, the police who were his retainers. A law, he concluded, does not march into a district on its own legs; it arrives in the hands of men, and in the sierra the men were spoken for. The experiment's lesson generalises beyond Peru, and he generalised it: protective legislation for a subordinated population, administered through the power that subordinates it, is a mechanism for converting grievances into files. The Indian's problem would be solved by changing the distribution of power in the countryside, or it would not be solved, and the constitution's fine articles would continue to be read aloud, as they had been for a century, in the hearing of the men they were written to restrain.

## Amauta as an institution: the journal that organised a generation

The journal's thirty-five issues repay study as an institution, not only as a bibliography, because the institution was the movement's school and its existence explains how a party could be founded, two years after the journal's launch, with a doctrine already formed. Its structure was deliberate. Each issue mixed the genres the movement needed: his own editorials and essays setting the line of analysis; translations and summaries carrying the European movement's debates to readers who had no other channel; literary pages that made the journal the house organ of the continent's new writing, publishing the poets and novelists of the vanguard beside the political argument; indigenista scholarship and reportage from the provinces, which gave the sierra a printed presence in the capital it had never had; and labour news, the strikes and congresses of the young union movement, reported as seriously as the literature. The contributors' list across the run reads as a roll of the generation: the poets of Peru and Mexico and Argentina, the painters of the indigenista school, the exiles and travellers of three continents. To edit such a journal from a wheelchair in Lima, financing it precariously, defending it against the regime's suspicions, it was raided and its editor briefly detained in the conspiracy scare of the late nineteen twenties, and sustaining its quality for four years, was an organisational achievement of the first order, and it was performed by a man whose party-political work has monopolised the obituaries. The journal also fixed his continental position. Amauta was read from Mexico City to Buenos Aires, its exchanges arrived from every capital, and its editor's correspondence made him, before any party recognised him, the Peruvian reference of the Latin American left. When the International's conference met in Buenos Aires, the deviation it confronted was not an obscure section's misunderstanding. It was the published position of the continent's best-connected Marxist editor, and the conference knew it.

### Where does his analysis place the state in the land question?

Largely offstage, and deliberately. The national state appears in his account as distant, weak in the interior, and captured at the point of application by the gamonal order, so that the land question is decided in the districts by the balance of local power, and only a transformation of that balance, not a ministry's decree, can settle it.


## The break with Haya: two roads from one generation

The quarrel with Haya de la Torre deserves its own section, because it was the formative dispute of the Peruvian left and because his side of it states his political theory in its most practical form. The two men were products of the same ferment: the university reform, the workers' universities in which both had taught, the anti-imperialist excitement of the postwar years. Haya, exiled by Leguia, built APRA first as a continental alliance and then as a Peruvian party, on a programme of national liberation: the unity of workers, peasants, students, and patriotic middle classes against imperialism and its local allies, the state reformed into an instrument of national development, socialism deferred to a later stage of the nation's growth. The programme had the wind of the decade behind it; it echoed the Kuomintang's construction in China and spoke to every class the oligarchy had excluded. His objection, delivered in the polemics of nineteen twenty-eight and in the founding of his party against APRA's Peruvian section, was anatomical. An alliance that includes the middle classes includes, in the Peruvian provinces, the gamonal's sons and clients, the merchants of the estate economy, and the professional men who staff its administration; such an alliance can fight the foreign company, whose expropriation costs its members nothing, and it cannot fight the land system, whose destruction would expropriate its own provincial base. Anti-imperialism without the agrarian revolution was, in his formula, a programme for changing the country's management while preserving its structure, and the national bourgeoisie it courted had demonstrated across a century that it preferred foreign partnership to domestic transformation. History's verdict on the dispute is a matter of record rather than of rhetoric: APRA became, in the decades after his death, a party of government and of accommodation, which never attempted the agrarian revolution and eventually administered the order it had been founded to overthrow, while the agrarian revolution, when it came in the late nineteen sixties, came from a military government acting on an analysis of the land question closer to his than to any other his generation produced. The verdict does not make his road the road taken; his party remained small and was broken by repression. It makes his anatomy of the alternative correct, which is the thing a profile is competent to judge.

## The reform that came from the barracks: the land argument's strange vindication

A generation after his death, Peru performed the experiment that tests the land argument in the only laboratory available, its own history. In the late nineteen sixties a reformist military government, having seized power amid the paralysis of the civilian order, carried out one of the most sweeping agrarian reforms in Latin American history: the great estates of the coast and the sierra were expropriated, the gamonal order as a system of rural power was destroyed, and the land passed, in large part, to cooperatives and to the communities themselves, whose legal standing the reform enlarged beyond anything the republic had contemplated. The reform's authors were soldiers, not Mariateguistas, and its doctrine was a nationalist developmentalism he would have criticised from several directions. Its bearing on his argument is nonetheless direct, and the profile should state it. The reform proceeded from his diagnosis: that the land system was the country's central blockage, that no development was possible across it, and that the gamonal had to be broken as a power before anything else could move. It proceeded, equally, against the counsel of both the framings he had refused: it was not accomplished by education, by administration, or by the market's maturation, but by the expropriation of the landholding class, the act his analysis had identified as the revolution's first task forty years before. And its limits confirmed his construction's importance in the negative: the reform distributed land without building the socialist order he had proposed to build on the communities, and its cooperatives, administered from above, decayed within a generation into individual holdings and renewed inequality, leaving the indigenous countryside freer of the gamonal and still poor. Vindication of a diagnosis is not vindication of a cure, and the Peruvian outcome is the cleanest possible demonstration of the difference: the country eventually performed the negative half of his programme, the destruction of the feudal land order, and omitted the positive half, the construction on the communal basis, and reaped exactly the result his three arguments, read together, would have predicted: liberation from the old obstacle, without the new foundation.

## Late Marx in the archive: how the convergence was noticed

The convergence at this profile's centre was invisible for half a century, and the story of its discovery is a chapter in the history of Marxology that explains his rising stock. Marx's Zasulich drafts slept in the archive through the whole period of his canonisation and his disgrace alike: printed once in a Russian scholarly journal in the middle nineteen twenties, they entered the working library of the movement only when the great scholarly editions of the postwar decades made the late Marx available to be read whole. The readers who assembled the dossier, the Japanese scholar-activists of the Marx revival, the editors of the new editions, and above all Teodor Shanin, whose collection Late Marx and the Russian Road presented the drafts and their context to the English-reading world in the early nineteen eighties, established what the drafts contained: a Marx who had refused, in writing, the unilinear sequence attributed to him, and who had entertained, in writing, the communal road for Russia. The discovery detonated quietly under the stage theory's pedigree. If the founder himself had held the multilinear position, then the orthodoxy that had condemned deviations in its name had been administering a selection, and the deviators who had reached the position independently acquired, retrospectively, a founder's warrant they had never claimed. His case was the most spectacular on file. The Peruvian who had been charged with populism for the communal argument turned out to have reproduced, without access, the founder's own analysis of the parallel case, down to the dualism of the communal form and the condition of contemporaneity. The scholars of the Latin American left, Jose Arico prominent among them, and the biographers and editors who rebuilt his corpus, Michael Lowy among the most tireless, drew the conclusion the evidence supported and stated it in the form this profile has adopted: the convergence is the credential, the strongest available proof that his Marxism was a creation in his own demanding sense, the method at work and not the catechism at rest.

### How was the convergence between his argument and the drafts established, if he never read them?

By the archive's chronology and the texts' comparison. The drafts were unpublished in his lifetime in every language he read, his formation and library are documented well enough to exclude a channel, and the structural correspondence between the two arguments, established by scholars who placed them side by side, is too systematic to be explained by the commonplaces both men shared.


## The other Peru: the coastal proletariat and the unions

The agrarian argument dominates his reputation, and it should not be allowed to erase the urban half of his construction, because the party he founded was an alliance in its design and the coastal term of the alliance was as considered as the sierra term. The Peru of the coast in his decade was a country of plantations, mines, oilfields, ports, and the textile mills and workshops of Lima and Arequipa, worked by a proletariat that was small by European measure and young by any, much of it first-generation, much of it indigenous or mestizo in origin, and already possessed of a fighting tradition: the great strikes that had won the eight-hour day in the capital at the decade's opening, the sugar workers' struggles in the northern valleys, the miners' camps of the central sierra where the companies ruled company towns as the gamonales ruled districts. His analysis gave this class the leading role in the coming revolution without flattering its size. The proletariat's claim to leadership rested, in his argument, not on its numbers, which he never exaggerated, but on its position: it alone stood inside the modern economy as its producer, it alone was organised by its conditions into collectivity, and it alone could ally with the peasantry on a programme, the agrarian revolution, that served the peasantry's interest rather than patronising it. The alliance was the strategy's whole content. A workers' movement that ignored the sierra would remain a coastal sect in a peasant country; a peasant movement without the workers' organisation and doctrine would remain a jacquerie, formidable in revolt and helpless in construction. The party and the confederation of his last years were the alliance's instruments, and the confederation's programme, uniting the unions on demands that joined the eight-hour day and the wage to the defence of the communities and the gamonal order's destruction, was the alliance written as a charter. That the construction was broken by repression after his death, the confederation driven underground and the party split and renamed, belongs to the sequel. The design itself, the worker and peasant alliance built on the land programme, became the default strategy of the Peruvian left for the rest of the century, cited by movements that had forgotten, or never known, whose drawing they were following.

## The communities in motion: congresses, committees, and risings

The indigenous population of his essays is sometimes read as a passive object of analysis, and the record of his decade corrects the reading: the communities were in motion, and he wrote as a contemporary of their movement, not as an antiquarian of their past. The years after the first world war saw an indigenous mobilisation across the southern sierra without precedent since the great rebellions of the colonial period. Communities sent delegations to Lima with petitions and titles in hand, walking for weeks to lay their land claims before a government that had promised the race its patronage; a committee for indigenous rights, taking the Inca name Tawantinsuyo, organised in the capital with indigenous and mestizo activists and carried the communities' cases into the press and the ministries; congresses of the communities met and demanded the restitution of their lands and the abolition of the unpaid services; and where petition failed, the sierra rose, in a scattered series of risings and estate invasions across Puno and the southern departments that the army suppressed district by district. The movement had its own leaders, and one of them stands in his circle's record with a clarity that forbids the passive reading: Ezequiel Urviola, an indigenous organiser from Puno who came to Lima as the communities' advocate, worked with the journal and the movement, and embodied in his own person the alliance the theory proposed, the community's delegate become a socialist militant without ceasing to be the community's man. The government's response to the mobilisation completed the lesson his essays drew from it. The regime that maintained a bureau for the race's protection answered the race's movement with troops, and the communities learned, in the phrase his analysis would have supplied, that their petitions were filed in the same districts where their enemies judged. His writing on the Indian was therefore not a Lima intellectual's speculation about a distant population. It was the theory of a movement in progress, whose petitions, congresses, and risings supplied his evidence and whose defeat, at the hands of the order he analysed, supplied his urgency.

### What evidence did he have that the communities could act as a political subject?

The mobilisation of his own decade: the delegations carrying land titles to the capital, the indigenous rights committee organising in Lima, the community congresses demanding restitution and the end of unpaid service, and the risings across the southern sierra when petition failed, a movement he observed at first hand and partly advised.

## The sources under the essays: Castro Pozo and the empirical floor

A word about the empirical floor of the communal argument is owed, because the romanticisation charge sometimes implies that he invented the ayllu from his reading of the chroniclers, and the implication is false. He worked from the best Peruvian evidence available to him, and its centrepiece was the work of Hildebrando Castro Pozo, a socialist official of the indigenous affairs bureau whose study of the indigenous community, published in the middle nineteen twenties after years of fieldwork in the sierra, documented the community's landholding, its authorities, its cooperative labour, and its legal disabilities with an administrator's precision. Castro Pozo's community was no idyll: his pages record the internal authorities' abuses, the community's exploitation by its own richer members, and the erosion of its lands, alongside the solidarity and the collective work. That his account and the official's account converge on the institution's existence, its functions, and its enemies is a fact the polemic against him rarely mentions. He also read the historians of the colony, the travellers, the indigenista lawyers who litigated the communities' titles, and the reports of the very bureau whose impotence he analysed, and his essays cite the Peruvian literature with a working scholar's familiarity. The floor matters for the grading of the concession made earlier in this profile. His error, where the later research found error, was not invention but extrapolation: he generalised the community's weight in the sierra's economy from evidence that was real but partial, drawn disproportionately from the regions where communal organisation was strongest, and he underweighted the commercial circuits that the regional archives later exposed. Extrapolation from partial evidence is the ordinary occupational disease of the analyst working before the archives open, and it is a different failing, with a different moral, from the fabrication of a golden age. The golden age rhetoric exists in his pages, in the Inca passages, and the objection is entitled to it. The foundation beneath the rhetoric was fieldwork, other men's and, through his movement's network of teachers, organisers, and indigenous delegates, effectively his own.

## His Lenin: the founder read from Lima

His relation to the Russian Revolution and its leader is a study in the independence this profile has documented from every other angle. He defended the revolution absolutely and from the beginning, against the Peruvian oligarchy's press and against the social democratic critics of Europe whose books he reviewed, and he honoured Lenin as the century's exemplary revolutionary: a creator, in his vocabulary, a man who had made a Marxism for Russia rather than administering one received. The Lenin he praised in print was precisely not the Lenin of the catechism. He praised the author of the April Theses, who had overturned his own party's stage theory when the Russian reality contradicted it; the tactician who treated doctrine as a guide to action and revised it in action; the founder who had insisted, against the pedants of his own movement, that the revolution would be made by the alliance of the workers with the peasantry of a backward country or not at all. The affinity is unmistakable and entirely conscious: his Lenin is the warrant for his own method, the proof at the tradition's summit that heroic creation is orthodoxy's true name. It follows that his quarrel with the International of his last years was never a quarrel with the Russian Revolution or with Lenin's memory, and his collaborators were exact on the point: he disputed the epigones' administration of the inheritance, the conversion of a method into a jurisdiction. The distinction may look convenient, and every dissident in the tradition's history has drawn it, but in his case the texts sustain it without strain. The Lenin writings are early, warm, and specific; the Buenos Aires documents are late, cold, and specific; and the philosophy that connects them, the Defence's account of Marxism as a method to be re-earned in each formation, is the same philosophy in both decades of his short career.

## The women of the movement: an unfinished chapter

A profile of his world should register, as his own practice registered, the women who formed part of it, because the movement he built was one of the few in his country's history in which women stood as intellectuals rather than as auxiliaries. His lectures at the workers' university included an address on the feminist demands of the age, in which he treated the emancipation of women as a part of the social transformation and not as a distraction from it, argued from the material basis of women's subordination in property and in the family economy, and refused both the conservative defence of the patriarchal home and the purely suffragist programme that would have left the home's economics untouched. The journal's circle included women writers of the first rank, among them the poet Magda Portal, whose work Amauta published and whose political path, through the aprista movement and out of it, crossed his in alliance and in polemic. The record here is thinner than the record elsewhere, and the profile should say plainly what it shows and what it does not. It shows a theorist whose framework gave him the instruments for the analysis of women's oppression and who used them, in lectures and in print, earlier and more seriously than most of his continent's Marxists. It does not show a developed theory of the question in his work, nor a movement in which women held the leadership their talents warranted, and the essays' silence on the indigenous woman, who carried the community's labour and the estate's pongaje in its most intimate form, is a real silence, which the feminist scholarship that has since taken him up has had to fill from outside his texts. The chapter is registered here as he would have registered a gap in any analysis: as a fact about the work's limits, in the same register as its achievements.

## The party after the founder: renaming, recantation, and survival

The sequel of the construction after April of nineteen thirty is the saddest passage in this profile and the most instructive. The International moved quickly to recover the Peruvian section its founder had kept at arm's length. Eudocio Ravines, a Peruvian communist formed in the International's own apparatus, took the leadership; the party was renamed the Peruvian Communist Party, in the form the International required; the programme was revised toward the line the Buenos Aires conference had defended; and the founder's collaborators were pressed to choose between recantation and expulsion, many of them drifting away into the independent socialist and intellectual life where his memory was kept without an organisation. The journal was already dead with him. The confederation was broken by the repression that followed the fall of Leguia and the military governments of the early nineteen thirties, in the same years that saw the aprista movement, his old rival, driven into clandestinity and massacre by the army. The Peruvian left thus entered its long middle age divided between the two heirs of the generation he had defined: a mass aprista party that had abandoned the agrarian revolution, and a communist party that had abandoned his analysis, while his texts circulated in the interstices, in the family publishing house's editions and in the memory of the teachers and organisers his journal had formed. The survival of the work through that middle age is itself a datum for the assessment of the man. Doctrines administered by apparatuses die with their apparatuses, as the International's own line of his decade died unmourned by everyone. His work survived without an apparatus, on the strength of being read, and when the continent's intellectual life revived at mid-century it was to his essays that the revival returned for its charter. The historian Alberto Flores Galindo, the finest Peruvian scholar of the following generation, built his account of the period around the polemic with the International and gave the dispute its definitive scholarly form, and the judgement of that scholarship has settled where this profile has settled: the deviation of nineteen twenty-nine was the orthodoxy's error, and the Peruvian's positions, on the Indian, on the land, and on the stages, were the tradition thinking at its best.

### Why did his work survive its party's repudiation of it?

Because the work existed in print, in a form any reader could reach, and because its claims were about Peru rather than about the movement's internal arrangements. A programme dies when its organisation reverses it; an analysis survives as long as the reality it describes is still there to be checked against it, and the sierra he described outlasted every line drawn in Moscow or in Lima.

## The heir who developed him: Quijano and the coloniality of power

Every major thinker in this series is graded partly by the quality of the development his work made possible in other hands, and his strongest developer was a Peruvian sociologist of the next generation, Anibal Quijano, who took the essays' central intuition and built from it one of the most influential frameworks in the Latin American social science of the following century. Quijano's point of departure was a fact the essays had placed at the centre and then, in his judgement, underexploited: that the classification of the world's population by race was not a prejudice surviving into capitalism but a structure created with it, in the Americas first, where the conquest assigned the Indian to serfdom, the African to slavery, and the European to the wage and to command, and made those assignments hereditary in the language of blood. From that starting point Quijano developed the concept of the coloniality of power: the persistence, long after colonialism's formal end, of the racial classification of labour and authority as the organising matrix of the capitalist world system, a matrix in which Peru's gamonal and the global division of labour appear as local and general forms of one structure. The development is faithful in the way that matters and unfaithful in the way that proves life. It preserves his founding move, the refusal to treat race as an epiphenomenon and land and labour as the reality beneath it is reversed and complicated, since Quijano makes the racial classification itself constitutive of the economic order rather than its reflection, and scholars argue still over whether the development corrects the master or corrects him away. What cannot be argued is the lineage's significance for the grading of the original. The land argument, in Quijano's hands, generated a research programme that conquered universities on three continents, which is not the fate of a provincial pamphlet. A profile concerned with evidence should record the development as the communal argument's most distinguished descendant, with the caveat the master himself would have entered: that a development is answerable to the reality it describes, and not to the ancestor it cites.

## The essay as his form: prose, fragments, and the unfinished book

Something should be said about the literary form of the work, because the form is part of the explanation of its survival. He was an essayist by trade and by conviction, formed in the daily press, and the Seven Essays are essays in the strong sense: attempts, soundings, arguments conducted at the length the material required and closed when the argument closed, without the treatise's obligation to fill its own table of contents. The form suited the country and the moment. Peru did not need, and could not have sustained, a Peruvian Capital; it needed a demonstration, performed on its own reality in public, that the materialist method could read that reality whole, and the essay sequence performed it at a length a newspaper reader could finish. The prose itself was an instrument of the demonstration. He wrote the best Spanish prose of any Marxist of his generation in any country, a prose of long balanced sentences and sudden aphorisms, formed on the Italian and French essayists and on the chroniclers of his own stone age, and its quality was a political fact: it made the doctrine readable by the literary class that set the continent's intellectual fashions, and it made the essays quotable, portable, and durable in a way that his decade's party literature, in its translated jargon, never was. The form's cost is the one the inheritance section recorded: an essayist leaves no system, and a system is what apparatuses and examiners alike prefer to inherit. His fragments have therefore been perpetually at risk of the quotation raid, the sentence lifted from its argument and conscripted. The defence, again, is the architecture: the essays argue in sequence, the three arguments lock together, and the reader who takes the book whole possesses something no raid can extract. That the book was two years old when its author died, and that its author described his own production, with justice, as the work of a man still forming himself, should be kept in view through every judgement this profile has passed. The Seven Essays are a first book. The tradition has few first books of their order, and none, in the Americas, of their consequence.

## The questions he left open: the state, the city, and the day after

An honest profile closes its analytical account with the silences, and his are instructive because they map the difference between his Peru and the Peru his successors inherited. He left no theory of the state adequate to his own programme: the gamonal order was to be destroyed and the communities developed, but the form of the power that would perform the destruction, its relation to the party he founded, its safeguards against becoming a new gamonalismo at national scale, are questions his texts pose by implication and answer nowhere, a silence he shares with most of his generation and which the following century's history has made conspicuous. He left the coastal economy's development underanalysed in the opposite direction from his sierra: the proletariat's role is asserted with brilliance and its sociology sketched, but the plantation and the mine, the enclave's internal order, receive nothing like the hacienda's anatomical attention, and the middle classes of the cities, the employees and professionals and small proprietors who would decide Peruvian politics for fifty years, appear in his pages mainly as the audience of other men's ideologies. And he left the day after unexamined: the communities developed, the estates broken, and then what organisation of industry, what relation to the world market whose technique he proposed to appropriate, what place for the market itself inside the new order, are matters on which the essays are silent and the journalism suggestive at best. These silences are not reproaches. A writer dead at thirty-five, two years after his first book, is entitled to his unfinished agenda, and several of the silences are the silences of the whole tradition in his decade, which had thought harder about the conquest of power than about its exercise. They are recorded here because the inheritance disputes surveyed earlier are largely fought inside them: each heir filled the silences from its own experience, and the fillings, more than the texts, are what the heirs disagree about. The reader who knows where the texts stop knows where the interpretations begin, and can grade the second by their distance from the first.

### What is the fairest single judgement on the work as a whole?

That it is the strongest analysis of its country produced by its generation, complete in its diagnosis and its anatomy of power, pioneering and partly overstated in its construction, and unfinished in the directions its author's early death froze, a first book that founded a tradition because the questions it posed were the questions its continent could not escape.

## The precursor: Gonzalez Prada and the debt acknowledged

He was not the first Peruvian to defend the Indian, and he said so, and the acknowledgement defines his originality by showing exactly where it begins. Manuel Gonzalez Prada, the great radical essayist of the generation before his, had indicted the republic's treatment of the Indian in essays of coruscating contempt two generations earlier: the Indian's degradation, Gonzalez Prada wrote, was the work of the landowner, the priest, and the official, the trinity of his oppression, and Peru would not be a nation while the majority of its people lived as its helots. The young Mariategui revered him, published in the tradition he founded, and took from him the essay as a weapon, the defence of the Indian as a Peruvian intellectual's first duty, and the contempt for the official republic's hypocrisy. What he added is the measure of the distance between radical literature and Marxist analysis. Gonzalez Prada's Indian was a victim to be avenged and a citizen to be educated; his remedies ran to moral regeneration, to the destruction of the priest's and the landlord's influence by enlightenment, and his economics never advanced beyond denunciation of the men who held the land to an analysis of the system that made their holding a mode of production. The successor's essays begin where the precursor's stopped: the trinity of oppressors becomes the gamonal system, the moral indictment becomes the land tenure analysis, and the Indian becomes a peasant whose emancipation is a transformation of property rather than an improvement of his masters' souls. He was explicit about the succession and generous in stating it, counting Gonzalez Prada the first lucid moment of the Peruvian conscience, and the generosity is itself diagnostic. A thinker secure in his method can afford ancestors. The catechist, who holds his doctrine by custody, must deny them; the creator, who holds his by demonstration, can honour the men who posed his questions before him, because his claim rests on the answers, and the answers were his.

## Valcarcel and the tempest: the indigenista alternative

The other contemporary against whom his position defines itself is Luis Valcarcel, the Cusco historian and anthropologist whose Tempestad en los Andes, Tempest in the Andes, appeared the year before the Seven Essays and prophesied, in a prose of biblical menace, the resurrection of the Indian and the storm that would sweep the white man's Peru away. Valcarcel was no enemy; the two men respected each other, shared the defence of the communities, and stood together against the oligarchy's contempt. The difference between them is the difference between indigenismo as a prophecy of race and his analysis of class, and he marked it with care. In Valcarcel's vision the Indian rises as the Indian, the heir of the Inca, and the coming order is the restoration, in modern dress, of the indigenous nation. In his own, the Indian rises as a peasant, in alliance with the workers of the coast and under a programme whose content is the land and whose form is socialism, and the coming order is not a restoration but a construction, in which the community's forms serve as material and the Inca past as a warrant of capacity rather than as a blueprint. The distinction mattered practically. A racial prophecy assigns the mestizo and the creole worker no role but submission or enmity, and divides the oppressed by blood at the moment they require unity; a class analysis assigns every Peruvian a place in the alliance by his position within the land system, and it was on that ground that he insisted the Indian problem would be solved by Indians and their allies together or not solved at all. Posterity has been kinder to Valcarcel's anthropology than to his prophecy and kinder to the essays' analysis than to either, but the pairing is the best short course in what his Marxism was: not a denial of the indigenous world, whose greatest contemporary student he treated as an authority, but a refusal to let that world be theorised as a race, when the evidence showed it to be a peasantry.

### How did he differ from the indigenistas with whom he is so often shelved?

In the direction of the explanation. The indigenista literature explained the Indian's condition by his culture's fate and looked to his culture's revival for the remedy; he explained the condition by the land system and looked to the system's destruction, treating the culture, with respect, as the form of the peasantry's life rather than as the cause of its oppression.

## The Mexican mirror: the ejido and the revolution that stopped

He wrote with one eye on Mexico, and the comparison disciplined his Peruvian argument in ways his readers should register. The Mexican revolution, a decade old when he returned from Europe, had carried out the hemisphere's first great agrarian upheaval: the haciendas of whole regions broken, the ejido, the communal land grant, restored and extended as the revolution's social charter, and a constitution that proclaimed the nation's ownership of its soil and subsoil. Here was the experiment his theory required, a neighbouring republic in which the land revolution had been attempted by a nationalist movement without a socialist party, and he studied its results with the attention of a man checking his predictions against a laboratory. The Mexican record, as he read it, confirmed the diagnosis and warned the construction. It confirmed that the land question was the continent's question, that no regime could stabilise itself above an unbroken latifundio, and that the peasantry, once armed with a programme of restitution, was the revolution's decisive force. It warned that a land reform administered by a bourgeois state would stop at the boundary of that state's class character: distribution without socialism, the ejido granted and then starved of credit and technique, the revolutionary generals become landlords in their turn, and the whole process congealing into a new order that had solved the political problem of the countryside while postponing its social transformation indefinitely. The moral he drew for Peru was the moral of the whole corpus. The agrarian revolution was necessary and insufficient; performed by a national front it would halt at the front's limits; performed as the first act of a socialist transformation, on the basis of the communities and under the alliance of the workers, it could go through to the end. Mexico is therefore the silent third party in the dispute with Haya, whose front proposed, in effect, to repeat the Mexican sequence in Peru, and whose programme he opposed with the Mexican evidence in his hand.

## The programme in practice: what the agrarian revolution was to do

His party's programme and the theses his movement carried to Buenos Aires state the construction's practical content, and a profile that has dwelt on the theory should set down the practice in the same detail, because the concreteness is part of the defence against the romanticisation charge. The agrarian clauses demanded the confiscation of the latifundios and the restitution of usurped lands to the communities; the abolition, without compensation to the system that had exacted them, of the servile labour relations of the sierra, the pongaje, the enganche, and the colono's unpaid services; the recognition and legal defence of the communities' property, with the community recognised as the basis for the cooperative organisation of agricultural production; the delivery to the peasantry of the credit, the technique, and the education that the estate system had monopolised; and the alliance of the indigenous peasantry with the coastal proletariat under a socialist programme as the political form of the whole. Two features of the programme deserve emphasis. The first is its treatment of the community not as a claimant among others but as the agrarian revolution's institutional heir: restitution was to run to the communities as bodies, and the cooperative development of their production was the programme's positive content, which is the communal argument translated into clauses. The second is the programme's silence where a romantic would have spoken. There is nothing in it of the Inca restoration, nothing of a separate indigenous state, nothing of the race; its categories are land, labour, credit, and power, and its promises are the promises of an agrarian code. The distance between the programme and the prophecy of a tempest in the Andes is the distance this profile has been measuring from its first page, and the programme, drafted by the man himself for the movement he founded, is the most compact evidence that the distance was his intention and not his interpreters' charity.

## The teachers: the movement's cadre in the sierra

One institution of his Peru receives less attention in the literature than in his practice, and restoring it completes the picture of how he expected the transformation to be carried into the districts his analysis described. The rural schoolteacher was, in the sierra of his decade, very often the only literate Peruvian in a community's world who did not belong to the gamonal's household: a mestizo or Indian of the province, trained in a normal school, paid a pittance by a ministry that forgot him, and placed by his function in daily contact with the community's children and its grievances. The teachers radicalised in numbers out of all proportion to their class, staffed the indigenous rights committees and the community delegations, wrote the petitions and kept the land titles' copies, and supplied the young labour and socialist movements with a cadre that knew the sierra from inside as no Lima organiser could. He understood their position exactly, because it reproduced his own: the teacher was the community's intellectual, formed by the lettered world and bound by origin and by daily work to the unlettered one, and his journal addressed them as such, printing the scholarship and the programme in a form a provincial teacher could carry into a village. The general point is one the series has met in the Italian profile, in the doctrine of the organic intellectual, and it is worth stating in his terms because he arrived at it, characteristically, from Peruvian evidence before he could have read it in the Italian's: every class and every community produces its own intellectuals, and a movement's first task is to find them, arm them with an analysis, and connect them to one another. The gamonal had his priest and his lawyer. The community had its teacher. The struggle for the sierra would be fought, in the first instance, between those two retinues, and his whole organisational practice, the journal, the lectures, the party school of his final years, was a machine for winning it.

## The funeral and the editions: how the corpus survived

His death came in Lima in April of nineteen thirty, and the city gave him the funeral his analysis would have predicted: the workers' organisations, the students, the teachers, and the indigenous delegations turned out in a multitude that the official Peru watched with unease, and the procession that carried a thirty-five-year-old journalist to his grave had the character, noted by every witness, of a political demonstration by a movement burying its founder. What followed the funeral is the material history of the corpus, and it belongs in this profile because the corpus's survival, on which everything else depends, was not automatic. The party was taken from his followers within the year. The journal was finished. The regime of the following decade alternated repression with a populism that had no use for him. The work survived because a widow refused to let it die. Anna Chiappe, the Italian wife of the European years, kept the house in Lima as the archive of his papers, founded and sustained the publishing house that bore the journal's name, and across the following decades issued, with their sons, the complete works in twenty volumes, volume by volume, through dictatorships and democracies, until the corpus existed in a form no further accident could destroy. The Defence of Marxism appeared in Chile within four years of his death; the essays were reprinted in Havana and Buenos Aires and Mexico City as the continent's left revived; the Seven Essays entered the curricula of universities that would not have admitted their author. The material history carries the profile's recurring moral in its plainest form. The apparatus that repudiated him is a footnote in the histories. The widow's publishing house is the reason the repudiation failed, and the texts, once safe in print across a continent, did the rest themselves.

## The English-language Mariategui: biography and boom

His arrival in English was late and partial, and its chronology explains the Anglophone world's long underestimate of him, which the convergence's discovery has been correcting. The Seven Essays appeared in English in the early nineteen seventies, in Marjorie Urquidi's translation, into a North American academy then discovering Latin America through the dependency debates, and the book was read, where it was read, as a precursor of those debates rather than as the theoretical construction it is. The first full biography in English, Jesus Chavarria's study of the man and his Peru, appeared at the decade's end and established the life's outline for Anglophone readers on archival foundations. The continental boom ran ahead of the translations: the nineteen seventies in Latin America saw the fiftieth anniversaries, the new editions, the recovery of the Buenos Aires documents, and the construction, by scholars in Peru, Argentina, Mexico, and Brazil, of the modern Mariategui literature, in which Flores Galindo's work on the Comintern polemic and Arico's on the Latin American Marx he belonged to are the load-bearing studies. The English reader's position has improved in the present century with the anthologies and with the late Marx scholarship's retrospective illumination, and the standard account in English now grants him the standing this profile has argued: the founder of an original Marxism in the Americas, and the independent discoverer of the multilinear road. The lag itself is instructive, and a series concerned with the movement of ideas should record its mechanism. He was untranslated because he was unclassifiable by the categories the translating world used, too national for the International's historians, too Marxist for the nationalists', too literary for the economists and too economic for the literary, and a thinker who fits no shelf waits long for a translator. The shelves have been rebuilt since, partly under his influence, and the wait is over.

### Why did the English-speaking world take so long to read him?

Because he fitted none of the categories through which it organised Latin American thought: not a party orthodox, not a nationalist caudillo of ideas, not a purely literary indigenista. His champions were Spanish-speaking scholars, his corpus mostly untranslated, and his strongest claim, the convergence with the late Marx, could not be stated until the founder's drafts were themselves published and studied.

## The foreigner in the economy: imperialism as he analysed it

His treatment of foreign capital completes the three economies' frame, and it is more exact than the anti-imperialist rhetoric of his decade because it is built into the classification rather than pasted onto it. Peru's modern sector, in his analysis, had been constructed from outside: the railways, the mines of the central sierra, the oil of the northern coast, the great sugar estates, financed in London and New York, managed for export, and connected to the Peruvian state by the mechanisms of the loan, the concession, and the customs house. The national bourgeoisie that such an economy produced was correspondingly deformed: a class of intermediaries, importers, estate owners supplying the export houses, lawyers and politicians servicing the concessions, whose prosperity depended on the foreign connection and whose politics therefore stopped, with a consistency he documented administration by administration, at the point where the connection was threatened. This is the analytical root of his refusal of the national front, and it should be distinguished from the cruder doctrine, later codified elsewhere, of a bourgeoisie simply bought. His coastal class was not a puppet cast. It was a real bourgeoisie with real interests, whose interests happened to be structurally allied to the foreigner's and structurally hostile to the transformation of the interior, since the interior's cheap servile labour and its gamonal order were among the foundations of the export economy's costs. Imperialism in his usage is therefore a relation inside the formation as much as a pressure upon it, and the conclusion follows with his usual rigour: a revolution that proposed to break the foreign connection while leaving the sierra's order intact would reproduce the connection in a new form, because the order was the connection's domestic half. The last editorials of his life, written as the world crisis of the new decade opened around him, applied the same frame to the crash: the crisis of the metropolitan economies would be discharged, he argued, onto the dependent economies least able to bear it, through the collapse of the export prices on which the whole coastal construction rested. He did not live to see the discharge, which duly arrived. The frame survived him, and the dependency theorists of the following generation, who built their school on the proposition that underdevelopment is produced rather than original, acknowledged the Peruvian as the proposition's first systematic American author.

## The Inca economy revisited: Murra and the vindication of the cell

The first essay's description of the Inca order as an agrarian communism was the element of his work most exposed to the romanticisation charge in its historical form, and its subsequent fate in the scholarship is a study in the difference between his rhetoric and his reading. The historians and anthropologists who rebuilt the Andean past in the decades following his death, John Murra's work standing at the head of the reconstruction, established an imperial economy organised without markets and without money in its core transactions, in which ethnic communities held land in the archipelago pattern across ecological floors, labour was mobilised by reciprocity within the community and by the state's levy upon it, and the state redistributed from its storehouses in famine and feast alike. Murra's Inca is not Mariategui's: the reciprocity that organised the system was also an ideology of the state that fed on it, the communal cell coexisted with an imperial extraction of great sophistication, and no modern scholar would sign the phrase agrarian communism without a page of qualifications. But the reconstruction vindicated the cell of his account against the liberal historiography he had fought, which had treated the Inca order as a despotism of terror relieved by nothing, and against the assumption that communal landholding was a colonial invention or a republican fiction. The community he proposed to build on had the pedigree he claimed for it, documented by a scholarship with no polemical interest in his reputation. The qualifications run in the direction his own analysis already pointed: the Inca state was an empire that used the community as the community's members used one another, and his essays, which called the order theocratic and authoritarian in the same pages that praised its agrarian achievement, are closer to the modern account than the commemorative literature that quotes only the praise. A reader grading the first essay by the current scholarship will mark the rhetoric down and the reading up, which is the same distribution this profile found in the communal argument as a whole, and the consistency of the distribution across forty years of hostile and friendly research is itself a finding about the quality of the mind that produced it.

### How has the later Andean scholarship treated his picture of the Inca order?

As a first approximation of remarkable quality. The reconstruction of the imperial economy on reciprocity and redistribution confirmed the communal cell and the absence of markets at the system's core, while discarding the simple phrase he used for it and restoring the extraction and the statecraft that his rhetoric had softened.

## The race theses: the document the conference condemned

The document at the centre of the Buenos Aires dispute deserves examination in its own right, because the conference condemned a summary and posterity has largely judged the summary. The theses on the racial problem in Latin America, drafted under his direction for the conference, argued a sequence of propositions that their critics found, and many readers still find, startling in their modernity. The indigenous population's oppression was real and specific, but its specificity was that of a class and agrarian oppression wearing a racial form, not that of a national oppression requiring a national solution. The demand for a separate indigenous republic, then circulating in the International's colonial discussions, was rejected as a reactionary utopia: it would isolate the Indian in a reservation state, divide the revolutionary forces along the line of blood, and leave the land system of the whole country, the actual machinery of the oppression, untouched outside the reservation's borders. The Indian's emancipation would come as part of the general emancipation, through the agrarian revolution and the alliance with the workers, and the communities' own organisation, not a racial party, was its vehicle. And the theses drew the conclusion his whole work implies about the mestizo and the coastal worker: that the revolution's subject in America was not a race but the exploited of all races, whose unity the land programme made possible and whose division the racial programme made permanent. The conference's critics heard in all this a refusal of the national question and said so. His defenders, then and since, have answered that the theses performed for the racial question the same relocation the essays performed for the Indian question entire: from the vocabulary of blood to the ground of land and labour, where a remedy could be built. The relocation has worn well. The indigenous republic formula vanished from the International's own practice within the decade, and the movements that later raised the indigenous banner in the Andes raised it, in their successful forms, on programmes of land and citizenship far closer to the theses than to the formula that defeated them on the conference floor.

## The avant-garde in the house: art as the journal's second front

Amauta's literary and artistic pages were not the journal's decoration but its second front, and his direction of them states his cultural politics in practice. The journal published the continent's new poetry and prose as they were written, carried the work of the Mexican muralists and the European vanguards in reproduction and in essay, and gave its covers and its visual identity to the Peruvian indigenista school of painters, Jose Sabogal at their head, whose woodcut Indians and Andean landscapes made the journal instantly recognisable on a Lima bookstall. The policy behind the pages was the policy of the whole construction. A movement that proposed to create a socialism for Peru required a culture in which Peru appeared as a subject rather than as a colony of European taste, and the journal set out to assemble that culture deliberately: the indigenous world rendered by its own painters, the new literature written from the country's own material, the European avant-garde imported as technique and example rather than as fashion. His criticism policed the policy with the same distinction he applied in politics. Art that used the Indian as local colour for the salon was dismissed as the cultural gamonalismo of the republic of letters; art that entered the indigenous world as its substance, however imperfect its politics, was defended as the beginning of a national culture. The cultural pages also explain the journal's continental reach better than its politics alone can do. A generation of writers who agreed on nothing else agreed on Amauta, because it was the one address in the Spanish-speaking world where the new art and the new politics were being thought together by an editor who was master of both trades. The party he founded is a date in the histories. The journal remains a presence in the culture, reprinted, exhibited, and studied as the moment the continent's modernity found its Peruvian form, and its editor's double mastery, the theorist who was also the best literary editor of his generation, is the biographical fact from which that presence flows.

## The civilista class: why no national bourgeoisie stood ready

One more piece of the classification requires its own statement, because the refusal of stages and of the front both depend on it: his account of the class the historians call civilismo, the coastal oligarchy of landowners, bankers, importers, and their political families that governed Peru, with interruptions, from the late nineteenth century into his own. The class's character, in his reading, was fixed by its origin in the guano and nitrate windfalls and by its permanent junior partnership with foreign capital: it had grown rich as the administrator of other men's enterprises and the landlord of the export valleys, and it had never performed, or needed to perform, the classic bourgeoisie's historical labours, the destruction of the feudal interior, the creation of a national market, the unification of the country as an economic space. Its liberalism was a costume worn for the club and the congress; its practice was the defence of the estate order that supplied its rents and its labour. The point of the portrait for his strategy is exact. A stage theory that assigned the coming revolution's first tasks to the national bourgeoisie assigned them, in Peru, to a class whose entire history demonstrated its refusal of them, and a front that proposed to ally with that class against the foreigner proposed an alliance with the foreigner's partners against the foreigner's rivals. The tasks would therefore be performed by the only forces whose interests required them: the workers and the peasants, organised under a programme that made the bourgeois revolution's unfinished business the first chapter of the socialist one. The portrait has been refined by later historians, who have found more enterprise and more internal division in the coastal class than his polemic allowed, and the refinement should be recorded with the others. Its strategic core, the absence in his Peru of a bourgeoisie capable of the national democratic programme, has not been overturned by the refinement, and the subsequent century of Peruvian politics, in which the transformation of the interior waited for soldiers and for crises rather than for the class the schema appointed, reads as its long confirmation.

## The communities after the gamonal: the survival that outlasted the argument

The communal argument's object has had a career since the argument was made, and a profile that grades claims against the record should follow the object to the end of the record available. The agrarian reform of the late nineteen sixties destroyed the gamonal order and, in doing so, changed the communities' situation in a direction his analysis would have recognised as ambiguous. The enemy was gone. The estates whose expansion had strangled the communal territory for a century were expropriated, and much of their land passed to cooperatives and to the communities themselves, whose legal personality the reform recognised in a new statute and whose numbers, as registered bodies, multiplied across the sierra. The community emerged from the reform as the Peruvian countryside's standard institution, the holder of its land, the organiser of its labour and its festivals, the unit through which the state itself administered the interior. And the development he had proposed to build upon it did not follow. The cooperatives of the reform era were administered from above and dissolved within a generation into family holdings; the communities, secure in their land at last, faced the market, the mine, and the city as smallholders in a national economy that offered their young people wages elsewhere and their produce prices set by others; migration drained the villages the reform had saved, and the communal forms persisted, as they had persisted under the colony and the republic, by adapting to an order they did not control. The record thus closes the argument with a symmetry he would have appreciated and deplored. The community survived the gamonal, as it had survived the encomendero and the liberal legislator, proving for the third time in five centuries the tenacity his construction had staked itself upon. What it never received was the transformation that was to have made the tenacity into a foundation: the power, the technique, and the national project under which communal production might have developed into a modern socialism rather than persisting as a refuge. The survival vindicates the analyst's eye. The persistence without development vindicates his insistence that survival alone was never the programme, and that the community's fate would be decided, as he always said it would, by the political order built around it or not built.

## The feudalism debate: his classification in the academy

The classification of the sierra as feudal, which his contemporaries contested, became, at the century's midpoint, the subject of a formal academic debate that a profile should register as the classification's second trial. Peruvian and foreign historians, Heraclio Bonilla prominent among the Peruvians, reopened the question with the archives open: had the hacienda of the sierra been a feudal enterprise, producing for its lord's consumption through servile labour, or a capitalist one, producing for the market through labour that was formally unfree but economically integrated? The debate ran for years through the journals and the universities, and its conduct vindicated his method even where its verdicts qualified his conclusion. The debaters found the formation mixed in exactly the pattern his three economies had described: estates selling wool and grain into national and world markets while organising their labour through servitude, communities producing for subsistence while supplying the estates and the mines with seasonal hands, a coastal capitalism whose profits rested on the interior's unfreedom. The pure labels fell away on both sides, and what remained standing was the compositional picture, the coexistence and interlocking of modes of production within one formation, which had been the first essay's founding claim. Scholars who began the debate intending to correct the amateur of nineteen twenty-eight ended it by rediscovering, with footnotes, the structure the amateur had drawn from the reports of his movement's teachers and delegates. The episode is the academic form of the convergence with which this profile began, and it licenses a general remark about his standing in the republic of scholarship. He has been corrected, repeatedly and properly, on the proportions of the things he described. He has not been corrected on the existence or the relations of the things themselves, and a theorist's errors of proportion, unlike errors of anatomy, are the kind that subsequent research exists to repair. The anatomy was his. The proportions are the academy's continuing work, conducted, whether the academy says so or not, inside the frame he built.

## Reading him whole: the profile's method restated

The sections of this profile have followed a discipline that should be made explicit at the end, because the discipline is the answer to the question with which the inheritance disputes began: how a reader is to take a thinker whom every faction quotes and no faction owns. The discipline has four rules, each drawn from the failures of the readings surveyed. Take the arguments in their order, diagnosis before construction, construction before the obstacle's anatomy inverted into strategy, because the order is the logic and the raid on a single row produces the caricatures. Hold the analytical claims apart from the polemical frames, grading the first by the evidence and the second by their occasion, because his rhetoric, formed in daily journalism and aimed at enemies who deserved it, regularly overstates what his analysis asserts, and the reader who grades the rhetoric as analysis will convict him of his metaphors. Apply his own standard to his own work, evidence over schema, because he asked for no other tribunal and because the work survives the application better than the work of the men who corrected him by instruction. And read the corpus as the record of a formation, the newsboy, the invalid, the exile, the editor, the founder, because the arguments' peculiar strengths, the concreteness, the impatience with abstraction unanchored in a district or a document, are the formation's strengths, and their limits, the unexamined state, the thin economics, are its limits too. A reader who observes the four rules will arrive where this profile has arrived, by a road the reader can retrace and check. The destination bears restating in its shortest form. A self-taught Peruvian journalist, working from a wheelchair in Lima with a first book and a journal, produced the first original Marxist theory in the Americas: a diagnosis of his country's central question that a century has confirmed, an anatomy of its rural power that the archives have sustained, a construction from its communal material that the evidence has qualified without overturning, and a method, the heroic creation, that stands as the tradition's permanent instruction to itself in every country where the doctrine is read as a catechism and obeyed as a fate. The convergence with the founder's unpublished thought is the certificate the tradition itself issued, half a century late, for the claim his anniversary editorial had made in advance: that socialism in America, thought from America's own reality, would not be a copy. It was not. It was the original the copies are now measured against.

## The siege of the journal: repression and the last years

The state whose structure he analysed was not an abstraction to him, and the record of his last years includes its attentions in their concrete form. The Leguia regime, which had begun in nineteen nineteen with a modernising rhetoric and settled into the dictatorship of a coastal oligarchy financed from New York, tolerated the socialist press while it remained a literary phenomenon and moved against it as it became an organisation. In nineteen twenty-seven the police announced the discovery of a communist plot, the pretext served for raids on the labour movement's premises and the journal's circle, and the editor, by then confined to his chair and in fragile health, was detained and held under guard in a military hospital while the journal's publication was suspended. The episode is worth more than its place in the biography, because it tested in practice the relation his theory described between the regime and the ideas. The plot was a fabrication of the police dossier kind, and the regime's own conduct conceded the point that mattered: it did not proceed against him as against a conspirator, for there was no conspiracy to try, but against the journal as an institution and the movement as a contagion, by suspension, surveillance, and the harassment of his collaborators. He emerged from the hospital to resume publication, to found the party the following year, and to conduct the Buenos Aires polemic from a house under observation, in a body that the detention had weakened and that gave him two more years. The state's theorists of the following decades, who inherited his country, have generally treated the repression of his circle as an episode of the dictatorship's ordinary brutality, and so it was. But its targeting was exact in a way ordinary brutality is not. The regime that lived by the foreign loan and the estate order struck, when it struck at ideas at all, at the one Peruvian who had explained in print how the loan and the estate order fitted together. The analysis had named the machine, and the machine, in its clumsy way, acknowledged the compliment.

### How did the regime's treatment of him confirm his analysis of it?

By its selectivity. A dictatorship of the coastal order, tolerant of literary dissent, moved against the journal when the journal became an organisation with a programme for the sierra, and proceeded by suspension and surveillance rather than by trial, because its quarrel was not with a plot but with an explanation of Peru that its own structure made dangerous.

## The miners: the proletariat the enganche built

The coastal proletariat of his analysis had a second detachment besides the plantation hands, and its experience completes the picture of how the formation's economies interlocked in human lives. The mines of the central sierra, copper above all, worked by North American capital from the first years of the century, drew their labour from the communities by the enganche's advance and held it in camps where the wage, the company store, and the altitude formed a regime unlike anything in the coastal valleys. The miner was the community's son working a season or a decade underground, remitting money that kept the communal economy alive, returning with silicosis and with experience of the most concentrated form of capitalist discipline in the country, and the mining camps became, in the decade of his party's founding, the sierra's schools of organisation, striking in the late nineteen twenties in the first great confrontations of Peruvian labour with foreign capital on Peruvian soil. His movement understood the miner's double belonging as the alliance's living mechanism. The miner did not need to be told that the community and the wage were one problem; his household lived the proposition, its land worked by his family under the community's tenure while its cash came from the camp, and his strike was the community's cause pursued by other means, as the community's land struggle was his. The party's and the confederation's organisers worked the camps deliberately on this understanding, and the mining proletariat's subsequent history, the federation, the massacres that answered its strikes in the following decades, the mining regions' permanent place at the centre of Peruvian conflict, runs on the track his analysis laid. The point for the profile's grading is the one the three economies' table exists to make: his class map was drawn from the movements of actual Peruvians between the economies, the enganchado in the mine, the colono's son on the estate, the teacher in the village, and not from the compartments of a schema, and the map has worn accordingly.

## The nation unfinished: nationality as a task, not a fact

Running through the essays is a claim about Peru itself that gathers the others into a single judgement: that Peru, a century after its independence, was not yet a nation. The claim was not a lament but a structural finding. A nation, in the usage his analysis implies, is a people made one by a common economic life and a common culture, and his Peru failed the definition on both counts: its economies faced outward to the ports and upward to the estates rather than inward to one another, its sierra and its coast lived in different centuries under different laws in fact, and its culture, the culture of the lettered coast, was the possession of a minority that treated the majority's culture as a survival to be pitied or a resource to be mined. The republic had the forms of nationality, the flag, the congress, the centenary orations of nineteen twenty-one at which he had listened as a young journalist, without the substance, and the official nationalism of the regime was in his account the ideology of the deficiency, a rhetoric of the fatherland performed precisely because the fatherland had not been built. From the finding the programme's deepest logic follows. The agrarian revolution was not, in his construction, an economic measure with national benefits as a by-product. It was the national construction itself, the act by which the majority of Peruvians would enter the common life as proprietors and citizens, the sierra be joined to the coast in one economy, and a Peruvian culture become possible as the culture of a whole people rather than of its lettered fragment. Socialism and nation-building, which the stage theory held in sequence and the nationalist front held in rivalry, are in his construction the same act described twice. The claim explains the otherwise puzzling reach of his appeal to Peruvians who never accepted his doctrine: the poets and the schoolteachers and the provincial lawyers who kept his memory were keeping, under his name, the proposition that their country remained to be made, and that its making was the task of their generation. A proposition of that size, once stated with his evidence, does not die with the doctrine that carried it. It passes into the national conscience as a standing accusation, and it is in that form, as much as in any party's programme, that his thought governs his country's reflection still.

## The world in his pages: the contemporary scene

His first book of the European return, The Contemporary Scene of nineteen twenty-five, collected the chronicles of the world crisis he had witnessed from Italy, and it deserves a place in this profile beyond its biographical function, because it shows the method operating on material where no Peruvian interest coached the conclusions. The volume reads the postwar world as a single crisis with national forms: the German inflation and the French occupation of the Ruhr, the British decline and the Irish settlement, the rise of fascism in the country he had watched at closest range, the Mexican revolution's consolidation, the new Turkey, the China of the first united front, the India of Gandhi, whose campaign he followed with a sympathy instructed by the same question he asked of Peru, what a colonised people's own forms could be made to do. Two judgements of the volume have had remarkable careers. The reading of fascism, written while the regime was still consolidating, treated it neither as a parenthesis nor as a mere reaction of capital but as the political form of a bourgeoisie that had lost the capacity to rule by consent and of a petty bourgeoisie ruined by the war and the inflation, an analysis the following decades' scholarship would rebuild at length without displacing its essentials. And the reading of the colonial world treated the anti-imperial movements of Asia as the coming centre of the world's revolutionary geography, a judgement made from Lima in nineteen twenty-five, when the International's own attention was still fixed on the European parties, and vindicated by the whole subsequent century. The volume's lesson for the profile is methodological. The man who insisted that socialism in America be thought from American reality was no provincial: he had read the world's crisis at first hand, in four languages, from inside its European epicentre, and the independence of his Peruvian construction was the independence of a cosmopolitan who had compared the originals and declined to copy any of them. The copy and calque sentence, so often quoted as a nationalist's slogan, is a comparativist's conclusion, and it carries the authority of the comparison performed.

## The school and the university: the education essays

Two of the Seven Essays take education as their subject, and their argument extends the land analysis into the republic's classrooms with the same refusal of pious generalities. The education the republic offered, in his account, reproduced the formation's divisions with pedagogical exactness. The primary school in the sierra, where it existed, taught the Indian's child in a language that was not his, from books that described a country he would never see, by methods whose practical function, whatever their intention, was to school him in his own inferiority and to select the few who would escape into the lettered world that despised his parents. The university, reformed in name by the student movement that had swept the continent from Cordoba in nineteen eighteen and whose Peruvian chapter he supported and criticised in the same essays, remained in substance the oligarchy's professional school, producing the lawyers and doctors of the coastal order and defending its privileges with the doctrine of autonomy whenever the order was questioned. Against this he set the principle that runs from the education essays to the workers' university he lectured in: that a people's education is the education its transformation requires, that the school's first material task in Peru was to teach the Indian as an Indian and a future proprietor rather than as a defective Spaniard, and that the university's reform would be real when the university served the country's transformation rather than its administration. The education essays are the least original of the seven in their economics and among the most prophetic in their institutional sense. The bilingual and intercultural education that Peru would spend the following century approaching and retreating from is argued in them in nineteen twenty-eight, from the land analysis outward, as a consequence of the diagnosis rather than as a reformer's afterthought. And the essays' treatment of the university reform movement, sympathetic to its students and unsparing about its limits, is the stage argument's educational form: a reform of the superstructure that left the formation untouched would decorate the oligarchy's school, not replace it.

## The religious factor: the church as landlord, the faith as the people's

The seventh essay's subject is the one a doctrinaire materialism would have handled worst, and his handling of it completes the evidence for the quality of his materialism. The Catholic Church in his Peru was, first, an economic power of the formation: a great landowner, the holder of urban property and of mortgages across the sierra, the gamonal's partner in the village and the ideological arm of the estate order, and his essay dissects that power with the land analysis's instruments and without a wasted word of anti-clerical rhetoric. But the essay refuses to stop at the dissection, and its refusal is the point. The religion of the Peruvian people, the Catholicism of the communities with its Andean depths, its saints who are also mountains, its fiestas that organise the agricultural year and the community's solidarity, was in his reading a real spiritual world and a real social bond, not a fraud practised by priests upon the ignorant, and a revolution that declared war on it would declare war on the people it proposed to liberate. The distinction he draws, between the church as an institution of the old order, to be dismantled with the order, and the faith as the people's own creation, to be respected as their property, is the communal argument's spiritual form: the same patience with the forms the people have made, the same refusal to begin by insulting the material he proposed to build with. It set him apart from the liberal anti-clericalism of his country's radical tradition, which he considered a coastal prejudice, and from the mechanical materialism that treated religion as a vapour above the economy, and it anticipated by decades the terms in which the continent's later movements, including the liberation theologians examined elsewhere in this series, would negotiate the same distinction. The essay is short, and its brevity is deceptive. It contains a complete theory of the revolution's cultural conduct, and its author's practice, the editor who filled his journal with the new art and the founder who treated the communities' fiesta as an institution rather than a superstition, was the theory in operation.

## Vallejo and the poets: the criticism as creation

His literary criticism, finally, deserves the profile's attention as evidence of the mind rather than as a garnish upon it, because the seventh essay's account of Peruvian literature performs on the republic of letters exactly the operation the first essay performs on the land. Peruvian literature, in his reading, was a colonial literature: formed in the metropolis's genres, written for the lettered minority, haunted by the absence of the country it claimed to describe, and periodised by him, in a scheme the historians of the literature still use, into a colonial period of imitation, a cosmopolitan period of influences, and a national period still struggling to be born, whose arrival would coincide with the arrival of the nation itself. Within that scheme his judgements were acts of construction. He championed Cesar Vallejo, the poet from the northern sierra whose work the Lima establishment found barbarous, as the first voice in Peruvian letters in which the Indian's world spoke in the first person, and the journal's pages and his own essays made the case that the subsequent century has ratified absolutely, Vallejo standing in the world's judgement where he placed him. He read the continent's new writing with the same instrument, finding in each work the formation that produced it and grading the work by its truth to that formation raised to art. The criticism matters to the assessment of the theorist for a reason his enemies never grasped. A man whose materialism could read a poem, find the land system in its silences and the unborn nation in its music, and be right in terms the poets themselves accepted, was not applying a schema to the world from outside it. He was reading the world in every register it offered, and the registers agreed with one another because the formation was one. The essays' unity, economics and literature and religion and education argued as a single reality, is the deepest sense in which the Seven Essays are a Marxist book: not in their conclusions, which the reader may dispute, but in their demonstration, performed on a whole country, of what the method is for.

## The popular universities: his first lecture hall

Before the journal and the party there was a lecture hall, and the institution that provided it belongs in this profile because it formed the audience his later work addressed. The popular universities named for Gonzalez Prada, founded in Lima in the first years of the nineteen twenties by the student leader Haya de la Torre and his generation, carried university teaching to the workers of the capital in evening classes held in union halls and borrowed rooms, with courses in history, literature, hygiene, and the social question taught by students and young intellectuals to audiences of artisans, drivers, and factory hands. The young Mariategui, returned from Europe with his head full of the continent's crisis, lectured in that circuit on the world situation and the Russian Revolution, and the lectures, later gathered and published by his students, show the teacher before the theorist: the same clarity, the same refusal to condescend, the same insistence that the audience's own country was the syllabus's final subject. The institution mattered to the formation in three ways. It gave him his first working audience and taught him the register in which the Seven Essays are written, the register of a man explaining a system to people who live inside it. It placed him, for a season, inside the student movement's project, from which he separated as its leadership turned toward the continental front politics he would later fight, so that the alliance and the rupture with Haya both have their roots in the same evening classrooms. And it embodied, in miniature, his lifelong conviction about education: that the school's value is measured by its service to the people's transformation, a conviction the education essays would state as theory after the lecture halls had taught it to him as practice. The popular universities were suppressed and revived with the political weather of the decade, and their alumni seeded the unions and the parties of the following generation, his own included.

## The Andean South: the argument's career in Bolivia and Ecuador

A theory built for one country is tested by its neighbours, and his construction faced the test immediately, because the formation he described did not stop at Peru's borders. Ecuador's highlands held the same hacienda order, the same servile concertaje by which the Indian was bound to the estate through debt, the same communal survivals, and the Ecuadorian socialists who founded their party in the middle of his decade worked in conscious dialogue with the Peruvian example, their programme placing the indigenous question and the land at the centre in his manner rather than in the manner of the coastal labour movements. Bolivia presented the case in its most extreme form: a country whose population was more indigenous than Peru's, whose mines were richer and whose estates were harsher, and whose politics, a generation after his death, produced the revolution of nineteen fifty-two and the agrarian reform that followed it, which broke the haciendas of the altiplano and distributed their land to the communities and the peasants in the hemisphere's second great land settlement. The Bolivian reform was not his work and its authors owed him no organisational debt, and the profile should claim no more for him than the record holds. But the settlement's content, the expropriation of the estate order as the precondition of the nation's existence, the community and the peasant union as its instruments, the miners as its armed vanguard, reads as his Peruvian programme enacted by other hands in the country whose formation most resembled his own, and the Andean scholarship has not been slow to draw the line. The general lesson is the one his method predicts. A theory built from a formation travels exactly as far as the formation extends, and no farther: his construction illuminates the Andean republics, where the land system and the communal cell were shared, and fades as the traveller moves toward the plantation coasts and the immigrant plains, where other Marxisms, built from other materials, did the corresponding work. The geography of his influence is thus the map of his analysis, and the map is a further piece of evidence that the analysis was of a real thing.

## Ink and paper: the material economy of the work

The material basis of his operation deserves a section, because a profile in this series is bound to practise on its subject the attention to material conditions that the subject practised on his country. The journal, the party school, the publishing ventures were not financed by any apparatus or any patron. They rested on the economics of the Lima print world he had grown up in: the family printing house, the Minerva workshop, which printed the journal and issued the Seven Essays under its imprint; the subscriptions of readers across the continent, collected in small sums through the mails; the sales of the book through the bookstalls of the capital; and the unpaid labour of the editor and his circle, for whom the work was a vocation conducted outside the hours of the employments that fed them. The fragility of that economy shaped the work's form as surely as any doctrine did. The essays are the length they are because a journal's pages and a small press's budget set the measure; the polemics are as sharp as they are because a publication that lives by its readers must be worth the coin; the corpus's survival, as the editions section recorded, rested after his death on a widow's determination to keep a small publishing house alive through decades in which its list could not have paid a commercial rent. There is a fittingness in the record that he would have been the first to point out. The man who argued that a Peruvian socialism must be built from Peruvian material built his own movement from the material at hand, a print shop, a subscription list, a circle of teachers and students, and made of those modest means an institution that outlasted the ministries and the parties of his decade. The heroic creation, examined at the level of its bookkeeping, turns out to have been a practice before it was a phrase.

## The state's Indian: official indigenismo and the absorbed defence

The final turn of the inheritance has a shape his analysis predicts and his admirers have not always enjoyed confronting. The defence of the Indian that he had made the centre of a revolutionary programme was, in the years after his death, adopted in diluted form by the Peruvian state itself. Official indigenismo, the institutes and the commissions and the ministries' bureaux devoted to the indigenous population, took over the vocabulary of protection, the celebration of the indigenous past, the anthropological attention to the communities, and administered them as a department of the very order whose land system he had identified as the problem. The absorption was not hypocrisy in the simple sense. The officials of the institutes included scholars of genuine devotion, and the communities gained defenders inside the state who cited his essays as their charter. But the structure of the absorption is exactly the structure his land argument describes: the defence was accepted in every register except the economic one, the Indian honoured as a culture, studied as a problem, protected as a ward, and left, in the matter of the land, where the estate system required him to be, until the soldiers of a later generation settled the account by expropriation for reasons of their own. His work thus suffered the fate he had anatomised in the protective legislation of the century before him, the fate of being administered by the power it was meant to restrain. The profile records the episode as the last confirmation of the relocation with which it began. Every element of his defence of the Indian that could be detached from the land was, in time, detached and officially embraced. The element that could not be detached waited forty years for an army. No verdict on the relocation's correctness could be more complete than the state's own conduct in adopting everything except it.

## The centenary: the official nation on display

One scene from his journalism fixes the origin of the unfinished nation argument better than any summary, and it belongs in the profile because he was present at it with a reporter's notebook. In the winter of nineteen twenty-one Peru celebrated the centenary of its independence with the full apparatus of official patriotism: foreign delegations, military parades, monuments unveiled, orations on the heroes, a capital dressed for the inspection of the world. The young journalist covered the festivities for his newspaper, and the chronicles he filed have the double register that would become his signature, respect for the genuine popular feeling in the streets and a cold eye for the ceremony's meaning. A republic was congratulating itself on a hundred years of nationhood in a city where the majority of the nation's people could not have read the congratulatory editorials, in a language many of them did not speak, above provinces whose inhabitants lived under a labour regime the independence had been proclaimed, in the official mythology, to abolish. The distance between the nation on the reviewing stand and the nation in the sierra was not, in his reading, an embarrassment to be regretted in the centenary's margins. It was the central fact of Peruvian history, the fact from which any honest account of the country had to begin, and the essays of the following years are, among other things, the centenary orations' systematic refutation, point by point: where the orations celebrated the republic's founding, the essays examined its land settlement; where they praised its heroes, the essays asked after its helots; where they declared the nation made, the essays set out the programme by which it might yet be made. Biographers sometimes treat a thinker's mature work as a break with the journalism of his youth. In his case the line is continuous and visible, from the notebook at the parade to the first essay's opening pages, and the continuity is worth a student's attention, because it shows the theory's birth certificate: not a doctrine imported and applied, but a spectacle witnessed and refused.

## The Soviet experiment as seen from Lima: knowledge and its limits

His defence of the Russian Revolution, absolute as it was, rested on knowledge of a particular kind, and the profile's standard of candour requires its limits to be stated as plainly as its grounds. He never visited Russia. His information came from the European press in four languages, from the Italian movement's debates, from the revolution's own published documents and its enemies' published indictments, and from the travellers and exiles who passed through the European capitals in his years there. On that basis he judged the revolution's essentials correctly and early: that it had broken the land system of an empire, that the commune and the peasantry were its social ground, that its survival against intervention and blockade was the century's decisive political fact, and that its leader's method, the subordination of schema to the Russian evidence, was the tradition's recovery of its founder. On the same basis he could not judge, and largely did not attempt to judge, the revolution's internal regime in its daily operation: the condition of its press, the fate of its oppositions, the texture of its administration reached Lima filtered through polemic on both sides, and his pages treat the Soviet state at the distance of a sympathiser who knew the distance. The honesty of the record matters for the assessment of the man, because it separates his independence from the two corruptions that surrounded it. He was not an apologist with a script, since no script reached him and his judgements track the evidence available to a reader of the European press rather than the requirements of any centre. And he was not a prophet of the revolution's later course, since the bureaucratisation that the following decades would debate was, in his lifetime, a tendency he registered through the International's conduct toward his own movement more than through Russian sources. The Buenos Aires dispute is, in this light, his most direct encounter with the Soviet state's machinery, and his conduct in it, the refusal to recant an analysis for an apparatus, is the judgement on that machinery that his information supported. He delivered it in the only court open to him, the court of his own movement's documents, and died before the appeal could be heard.

## The other diagnosis: Basadre and the republic's historian

His generation produced one other account of Peru that has lasted, and the comparison between the two is the seminar's best instrument for isolating what his Marxism added, because the two men shared the question, the evidence, and the city, and differed in the instrument. Jorge Basadre, the republic's great historian, a few years his junior, published in the year after the essays' author's death the book that founded modern Peruvian historiography, Peru: Problem and Possibility, and its title alone states the kinship: the country as a problem to be solved, its history as the record of a promise repeatedly made and repeatedly defaulted. Basadre's Peru is a failure of the state and of the elites who staffed it, a republic whose institutions never acquired the reality their paper forms proclaimed, and his remedy runs to the slow construction of a competent state, an educated citizenry, and a national history honestly faced. The two diagnoses agree on the symptom list to a degree that startles readers who meet them together: the fictitious republic, the abyss between the official country and the real one, the land and the Indian at the centre of the national failure, the centenary's rhetoric as the ideology of the default. They divide on the mechanism and therefore on the cure. For the historian, the mechanism is political and institutional, a state that never became one, and the cure is the state's patient construction from above by an enlightened generation. For the essayist, the mechanism is economic, a land system that made the state's fictitiousness necessary and profitable to the class that governed, and the cure is the system's destruction from below by the classes it held down. The division is the whole difference between a liberal and a Marxist diagnosis of the same patient, and Peruvian history since their deaths has run the experiment between them: the state was repeatedly reformed, modernised, and rebuilt from above, by civilians and by soldiers, and the land system outlasted every reform until it was broken by the method the essayist prescribed and the historian's school deplored, expropriation under pressure of the classes beneath. Basadre himself, to his honour, revised his account across a long life toward the economic depths his contemporary had sounded, and the two books now stand in the national library as the twin charters of the country's self-examination, the problem stated by the historian and the mechanism named by the Marxist. A reader who holds both possesses the Peruvian question entire, and can watch, in the space between them, the exact size of the originality this profile has been measuring.

### What does the comparison with Basadre isolate in his work?

The mechanism. Both men diagnosed the fictitious republic and the unfinished nation from the same evidence; the historian located the failure in a state that had never been built, the essayist in a land system that made the unbuilt state profitable to its governors, and the remedies they prescribed, construction from above against transformation from below, follow from that single difference.

The European press of his final years was full of the Russian succession struggle, and his handling of it completes the picture of the distant sympathiser. The expulsion of the opposition leaders, the exile of Trotsky, the consolidation of a single line in the International whose effects he was feeling in his own movement's affairs, all reached him as newsprint, and his pages register the facts without the enthusiasm of the apparatchik or the horror of the liberal. His loyalty was to the revolution as a historical act, the breaking of the old order in Russia and the example it set the colonised world, and he declined, in print, to make the internal arrangements of its party a test of that loyalty in either direction. Posterity can read the restraint two ways, and the profile should present both. It was, on one reading, the prudence of a man who knew his evidence's limits and refused to pronounce on a household he had never entered. It was, on another, the characteristic blindness of his generation of the revolution's friends, for whom the act of nineteen seventeen so outshone its sequel that the sequel's character was indefinitely postponed as a question. Both readings are true of the record he left, and the record's honesty consists in containing nothing that contradicts either. What can be said without qualification is that the one piece of the machinery he met at first hand, the International's discipline over a distant section's analysis, he judged on the evidence and resisted at the cost of his movement's standing. A man is entitled to be graded by the encounters he actually had.

### Did he defend the Russian Revolution without reservation?

He defended the revolution itself, its land settlement, its survival, and its founder's method, without reservation and against all its critics. The reservations he held concerned the administration of its inheritance by the International of his final years, whose discipline over his own movement he resisted on the evidence, and he stated that distinction in documents rather than in private complaint.

## The names he chose: Amauta, Socialist, and the semiotics of founding

The names of his foundations repay the attention he gave them, because each was an argument. The journal was called Amauta, the Quechua word for the wise man, the teacher of the Inca order, the keeper and transmitter of a people's knowledge, and the choice announced the whole editorial programme in a single word: the wisdom this publication served would be Peru's own, its teacher would speak from inside the country's reality, and the indigenous word on the masthead of a Marxist journal declared, before a line was read, that the doctrine and the nation were to be thought together or not at all. The party was called Socialist, against the prescribed Communist, for the reasons the dispute section examined, and the name carried the same double declaration, fidelity to the doctrine's substance and independence in its national form. Even the confederation's plain functional title makes the point by contrast: where no argument was needed, he named plainly, and where an argument was needed, the name made it. The semiotics of the founding were understood by his enemies, which is why the alignment after his death began with the names, the party renamed before its programme was even debated, as if the word were the deviation's root. In a sense the instinct was sound. A movement that calls its journal by the Inca teacher's title has already answered the question the conference was asking, about where its theory comes from and to whom it answers, and no subsequent conformity of programme could wholly unsay the answer printed on every cover. Names in politics are usually decoration applied after the fact. His were the fact itself, stated in advance, and the profile closes its evidence with them because they compress his entire position into the space a masthead affords: a Marxism that would teach Peru in Peru's own vocabulary, or would not pretend to teach it at all.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The profile is built for the seminar room in one respect above others: its three arguments separate cleanly into three sessions, and each session has a document that disciplines the discussion. The land session should read the first two essays against a concrete hacienda case from the scholarship, so that gamonalismo is examined as a working system and not recited as a definition. The stage session belongs to the Buenos Aires documents and the anniversary editorial together, set against the conference's own resolutions, with students required to argue the International's case before they are permitted to judge it. The convergence session needs the founder's Russian drafts in extract beside the theses, and it teaches best as a detection exercise, the parallels listed by the students before the profile's account is opened. The romanticisation objection makes the strongest closing exercise in the sequence: assign the charge to one group with Jacobsen's evidence in hand, the defence to another with the land essay open, and the concession, the unproven penetration claim, as the ground on which the class must settle the verdict. Teachers will find the comparison with the Italian profile in this series instructive for students, since the two men pose the same problem of theory's national rooting from opposite ends of the same decade.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The corpus rewards archival work and punishes quotation from secondary summaries, and three cautions govern its research use. The editions matter: the complete works in twenty volumes, issued by the family publishing house across the middle decades of the century, are the standard text, and the journalism scattered through the Lima press of his first period is still being recovered, so a citation's provenance should be checked against the volume and the year rather than against any anthology. The untranslated state of most of the corpus, registered in the regional guide's note on sources, means that Anglophone scholarship rests on a fraction of the evidence, and researchers working in English should treat every strong claim about his positions as provisional until the Spanish text is consulted, the Defence of Marxism and the Buenos Aires documents above all. The house in Lima where he lived and worked survives as a museum and archive, and the Peruvian scholarship, Flores Galindo's generation and its successors, remains the indispensable secondary literature, with the late Marx scholarship supplying the comparative frame. Journalists writing on the Peruvian countryside will find his categories still load-bearing in the field, the community, the estate's successors, the teacher and the priest, and should use them as he used them, as instruments to be checked against the district in front of them rather than as labels to be applied to it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Jose Carlos Mariategui?

A Peruvian journalist and theorist, born in Moquegua in eighteen ninety-four and dead in Lima in nineteen thirty, who founded his country's socialist movement and wrote its founding analysis. Self-taught in the newspaper offices of the capital, formed by three years in postwar Europe, and confined to a wheelchair after the amputation of a leg, he edited the journal Amauta, published his Seven Essays on Peruvian Reality in nineteen twenty-eight, and organised the party and the labour confederation in his final two years.

### Q: What are the Seven Interpretive Essays about?

Seven studies of one country under one method, published in nineteen twenty-eight. The essays trace the evolution of the Peruvian economy from the Inca order through the colony to the republic, examine the problem of the Indian and the problem of the land as the country's central questions, and then treat education, religion, regionalism, and literature as the same formation viewed from its other sides. Their unity is the argument: a feudal and colonial land system explains the republic's failures in every field the book touches.

### Q: Why does Mariategui say the Indian problem is the land problem?

His reasoning runs through the failure of every alternative remedy. Protections decreed in Lima dissolved on contact with the estate system, schools could not function where the child's labour was owed elsewhere, and juridical equality left untouched the tenure that made the Indian a serf in practice. What remained standing, as the cause of the rest, was the distribution of land and the servile relations organised around it, which is why his programme begins with expropriation and restitution rather than with pedagogy or law.

### Q: What is Indo-American socialism?

His name for a socialism created from the continent's own conditions rather than imported as a finished model. The phrase carries a method and a material: the method is the refusal of the copy, the doctrine re-derived from the formation under analysis, and the material is the indigenous communal tradition of the Andes, whose surviving institutions he proposed as the basis on which a modern collectivism could be built. It is a claim about how theory is made, before it is a claim about any single policy.

### Q: Did Mariategui think Peru could skip capitalism?

He rejected the question's machinery rather than answering it in its own terms. Peru's formation already contained capitalist elements on its coast, so there was no pure feudal stage waiting to be lived through, and the transformation he proposed was not a leap over a stage but a different road altogether, in which the agrarian revolution and the socialist construction proceeded as one process. The distinction matters because it separates his position from both the orthodoxy that demanded a bourgeois period and the romanticism that dreamed of a return.

### Q: How does this compare with Marx on the Russian commune?

The resemblance is structural and the independence is documented. Writing on Russia in the eighteen eighties, the founder allowed that a surviving commune might serve as the starting point of a collectivist development if a revolution supported it, and refused to convert his Western European sketch into a universal road. The Peruvian reached the matching position for the Andes in the nineteen twenties without access to those texts, which were unpublished or untranslated in his lifetime. Scholars treat the parallel as the strongest single evidence for the originality of his method.

### Q: Did the Comintern criticise Mariategui?

Yes, formally and in his lifetime's final year. At the Buenos Aires conference of nineteen twenty-nine his movement's documents on the racial and agrarian questions were attacked by the International's representatives as deviations, his party's name and programme were pressed toward the prescribed forms, and after his death the Peruvian leadership was replaced and his positions were set aside by the organisation he had founded. The dispute is documented on both sides, and the later scholarship has largely judged the documents in his favour.

### Q: What is gamonalismo?

His term for the rural power system of the Peruvian sierra, named for the gamonal, the local boss in whom landlord, political chief, and magistrate's patron were one person. The word denotes a structure rather than a character: the concentration of land, office, coercion, and credit in a single local figure, sustained by servile labour and by the indifference or complicity of the state. Because the power is structural, his remedy is structural as well, the destruction of the land system that produces the figure, rather than the replacement of bad bosses with better ones.

### Q: Why is Mariategui claimed by so many currents?

Because the inheritance was left genuinely open. He died at thirty-five with his party two years old, his programme argued but never tested in power, and his corpus built from essays that later movements could enter from different doors: the land argument for the agrarian reformers, the heroic creation for the national lefts, the communal construction for the indigenous movements, the Buenos Aires dispute for every critic of imported orthodoxy. The competing claims are evidence about the corpus's range, and they are graded, in serious discussion, by fidelity to the texts rather than by the claimant's strength.

### Q: Where should you start with Mariategui?

With the Seven Essays themselves, in the order of their arrangement, since the book is short, programmatic, and the source from which every later debate proceeds. The land essay repays the closest reading, and the essay on the Indian problem should be read beside it rather than separately. From there the Defence of Marxism supplies the method in his own statement, and the documents of the Buenos Aires dispute supply the politics. The English reader has the essays in translation and should treat the anthologies as supplements to the book, not substitutes for it.

### Q: How does the independent convergence grade his originality?

It converts an assertion into a measurement. Originality is usually claimed by comparing a thinker with his contemporaries, a contest easily rigged by choosing the field. The convergence compares him instead with the founder's own unpublished reasoning on a parallel case, a standard no advocate selected, and the match in both conclusion and structure shows a method independently arriving where the tradition's deepest thinking arrived. It does not make him the founder's equal. It establishes that his claim to have created rather than copied survives the hardest test available.

### Q: What should a student separate between a communal form's survival and its purity?

The evidence from the polemic. A community can be shown to have persisted, holding land and organising labour across centuries, without being shown to have persisted unchanged, sealed against the estate, the market, and the money economy pressing on its borders. His argument requires the persistence and builds nothing on the purity, since a form penetrated by the surrounding economy is exactly the kind of material a transformation works with. Examiners reward the separation because confusing the two either inflates the claim into romance or deflates it into dismissal.

### Q: Why does he begin from land rather than from education or administration?

Because his diagnosis assigns the school and the ministry their place in the causal order. An education reform operates on children whose labour and loyalty are claimed by the estate system, and an administrative reform operates through officials whom that system appoints or captures, so both reforms are processed by the structure they were meant to correct. Land is where the structure itself sits. Beginning there is not a preference for economics over culture, in a book that gives culture three of its seven essays, but a judgement about which term moves the others.

### Q: How does the three arguments table order his case?

By dependency. The land row states the diagnosis, the mechanism of Peruvian backwardness located in tenure and servile labour, and it must be established before the rest can stand. The stage row draws the diagnosis's consequence for strategy, refusing the sequence that would postpone the transformation until a bourgeoisie performed tasks its history showed it would not perform. The party row supplies the instrument, an organisation rooted in the peasantry's own institutions rather than administered from abroad. Read downward, the table is the profile in miniature: analysis, then strategy, then organisation.

### Q: What separates his use of the communal tradition from romanticising it?

The direction of the proposal. A romantic treatment admires the community as a survival, a fragment of a better past to be contemplated or preserved in its picturesque integrity. His treatment is instrumental and forward-looking: the community is valued as an organisation of production and solidarity on which a modern collectivism could be built, with schools, credit, and technique transforming it from within. He also records its defects, its narrowness and its internal hierarchies, without flinching. The test in any passage is whether the community is being used as an argument about the future or as an elegy for the past.
