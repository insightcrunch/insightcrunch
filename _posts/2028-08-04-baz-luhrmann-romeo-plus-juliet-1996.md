## Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet: The Verdict on Verona Beach

Baz Luhrmann's *Romeo + Juliet* is the boldest act of translation in the play's screen history, and the verdict of this profile is that its famous style serves the verse far more often than it swamps it. The film takes the whole of its dialogue from Shakespeare, in the words of the Oxford Shakespeare edition of the play edited by Jill L. Levenson, and then rebuilds everything around those words: the city, the weapons, the music, the news media, the very frame through which the story reaches its audience. What looks at first glance like decoration is in fact a system. The Prologue becomes a television news bulletin. The swords become handguns whose barrels carry the names Sword, Dagger and Rapier. The feud becomes a war between two business empires whose towers face each other across a beach resort. Every substitution answers a phrase in the text, and the substitutions hold together because they all perform the same operation, which is to give a 1996 cinema audience the experience the play's first audiences had: a loud, fast, violent, funny and heartbreaking entertainment that assumed no reverence and asked for no homework.

![Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet and the Verona Beach film world - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-47.webp)

The costs are real and this profile counts them. Roughly half the play, on the most generous contemporary count, was cut to fit a running time of exactly two hours. Some verse is shouted over gunfire or drowned in score, a choice the director defended as intentional and his angriest critic treated as vandalism. The film's Friar is a tattooed priest with a greenhouse of herbs, its Prince a police captain, its Mantua a trailer park in the desert, and none of these inventions is neutral. Yet the counterfactual matters. The reverent alternative had been tried, at vast expense, in 1936, and had embalmed the play. Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film had set the terms of fidelity for a generation: real teenagers, real Italian stone, a pruned text spoken gently. Luhrmann's film is best understood as an argument with that inheritance, and the argument is conducted inside the play's own language, which is why the argument could be won. A worldwide gross of some $147 million on a $14.5 million budget, a soundtrack album that reached No. 2 on the Billboard 200, and three decades of classroom use are the receipts. The quarrel among critics, examined in full below, divides between those who heard the verse survive the noise and those who heard only the noise. The evidence assembled in this profile supports the first party, with the second party's strongest point, the treatment of the verse at full volume, conceded and priced in. In the account that follows, the film is taken device by device, from the anchorwoman's desk to the brand names on the gun barrels, and each device is tested against the mechanism of the play it claims to translate. The record assembled here, of workshops and hurricanes, of casting crises and soundtrack sales, of a modest wager that returned ten times its cost, is the evidence on which the translation thesis stands or falls. The verdict defended throughout is that it stands, and that the film's excesses are the price of a method that has nowhere to hide.

## How Romeo + Juliet Was Financed: A Workshop in Sydney

The production began as an act of persuasion. Luhrmann had made one feature, *Strictly Ballroom* (1992), a low-budget Australian success built on the same conviction that a heightened, artificial style could carry sincere emotion. For his second film he proposed the least fashionable property in Hollywood: a Shakespeare tragedy, played in the original language, by young actors, in a setting that belonged to no century. Twentieth Century Fox, which distributed the finished film, was unconvinced, and the project turned on a workshop. Luhrmann assembled teaser footage in Sydney, shooting the opening fight as a proof of concept, and the young actor he wanted for Romeo paid his own way to Australia to take part. Fox saw the fight sequence and backed the film. The production company was Luhrmann's own Bazmark, the producers Luhrmann and Gabrielle Martinelli, and the screenplay was written by Luhrmann with his long-standing collaborator Craig Pearce, the co-writer of *Strictly Ballroom*.

### Why did Leonardo DiCaprio pay his own way to Australia?

DiCaprio flew to Sydney at his own expense and worked with Luhrmann for two weeks on workshop footage, because the film was not yet financed and the workshop was the audition the studio required. Fox committed only after seeing the teaser material, including the fight. Romeo was not cast from a list but demonstrated, in Luhrmann's style, on film.

Development and post-production stayed in Australia, with pre-production shared between Australia and Canada, an arrangement that kept the film's design culture in the hands of the small team that had made *Strictly Ballroom*: Catherine Martin, Luhrmann's wife and creative partner, designed the production; Kym Barrett designed the costumes; Donald McAlpine photographed the film; Jill Bilcock edited it. The score was composed by Nellee Hooper with Craig Armstrong and Marius de Vries. This continuity matters to the verdict. The film's style is not a studio's marketing skin applied to a classic property; it is the house style of a theatre-trained Australian company, scaled up. Luhrmann described his governing question in the production's own publicity: he wanted to consider how Shakespeare might make a movie of one of his plays if Shakespeare were a film director, and he reasoned that the playwright who played to three thousand punters, with bear-baiting as competition, was "a relentless entertainer" who used every device available to him. The film's devices are defended by its director as the playwright's own method, translated into the technology of a different century.

The workshop method deserves emphasis because it explains the finished film's confidence. Luhrmann was theatre-trained, and his company developed the production the way a theatre company develops a staging: a long rehearsal and design period in which the world of the film was built as a complete system before the cameras turned, every department working from the same premise that Verona Beach was a real place with its own laws of dress, weaponry, worship and news. The Sydney teaser was not a trailer in the marketing sense but a fragment of that system, the petrol-station fight shot as it would appear in the film, offered to the studio as evidence that the system worked. Fox's hesitation is itself part of the record of the period: a major studio in 1995 had no recent evidence that Shakespeare's language, undiluted, could open a film at number one, and the last prestige attempt in studio memory, decades of respectable stage Shakespeare notwithstanding, had taught caution. What changed the calculation was not an argument but a demonstration, four minutes of footage in which young men in Hawaiian shirts traded the thumb-biting exchange at a burning petrol station and the exchange played, instantly, as both comedy and threat. The film was financed the way its director believed Shakespeare was written: by proving, in front of an audience, that the entertainment worked before anyone was asked to admire it.

## Verona Beach: Building a Fictional City Out of Mexico

Verona Beach is an invented city, and the film is careful never to place it on a map. Its name fuses the play's city with the beach culture of the American Pacific and the Gulf, and its skyline is a composite assembled chiefly in Mexico. Principal photography centred on Mexico City and on Boca del Río in the state of Veracruz, with some material shot in Miami. The Capulet mansion is Chapultepec Castle, the nineteenth-century palace on its hill in Mexico City. The ballroom inside it, however, is a set, built on Stage One of the Churubusco Studios, because the sequence needed a space the production could fill with water, light and a stage for a drag performance. The church whose exterior serves as St Peter's, where the secret marriage and the final catastrophe are staged, is the Templo del Purísimo Corazón de María in the Del Valle district of Mexico City. Boca del Río supplied the beachfront resort the film calls Sycamore Grove: the boardwalks, the derelict theatre arcade, the petrol station and the sand on which Mercutio dies.

### Which real buildings stand in for the Capulet mansion and the church?

Chapultepec Castle plays the Capulet mansion, its terraces dressed as the seat of a business dynasty, while the ballroom was purpose-built on Stage One at Churubusco Studios. The church is the Templo del Purísimo Corazón de María in Mexico City's Del Valle district. Neither building is named on screen; both lend Mexican grandeur to a Verona that exists nowhere.

The design logic is heraldic. Two corporate towers dominate the skyline, one lettered Montague, one Capulet, and between them, on the waterfront, rises a colossal statue of Christ, arms spread over the city. The statue is the film's thesis in a single image: Verona Beach is a Catholic city whose religion has become scenery, looming over a feud it cannot stop. Catherine Martin's production design fills the frame with shrines, votive candles, dashboard Madonnas, sacred-heart tattoos and neon crucifixes, so that the play's language of heaven and hell has a visible world to refer to. The Prince of the play, Escalus, becomes Captain Prince, the city's police chief, played by Vondie Curtis-Hall; the "ancient grudge" of the Prologue (Prologue.3) is reimagined as a turf war between conglomerates whose younger men dress as gang soldiers and whose elders, Ted Montague and Fulgencio Capulet, conduct themselves as rival chief executives. What the setting refuses is period. Costumes mix Hawaiian shirts under suit jackets, body armour, ballgowns, fancy dress and priestly tattoos; the effect is not the modern world documented but a storybook world assembled from the modern world's wardrobe, at one remove from realism, which is exactly the remove at which Shakespeare's language can be spoken without embarrassment.

One further layer of the design requires naming, because it structures the film's visual argument from the opening titles to the closing church. Verona Beach is saturated with Catholic iconography: neon crosses and sacred hearts, roadside shrines, statues of the Virgin, votive candles by the hundred, a monumental Christ presiding over the district where the young men fight. The saturation is partly Mexico speaking through the locations, the production absorbing the visual Catholicism of the country it had moved to, and partly a deliberate heraldic choice. Within the film's system, the sacred imagery belongs overwhelmingly to the Capulet side of the city, the dynasty of the church wedding, the candlelit bier and the baroque mansion; the Montague world of towers, petrol and beach is comparatively secular, commercial, unshriven. The design thereby restates the feud as a collision of two civic religions, capital and Catholicism, with the lovers moving between a boardroom's city and a cathedral's, and finding their only sanctuary in the one institution that serves both: Father Laurence's church, itself dressed as a crossroads of the two, part laboratory, part shrine, its priest tattooed like a sailor and learned like a herbalist. When the catastrophe gathers the whole cast in that church at the end, parents, police and cameras arranged beneath the candles and the neon, the film's geography completes itself: the private tragedy is surrendered to the sacred space, because no secular space in Verona Beach has been built to hold it.

## A Production Under Siege: Hurricane, Fever and a Three-Hundred-Dollar Kidnapping

The Mexico shoot became part of the film's legend because so much went wrong in ways that sound invented. Luhrmann has retold the production's disasters in interviews across the years, and the retellings agree on the main events. A hurricane came in from the Gulf while the company was filming Mercutio's death on the Veracruz shore and destroyed the sets built there; rather than wait, the production shot in the storm's approach, and the tempest that tears at the beach sequence, the wind that scatters the actors' clothes and voices as Mercutio curses both houses, is partly the real weather. Illness then moved through the company until filming stopped for a week, with Luhrmann himself fevered. Members of the crew were robbed; most famously, the production's hair designer, Aldo Signoretti, a veteran of Fellini's sets, was kidnapped and held by men who telephoned a ransom demand of three hundred dollars. The make-up artist Maurizio Silvi carried the money to the rendezvous outside a hotel and threw the bag to the kidnappers, who threw Signoretti out of their car and broke his leg. Luhrmann's verdict on the ransom, given in interview, was that he thought it rather a bargain.

The schedule overran by some three months in the usual telling, and the budget discipline frayed, yet the production's troubles serve the analysis in one respect: the film's celebrated energy was not the product of a controlled studio machine but of a company improvising at the edge of chaos, and the director's stated method was to embrace whatever happened and fold it into the film. A storm became a lighting effect. A beach resort became a war zone. The play the company was filming is itself about young people whose improvisations outrun every structure built to contain them, and about a city where, as the Prince warns, disorder is answered with the threat that "Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1.93-94, in the Oxford edition's numbering). The parallel should not be pressed into mysticism, but it explains the film's texture: this is a *Romeo and Juliet* that feels dangerous to watch because, in stretches, it was dangerous to make.

## The Director's Method: A Created World and the Groundling Test

Luhrmann's approach had a name within his own company: the created world, a heightened environment with strict internal rules, inside which heightened behaviour becomes natural. *Strictly Ballroom* had built such a world out of competitive ballroom dancing, with its own costumes, hierarchies and rituals, and treated that world's artificiality as the condition of its sincerity. *Romeo + Juliet* applies the identical discipline at city scale. Verona Beach is not the modern world photographed; it is a designed cosmos in which every object has been passed through the same filter of baroque Catholicism, corporate heraldry, beach culture and television. The guns carry sword names because the world's laws require word and object to match. The Chorus is a newsreader because the world's laws assign summary to television. The lovers meet through an aquarium because the world's element for love is water. Once the laws are granted, nothing in the film is arbitrary, and the test of each device is internal: does it obey the world, and does the world illuminate the text?

The philosophy behind the method is the groundling test quoted earlier: Shakespeare as a relentless entertainer competing for a vast, mixed, distractible audience, deploying every device of his stage to hold it. Luhrmann's wager was that fidelity to that competitive situation is a deeper fidelity than any museum reconstruction of the plays' surfaces. The play's own Prologue states the stakes in the language of fate, "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue.6), but it states them to an audience that had paid to be diverted and would drift to the bear pit if it were not; the film reproduces that contract by treating its audience as groundlings with remote controls, one channel-change away from defection, and fighting for their attention with every weapon of 1996 cinema. The method explains the film's otherwise puzzling combination of liberties and literalism. The language is untouchable because the language is the attraction's core, the thing the groundlings of the Globe actually came for once the spectacle had seated them; the surfaces are disposable because surfaces were always the period's own, rebuilt nightly by a company playing in daylight to an audience that could see the wires. Critics who charged the film with betraying Shakespeare's world mistook which world was being honoured: not the world the play depicts, but the world the play was built to conquer.

## How Much of Shakespeare Survives? The Text, Cut and Reordered

Every word of dialogue spoken in the film is Shakespeare's. That claim, often doubted by first-time viewers who assume the slang of the setting must be newly written, is the film's foundational fact and its proudest boast; the screenplay credited to Pearce and Luhrmann is an edition of the play, not a paraphrase of it. What the screenplay does is cut, reorder and reassign. The play in full runs to some three thousand lines; the film runs 120 minutes, which is, to the minute, the duration its own Prologue promises for the stage: "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue.12). Something had to go, and the disagreement is over how much.

Contemporary and later accounts divide, and the division is instructive. Chris Tookey, among the film's most enthusiastic reviewers, described a film that "contains only Shakespeare's text, but this has been pared down to half its length". Gary Crowdus, surveying the hostile response in *Cinéaste*, reported the opposing charge: that Luhrmann and Pearce had "jettisoned most of Shakespeare's text retaining just enough to provide a reasonably coherent story-line". No production-side line count was ever published, so the honest statement is a range: between roughly half the play and somewhat less than half survives, with the lovers' scenes protected and the connective tissue, the servants, the citizens, much of Paris, much of the Friar's moralising and the whole of Friar John sacrificed. The cuts follow a principle the film shares with Zeffirelli's: the story is re-centred on the young. Where Luhrmann differs is in what replaces the missing text. Zeffirelli replaced it with landscape and pageantry. Luhrmann replaces it with information delivered by other means: headlines, news broadcasts, freeze-frame captions that name each character on first appearance, tattoos, licence plates, billboards. The exposition the verse carried is carried instead by the visual systems of a media-saturated city, which is why the film can cut so much and still feel dense.

### Did Luhrmann invent any dialogue of his own?

No invented dialogue exists in the film; the spoken text is Shakespeare's throughout, cut, reordered and occasionally reassigned between speakers. The screenplay's inventions sit outside the dialogue: modernised names such as Father Laurence and Captain Prince, family allegiances swapped for Gregory, Sampson and Abra, Friar John abolished for a courier firm, and the text of signs, headlines and captions citywide.

The scale of the cutting can be fixed against the text the screenplay started from. Modern editions of the play, conflating the Second Quarto of 1599 with the Folio, print some 3,000 lines; the series' study of the play's running time records the Second Quarto's 3,052 lines as a performance of roughly three hours at the period's pace of delivery. Luhrmann's film runs exactly half that span, and it uses the recovered hour as Zeffirelli never could: not as leisure but as velocity, cramming the surviving text into a frame whose cutting rate makes two hours feel like ninety minutes. The proportion question that divided Tookey from the hostile critics is therefore better posed as a question about density. A film that keeps half the lines and all the plot, and replaces the missing exposition with headlines, captions and design, has not simply abridged the play; it has changed the channel through which a third of the play's information travels, from the ear to the eye. Judged as an edition, the screenplay is ruthless. Judged as a translation between media, the ruthlessness is the method, and it is applied evenly: no character, however beloved, is exempt from the economy that the two-hour frame imposes.

The reorderings repay attention because they show the screenplay thinking. The Prologue is spoken twice over: once by the news anchorwoman, Edwina Moore, in the flat cadence of a bulletin, and once as a montage of screaming headlines and captions while the opening titles assemble the feud's dramatis personae. The Chorus, that most theatrical of devices, is thereby translated into the two media forms a modern audience trusts for summary: the newsreader and the front page. At the film's end the device closes like a frame, and the anchorwoman returns to deliver the closing lines to camera as the picture shrinks back into a television set and dissolves into static. The Prince's closing speech, with its distribution of blame and pardon, is compressed and partly reassigned to Captain Prince at the scene of the deaths. These are the decisions of editors who understood the play's architecture: the frame that the Chorus builds around the tragedy is preserved, but rebuilt in the materials of 1996.

## What the Film Leaves Out: Rosaline, the Musicians and the Watch

An adaptation is defined as much by its absences as by its inventions, and Luhrmann's absences follow the same law as his substitutions: whatever cannot be translated into the film's systems is cut, and whatever can be is kept at full value. Rosaline is the largest absence. In the play she never appears either, but she is everywhere in the first act's language, the unseen standard against which Romeo's love for Juliet is measured: "Love is a smoke raised with the fume of sighs" (1.1.190) is spoken of Rosaline's coldness, and the Friar's astonishment at the transfer of Romeo's affections drives the second act's moral argument. The film keeps Rosaline as a name, a rumour and a reason for gate-crashing the Capulet party, and lets her function exactly as she functions in the text, as the offstage pressure that makes the onstage conversion legible. What the film loses is the Friar's full cross-examination of that conversion, much of it trimmed with the rest of his moralising; what it gains is speed, and the loss is honestly registered in the film's own terms, since a Friar who cannot slow the action cannot warn it either.

The smaller absences map the film's priorities with equal clarity. The musicians who play at the wedding feast and joke over Juliet's supposed corpse, one of the play's strangest tonal pockets, are gone, their function absorbed by the soundtrack itself. The citizens and the Watch, the play's machinery of public order in the final scene, are replaced by the police cordon and the television crews, institutions the film has already established. Peter and the clownish servants survive only as fragments inside the opening brawl's choreography. Lady Montague, whose death of grief the play reports in its final scene, is not reported here; Christina Pickles's Caroline Montague is simply present at the catastrophe, her ruin left for the audience to read in her face. Paris is reduced most consequentially: Paul Rudd's Dave Paris, the governor's son, keeps his name, his entitlement and his death at Romeo's hand outside the church, but loses the dying request to be laid beside Juliet that gives the play's Paris his single moment of pathos. The pattern across all these cuts is consistent enough to state as the film's editorial constitution. The public and ceremonial play, the play of citizens, servants, musicians and formal mourning, is thinned to the minimum the plot requires. The private play, the lovers, the Friar, the Nurse, Mercutio, is kept nearly whole. Zeffirelli had made the same choice in gentler proportions; Luhrmann's radicalism is one of degree, and the degree is what the argument about the film is really about.

## Swords Into Guns: The Brand Names on the Barrels

The film's most quoted device is its solution to the play's weaponry. The text is saturated with sword language: the opening brawl begins with servants trading insults about bitten thumbs until blades are out, Benvolio cries "Put up your swords" (1.1), old Capulet, hearing a disturbance at his own feast, calls "My sword, I say!" (1.5), and Tybalt is introduced by the Chorus of opinion as a master of the duello. A modern-dress production that keeps the words must either keep the swords, as many stage productions do, and accept the anachronism, or find a modern object that can carry the word. Luhrmann's production found it in the handgun, and closed the last gap between word and object by branding the guns themselves: the firearms of Verona Beach are manufactured under the model names Sword, Dagger and Rapier, their designations engraved where a camera can read them in close-up. When a Capulet levels a Rapier at a Montague, the verse and the image say the same word.

The opening sequence shows the system at full stretch. Shakespeare's first scene, the servants' brawl that escalates until the Prince stops a riot, becomes a shootout at a Sycamore Grove petrol station, intercut at music-video speed, with freeze-frames that caption each combatant by name and house. The thumb-biting exchange survives inside it, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1), converted from a coded insult between servants into a taunt between pumped-up young men in cars and Hawaiian shirts, and it plays as comedy before it plays as violence, which is the scene's original shape. The sequence was the workshop footage that persuaded Fox to finance the film, and its function in the finished work is contractual: it teaches the audience, in four minutes, how to watch everything that follows. Accept the translation rules here, the film says, and the balcony scene will need no footnotes.

The device has a cost, and the film pays it knowingly. Guns kill faster and uglier than swords, and the deaths of Mercutio and Tybalt lose the formal symmetry of the duel. Luhrmann compensates by staging the killings as the play's text directs attention: Mercutio dies not in a firefight but at close quarters on the beach, stabbed in the confusion under Romeo's arm in substance if not in blade, and his curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99), is screamed into an approaching storm. The gun that kills Tybalt is fired by a Romeo who has first beaten him in a car chase and a crash, so that the killing reads, as it must, as a street execution rather than an honourable revenge. The brand names on the barrels are a witty solution to a translation problem; the staging around them is a serious one, and it grasps that in this play the weapons matter less than the speed with which young men reach for them.

## Casting Youth: DiCaprio at Twenty-One, Danes at Seventeen, Portman at Thirteen

The film's argument begins in its casting, because its casting is a direct reply to the screen history of the play. Cukor's 1936 film had starred a Romeo of forty-three and a Juliet in her mid-thirties, and had been embalmed by its own reverence. Zeffirelli had corrected the error in 1968 by casting teenagers who looked the parts and letting their inexperience stand as authenticity, the settlement examined in [Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film of the play](/2028/07/21/franco-zeffirelli-1968-film/). Luhrmann wanted young faces with professional technique, and the search for them produced the production's most retold story. Luhrmann had seen a magazine photograph of the nineteen-year-old Leonardo DiCaprio, fresh from an Academy Award nomination for *What's Eating Gilbert Grape*, and wanted no one else; DiCaprio's workshop performance in Sydney settled the matter. For Juliet, the production first cast Natalie Portman, who was thirteen, Juliet's own age in the text. Portman rehearsed, and the rehearsal footage exposed the impossibility: opposite a Romeo of twenty-one, a Juliet of thirteen made the romance look, in the word that attached itself to the episode in later accounts, like molestation. Portman herself, looking back, called it a complicated situation and a mutual decision: "at the time I was 13 and Leonardo was 21 and it wasn't appropriate in the eyes of the film company or the director, Baz."

The Portman episode is worth dwelling on because it was, in effect, the film's first honest edit, performed on reality rather than on the text. The production had wanted the documentary truth of Juliet's age, and the rehearsal footage supplied the truth's refutation: actual thirteen did not read as the play's thirteen, it read as a violation of the fiction's terms, because cinema photographs what the stage merely signifies. A stage Juliet of thirteen is a convention the audience agrees to; a photographed Juliet of thirteen is a fact the audience cannot unsee. Luhrmann's recasting is therefore not a failure of nerve but the discovery of the film's governing aesthetic law, that everything in it would be one degree heightened from the real, stylised enough to let the verse live, and the discovery was made, fittingly, in the casting office before it was made in the editing room.

Sarah Michelle Gellar was approached and declined for scheduling reasons. DiCaprio then championed Claire Danes, his contemporary from American television's *My So-Called Life*, on the grounds, reported at the time, that she was genuine in her delivery of the lines and did not play to impress him. Danes, born in April 1979, was seventeen in the standard accounts of the production, and the pairing worked because the small age gap between the leads read as a shared adolescence rather than a transaction between an adult and a child. Christian Bale read for Mercutio; the part went to Harold Perrineau, whose performance, in drag at the ball and in fury on the beach, became one of the film's signatures. Around the young leads Luhrmann stacked character actors of weight: Pete Postlethwaite as Father Laurence, Miriam Margolyes as the Nurse, John Leguizamo as Tybalt, Paul Sorvino and Diane Venora as the Capulets, Brian Dennehy and Christina Pickles as the Montagues, Paul Rudd as Dave Paris, the governor's son, and M. Emmet Walsh as the Apothecary. Ebert, the film's most famous enemy, exempted two performances from his condemnation, writing that Postlethwaite and Margolyes were "the only actors in the movie who seem completely at home". The exemption is revealing: the verse-speaking the critic trusted came from the two oldest members of the company, while the film's wager was that the verse could also survive in the mouths of the young. The box office and the Berlin jury, which gave DiCaprio its Silver Bear for Best Actor, settled that wager in the film's favour.

## The Grown-Ups: Verse-Speaking as Ballast

A film that asks its audience to accept teenagers speaking blank verse at gunpoint needs ballast, and Luhrmann cast it deliberately. Postlethwaite was a veteran of the Royal Shakespeare Company; Miriam Margolyes brought a character actress's armoury of timing; Brian Dennehy and Paul Sorvino carried the specific gravity of American screen authority; Pete Postlethwaite's Father Laurence, in particular, performs the film's most exposed verse-speaking feat in the cell scene of the third act, where he receives the distraught Romeo and delivers the play's great rebuke to despair: "Affliction is enamour'd of thy parts, / And thou art wedded to calamity" (3.3). The speech is long, rhetorical and static, everything the film's detractors claimed its style could not accommodate, and it is played in a single interior at something close to stage tempo, the camera trusting the actor's instrument. That it convinces is the strongest internal evidence against the thesis that the film's speed was a compensation for verse it feared. The speed stops whenever the film meets a performer who can hold a room.

Margolyes's Nurse works the same proof from the comic side. The Nurse's great set pieces, the weaning reminiscence and the breathless report of the marriage arrangements, are among the play's most demanding prose, and the film keeps their substance inside a performance of bustle, warmth and eventual failure: this Nurse adores her charge, serves her faithfully, and then, at the crisis of the forced marriage, counsels surrender, the betrayal Juliet answers with the coldest line in the scene, her resolve to trust the Friar or die. Juliet's foreboding as she takes her leave of the Nurse's counsel, "O God, I have an ill-divining soul!" (3.5.54), is the moment the film's Juliet grows past every adult around her, and it lands because the adults have been played at full size. John Leguizamo's Tybalt deserves separate notice in this ledger of ballast: a performance of coiled, balletic menace, played in Spanish-inflected registers of honour and display, which makes the Capulet prince of cats a figure of genuine aristocracy inside the gang idiom, so that his death diminishes the film's world as the text requires. The casting verdict of the Berlin jury and of the box office has already been recorded. The structural verdict is the one that matters here: the young leads did not carry the verse alone, and were never asked to. The film distributes Shakespeare's language across three generations of performers, as the play does, and the distribution is why the language survives the noise.

## The Capulet Ball: A Fish Tank, a Drag Act and a Shared Sonnet

The Capulet ball is the film's centrepiece and its fullest demonstration that the spectacle is organised, not random. The sequence begins in the play's own register of preparation and threat: Mercutio, delivering the Queen Mab speech in a state of manic exaltation on the steps of Sycamore Grove, presses an ecstasy tablet on Romeo, so that the play's great set piece about dreams becomes, in the film's pharmacological translation, the moment the drug is taken. The Mab speech is the play's first collision between poetry and the sordid machinery underneath it, a vision of fairies curdling into corruption, and the film honours that structure by letting the speech play as both entertainment and contamination: the party Romeo enters is already, chemically and socially, a place where dreams will turn.

### Why does Mercutio hand Romeo a tablet before the party?

The tablet translates the Queen Mab speech's logic into the film's pharmacology. Mercutio's aria calls dreams the children of an idle brain, begotten of vain fantasy, and Luhrmann has the dream delivered as a pill, heart-shaped as it changes hands. Romeo enters the Capulet house intoxicated, which is why the ball plays as delirium, not as a social occasion.

Inside, the ball is a fancy-dress pageant with a heraldic code the audience can read at a glance: Romeo in a knight's armour, Juliet in the white wings of an angel, Dave Paris in an astronaut's suit, Tybalt horned as a devil, Gloria Capulet as Cleopatra, and Mercutio, in platinum drag, performing Kym Mazelle's "Young Hearts Run Free" on a staircase stage with a troupe of dancers. The costumes are the film's character captions made wearable: Paris the astronaut is the suitor from another world, rich, sterile and weightless; Tybalt the devil is the family's id in fancy dress; Romeo the knight is the pilgrim of the sonnet he is about to speak, armoured for a shrine. The heraldry extends to the crisis that drives the plot out of the ballroom. Tybalt recognises the intruder by voice, as the text has it, "This, by his voice, should be a Montague" (1.5), and moves to expel him; old Capulet restrains the young man with the play's own mixture of hospitality and menace, granting the unknown guest the protection of the house: "He bears him like a portly gentleman" (1.5). The film plays the exchange at full value inside the noise, a reminder that the feud's rules of honour survive translation as completely as its violence does, and that the tragedy is set in motion not by the ball's spectacle but by a sentence of welcome spoken over it. Then the noise stops. Romeo, escaping the crush to wash his face, looks through the glass of a vast tropical aquarium, and on the other side of the glass, in her angel's wings, Juliet looks back. The first meeting of the lovers, which on stage is a dance-floor encounter sealed by a shared sonnet, is staged through water and glass, in silence except for Des'ree singing "Kissing You" on the ballroom stage, the song the production made the lovers' theme. The sonnet itself, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand this holy shrine" (1.5.92), "My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand" (1.5.93), is then spoken whole and quietly, the two of them alone by the aquarium and later in a lift, while the party roars on elsewhere. This is the film's method in miniature and the strongest single answer to the charge that its style swamps its text: at the moment of highest importance, Luhrmann clears the frame of everything except two faces and fourteen lines of verse, and the verse lands with a force that the surrounding spectacle has been arranged to purchase. The aquarium is not decoration. It is a held breath.

## The Balcony in a Swimming Pool: Water as the Film's Second Language

The balcony scene presented the production with its emblematic problem. The play's most famous staging image, a girl at a high window and a boy in the orchard below, depends on vertical separation: "But, soft! What light through yonder window breaks?" (2.2.2) is spoken upward, and the scene's erotics are the erotics of distance, of a love conducted across a guarded architectural gap. Luhrmann abolished the gap. His Juliet does not stay above; she comes down in a lift to the courtyard, in her angel costume, and begins the scene walking the terrace while Romeo hides among the statuary, and within minutes the two of them are in the Capulet swimming pool, fully clothed, conducting the great exchange face to face at water level while a security guard patrols and the house's cameras watch. The scene is repeatedly interrupted by the machinery of surveillance, and the lovers' final pledges are whispered in the water as they cling to the pool's edge. The translation at work deserves a precise name. In the text, the obstacle between the lovers is architecture and its guardians: "The orchard walls are high and hard to climb, / And the place death, considering who thou art" (2.2.63-64), Juliet warns, in a world where a wall and a watchful household stand for the feud's whole apparatus of separation. Verona Beach has no orchard walls worth the name; its apparatus is electronic. The film therefore converts the wall into cameras, the watch into a security patrol, and the orchard into a floodlit terrace, and the conversion is exact, because the function is identical: a system that exists to keep the houses' children apart, and that the lovers must defeat by stealth for exactly as long as a scene lasts. What the text distributes between masonry and kinship, the film distributes between masonry's modern heirs, and the scene's suspense, will they be seen, will the patrol pass, is the suspense of 2.2 with its technology updated and its stakes untouched.

Critics who disliked the film treated the pool as the emblem of its vandalism, Shakespeare drowned in a gimmick. The defence is stronger. The pool completes a system of water imagery that runs through the whole film and does interpretive work: the aquarium at the meeting, the rain that drenches the street scenes, the beach on which Mercutio dies cursing both houses as the storm breaks, the sea into which the city's violence keeps spilling. Water in this film is the element of the lovers, the medium in which they are weightless and equal, set against the film's other elements, the fire of the gun battles and the dust of the Mantua desert to which Romeo is exiled. In the pool, the hierarchy of the balcony, Juliet enthroned above her suitor like a saint above a pilgrim, is dissolved into an equality of immersion that suits the film's reading of the scene: not a courtship conducted by the rules of distance but a conspiracy of two people at the same level, in the same element, against the house that owns the water. The verse survives the translation because the translation is of the scene's meaning, its negotiation of risk and surveillance, rather than of its furniture. What is lost is real: the vertical ache of the original, the saint-and-pilgrim geometry that the shared sonnet established. What is gained is a scene a 1996 audience could read without instruction, and the exchange, spoken inches apart in the water, is among the most intimate renderings of 2.2 on film.

## The Hinge: Mercutio's Death on a Beach in a Rising Storm

Every account of the play's structure agrees that the tragedy turns on a single scene, and Luhrmann stages that turn with the full resources of his method. The third act's beach confrontation begins in holiday register: Mercutio and Benvolio at the Sycamore Grove shore, the heat shimmering, Tybalt arriving with his men in search of Romeo, and Romeo, newly married, refusing the quarrel in the play's most quietly revolutionary speech, his plea that "the reason that I have to love thee / Doth much excuse the appertaining rage / To such a greeting" (3.1.65-67). The film plays the refusal straight, DiCaprio's Romeo gentle to the point of provocation, so that the audience understands what Mercutio cannot: the peace is real, and it is illegible, and its illegibility is what kills him. Mercutio fights in Romeo's place, performs the fight as one of his turns, all flourish and contempt, and is run through in the scuffle as Romeo intervenes, the fatal wound arriving, as the text arranges, under the peacemaker's arm.

What follows is the film's most famous fusion of accident and design. The production was shooting this sequence on the Veracruz shore when the hurricane arrived, and Luhrmann, by his own oft-repeated account, kept the cameras turning as the weather broke; the wind that tears at the dying Mercutio, the surf and flying sand that scour the beach as he delivers the curse on both houses and staggers into the storm's edge, are the leading edge of a real tempest photographed on a schedule no studio would have bought. The sequence demonstrates the created-world method under maximum pressure: a company that had built its Verona as a system could absorb a hurricane into that system, because the system had already assigned weather a meaning. Storm in this film is fate's medium, the element in which the star-crossed machinery becomes visible, and Mercutio, the play's own meteorologist of dreams and curses, dies inside it. Romeo's pursuit of Tybalt, a car chase ending in a crash and a point-blank shooting beneath the district's great Christ statue, then completes the hinge: the lover of the pool and the aquarium becomes a killer in under a minute of screen time, and the film's colour temperature changes with him. Critics who praise the film praise this sequence; critics who condemn it rarely engage it, because it is the hardest evidence to answer, a stretch in which the style, the verse, the performances and the weather are all saying the same thing at the same time.

## Father Laurence, a Courier Firm and the Desert of Mantua

Pete Postlethwaite's Father Laurence is the film's most substantial reinterpretation of a supporting role. The Friar of the play is a herbalist and a schemer whose plot, built on a sleeping potion and a letter, collapses through a quarantine and a missed delivery. Luhrmann's Father is a priest whose chest carries a tattooed cross, whose cell is part greenhouse and part laboratory, and whose authority in Verona Beach is that of a man who serves both families and officiates at the city's rituals: he marries the lovers in a church blazing with candles, with the boy singer Quindon Tarver performing Prince's "When Doves Cry" from the choir. The marriage is played, unusually in screen versions, at full emotional weight, and the Friar's warning is given its textual prominence: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9). Ebert's exemption of Postlethwaite from his general condemnation registers what most viewers feel: the performance anchors the film's second half, and its gravity is the counterweight the lovers' story needs.

The potion plot receives the film's neatest piece of visual exposition. Where the play must let the Friar explain his plan in a single long speech and trust the audience to hold it, the film illustrates the explanation as he gives it, flashing forward through the funeral, the vault and the waking that the plan intends, so that the audience carries a complete picture of the design into the catastrophe and can measure every deviation from it. The device is sometimes mistaken for a music-video flourish; it is in fact a solution to the oldest staging problem in the play's fifth act, the audience's need to know precisely what should have happened in order to feel what did. Shakespeare solves it with a Chorus and a soliloquy. Luhrmann solves it with a montage, and the montage's clarity is why the film's final act, for all its speed, is never confused: every viewer in the cinema knows where the letter is, where Romeo is, and where Juliet lies, and knows that the three lines are converging on the church.

The machinery of the catastrophe is modernised with the same systematic care as the weaponry. The play's Friar John, quarantined by plague in a searched house, is abolished. In his place the film invents a courier company, Post Haste, whose overnight letter to Mantua becomes the tragedy's hinge: the letter is dispatched, the letter travels, the letter is delivered to Romeo's trailer in the desert and left for him, and Romeo, already riding back to Verona Beach on Balthasar's false report, does not see it. The device is more than a joke about postal services. It converts the play's theology of accident into a modern idiom every viewer has lived: the message that existed, that was sent in time, that arrived, and that failed anyway because delivery is not the same as receipt. Mantua itself is rendered as the anti-Verona, a trailer park in a bleached desert where the exiled Romeo lives in a caravan under an enormous sky, and where M. Emmet Walsh's Apothecary, a gaunt figure in a derelict shop, sells him the poison. Romeo's defiance, "I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), is delivered in that emptiness, a young man shouting at a heaven that in Verona Beach is crowded with statues and in Mantua is bare. The contrast is the film's geography of the soul: the city smothers its children with family, faith and noise; the desert abandons them to fate; neither place lets them live.

## The Ending: A Waking, a Church of Candles and a News Flash

The film's boldest textual intervention is saved for the tomb, which it relocates to the interior of the great church, where Juliet lies on a bier in a sea of candles and neon crosses, less a corpse in a vault than a saint on an altar. Romeo enters through a police cordon, past Captain Prince's men, and speaks his farewell over the body. The play's sequence is then altered at its most sensitive joint: as the poison takes hold, Juliet wakes. The two lovers see each other, living, for a suspended moment that the play forbids, theatrically unbearable precisely because it comes a breath too late; Romeo dies in her arms, and Juliet, after a silence the film holds, takes his gun and shoots herself. Shakespeare's Juliet stabs herself with Romeo's dagger, crying "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath" (5.3.169); the film's substitution of the gun keeps the translation system consistent to the end, the blade having been a firearm all along, and the change of weapon matters less than the change of timing, which converts the play's irony of near-simultaneity into an image of mutual recognition. Purists objected that the alteration sentimentalises the catastrophe, letting the lovers a consolation the text denies them. The defence is that the film earns the moment by having withheld sentimentality elsewhere, and that the waking, which stage productions have occasionally risked, is here prepared by the film's whole visual argument: a Juliet displayed as a living saint among candles was never, in this film's language, entirely a corpse.

One withholding completes the ending's design. The play closes with the machinery of reconciliation: the fathers clasp hands, and Montague promises a statue of Juliet in pure gold, "For I will raise her statue in pure gold" (5.3.298), with Capulet vowing the same for Romeo, the feud purchased off with monuments. The film dispenses with the statues. Its fathers are left standing in the glare on the church steps, berated by Captain Prince, their children already sealed in ambulances, and no handshake is staged for the cameras that crowd the scene. The omission aligns the film, perhaps unexpectedly, with the bleakest modern stage readings of the ending, in which the reconciliation is refused as obscene; here the refusal is achieved by ending the story where the news ends it, at the kerb, with the moral unspoken because the city that would speak it has spent the whole film demonstrating its bankruptcy. The golden statues belong to a Verona that believes its monuments; Verona Beach builds statues to Christ and lets its children die beneath them unbuilt and unraised.

The public frame then reasserts itself. The bodies are carried out past the assembled parents to the ambulances, Captain Prince delivers his rebuke to the two fathers on the church steps in the glare of the news cameras, and the film retreats into the television set from which it began, the anchorwoman intoning the closing lines as the picture dissolves to static. The play ends with the Prince's promise that "some shall be pardon'd, and some punished" and with the couplet "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). The film assigns the epitaph to the news, which is its final act of translation and its bleakest: in Verona Beach, even the moral of the tragedy is broadcast content, and the city's last word on its dead children is a bulletin. Critics divided over whether this frame honours or cheapens the ending. It honours it, on this profile's reading, because the play's own ending is also a public ceremony of blame conducted over the bodies, and because the film has consistently used the news as its Chorus: the device that tells the audience what the city will make of what it has seen. The lovers escape the frame for exactly one scene, the pool, and the frame closes over them at the end. That is the tragedy's shape, preserved.

## The Feud as Corporate War: Two Towers and One Statue

Luhrmann's feud is fought between companies. The Prologue's "two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1) becomes, in the film's opening montage, two office towers, two fleets of cars, two sets of young men in house colours, and two ageing patriarchs whose authority is corporate before it is paternal. The causes of the hatred are no more explained than in the play, where the grudge is "ancient" and its origin beyond recovery; readers who want the play's own account of [why the Capulets and Montagues hate each other](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) will find the same vacuum at the centre of the film, deliberately preserved. What the film adds is an economy. The Capulets are presented, in the production's own conception, as a Latin dynasty, played by Latin-American and Italian-American actors, with Fulgencio's empire coded through Catholic iconography, machismo and display; the Montagues are coded Anglo and corporate, their violence cooler in dress and hotter in temper. The asymmetry has drawn scholarly attention, some of it uneasy, and it gives the feud a legible modern texture: not a quarrel between identical houses but a contest between two immigrant-century ideas of power, old money against new display, fought by their sons in the street.

The feud is also conducted in print. The film's Verona Beach is papered with the feud's own media: newspaper front pages that scream the houses' names, billboards that carry their corporate marks, wanted posters, television bulletins that interrupt the story to update it. Scholarship on the film's media systems, notably Nicoleta-Mariana Iftimie's study of the role of media in the film, has catalogued how completely print and broadcast journalism saturate the mise-en-scène, and the saturation has a textual warrant: the play's Verona likewise learns of its own disorder through report, proclamation and rumour, from the Prince's public edicts to the letter whose failure to travel kills the lovers. Luhrmann's insight is that a modern city's feud would be a media event before it was anything else, fought in headlines as much as in petrol stations, and that the houses' patriarchs, arriving at the opening riot to be reprimanded like rival chairmen at a press conference, understand their honour as a form of publicity. The Prince's warning to the brawling houses, that further disturbance will be answered with their lives (1.1.93-94), becomes in Captain Prince's mouth a police ultimatum delivered at gunpoint's edge, and his later banishment of Romeo is enforced by patrol cars and a manhunt rather than by heraldry. The state in Verona Beach is not above the feud; it is merely its best-armed participant, a point the film underlines by dressing its police in the same visual grammar of helicopters, floodlights and televised pursuit as the gang war they police. When the catastrophe comes, the state's spokesman does not sum up a moral order restored; he berates two fathers in front of the cameras while their children's bodies are loaded into ambulances. The feud's corporate form is therefore not a gimmick laid over the play but an interpretation of it: the play's Verona is a city whose public institutions, the Prince's court included, have been captured by the quarrel, and Luhrmann's conglomerates and police helicopters say in 1996 imagery what the play says in the language of civil brawls bred of an airy word.

## Style as Rhetoric: Freeze-Frames, Captions and the Charge of MTV

The film's editing, by Jill Bilcock, is its most imitated and most denounced feature. Scenes are built from whip pans, crash zooms, speed-ramped chases, freeze-frames and captions that introduce characters by name; the opening petrol-station sequence cuts at a pace contemporary reviewers compared to a music video, and the label "MTV Shakespeare" attached itself to the film in its first week and never left. The label is half a description and the whole of a dismissal, and both halves need testing. As description it is accurate about tempo: the film's first movement is edited like a trailer for itself. As dismissal it assumes that tempo is the enemy of sense, an assumption the film refutes in practice, because the cutting is not uniform. Luhrmann and Bilcock vary the rate by the play's own emotional register: the brawls and chases are frenzied, the Queen Mab speech accelerates toward derangement, and then the film repeatedly stops dead, for the aquarium, for the pool, for the marriage, for the bier among the candles, holding shots in a stillness the faster cutting has made precious. The rhetoric is contrast. A film that never slowed down would indeed swamp its verse; this one spends its speed to buy its silences.

The stage had reached modern dress long before. Michael Bogdanov's 1986 Royal Shakespeare Company production, in suits and sunglasses with its own pop soundtrack, had already proved that the play could wear the modern world as an interpretation rather than a stunt, as [Michael Bogdanov's modern-dress RSC production](/2027/11/19/bogdanov-modern-dress-rsc-1986/) demonstrated to British audiences a decade earlier. Luhrmann's advance on Bogdanov was not the idea but the medium: cinema let him build the modern world to the horizon, brand the guns, caption the players and score the whole with the pop music his audience actually lived by. The freeze-frame captions deserve a specific defence, because they are usually cited as the reductio of the film's method. They are in fact the dramatis personae of the early quartos, the list of persons that print supplied and performance cannot, translated into the grammar of television sports coverage. A young audience meeting twenty characters in strange dress and stranger speech is given, at each first appearance, a name and a house; the device is pedagogy disguised as swagger, and teachers who adopted the film in classrooms understood it as such.

### What does the label MTV Shakespeare get wrong?

The label mistakes a range of speeds for one tempo. The editing is fastest where the play is violent or manic and slowest where it is intimate, and the quiet scenes are held longer than on most stages. MTV supplied the grammar of captions and cuts; the architecture beneath, which verse is protected and which is spent, is rhetoric.

## Television as Chorus: The News That Frames the Tragedy

The film's frame deserves a section of its own, because it is the device through which every other device is delivered. The play opens with a Chorus who stands outside the fiction and tells the audience, in advance, exactly how the story ends; the film opens with a television set floating in blackness, a newsreader at a desk, and the same sonnet delivered as a bulletin, after which the story proper begins as a montage of headlines, captions and file footage that assembles the feud's history like a news package. The symmetry at the close, the retreat into the set and the dissolve to static, completes a proposition: in Verona Beach, public events exist only as broadcast events, and the tragedy will be processed by the city in the only form the city trusts. Iftimie's study of media in the film makes the structural point that the whole narrative is thereby embedded inside a news programme, the audience placed in the position of viewers watching a story break in something like real time.

The device has a warrant inside the play that is easy to miss. The tragedy's public scenes are themselves acts of proclamation: the Prince enters the first brawl not as a combatant but as a herald, reciting the feud's record to the crowd like a bulletin, "Three civil brawls, bred of an airy word, / By thee, old Capulet, and Montague, / Have thrice disturb'd the quiet of our streets" (1.1.87-89), before issuing the edict that governs the plot. Verona in the play is a city that narrates itself through official speech; Verona Beach narrates itself through television, and the film simply promotes the Prince's heraldic function to the medium that performs it in a modern city, then splits the office between a police captain who proclaims in person and a newsreader who proclaims to everyone. The freeze-frame captions that introduce the characters belong to the same system: they are the lower-third graphics of a news package, name and affiliation supplied for viewers joining mid-story. Even the film's typography argues. The houses' names appear as corporate logos, mastheads and signage, so that identity in Verona Beach is always already published, a brand under which young men are willing to die. When the lovers' bodies are finally carried out to the ambulances, the cameras are waiting, and the film's last translation is complete: the Chorus has become the press corps, and its closing summary of the tragedy is indistinguishable from the evening news. Critics who found the frame a gimmick tended to underrate how much of the play's own machinery it preserves. The Chorus was never a decoration on the tragedy; it is the tragedy's theory of itself, the voice that insists the deaths were a public event with a public meaning. Luhrmann's news frame insists on the same thing in the idiom of a city that has forgotten every other idiom for public meaning.

## Designing Desire: Costume as the Film's Fastest Language

Kym Barrett's costumes perform the film's quickest translations, and they begin from the play's own most famous question about naming: "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44). Juliet's argument is that names are accidents and essences are real. Verona Beach answers her with a world in which names are essences: branded on gun barrels, lettered on towers, printed on mastheads, and, at the ball, worn on bodies. The costume plot is a naming system. The Montague boys wear open Hawaiian shirts over vests, a uniform of beach sloth and sudden violence; the Capulet men wear tighter, darker, more formal clothes, leather and tailoring that signal the dynasty's Latin formality; Tybalt's waistcoats and jewellery mark him as the family's duelling aristocrat. At the ball the system becomes emblematic fancy dress: Romeo's armour makes him the pilgrim-knight of his own sonnet, Juliet's wings make her the angel her name-day imagery promises, Paris's astronaut suit exiles him from the earth's gravity of the plot, Tybalt's horns confess him, Gloria Capulet's Cleopatra gown declares the dynasty's theatrical ambition, and Mercutio's drag turns the evening's entertainment into a coronation of the character the second half will kill.

The design intelligence extends to the film's most intimate register. Romeo's clothes loosen as the film proceeds, the armour and the party shirt giving way to the open shirt of the Mantua exile and the final church entry, a man progressively stripped of every uniform until nothing remains but the body that will lie beside Juliet's. Juliet's white, established at the ball, is maintained through the wedding to the bier, so that the candlelit church reads as a ghastly reprise of the ballroom: the same girl in the same colour, the same watching crowd replaced by statuary and neon. Barrett received a Saturn Award nomination for the work, and the film's single Academy Award nomination went to its design department, for Catherine Martin's production design with Brigitte Broch's decoration; the industry's honours, as the awards record shows, ratified the design system as the film's chief claim. The deeper point is interpretive. A production that dresses its argument can cut its text, because part of the exposition has migrated into cloth. When hostile critics complained that the film showed what the verse should have told, the costumes were the clearest instance of the migration, and the clearest test of the viewer's verdict on the whole method: translation, or eviction. On this profile's reading the ball alone settles the question, since no audience that has seen the knight and the angel through the aquarium glass needs the sonnet explained, and the sonnet, when it comes, arrives as recognition rather than information.

## The Soundtrack: A Second Script, Sold Separately

No previous Shakespeare film had carried a pop soundtrack of commercial consequence, and none has matched this one's. The music was supervised and in large part composed by Nellee Hooper, a central figure of the Bristol trip-hop scene, working with Craig Armstrong and Marius de Vries on a score that moves between orchestral writing, choral pieces and the licensed songs. The first soundtrack album, released by Capitol Records on 29 October 1996, three days before the film opened in the United States, collected the songs: Garbage's "#1 Crush", Radiohead's "Talk Show Host", Everclear, the Butthole Surfers, Mundy, Stina Nordenstam, the Wannadies, and the three tracks that became the film's public signature. Kym Mazelle's "Young Hearts Run Free" powers the ball. Des'ree's "Kissing You", performed by the singer herself on the ballroom stage, is the lovers' theme, reprised in instrumental form at the marriage and the tomb. The Cardigans' "Lovefool", which plays over the film's happiest stretch as Juliet waits and hopes, became the soundtrack's commercial engine on re-release in 1997: it reached No. 2 on the United Kingdom singles chart, topped the charts in New Zealand, and in the United States reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 Airplay chart and No. 1 on the Top 40 Mainstream chart, without ever appearing on the Hot 100 itself, because no commercial single was issued there and the chart rules of the time excluded airplay-only songs.

The album reached No. 2 on the Billboard 200 and was certified triple platinum in the United States; in Australia it was the second-highest-selling album of 1997 and went five times platinum. A second volume followed in 1997, devoted to the score and to dialogue excerpts, an almost unheard-of artefact: an album of Shakespeare speeches marketed to the film's young audience as a pop product. The score itself repays the attention the album gave it. Its signature is a choral writing of genuine scale, the massed voices of the "O Verona" material that crashes over the opening titles and returns at the catastrophe, so that the film's frame is sung as well as spoken: the Chorus of the play, divided in this production between a newsreader and a choir, performs its ancient office in the two musical and media registers Verona Beach understands. Quindon Tarver, the child singer cast as the church's choir boy, threads through the score's sacred side, performing "Everybody's Free (To Fall in Love)" on the first album and Prince's "When Doves Cry", arranged as a hymn, at the lovers' wedding in the film. The wedding sequence is the soundtrack's thesis in miniature: a Prince song, a gospel child, a candle-filled church and Shakespeare's marriage rite, four cultural registers occupying one scene without any of them yielding, which is either the film's whole method or its whole offence, depending on the critic. The music's honours matched its sales when Hooper, Armstrong and de Vries won the BAFTA for film music. The soundtrack matters to the verdict on the film because it was not merchandising appended to a finished work; it was the film's argument conducted in another medium. The play's own ball scene is cued by a stage direction of perfect economy, "Music plays, and they dance" (1.5), and Luhrmann's production understood that a film about young people in 1996 would be believed or disbelieved, by young people, on the evidence of its music before a line of verse was judged. The songs date the film, as period scores date Zeffirelli's, and they also carried the play into cars, bedrooms and radio playlists that no Shakespeare film had ever reached, which is why the soundtrack's chart history belongs in the film's critical record and not merely in its marketing file.

## Box Office: The $147 Million Answer

The commercial record is the ground on which the critics' quarrel was fought, because the film's success was immediate, large and demographically specific. Released by Fox on 1 November 1996 after a premiere at Mann's Chinese Theatre on 27 October, it opened in 1,276 theatres in the United States and Canada and took $11.1 million in its first weekend, finishing first. It went on to $46.3 million in North America and, on the standard Box Office Mojo tally, $147,554,998 worldwide, a figure the trade press rounds variously between $147.3 and $147.6 million. Against a production budget of $14.5 million, with some accounts giving $15 million, the film returned roughly ten times its cost before the soundtrack's separate millions are counted, and it stands in the record as the most commercially successful screen adaptation of a Shakespeare play. Its Australian release, opening on Boxing Day 1996, repeated the pattern in the director's home market: the film took A$3.3 million in its first week to head the Australian chart, held first place for a second week, returned to the top in its fourth, and finished as the ninth-highest-grossing release of 1997 in Australia with a calendar-year gross of A$12.9 million, on its way to some US$12.6 million in that market alone. The geography of the gross matters to the film's meaning. Verona Beach was a Mexican-built fiction of an American city, financed by an American studio, directed by an Australian company; that it played as a local triumph in Sydney and as a number one opening in Los Angeles is the commercial form of the film's central claim, that its invented city belonged to everyone because it belonged to nowhere. The audiences polled by CinemaScore graded it A minus, and the youth-market honours followed the money: at the 1997 MTV Movie Awards, Danes won Best Female Performance, and the pair were nominated for Best Kiss and Best On-Screen Duo, categories no Shakespeare film had ever troubled.

These numbers are not decoration on the film's reputation; they are evidence in its defence. The proposition Luhrmann had sold to Fox was that the play, staged as its first audiences experienced it, as popular entertainment built to outshout bear-baiting, would find a mass young audience without a word of its language modernised. The box office is the experiment's result. It does not prove the film is good; plenty of empty spectacles have outgrossed it. It proves the film's central wager was sound, that the barrier between young audiences and Shakespeare's language was a barrier of staging and packaging rather than of comprehension, and it changed what the industry believed: the cycle of youth-market Shakespeare films and television adaptations that followed in the next decade was financed in the light of Verona Beach's receipts.

## The Critics' Quarrel: Mess, Method or Mirror?

The contemporary reviews split along a line that has organised the film's reputation ever since, between critics who judged the film as a reading of the play and critics who judged it as an event in popular culture, and the quarrel is sharpest in the two most quoted notices. Roger Ebert, in the *Chicago Sun-Times*, gave the film two stars and the most famous hostile sentence written about it: "I have never seen anything remotely approaching the mess that the new punk version of *Romeo & Juliet* makes of Shakespeare's tragedy." Ebert's charge was structural. The film, he wrote, "will dismay any lover of Shakespeare, and bore anyone lured into the theater by promise of gang wars, MTV-style", a production he called "a very bad idea", whose "desperation" to update the play and make it relevant he found greatly depressing; only Postlethwaite and Margolyes seemed to him completely at home in it. Janet Maslin, in *The New York Times*, wrote the split verdict that history has sided with: she called the film "a witty and sometimes successful experiment, an attempt to reinvent *Romeo and Juliet* in the hyperkinetic vocabulary of post-modern kitsch", judged its lovers "played radiantly by Claire Danes and Leonardo DiCaprio", who "speak their lines with passionate conviction", and compressed her whole ambivalence into the phrase that outlived her review: "headache Shakespeare, but there's method to its madness". Desson Thomson in the *Washington Post* supplied the quarrel's best joke, that the film "puts Shakespeare's greatest romance in a choke-hold and takes it slam-dancing", a sentence that condemns and celebrates in one breath. James Berardinelli, awarding three stars, staked the moderate ground: that any adaptation stands or falls on its director and its leads, and that Luhrmann, Danes and DiCaprio placed this one "in capable hands". At the far pole from Ebert stood the British critic Chris Tookey, who called the film "the best Shakespeare picture ever made" and "one of the few truly revolutionary films of recent years".

Leonard Maltin's film guide, the reference shelf's voice of record, delivered the quarrel's most balanced capsule: "Arrestingly original, post-modern MTV-style '90s version", its words "all authentic", its images "dizzyingly clever", with the reservation that "the conceit wears thin after a while" and the concession that the earnestness of the two young leads keeps it alive. The guide's entry also performs an accidental service for the film's defenders: it locates the action in Miami, a city the film never names and only glancingly used, which is as neat a demonstration as exists that Verona Beach succeeded in being nowhere in particular. A reference work of record, summarising from memory, placed the invented city in the wrong state on the wrong coast, and was in a sense correct to do so, since the film's composite geography was designed to be misremembered as any sunlit American city of guns and water.

The scholars inherited the quarrel and gave it better terms. Kenneth S. Rothwell, the film historian whose *A History of Shakespeare on Screen* is the standard survey, located the film's method precisely: it "has been filtered through John Woo's Hong Kong action movies, and the hiphop and gangster rap of MTV, yet the characters speak in Elizabethan English". For Rothwell the incongruity is the achievement, a postmodern Shakespeare aimed at Generation X that treats the collision of registers as the meaning rather than the problem. Barbara Hodgdon, the leading critic of Shakespeare on screen, devoted her *Shakespeare Survey* essay "Everything's Nice in America?" to the film's double life, recording how conservative critics balked at its "over-determined commodifications" of the text and produced "resisting readings tied to notions about verse-speaking protocols", while she herself tracked the film's resonance within both Shakespeare-culture and global popular culture, concluding in a famous formulation that the film "has been watching us". Hodgdon's essay is the honest ambivalence of the academy: the film is a commodity engineered to sell Shakespeare as youth product, and it is also a genuine interpretation that exposes the audience's own habits of looking. Courtney Lehmann, in *Shakespeare Remains*, read the film through the question of authorship, treating its montage, citation and pop appropriation as a serious cinematic thinking about who owns Shakespeare, the play surviving as "remains" that each age contests and reassembles. Where Hodgdon doubts and Rothwell celebrates, Lehmann supplies the theory that reconciles them: the film's style is its argument about inheritance, and the frenzy is a position, not a failure of taste.

The adjudication this profile reaches follows from the evidence of the preceding sections. Against Ebert: the film is not a mess, because its devices form a consistent translation system, each substitution keyed to the text it replaces, and a mess does not produce the aquarium, the pool or the news frame, which are among the most disciplined inventions in Shakespeare on film. His narrower complaint, that some verse is delivered at a shout or under competing noise, is sustained by the record and conceded in this profile's account of the text; it prices the film's method without overturning it. Between Rothwell and Hodgdon: Rothwell wins on the film as cinema, since the collision of registers demonstrably carried the play to an audience of millions who had no other route to it; Hodgdon wins on the film as culture, since the machinery of commodification she anatomised, the soundtrack albums, the star photography, the fashion, is inseparable from the achievement and became the template for selling Shakespeare to the young thereafter. Lehmann's reading explains why both victories can stand: the film is an adaptation that knows it is an appropriation, and builds that knowledge into its form. The quarrel's later history offers a footnote in the film's favour. Stephanie Zacharek, who in *Salon* in 1996 had consigned the film to "the trash heap of Shakespeare adaptations", revisited it a quarter-century later in *Time* under the headline "I Panned *Romeo + Juliet* in 1996. Now I Think It's One of the Best Shakespeare Adaptations". Reputations in this quarrel have moved in one direction.

## Afterlife: Classrooms, Teen Films and the Verona Beach in the Mind

The film's longest life has been in education. In 2005 the British Film Institute placed it on its list of the fifty films a viewer should watch by the age of fourteen, an institutional verdict that matches its classroom reality across the English-speaking world: for a generation of students, this is the version screened beside the text, and the fish tank, the pool and the branded guns are the images the play summons before any stage production is seen. Teachers who build lessons around the InsightCrunch Luhrmann profile can assemble its records into slides and present them in a browser through [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), moving between the profile's table and the film's scenes as the text is read. The film also refounded a genre. The wave of modernised Shakespeare aimed at young audiences that followed it, in cinemas and on television, worked the seam it opened, and the play's own screen descendants multiplied; the story's migration into the modern teen film, in which the feud and the lovers are rebuilt under new names and new rules, is traced in the survey of [the play's modern teen-film descendants](/2029/04/27/teen-film-descendants/). Verona Beach itself entered the culture as a reusable place: in 2018 the immersive company Secret Cinema built a full-scale Verona Beach around screenings of the film, with thousands of spectators assigned to houses and dressed for the ball, an afterlife examined in this series' study of immersive production, whose existence as an event at all is a measure of how completely Luhrmann's invented city had become a location its audience felt it knew.

The film's place in its director's own canon was fixed in 2002, when it was reissued as the centrepiece of the Red Curtain Trilogy box set, between *Strictly Ballroom* and *Moulin Rouge!*, the three films presented as a single body of work in the created-world method. The packaging made explicit what the casting of Verona Beach had implied: the Shakespeare film was not an excursion from Luhrmann's style but its fullest early statement, the production in which the method's stakes were highest because the material was the most contested. In classrooms the film settled into a durable double life, screened in harness with Zeffirelli's, the two versions used as each other's controls: the pastoral against the pop, the pruned text spoken gently against the pruned text spoken at full volume, so that students could watch the same play survive opposite theories of fidelity and judge the theories by the survival. Few adaptations earn that pedagogical role, which requires being wrong in interesting ways as well as right ones, and the pairing has probably done more than any single production to teach a generation how adaptation works.

That is the final term of the verdict. Cukor's film preserved the play as a monument and Zeffirelli's as a pastoral, and both are honoured as such in this series. Luhrmann's film did the riskier thing: it treated the play as news, as pop, as a story a city tells about its own violence in the media it actually consumes, and it trusted Shakespeare's words to survive the treatment at full volume. The words survived. What the film changed was the audience, enlarging it by millions and resetting its age, and what it proved, against the most distinguished hostile witness of its opening week, is that the verse and modernity were never the opposites the cliché supposed. They were waiting for a production willing to stage their collision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Luhrmann profile, and what does it record about the film?

The InsightCrunch Luhrmann profile is the reference table in this article that sets out the documented record of Baz Luhrmann's film in one place. It records the credits, from Luhrmann and screenwriter Craig Pearce to cinematographer Donald M. McAlpine and editor Jill Bilcock; the production facts, including the Mexico locations and the buildings that play the Capulet mansion and the church; the money, from the $14.5 million budget to the worldwide gross of some $147.5 million; and the honours, from the single Academy Award nomination to the four BAFTA wins and the two Berlin prizes. Where the standard tallies divide, as the worldwide gross does between $147.3 and $147.6 million, the profile records the division instead of hiding it.

### Q: Who directed Romeo + Juliet, and who wrote its screenplay?

Baz Luhrmann directed the film, and the screenplay is credited to Luhrmann and Craig Pearce. The Australian director produced the film as well, through his company Bazmark Productions, with Gabrielle Martinelli as co-producer, and Twentieth Century Fox distributed it. The screenplay credit needs a word of explanation: Pearce and Luhrmann did not write new dialogue, because every spoken line in the film is Shakespeare's. Their work was editorial and architectural: cutting the play to roughly half its length, reordering scenes, reassigning speeches, modernising names such as Friar Laurence into Father Laurence, and inventing the visual systems, the news broadcasts, headlines and captions, through which the cut material's information still reaches the audience.

### Q: Where was Romeo + Juliet filmed?

The film was shot chiefly in Mexico, in Mexico City and in Boca del Río in the state of Veracruz, with some material filmed in Miami. Mexico City supplied the grand architecture: Chapultepec Castle became the Capulet mansion, the ballroom was built on Stage One of the Churubusco Studios, and the Templo del Purísimo Corazón de María in the Del Valle district served as the church. Boca del Río supplied the beachfront of Verona Beach and the Sycamore Grove resort frontage where the opening shootout and Mercutio's death were staged. Development and post-production were based in Australia. The Mexican locations were chosen because their baroque churches, palaces and decaying resort grandeur could be composited into a Verona that belonged to no real map.

### Q: How old were Leonardo DiCaprio and Claire Danes when the film was made?

DiCaprio was twenty-one during filming and Danes was seventeen in the standard accounts of the production. DiCaprio, born in November 1974, had been Luhrmann's only choice for Romeo since the director saw a magazine photograph of the nineteen-year-old actor, and he confirmed the casting by flying to Sydney at his own expense to shoot workshop footage. Danes, born in April 1979 and already known from the television series *My So-Called Life*, replaced Natalie Portman, who had been cast at thirteen and rehearsed before the footage showed her to be too young opposite a Romeo of twenty-one. The leads' closeness in age was the production's answer to the play's screen history, in which the lovers had repeatedly been played by adults a generation older.

### Q: Why is the film's city called Verona Beach?

The name Verona Beach fuses the play's city with a beach resort to announce the film's method in a single phrase: the story stays, the setting is transplanted. Luhrmann invented a composite coastal city, assembled from Mexico City, Boca del Río and glimpses of Miami, that belongs to no country and no map, so that Shakespeare's language would sound native nowhere and therefore possible anywhere. The beach element does thematic work as well. Verona Beach is a resort of pleasure and display built over a gang war, its boardwalks, boarded theatres and petrol stations forming the public stage on which the feud performs itself, while the sea and the storms gather the lovers' private scenes. The fictional name also warns the audience, from the first title card, to expect translation rather than documentary realism.

### Q: Why do the guns in the film carry names like Sword, Dagger and Rapier?

The guns carry sword names because the film keeps Shakespeare's dialogue, and the dialogue keeps saying "sword". Rather than ask the audience to accept modern firearms described by an archaic word, the production branded its handguns with the model names Sword, Dagger and Rapier, engraved on the weapons where the camera can read them in close-up. The device closes the gap between word and object: when Benvolio cries "Put up your swords" in the first scene, the weapons being lowered on screen are, by their own manufacturer's designation, swords. The branding extends the film's heraldic design, in which the two houses mark their property, cars, towers, tattoos, with names and emblems, and it remains the most quoted example of Luhrmann's systematic approach to translation.

### Q: Why does a television newsreader speak the Prologue?

A television newsreader speaks the Prologue because the film translates the Chorus, the play's own framing device, into the modern medium that performs the same job. In Shakespeare's theatre the Chorus steps forward to summarise the story and set its moral frame; in Verona Beach that function belongs to the television bulletin. The anchorwoman, played by Edwina Moore, delivers the sonnet in the flat cadence of news, and the film then repeats the Prologue as a montage of screaming headlines before the story begins. At the end she returns to deliver the closing lines as the picture shrinks into a television set and dissolves into static. The device frames the whole tragedy as a news event, which is the film's bleakest comment on the city: Verona Beach experiences even its catastrophe as broadcast content.

### Q: What happens at the Sycamore Grove petrol station in the opening sequence?

The opening sequence restages the play's first scene, the servants' brawl, as a shootout at a petrol station on the Sycamore Grove beachfront. Montague young men in Hawaiian shirts taunt their Capulet counterparts, the thumb-biting insult from Shakespeare's text is traded at the pumps, guns come out, and the confrontation escalates through fire, explosions and freeze-frame captions that introduce each fighter by name and house before Captain Prince's police break it up. The sequence was the workshop footage that persuaded Twentieth Century Fox to finance the film, and its place at the head of the finished work is contractual: in a few minutes it teaches the audience the film's translation rules, its speed, its comedy and its violence, so that nothing later requires explanation.

### Q: Why does the balcony scene take place in a swimming pool?

The balcony scene takes place in a swimming pool because Luhrmann abolished the vertical distance on which the scene conventionally depends and replaced it with the film's own element. His Juliet leaves the house by lift and crosses the courtyard at ground level, Romeo hides among the statues, and the two conduct the exchange face to face in the Capulet pool, whispering at the water's edge while a security guard patrols and the house cameras watch. The staging completes the film's system of water imagery that runs from the aquarium at the first meeting to the storm on the beach, an element in which the lovers are weightless and equal, and it converts the scene's meaning from courtship across a guarded gap into conspiracy at a shared level against the house that owns the water.

### Q: How do Romeo and Juliet first see each other at the Capulet ball?

Romeo and Juliet first see each other through the glass of a tropical aquarium. Romeo, overwhelmed by the ball and by the tablet Mercutio has given him, escapes to wash his face, and through the tank's glass he meets the gaze of Juliet, standing on the other side in the white wings of her angel costume while he wears a knight's armour. The moment is held in near silence, with Des'ree singing "Kissing You" on the ballroom stage, before the two find each other in person and speak the shared sonnet of the text, "If I profane with my unworthiest hand", in a lift and in corners away from the crowd. The aquarium is the film's emblem of its method: maximum spectacle arranged so that, at the decisive instant, nothing remains in the frame except two faces and the verse.

### Q: Why does Mercutio perform in drag at the Capulet ball?

Mercutio performs in drag at the Capulet ball because the ball is staged as a fancy-dress pageant in which costume announces character, and Mercutio's platinum wig and gown announce his as the production's licensed exhibitionist. Harold Perrineau's Mercutio takes the ballroom stage to perform Kym Mazelle's "Young Hearts Run Free" with a dance troupe, turning the play's wittiest figure into the party's master of ceremonies and its brightest light. The performance is functional as well as spectacular. It establishes the charisma that makes Mercutio's death, an hour later on the beach, the tragedy's true hinge, and it fulfils the character's textual nature: the Mercutio of the play is a performer, of the Queen Mab speech above all, whose brilliance is a public display with a manic edge, and the drag number gives that display a 1996 form.

### Q: Who is Captain Prince in Luhrmann's film?

Captain Prince is the film's translation of Prince Escalus, Verona's ruler in the play, recast as the chief of police of Verona Beach and played by Vondie Curtis-Hall. The change follows the film's systematic logic: in a city where the state has no court, authority wears a uniform, and the Prince's edicts become police ultimatums. Captain Prince breaks up the opening shootout and delivers the warning that further brawling will cost the houses their lives, banishes Romeo after Tybalt's killing with a manhunt of patrol cars and helicopters, and at the end berates the two fathers on the church steps in front of the news cameras while the bodies are loaded into ambulances. His reduction from prince to policeman is one of the film's quiet judgements: the state in Verona Beach polices the feud but no longer stands above it.

### Q: How successful was the film's soundtrack album?

The soundtrack album was a major commercial success in its own right. The first volume, released by Capitol Records on 29 October 1996, reached No. 2 on the Billboard 200 album chart and was certified triple platinum in the United States, while in Australia it became the second-highest-selling album of 1997 and went five times platinum. Its singles had long chart lives, led by the Cardigans' "Lovefool" and Kym Mazelle's "Young Hearts Run Free". A second volume followed in 1997, devoted to the score by Nellee Hooper, Craig Armstrong and Marius de Vries and to excerpts of the film's dialogue, and the three composers won the BAFTA award for film music. No Shakespeare film before or since has produced a soundtrack with a comparable commercial footprint.

### Q: Why did "Lovefool" become a hit without appearing on the Billboard Hot 100?

"Lovefool" missed the Hot 100 because Billboard's rules at the time required a song to be released as a commercial single in the United States to qualify for that chart, and no commercial single was issued. The Cardigans' song was an airplay phenomenon instead: after its re-release in 1997, carried by the film, it reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 Airplay chart, spent seven weeks at No. 1 on the Top 40 Mainstream chart, reached No. 2 on the United Kingdom singles chart and topped the chart in New Zealand. The anomaly is a tidy emblem of the film's own cultural position in its first years: a presence so large on radio, television and in cinemas that the official instruments for measuring pop success had to be read sideways to see it.

### Q: How much money did Romeo + Juliet make, and why did the result matter for Shakespeare on screen?

The film grossed $46.3 million in the United States and Canada and some $147.5 million worldwide, on a production budget of $14.5 million; the worldwide tally is given as $147,554,998 in the standard Box Office Mojo count, rounded variously between $147.3 and $147.6 million in the trade press. The result mattered because it falsified the industry's standing assumption, inherited from the failure of the costly 1936 Cukor film, that Shakespeare in the original language could not be sold to a mass young audience. Luhrmann's receipts, roughly ten times the budget before the soundtrack's earnings are counted, made the film the most commercially successful Shakespeare adaptation on record and underwrote the cycle of youth-market Shakespeare productions that followed it in cinemas and on television over the next decade.

### Q: Which Academy Award was the film nominated for, and what did it win at BAFTA and in Berlin?

The film's single Academy Award nomination was for Best Art Direction, for production designer Catherine Martin and set decorator Brigitte Broch, and it did not win. The honours came elsewhere. At the BAFTA awards the film won four of its seven nominations: Best Direction for Luhrmann, Best Adapted Screenplay for Luhrmann and Pearce, Best Film Music for Nellee Hooper, Craig Armstrong and Marius de Vries, and Best Production Design for Catherine Martin. At the 1997 Berlin Film Festival, where the film competed for the Golden Bear, DiCaprio won the Silver Bear for Best Actor and Luhrmann received the Alfred Bauer Prize. The pattern of the awards mirrors the film's reputation: the industry honoured its direction, design and music, the crafts of its style, while its acting prize went to the Romeo at the centre of its casting wager.

### Q: Why did Roger Ebert dislike the film while the New York Times praised its lovers?

Ebert disliked the film because he judged its method a confusion of purposes: in his two-star review he called it "the mess that the new punk version of *Romeo & Juliet* makes of Shakespeare's tragedy", argued that it would dismay lovers of Shakespeare and bore viewers drawn by the gang warfare, and found only Pete Postlethwaite and Miriam Margolyes completely at home in the verse. Janet Maslin in the *New York Times* judged the same method a "witty and sometimes successful experiment" and praised Danes and DiCaprio as lovers who "speak their lines with passionate conviction", summarising her ambivalence as "headache Shakespeare, but there's method to its madness". The split was not about the facts of the film but about the standard of judgement: Ebert measured it against the stage tradition it refused, Maslin against the young audience it addressed.

### Q: What does the giant statue overlooking Verona Beach mean in the film's design?

The giant statue of Christ that rises between the Montague and Capulet towers is the film's thesis about religion in Verona Beach: faith is omnipresent as scenery and impotent as authority. Catherine Martin's design fills the city with shrines, votive candles, dashboard Madonnas, sacred-heart imagery and neon crosses, so that the play's language of heaven, saints and pilgrims has a visible world to refer to, while the feud flourishes untouched beneath the outstretched arms. The statue also organises the skyline's meaning. The two corporate towers face each other as equals under a heaven that watches and does not intervene, which is the film's visual equivalent of the play's star-crossed machinery: in this Verona, God is part of the cityscape, and fate is part of the weather.

### Q: What is Sycamore Grove in the film?

Sycamore Grove is the beachfront resort district of Verona Beach where the film stages its public violence and its most famous set pieces. Built and dressed on the shore at Boca del Río in Veracruz, it is a pleasure strip of boardwalks, arcades and a derelict theatre, named for the grove outside Verona's walls where Shakespeare's Romeo walks in lovesick solitude at the play's opening. In the film the district hosts the petrol station of the opening shootout, the steps on which Mercutio delivers the Queen Mab speech, and the beach on which he dies as the hurricane approaches. The name is one of the screenplay's quiet acts of fidelity: a throwaway place-name from the text's first act, promoted into the film's permanent public stage, where private feeling keeps being dragged into the open and destroyed.
