Marxism in India names two histories that are usually told apart and should not be. One is political: communist parties that organised workers and peasants, survived illegality and repression, contested elections under a constitution they had not written, and repeatedly won state power through the ballot. The other is intellectual: a tradition of historians, economists and social theorists who took Marxist categories into a society organised by caste, dominated by agriculture and shaped by two centuries of colonial rule, and who changed the categories in the process. Each history explains the other. The parties supplied the questions, about land, caste and colonial exploitation, that the scholarship then formalised. The scholarship, in turn, gave the parties an account of the society they were trying to organise, an account sharp enough to argue with and durable enough to outlast several of the organisations that commissioned it.

![A study desk in India with the streams table, party histories, agrarian studies and volumes of economic history, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Start with the political fact, because it is the one the rest of the world has largely failed to register. India is a large parliamentary democracy, federal in structure, in which communist parties have won free elections and formed governments at the level of the state again and again across seven decades. One communist-led front held a single state continuously for well over three decades, an unbroken run of electoral victories without parallel in the history of democratically elected communist government anywhere. Governments led by communists took office through elections, implemented programmes, lost elections, and handed power to their opponents in the ordinary constitutional way. Whatever else that record means, it means that the Indian case is the world's principal test of a question Marxist theory posed but rarely faced in practice: what does a communist party do with state power when it must win that power repeatedly, share a federation with a hostile centre, and govern inside an economy it does not control? The sections that follow take the record first, then the thought.

## Marxism in India at a glance: two histories, one tradition

The canonical definition is worth stating plainly before the complications begin. Marxism in India comprises the communist political movement, which has governed several states through elections, and a substantial intellectual tradition in history, economics and social theory that refitted Marxist categories, patiently and in public argument, for a caste ordered and overwhelmingly agrarian society remade over two centuries of colonial rule. Every clause of that definition carries weight. Governed through elections distinguishes the Indian movement from every communist party that ruled by other means. Adapted signals that the categories did not arrive as finished doctrine and survive unchanged; they were tested against a caste order that class analysis had no ready drawer for, against a peasantry whose differentiation was contested in print for two decades, and against a colonial economy whose distortions forced a rethinking of how exploitation travels across borders.

Two kinds of reader come to this subject with opposite handicaps, and a guide has to serve both. A reader outside the country typically knows the names of no Indian communist and has never heard of the field's central debate; what such a reader needs first is the anomaly, the sheer strangeness of an elected communism, established without condescension. A reader inside the country knows the parties from the newspapers and the ballot paper, may have voted for or against them, and needs something else entirely: the analysis underneath the familiarity, the reasons the movement took the shape it did and the arguments its intellectuals conducted when the headlines were elsewhere. The design of this article assumes both readers are present. Political narrative is linked rather than retold where a companion article carries it in full, and analytical material is built up from the questions the politics itself generated.

Why did the tradition root itself so unevenly, flourishing in a handful of states while remaining marginal across most of the country? Why did a movement organised for revolution spend its longest energies on the administration of single provinces inside a federation? And why did its most original theoretical work concern, of all subjects, the classification of agricultural relations in the decades after independence? These are the questions that give the subject its shape, and they recur through every section below. A reader who keeps them in view will find that the parties, the historians, the economists and the social theorists in the streams table are not four separate topics stapled together. They are four answers, in different registers, to one problem: how a doctrine formed in industrial Europe was to comprehend, and then to act upon, a civilisation whose hierarchies were ritual as much as economic and whose countryside was the decisive terrain.

### Why is the Indian case missing from the international literature on communism?

Partly because the comparison embarrasses both camps of the usual argument: it fits neither the story of communism as imposed rule nor the story of its total failure at the ballot box, so general histories quietly drop it. Language, distance and the regional organisation of scholarship did the rest.

A further reason deserves its own sentence. The international literature on communism was written largely around the states that claimed to embody it, and India never claimed to be a socialist state; its communists were a permanent, legal, quarrelsome opposition inside a democracy, which made them illegible to a literature organised by regimes. The anomaly is therefore doubly hidden: unusual in itself, and filed under the wrong heading by the scholarship that might have publicised it. Recovering it is this article's first task.

Each row conceals a shelf of disputes, and the rows also disagree with each other about what the tradition is. Party documents treated theory as an instrument of organisation, produced for congresses and campaigns. University scholarship treated the same doctrines as hypotheses to be tested against archives and field data, and was willing to return verdicts the parties disliked. Between those poles the tradition developed its characteristic texture: an unusual traffic between pamphlet and monograph, between the resolution and the research paper. Indian Marxism was never only academic, and never only agitational, and much of its distinctiveness lies in the friction between the two callings sharing one vocabulary.

### How should a reader use the streams table?

As a map rather than a ranking. Fix the four questions first, since each stream is defined by the problem it would not abandon, then attach names to questions, and only afterwards follow any single row into its companion article. Readers who start from names alone memorise a directory; readers who start from questions acquire the field.

Notice, too, what the table leaves out, because the omissions mark live disputes. Trade union organisation, the cultural front, the women's movement within the left, and the insurgent current that broke from parliamentary politics all shaped the tradition and none receives a row; each is treated where the series gives it room, and each would complicate the period column of the party row if forced into the grid. A table is a wager that clarity bought by exclusion repays its cost, and this one is offered in that spirit: as scaffolding for the argument, not as the building.

## The democratic anomaly

The namable claim of this article can now be stated in full. The democratic anomaly holds that India is the only large parliamentary democracy in which communist parties have won repeated free elections and governed for decades, which makes it the world's principal test case for what a communist party does with state power under constitutional constraint, and that this case is almost entirely absent from the international literature on communism. Each element of the claim does work. Only large parliamentary democracy excludes the small European parties that joined coalition governments without ever leading them for long. Repeated excludes the single wartime or transitional episode. Governed for decades excludes the brief ministry that falls before it can act. Under constitutional constraint names the condition that makes the case theoretically interesting: these governments operated inside a constitution that protected private property, under courts that could strike their laws down, within a federation whose centre was usually held by their opponents, and before electorates that could dismiss them, and did.

Set the anomaly against the standard narrative of twentieth-century communism and its force becomes plain. In the familiar story, communist parties took power by revolution, civil war or foreign imposition, and then abolished the electoral mechanism that might have removed them. Parties that chose the parliamentary road, in that story, either dwindled into social democracy or were destroyed by the reaction their success provoked. The Indian record refuses both branches of the dilemma. The parties that govern Indian states have retained their names, their programmes and their self-understanding as communist organisations across decades of electoral politics, and they have accepted dismissal by voters as a normal event rather than a catastrophe to be prevented by any means. A tradition that can lose an election and survive it has solved, in practice, a problem that the classical theory of the party never seriously addressed.

The anomaly also sharpens a question for democratic theory rather than for Marxism alone. Voters in the states concerned were not, in the main, voting for a transition to socialism, an outcome no state government could deliver in any case. They were voting, on the evidence of the scholarship, for a combination of land reform, administrative honesty, welfare provision and dignity for social groups that other parties had governed over without governing for. The communist parties became, in those places, the credible vehicle for demands that were reformist in content and radical in implication, since meeting them required confronting the landed and the locally powerful. Whether that vehicle deserves the name communist is precisely the objection taken up at full strength in a later section. For the moment, register the anomaly in its cleanest form: nowhere else did parties of this family hold governmental power so long, so often, and so entirely by consent of the governed.

### Has any communist party governed democratically in India?

yes, repeatedly. Communist-led governments have been elected in several Indian states since the 1950s, and one state was governed by a communist-led front continuously for thirty-four years, which is the longest democratically elected communist government anywhere in the world. Those governments took office through elections and, in most cases, later left office through them.

The snippet answer above is the factual spine of the article; everything after it is interpretation. Note the modesty of the closing phrase, since the record includes one dismissal by the central government, in Kerala in 1959, which belongs to the history of Indian federalism as much as to the history of communism. Even that episode underlines the anomaly rather than dissolving it: the dismissed ministry's party returned to contest elections, won office again in the same state within the decade, and treated the ballot as the arbiter throughout. The anomaly is not that Indian communists were never obstructed. It is that obstruction never converted them, institutionally, into anything other than an electoral party with a revolutionary inheritance.

## The governing record: what electoral communism did in office

The record begins in Kerala in 1957, when the first communist-led ministry in the country took office in a state election held within a decade of independence, under a ministry headed by E. M. S. Namboodiripad. Its programme announced the template that later governments would adapt: land reform aimed at tenancy and landlordism, an education bill aimed at the private managements that dominated schooling, administrative reform, and a rhetoric of governing for the labouring poor. The centre dismissed the ministry in 1959 after a mass agitation against its education and land measures, and the dismissal became a founding grievance of Indian federalism. Yet the pattern was set. Communist-led governments returned to office in Kerala repeatedly across the following decades, alternating with Congress-led fronts in a rhythm of competitive politics so regular that the state became the standard Indian example of two-front alternation.

West Bengal supplies the anomaly's longest chapter. A Left Front dominated by the larger of the two communist parties won the state in 1977 and then kept winning, election after election, until 2011, a tenure exceeding three decades in a state of tens of millions. The front's early programme concentrated on the countryside: registration of sharecroppers to secure their tenure, distribution of vested ceiling-surplus land, and the revival of elected village councils, the panchayats, as instruments of local administration and political organisation. In its later decades the same front attempted an industrial revival and collided with the agrarian base its earlier reforms had created, a collision whose tensions the companion article on Bengal examines in full. Tripura, in the north-east, completes the trio of durable cases: a Left Front governed there for long stretches across the closing decades of the century and beyond, in a small state where the movement had fused with tribal and refugee grievances in distinctive ways.

What did these governments control, and what lay beyond their reach? A state government in the Indian federation administers land, agriculture, education, health, police and much of welfare, which is not a small estate; land reform, the single most consequential redistributive instrument available anywhere in the system, sits squarely within state competence. Against that, the commanding heights of the economy, currency, banking, foreign trade, heavy industry licensing for much of the period, and the fiscal transfers on which poor states depend, belong to the centre. A communist government in Kolkata or Thiruvananthapuram therefore governed the social relations of the countryside and the services of the welfare state while the economy around it remained capitalist and nationally directed. That division of powers is not an excuse invented after the fact. It is the constitutional architecture within which every judgement of the record has to be made, and it structures the strongest objection as much as it structures the defence.

### What did communist governments in Indian states actually control?

Land, agriculture, education, health and local administration, which placed tenancy, land redistribution, schooling and primary welfare inside their reach. Currency, banking, trade and the fiscal transfers poor states depend on stayed with the central government, so the commanding heights of the economy remained outside any single state's ministry from the start.

The everyday content of the record matters as much as its constitutional frame. Registration of sharecroppers in Bengal gave a class of cultivators who had worked land at the landlord's pleasure a recorded, heritable right to remain and a legally fixed share of the harvest, and it was carried out through camps and local organisation rather than by decree from the capital. Panchayat elections, held regularly when many states let them lapse, carried political participation into villages that the older order had governed through caste and landholding elites. In Kerala, the communist-led governments' reputation rested on public health and education networks, on a public distribution system for food that reached further than most, and later on a people's planning campaign that devolved a large share of plan funds to local bodies. None of this abolished capitalism in a single district. All of it altered who held power locally, which the tradition itself has always counted as a material change and not a cosmetic one.

A balanced account must also record the limits that voters themselves eventually enforced. Long incumbency bred the pathologies of any machine politics: the fusion of party and administration, the disciplining of dissent inside the movement's own constituencies, and in Bengal a growing distance between a government courting industrial investment and the rural electorate its land reforms had enfranchised. The fronts lost, when they lost, for recognisable reasons of governance as much as of ideology, and their acceptance of those defeats closes the argument of this section. The record is neither a triumph to be celebrated nor a fraud to be exposed; it is the only sustained evidence available anywhere of what this kind of party does with democratic power over generational time, and it deserves to be read with the care due to evidence that cannot be replicated.

## Why the movement rooted in some states and not others

Communist strength in India was always regional, and the geography demands explanation because it refutes the lazy assumption that poverty alone recruits for the left. Several of the poorest regions of the country never developed a durable communist presence, while Kerala and Bengal, distinctive rather than simply destitute, became the movement's heartlands. The scholarship points to a conjunction of conditions. Agrarian structures mattered: where tenancy was widespread, rents heavy and landlordism visibly parasitic, a politics of land had material to work with, and both heartlands carried histories of tenancy agitation that predated the parties themselves. The colonial land settlements had created different countrysides in different provinces, and the parties grew where the settlement had left a landlord class exposed and a tenant class organisable.

Organisation mattered as much as grievance. In Kerala, the movement grew inside the nationalist mobilisation itself, as socialist and then communist organisers built the trade unions, reading rooms and peasant associations of Malabar and Travancore into a durable infrastructure of everyday allegiance; a library movement, a theatre movement and a network of cooperatives carried the politics into ordinary life. In Bengal, the party inherited the aura of the freedom struggle's most militant wing, built strength among industrial workers in the jute belt and among refugees from the east who arrived with grievances the state seemed unable to answer, and sank roots into the sharecropping countryside of the north and west. Tripura's movement fused communist organisation with the land and identity claims of its tribal population. In each case the party became, over decades, a feature of the social landscape rather than an import competing for attention at election time.

Social reform histories form the third strand of the explanation. Kerala's communist growth followed, and partly absorbed, powerful anti-caste movements and a princely state tradition of public education and health provision, so that egalitarian politics had a vernacular long before the party codified it. Bengal's educated middle class, the bhadralok, supplied the movement with organisers and intellectuals out of proportion to its size, and a crisis of that class under colonial economic change gave radical politics a hearing in the universities and the professions. Contrast the regions where landlord power fused with caste authority and nationalist organisation early enough to absorb peasant discontent into a Congress machine, or where politics organised around religious community before class appeals could consolidate, and the patchwork of the left's map becomes intelligible. The lesson the tradition drew from its own geography was sobering: there was no all-India agrarian structure and therefore no single agrarian politics, only a federation of countrysides to be studied one by one. Its best scholarship took the lesson seriously, which is why the agrarian debate described later in this article proceeds village study by village study.

National power, by contrast, never came within reach, and the reasons are structural rather than accidental. A movement concentrated in three states, two of them linguistically and culturally distinctive, faced a first-past-the-post system that rewards geographically concentrated support with state victories but converts dispersed national minorities into almost nothing at the centre. Governing states, moreover, consumed the movement's best organisers in administration, while the compromises of office blurred the profile a national opposition needs. The parties influenced national politics at high points, through the wider left's parliamentary presence and through their role in coalition arithmetic in hung parliaments, but influence is not power, and the distinction is one the tradition's own strategists debated without resolution.

### Why did national power stay beyond the movement's reach?

Their support grew deep in a few states and stayed shallow across the rest, and the electoral system converts concentrated depth into state governments while giving a dispersed minority almost no path to the centre. Administration then absorbed the organisers a national campaign would have required.

That answer belongs here in the body of the article rather than only among the closing questions because it defines the tradition's strategic horizon. A party that can plausibly win Bengal and Kerala but cannot plausibly win the country inhabits a different strategic world from a party contesting national power, and Indian communist strategy, with its recurring debates about alliances, fronts and the use of state governments as showcases, was made inside that horizon. The full narrative of splits and campaigns belongs to [the history of the communist movement in India](/2017/12/15/communist-movement-india-history/), which this guide links rather than repeats.

## The parties as organisations: formation, split and afterlives

The organisational stream begins in the 1920s, when a communist party was founded in the conditions of colonial illegality, shaped by emigre organisers, Comintern directives and small circles of trade unionists and nationalists at home. M. N. Roy, the movement's most famous founding figure, carried Indian communism into the inner debates of the international movement before breaking with it, and his trajectory, from Comintern emissary to independent radical humanist, prefigures the tradition's recurring pattern of brilliant heresy. Through the following decades the party built peasant and union fronts, endured bans and imprisonments, participated in the freedom struggle with a record its critics and defenders still dispute, and emerged at independence as a significant but regionally uneven force. The detailed chronology, including the contested founding dates each successor party claims, is laid out in the companion history; what a concept guide must fix is the structure of the inheritance.

The inheritance divided in 1964, when the party split into two organisations that persist as the principal communist parties of the country. The split had international triggers, in the Sino-Soviet dispute, and domestic ones, in arguments over the character of the Indian state, the attitude to take toward the ruling Congress, and the lessons of the Kerala dismissal. The larger successor organisation, which would dominate the Left Fronts of Bengal and Tripura and share the Kerala alternation, defined itself against what it saw as revisionism on one side and adventurism on the other. A further rupture at the end of the decade, born of the Naxalbari uprising of 1967, produced the revolutionary current that rejected parliamentary politics altogether; that current and its descendants are treated in their own article on [Naxalism and the Maoist current](/2018/03/15/naxalism-maoist-current-india/). Later formations, splinters and reunifications multiplied the alphabet of the Indian left without changing its basic geography.

Two features of this organisational history matter for everything else in the guide. The first is longevity under legality: for most of their existence the main parties have operated openly, publishing newspapers, contesting elections, running unions and cultural fronts, and maintaining publishing houses and research institutes, an institutional depth rare among communist parties outside power anywhere. The second is the federalisation of the movement's imagination. Because power was available at state level and not beyond, party strategy came to be written state by state, and the Kerala and Bengal experiences diverged enough that each generated its own internal literature, its own heroes and its own cautionary tales. When scholars in the tradition asked what Indian Marxism was, the honest answer had to be plural: a family of state-level political practices bound by a common doctrine, a common publishing world and a common set of arguments.

### What separates the party tradition from the academic tradition?

The parties wrote theory as an instrument of organisation, for congresses, campaigns and cadre education, and judged it by whether it guided action. The universities wrote it as inquiry, judged by evidence and argument, and their verdicts repeatedly discomforted the party documents that shared their vocabulary and their ancestors.

Hold that distinction gently, because the personnel overlapped. Party intellectuals held university posts; academics drafted party resolutions; the same journals carried both kinds of writing. The tension was therefore internal to many individual careers, and it produced a literature more self-critical than the output of either a pure apparatus or a pure academy usually manages. It also produced a standing argument about who owned Marx in India, which the mode of production debate turned into the field's finest public performance.

## The mode of production debate: the field's central argument

Every intellectual tradition has one argument that concentrates its method, and for Indian Marxism that argument concerned the character of agriculture in the decades after independence. The setting requires a moment's reconstruction. Independence and the land reforms of the 1950s had abolished the great intermediary tenures of the colonial period, the zamindaris, without equalising the countryside. What remained was a mosaic: rich peasants hiring labour and selling surpluses, small proprietors working family land, tenants and sharecroppers under landlords who were also moneylenders and traders, and a vast class of labourers with little or no land. A political movement whose theory told it that the identity of the ruling relations in agriculture would decide its strategy, its alliances and its programme needed to know what this mosaic was. Was Indian agriculture still, in the technical sense, semi-feudal, dominated by pre-capitalist relations of dependence? Or had it become capitalist, organised around wage labour, accumulation and production for profit? From the late 1960s through the 1980s, principally in the pages of the Economic and Political Weekly, economists, historians and party theorists argued the question with a rigour and stamina that made the exchange the Indian counterpart of the famous transition debate through which European Marxism had argued about the passage from feudalism to capitalism a generation earlier.

Understand what rode on the classification, because otherwise the passion is unintelligible. If agriculture was semi-feudal, then the landlord remained the principal enemy in the countryside, the tasks of the democratic revolution, land to the tiller, abolition of usury and landlordism, remained unfinished, and the correct alliance embraced the whole peasantry against a narrow class of parasites. If agriculture had become capitalist, the enemy had changed shape: the rich peasant and the agrarian bourgeoisie stood inside the village rather than above it, the rural poor faced them as wage labourers face employers, and a strategy aimed at landlords of the old type would mistake the target entirely. Classification also decided how to read the evidence of one's eyes. The same sharecropper could be a semi-feudal tenant bound by debt and custom, or a disguised wage worker in a labour market wearing older clothes, and the two readings implied opposite politics. The debaters were not classifying for pleasure. They were fighting over the identity of the adversary in the arena where most Indians lived.

### What was at stake in calling Indian agriculture semi-feudal?

Everything strategic. It fixed the chief adversary in the countryside, the tasks of democratic revolution still outstanding, and the alliance a communist movement should build among the peasantry. Call the relations capitalist instead, and the adversary, the alliance and the programme all had to be redrawn from the beginning.

The semi-feudal case found its sharpest formulation in Amit Bhaduri's 1973 study of agricultural backwardness in eastern India, published in the Economic and Political Weekly and debated for years afterwards. Bhaduri modelled a landlord who combined three roles in one person: lessor of land to sharecroppers, lender of consumption credit to the same tenants, and buyer of their output. Interlocked in this way, the landlord's income depended on the tenant's dependence. A more productive tenant, one who adopted new techniques and escaped the cycle of borrowing, would need less credit, surrender less output in interest and trade margins, and eventually loosen the landlord's grip altogether. The rational landlord of the model therefore had an interest in the tenant's backwardness, blocking innovation not from stupidity or tradition but from a clear-eyed defence of the relations that paid him. Bhaduri called the resulting compulsion on the tenant forced commerce: the cultivator was dragged into markets, not as a free seller choosing his moment, but as a debtor delivering his harvest against advances, in transactions that transferred his surplus without ever letting him accumulate.

Forced commerce did the conceptual heavy lifting of the whole thesis, and it deserves a slow reading. Ordinary commerce, in the capitalist picture, rewards the efficient and punishes the backward until relations modernise themselves. Commerce under interlocked dependence does the opposite: the more the tenant trades, the deeper his dependence runs, because every transaction is with the same counterparty who sets the rent, the interest and the price. Surplus leaves the village through three channels at once, and the tenant's exertion enriches precisely the relations that imprison him. On this account the persistence of backward techniques, tiny holdings and stagnant yields in the eastern countryside was not a failure of the market to arrive. It was the equilibrium of a system whose dominant figure profited from stagnation, and whose victims experienced the market as a trap rather than an opportunity. Pradhan H. Prasad, writing from Bihar in the same journal through the 1970s, supplied the case with its most persistent empirical defence, documenting the interlocking of land, credit and labour in the villages he studied and disputing, point by point, the evidence assembled against the thesis.

The semi-feudal reading also carried an account of the state and of politics. A countryside governed by such landlords would find its local state, its police, courts and revenue offices, woven into the same relations of dependence, so that formal land reform laws would be sabotaged in implementation by the very machinery charged with executing them. The reading therefore predicted, and claimed to observe, the systematic frustration of reform from below wherever a movement had not first broken landlord power organisationally. Eastern India, with its record of militant peasant struggles and its surviving landlordism, served the thesis as both evidence and indictment: evidence that dependence persisted, indictment of any strategy that trusted legislation to dissolve it.

### How did Bhaduri explain the persistence of agricultural backwardness?

Through interlocking. Where one figure was landlord, moneylender and trader together, his income flowed from the tenant's dependence, so innovation that freed the tenant threatened the income itself. Backwardness persisted because the dominant interest in the village was paid, reliably, to preserve it.

Critics of the model pressed on its generality, and their questions were fair ones. How much of the countryside did the interlocked landlord actually govern, in a country of small proprietors and regional variation? Did the model describe Bengal and Bihar while mistaking them for the whole? Could a landlord class so placed really veto technical change across decades, against seed varieties and irrigation that raised yields on the landlord's own supervised farms elsewhere? The defenders answered with regional specification, conceding variety while insisting that the eastern pattern was the politically decisive one. Already the shape of the whole debate is visible: a powerful model, empirically anchored in one region, tested against a subcontinent that refused to be one thing.

## The capitalist case and the argument over evidence

The opposing case began from a different village. Ashok Rudra, a statistician-economist of formidable polemical gifts, reported field investigations in Punjab and elsewhere in the Economic and Political Weekly at the start of the 1970s under a title that announced his conclusion as a question of existence: he went in search of the capitalist farmer and reported finding him. The figure Rudra described hired wage labour on a large scale, invested in machinery and inputs, produced for the market, calculated in terms of profit, and behaved, in short, as agrarian capital rather than as a rentier of the old type. Where such farmers dominated, Rudra argued, the language of feudalism misdescribed the present and misdirected politics: the tenant's dependence, where it survived, survived inside a labour market, and sharecropping itself could function as a form of wage payment adapted to supervision problems rather than as a feudal due. Rudra also attacked the semi-feudal thesis at its evidentiary root, disputing whether the tenancy and debt relations on which it rested were as widespread, or as binding, as its proponents claimed.

Utsa Patnaik gave the capitalist reading its most systematic form. Working with village data, she proposed to classify households by a labour exploitation criterion, measuring the extent to which a household hired in the labour of others against the extent to which it hired out its own, and she traced the differentiation of the peasantry into classes along that gradient. Her studies of regions in Haryana and elsewhere found substantial capitalist development: a class of rich peasants and capitalist landlords accumulating through wage labour, a middle peasantry squeezed between them and the market, and a swelling class of poor peasants and labourers for whom wage work was the main livelihood. On her reading, tenancy itself had changed character, increasingly taken up by substantial farmers leasing in land to expand capitalist operations rather than by dependents bound to a lord. The debate between her school and the semi-feudal school turned, at its best, into an argument about how to measure class in a countryside where the same household might cultivate its own plot, lease in another, and send a son out for wages in a single season.

### What evidence did the capitalist side of the debate put forward?

Field surveys of substantial farmers who hired wage labour, invested in machinery and produced for profit, together with household classifications showing the peasantry differentiating into accumulators and wage workers. On that evidence, dependence survived inside labour markets rather than above them.

Other voices complicated both poles, and the complications are where the debate earned its reputation. Jairus Banaji warned against identifying a mode of production with the surface forms of its labour relations: capitalism, he argued, had historically worn many costumes, including sharecropping and debt bondage, and the inference from archaic forms to a feudal essence was the debate's characteristic fallacy. His interventions pushed the argument toward the laws of motion of capital and away from checklist anthropology. Hamza Alavi proposed that colonial rule had generated something neither classical category fitted, a colonial mode of production in which metropolitan capital subordinated and distorted local relations without transforming them on the European pattern; the proposal drew on his celebrated analysis of the postcolonial state and gave the Indian argument a bridge to the dependency literature then flourishing elsewhere. Daniel Thorner, an earlier American student of Indian agriculture whose writings the debaters mined, had already cautioned that Indian agrarian structure was a built-in depressor of a peculiar kind, resistant to the feudal and capitalist labels alike. The debate's bibliography thus reached outward in every direction: to Lenin's study of agrarian capitalism in Russia, to Kautsky's agrarian question, to Chayanov's theory of the family farm, and to the Dobb and Sweezy exchange on the European transition, which supplied the template of a great classification fought out in a journal.

The methodological core of the argument is what lifts it above a regional quarrel, and contemporary readers neglect the Indian exchange largely because they have not been told this. How does an observer identify a mode of production? By the forms of surplus extraction visible in contracts, by the compulsion or freedom of the direct producer, by the destination of the surplus, by the system's dynamic, whether it accumulates or merely reproduces dependence? Each criterion, honestly applied, sorted the same villages differently, and the debaters knew it, which is why the exchange kept returning to first principles with an explicitness that classification debates rarely achieve. When Utsa Patnaik insisted on the labour exploitation ratio and Bhaduri on the structure of interlocked transactions, they were disagreeing about what kind of fact decides what kind of society one is in. That is a philosophical question wearing statistical dress, and the Indian debate remains one of the few places where it was argued at length, with data, by writers who also had parties and programmes depending on the answer.

Why did the exchange fade without a verdict? Partly because the object under study moved: commercialisation, mechanisation and the new seed and fertiliser technology of the Green Revolution altered tenancy and labour relations through the 1970s and 1980s, so that the semi-feudal label described a shrinking, if still substantial, territory. Partly because scholarship itself moved on, to questions of caste, gender, environment and the state that the classification had crowded out. And partly because the debate's political stakes cooled as the parties settled into the routines of state government and alliance management, for which the difference between semi-feudal and capitalist agriculture in a Bihar village was no longer the hinge of strategy. The exchange remains, nonetheless, the strongest claim Indian Marxism can make to an original theoretical contribution: a decade-long, data-carrying argument about the identification of modes of production, conducted in the open, in a journal any reader could buy.

### Why did the mode of production debate fade without a verdict?

Because its object moved beneath it. Commercialisation and new farm technology transformed tenancy and labour relations while the argument proceeded, scholarship's attention shifted to caste, gender and the state, and the parties' strategy no longer turned on the classification as it once had.

A verdict of a kind did emerge, though it belonged to no camp: the subcontinent was too various for a single label, and the future of the research programme lay in regional studies that specified relations district by district. The historians' parallel argument about feudalism in the early medieval period, taken up in the companion article on [Indian Marxist historians and their method](/2018/03/01/indian-marxist-historians/), proceeded on the same terms, hypothesis against evidence, and the two debates together taught a generation of Indian scholars a style of Marxist inquiry in which the category had to earn its keep against the archive and the survey. That style, more than any single conclusion, is the debate's bequest.

## The agrarian question beyond the classification

Behind the debate about labels stood the older agrarian question, the inquiry into what happens to the peasantry as capitalism penetrates agriculture, and Indian writers worked that inquiry with their own materials. The classical alternatives were Lenin's account of differentiation, in which the peasantry dissolves into a rural bourgeoisie and a rural proletariat, and Chayanov's account of the family farm, in which differentiation follows the demographic cycle of households rather than the logic of accumulation. Indian village studies found evidence for both processes in different regions and sometimes in the same village, and the effort to hold the two together produced some of the tradition's most careful empirical writing. The stakes were again practical. A movement organising the countryside needs to know whether the middle peasant is a dying class to be pitied, a rising employer to be opposed, or the swing constituency on whose allegiance rural politics turns, and the Indian literature's answer was characteristically regional: it depends on the land settlement, the irrigation, the caste order and the history of the local movement, in proportions that only fieldwork can settle.

The agrarian question also carried the tradition's argument about technology. The Green Revolution's package of seed, fertiliser, water and credit arrived in the middle of the classification debate and was read in opposed ways at once: as capitalist development vindicated, since it spread wage labour, machinery and market production; and as a deepening of dependence, since its inputs bound cultivators to credit and to state supply chains while its benefits pooled in the irrigated north-west. Regional inequality widened as the new technology favoured watered land, and the geography of agrarian prosperity began to diverge from the geography of communist strength in ways that complicated every simple story the movement told itself. Eastern India, the semi-feudal heartland, largely missed the first Green Revolution, which its theorists read as confirmation of their model and its critics read as evidence of the model's regional confinement. Both readings survive in the literature, unresolved in the productive way that keeps a research programme alive.

### What is the agrarian question in the Indian setting?

The inquiry into what becomes of the peasantry as markets, credit and new technology penetrate agriculture: whether cultivators differentiate into employers and wage workers, persist as family farms, or remain trapped in dependence on landlord, lender and trader, and in what proportions, region by region.

Land reform sits at the junction of the agrarian inquiry and the governing record, and the junction deserves a section of its own in a later part of this guide. For now it is enough to mark the distinction the tradition itself insists on: between reform legislated and reform accomplished. India's statute books filled early with tenancy acts and ceiling laws; their implementation varied with the balance of rural power, which is why the same legal text protected tenants in one state and gathered dust in another. The scholarly literature on land reform, much of it written within or beside the tradition, made implementation rather than legislation its object, tracing vested land from notification to possession and counting the evasions, the benami transfers and the partitions among heirs by which ceilings were dissolved before they could bind. That literature taught the movement its most durable lesson in political economy: the state is not an instrument that executes intentions, it is a terrain on which rural classes fight with unequal weapons, and paperwork is one of the weapons of the strong.

## Caste: the problem class analysis could not dissolve

If agriculture supplied the tradition's central debate, caste supplied its central embarrassment and eventually its most distinctive theoretical work. Class analysis arrived in India with categories cut for a society in which economic position and social rank roughly coincide; it met a civilisation in which rank was assigned by birth, sanctified by religion, enforced by custom and only loosely correlated with wealth or occupation. A landless labourer and a prosperous merchant might stand, in the ritual order, exactly where their grandfathers stood, and a poor priest might outrank the rich man who employed his sons. Any theory that proposed to explain Indian society, and any movement that proposed to organise it, had to say what caste was: a superstructure reflecting class relations and destined to fade as they changed, a parallel system of oppression with its own logic, or the very form that class relations took on Indian ground. The tradition's history on this question is a history of successive answers, each abandoned under the pressure of the evidence and of criticism from those who lived the hierarchy's lower rungs.

The early movement's answer, shared with much of the nationalist left, treated caste as a feudal survival, an ideological fog over real economic relations, which development and class organisation would dispel. Practice refuted the confidence faster than theory could adjust. Caste determined access to land, credit, water and work; caste associations mobilised voters more reliably than unions mobilised workers; and the labourers whose class position was clearest experienced their subordination as caste subordination first, in the well they could not draw from and the temple they could not enter. A misreading repeated in summaries of the tradition, that Indian Marxists treat caste as merely economic, describes the position serious work in the field abandoned early and spent decades correcting. D. D. Kosambi, the tradition's most original historical mind, had already proposed in the 1950s that caste be understood as class at a primitive level of production, a formulation that made caste a real structure of exploitation rather than a mirage, while still subordinating its history to the history of production. Kosambi's own historical practice, attentive to tribe, cult and kinship, was subtler than the formula, and later writers quoted the formula and practised something richer.

The correction deepened through the century's second half. Gail Omvedt, working between Marxism and the anti-caste movements, argued that caste oppression could not be derived from class relations and then dismissed, since it organised the division of labour itself, assigning hereditary occupations and stigmatising the labour of the lowest as polluting. Her work insisted that a materialism worthy of the name had to explain why the material order wore ritual dress, and that the movements of the oppressed castes were not diversions from class politics but one of its principal Indian theatres. Sharad Patil attempted a formal synthesis, reading the ancient texts against the grain to construct a Marxist account of the caste order's origins in the subjugation of peoples and the organisation of surplus, in a project he associated with the legacies of Marx, Phule and Ambedkar together. Such syntheses remain contested inside and outside the tradition, and the argument they belong to is conducted at full length in the companion article on [caste and class in Indian Marxism](/2018/02/15/caste-and-class-debate-india/), to which this guide defers for the detailed adjudication.

What a concept guide must register is the shape of the mature position the argument produced. Caste and class are treated, in the field's serious literature, as distinct structures that articulate: neither reducible to the other, each conditioning how the other operates, so that exploitation arrives wearing the dress of inherited rank and hierarchy draws material sustenance from control of land and work. The political consequences were as large as the theoretical ones. Communist parties that organised labourers without confronting the caste humiliations those labourers suffered daily found their class politics answered by caste politics at the polling booth, while movements that fused the two, organising agricultural labourers who were overwhelmingly of the oppressed castes, built the durable rural bases the governing record rests on. The debate with B. R. Ambedkar, who had delivered the most devastating internal critique of the Indian left's caste blindness a generation before the parties were ready to hear it, runs through this whole development as an unquiet conscience, and receives its own treatment in the article on [Ambedkar and Marx](/2018/02/01/ambedkar-and-marx/).

### Why could caste not be read off from economic position?

Because rank in the caste order is assigned at birth and sanctified by religion, enforced through occupation, marriage and access to common resources, and only loosely matched to wealth. A poor man could outrank his rich employer; a rich man could remain, ritually, his inferior for life.

The persistence of caste also disciplined the tradition's understanding of the state and of democracy. Universal franchise gave the numerically large oppressed castes a weapon the old order had never faced, and the politics of the later twentieth century, in which parties of the backward and oppressed castes captured state power in the north, posed the class framework a question it had posed itself too late: whether the democratisation of caste identity was a detour from class politics or one of its historical forms in India. The literature's better answers refused the disjunction. Caste mobilisation that redistributed dignity, office and resources was doing, in the idiom available to it, work that class politics elsewhere did in another idiom, while caste mobilisation that merely rotated elites left the structures of land and labour untouched. Judging which was which, case by case, became one of the field's standing tasks, and the streams table's fourth row, the social theorists, exists because that task generated a literature of its own.

## The historians: a school that rewrote the Indian past

The second stream of the streams table commands respect well beyond the tradition's borders, because Indian Marxist historiography permanently changed how the country's past is written. Its founding figure, D. D. Kosambi, was a mathematician who taught himself Sanskrit, numismatics and field archaeology, and whose Introduction to the Study of Indian History of 1956 proposed that the Indian past be read through the development of its productive forces and the relations of its production, using myths, coins, inscriptions and surviving tribal practice as evidence of social formations rather than as illustrations of dynastic narrative. Kosambi's India had no golden ages and no dark ones; it had formations succeeding one another as techniques, property and surplus extraction changed, and caste itself appeared in his pages as the fossil record of tribes absorbed into a class society. The book's manner mattered as much as its method: ironic, compressed, contemptuous of piety, it modelled a way of writing history in which the historian's questions came from a theory and the answers were hostage to the evidence.

A generation built on the foundation. R. S. Sharma's work on early India, above all his study of Indian feudalism, argued that the early medieval centuries saw a feudal formation arise from land grants to officials and priests, the subjection of the peasantry and the decline of trade and towns, a thesis that organised research for decades and attracted the full artillery of criticism a governing paradigm earns. Irfan Habib's agrarian history of Mughal India reconstructed the revenue system of an empire from its own documents, demonstrating how a centralised apparatus extracted the peasant's surplus through a land tax that consumed, on his analysis, a crushing share of the produce, and how the resulting pressure on the peasantry powered both the empire's wealth and its eventual crisis. Habib's later essays on the colonial economy and on the potentialities of capitalist development under British rule extended the same documentary rigour to the question of why industrial capitalism, so visibly possible, was systematically stunted. Around these figures a school formed, headquartered in Delhi and Aligarh, training research students, filling the Indian History Congress with its debates, and supplying Indian universities with a syllabus in which social and economic history displaced the chronicle of kings.

### What did Kosambi contribute to the study of Indian history?

A method and a licence. The method read myths, coins, inscriptions and surviving custom as evidence of productive relations changing through time. The licence freed Indian historiography from dynastic narrative and golden age piety, and made the material life of ordinary people its central subject.

The school's internal life was argumentative in the way this guide has come to expect. The feudalism thesis was contested by historians working in the same broad tradition, who disputed the decline of trade, the serfdom of the peasantry and the applicability of a European category to Indian materials; the controversy reproduced, in an earlier period and with Persian and Sanskrit sources, the structure of the mode of production debate in economics. Critics outside the tradition charged the school with economic reductionism and with forcing Indian evidence into imported periodisations, and the school's defenders replied that the imported categories were precisely what the evidence was invited to break. The mature assessment most historians would sign is that the school won its central battle: nobody now writes Indian history as a pageant of dynasties, and the questions of land, labour, revenue and caste stand permanently at the centre of the discipline. The detailed treatment belongs to the companion article on the historians; here the point for the synthesis is that the tradition's historiography is the part of Indian Marxism with the strongest claim to have conquered its academic field outright.

## The economists: planning, empire and the national economy

The third stream divided its energies among three problems: how a poor country should plan, how its countryside worked, and what colonialism had done to its economy. The planning problem engaged the tradition at the moment of independence, when the new state's Second Five Year Plan adopted a strategy of state-led heavy industrialisation whose intellectual architect, the statistician P. C. Mahalanobis, worked in dialogue and argument with economists of the left. K. N. Raj, perhaps the most influential Indian economist of his generation in this orbit, moved between the Planning Commission's world and the universities, insisting that the agrarian structure set the binding constraint on any plan: without land reform, investment programmes would founder on the countryside's distribution of income and demand. The tradition's economists thus carried the agrarian question into the heart of macroeconomic strategy, arguing that the classification of village relations was not a scholastic exercise but the premise of national policy, an argument that gave the mode of production debate its unusual public weight.

The colonial problem produced the stream's most far-reaching work. The drain of wealth, the proposition that Britain systematically transferred India's surplus abroad through the mechanisms of trade, taxation and salaries, had been formulated by Dadabhai Naoroji in the nineteenth century and absorbed into nationalist common sense; Marxist economists rebuilt it as analysis. Amiya Kumar Bagchi's study of private investment in colonial India demonstrated how the structure of British managing agencies, banks and state policy channelled Indian capital into subordinate grooves and starved indigenous industry, while his essays on underdevelopment placed India inside a general theory of how the world economy's centre organised the periphery's stagnation. Utsa Patnaik extended the drain analysis to the whole colonial period with estimates that later became famous in public argument, an argument the figures protocol of this series requires us to report as a scholarly finding rather than a datum. Prabhat Patnaik, working from the 1970s onward, connected the colonial analysis to the theory of imperialism and to macroeconomic policy in the independent republic, defending planning and demand management against the liberalisation consensus in a long sequence of books and in the journal Social Scientist, which he edited. Krishna Bharadwaj's studies of production conditions in agriculture brought classical political economy's value categories to bear on exchange relations in backward agriculture, showing how interlocked markets could be analysed as structures of surplus appropriation rather than as imperfections awaiting competition.

### What did the economists of the tradition add to the study of colonialism?

They converted the nationalist charge of a drain of wealth into documented analysis: of managing agencies and banks channelling Indian capital into subordinate uses, of fiscal systems designed to realise a transfer, and of agrarian structures whose constraints every national plan had to answer.

The stream's venue mattered. The Economic and Political Weekly carried the debate about agriculture; Social Scientist, founded in 1972, carried the theoretical and political economy work of the university left; and party publishing houses carried both into cadre education in several languages. An economics of this kind, simultaneously scholarly and programmatic, required institutions that would print long, technical, uncommercial arguments and readers who would follow them across years, and the Indian left built both. The contrast with the economics profession's subsequent migration to American journals and techniques is a standard lament of the tradition's elders, and like most such laments it is half a fair observation and half a refusal to notice what the new techniques made visible. What no side disputes is the fact that for three decades the most widely read economic argument in the country took place in venues the tradition created.

Planning's decline forms the stream's melancholy coda. As the republic liberalised its economy from the 1980s and decisively in 1991, the questions the tradition's economists had organised themselves around, the pattern of investment, the terms of trade between agriculture and industry, the constraint of the home market, lost their policy addressee, and the stream's centre of gravity shifted to critique: of liberalisation's effects on agriculture and employment, of financial globalisation, of the fiscal retreat of the state. Critique of a high order it remained, and Prabhat Patnaik's later work on imperialism in the age of finance gave the tradition a contemporary theoretical voice of genuine reach. But the shift from proposing to opposing changed the stream's sociology, a story the next sections' treatment of rival accounts must keep in view, since the market's own account of those same decades was being written at the same time, from the opposite premise, with the state's ear increasingly its own.

## Social theory, the national question and M. N. Roy

The fourth stream gathered around the problems the other streams generated but could not finish: caste, already treated, and the cluster of questions about nation, language, religion and community that Indian politics kept posing to a theory formed on European materials. Was India a nation, several nations, or a civilisation falsely unified by its rulers? The colonial state's creation, a single administration over a subcontinent of languages and peoples, was treated by the tradition's early theorists as the raw material from which a nation was being forged in struggle, and the demand for linguistic states, conceded by the republic in the 1950s, was absorbed into the left's programme as democratic recognition of nationality within a union. The communal question cut deeper. A theory that explained division by class interest had to explain why Indian workers and peasants so often killed one another as Hindus and Muslims, and the tradition's honest writers conceded that religion in India was not a mask interests wore but a material organisation of life, of law, marriage, charity and neighbourhood, which no exposure of its economic roots dissolved. The best social theory of the stream, in journals and in the work of scholars moving between Marxism and other traditions, treated community as a reality to be analysed in its own terms rather than an error to be corrected, and the argument over how far that concession should go remains open in the literature.

M. N. Roy haunts this stream as he haunts the party stream, because his questions outlived his affiliations. Roy had argued, against Lenin himself in the Comintern debates of 1920, that in the colonies the national bourgeoisie would compromise with imperialism and that the revolutionary weight would fall on the masses directly, a thesis whose confidence and error the century argued over. His later work, after expulsion from the communist movement and a passage through prison and independent politics, turned to the philosophical foundations of materialism and to a radical humanism that asked whether the tradition's economics had eaten its ethics. Orthodox historiography in the movement wrote Roy out; the subcontinent's intellectual historians wrote him back in, as the first Indian to force the international movement to think about colonial society in terms the colonial world supplied. A guide that must characterise every figure by formation and position, and none by present controversy, can place Roy cleanly: the founding generation's most original mind, whose heresies mark the points where Indian experience strained the inherited doctrine earliest and most instructively.

## The institutions of the field: journals, universities and publishing houses

Ideas of this durability require carriers, and the tradition built an institutional ecology that any account of its influence must map. At its centre stood the Economic and Political Weekly, a journal of record for the Indian social sciences, which published the mode of production debate, the planning controversies and generations of field studies, and which demonstrated that a weekly journal could sustain scholarly argument at a level monthly reviews elsewhere rarely matched. Social Scientist supplied the theoretical register, carrying translations, critiques of imperialism and the university left's longer essays. The Indian History Congress gave the historians an annual parliament where the feudalism controversy and its successors were fought in the open. Party publishing houses, above all the houses maintained by the two principal parties in Delhi and Kolkata, issued the classics and the contemporary literature in English and in the regional languages at prices students could pay, while the left bookshops of the university towns functioned as the field's informal distribution system.

The universities completed the ecology. Centres in Delhi, Kolkata, Hyderabad and elsewhere trained the historians and economists whose work fills this article, and the tradition's presence in syllabi meant that Indian students of history or economics met Marxist categories as a normal part of professional training, in a way that has no real parallel in the Anglophone world. That curricular presence explains a recurring datum of Indian intellectual life: scholars who are not Marxists nonetheless argue in a conversation Marxists framed, disputing the periodisation, the feudalism thesis or the drain analysis on terrain those works staked out. Presence in the syllabus is the quiet form of intellectual power, and the tradition held it in its core disciplines for two generations.

### What role did a weekly journal play in sustaining the field?

The Economic and Political Weekly gave scholars, party intellectuals and field researchers one shared arena with a fast rhythm, where a classification could be proposed, attacked with data and defended within a single season. It turned private doctrines into public arguments and trained its readers to expect evidence.

A field's institutions also decide its survival across political weather. The parties' decline in a region could empty its bookshops, but the journals, the university centres and the syllabus carried the intellectual tradition across the troughs of the movement's electoral fortune, which is why the misreading that equates Indian Marxism with its parties' vote shares misleads so completely. The intellectual presence proved larger and more durable than the electoral one, as the brief for this article insists, and the institutions explain the disparity: parties must win repeatedly or dwindle, whereas a literature, once installed in curricula and libraries, renews itself through the ordinary reproduction of scholarship. Where the work is published, then, is not a bibliographic footnote to the subject. It is a principal part of the explanation of the subject's shape, and it is why this guide treats the Weekly, the Congress and the publishing houses as actors in the story rather than as scenery.

## Land reform: the junction of the record and the theory

The section on the agrarian question promised a fuller treatment of land reform, and the promise should be kept, because land reform is where the governing record and the theoretical tradition meet and test one another. Begin with the distinction the scholarship insists on. The first generation of reform, in the 1950s, was national and legal: abolition of the great intermediary tenures, tenancy acts, ceilings on holdings. It was drafted by a nationalist government advised by economists of several schools, and its achievement, the destruction of the zamindari layer, was real. Its limits were also real, and they were located precisely where the tradition's analysis predicted: in implementation, where local officials, courts and legislatures, colonised by the landed, converted redistributive law into litigation, evasion and delay. The communist-led state governments of the later period are best understood as attempts to run the implementation gauntlet with a different instrument, organised political power in the village itself.

Kerala's land reforms, legislated by its early governments and carried through in amended form from 1970, abolished tenancy in law and gave hutment dwellers, the kudikidappukar who lived on tiny plots within other men's land, ownership rights over their homesteads. Bengal's Operation Barga, from 1978, took the subtler route of recording sharecroppers, bargadars, in the land records through village-level camps, giving them security against eviction and a statutory share of the produce without formally expropriating the owners. Vested ceiling-surplus land was distributed to the landless in parallel, in small plots whose economic viability critics questioned and whose political and social effect, a floor of independence beneath the poorest rural families, defenders stressed. The scholarly assessments, including those by economists sympathetic to the reforms, agree on the mechanism that distinguished these episodes from the national pattern: the governing party treated its village organisation as the enforcement machinery of the statute, substitutes for the neutral bureaucracy that the theory said did not exist. Where that organisation was dense, the law bit; where it thinned, the law slept in the same old way. Few episodes in the comparative history of reform illustrate the tradition's theory of the state more cleanly, or more uncomfortably for theories that expect administration to be impartial.

### What did land reform in the communist governed states change?

It converted paper rights into recorded ones where the governing party's village organisation enforced them: tenants secured against eviction, sharecroppers protected in their crop share, hutment dwellers made owners of their homesteads, and ceiling surplus land distributed to families who had held none.

Honesty requires the other ledger in the same paragraph. The reforms operated inside holdings structures that remained highly unequal by any comparative standard; ceilings were set and reset under pressure; the land distributed was often marginal; and the reformed countryside of Bengal settled into a smallholder agriculture whose productivity and investment problems became the next generation's debate. The tradition's own economists supplied much of this criticism, which is one reason the field's land reform literature reads as analysis rather than as commemoration. What the reforms indisputably changed was the distribution of security and standing in the village: the landlord's power to evict, to summon labour, to command deference, contracted, and the political consequences of that contraction, a rural electorate that could defy local elites, supplied the foundation on which the governing fronts rested for their long tenures.

## Kerala and Bengal: the two cases compared

The two durable cases repay comparison because they differ in almost everything except the label of the governing parties. Kerala's left grew through a society of high literacy, dense associational life and powerful anti-caste movements, in a state where land was scarce, holdings tiny and the economy increasingly sustained by education, services and the earnings of migrants abroad. Its governments' signature achievements were social: public health and schooling of a standard the rest of the country treated as a model, food distribution, and periodic experiments in decentralised planning that mobilised local volunteers in large campaigns. Bengal's left grew through tenancy struggle and refugee politics in a state that had been the subcontinent's industrial leader and spent the governing decades managing relative industrial decline; its signature achievements were agrarian and political, the sharecropper registration, the panchayats, the stabilisation of a countryside that had known famine and insurgency within living memory. Where Kerala's problem became the financing of welfare in a slow-growing economy, Bengal's became the revival of industry in a countryside its own reforms had made politically powerful.

The comparison disciplines the anomaly claim. What the two cases share is not a policy template but a political form: a cadre party with deep local organisation, governing under constitutional constraint, using state power chiefly over land, services and local institutions, and accepting electoral discipline over a long period. What they do not share warns against speaking of an Indian communist model in the singular. Exporting Kerala's welfare achievements without Kerala's literacy, associational density and remittance economy, or Bengal's agrarian settlement without Bengal's tenancy history, mistakes a conjuncture for a recipe, and the tradition's better comparative writing says so explicitly. The cases are treated separately, at the length they deserve, in the articles on [Kerala's communist experiment](/2018/01/01/kerala-communist-experiment/) and [the Left Front in Bengal](/2018/01/15/west-bengal-left-front-analysis/), and the present guide's business is the pattern they jointly establish for the theory of the whole.

Tripura, the third case, adds the dimension of the frontier. In a small hill state of tribal peoples and Bengali settlers, the movement's durability rested on its defence of tribal land against alienation and its organisation of the refugee poor, fusing the class programme with the protection of a people's place in their own territory. The case matters theoretically because it shows the tradition adapting, in practice, to a politics of indigeneity that its categories had not anticipated, much as the caste question forced adaptation in the plains. A theory repeatedly bent by the societies it organises is either degenerating or learning, and the judgement between those verdicts is one this guide leaves to the reader with the evidence assembled; the objection section will state the harsher verdict at full strength.

## Colonialism: the analysis that framed the rest

No part of the tradition makes sense without the colonial analysis, because colonialism was the condition every other question presupposed. Marx himself had written on India in the 1850s, in newspaper articles that combined a searing account of British destruction of the village economy with a notorious suggestion that British rule, by laying railways and dissolving the old order, was performing an unconscious historical task. Indian Marxists inherited both halves of that inheritance and argued over them for a century: the destruction was undeniable and documented in their own histories of deindustrialisation and drain; the progressive mission was rejected, increasingly, as the apologia it had always risked becoming. The mature position of the school treated colonialism as a structure, not an episode: a system that integrated India into the world economy as a supplier of raw materials, a market for manufactures, a source of tribute and a field for British capital, and that reshaped Indian classes in the process, retarding some, creating others, and leaving the independent republic an economy whose distortions were the starting point of every plan. The general theory within which this analysis moves is laid out in the series article on [Marxism and imperialism](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/), and the Indian contribution to that theory, the drain rebuilt as economic history, has been described in the economists' section above.

The colonial analysis also fixed the tradition's interpretation of the freedom struggle, the most sensitive ground in Indian political memory. The communist reading held that the national movement was a bourgeois-led movement of genuine mass depth, that its leadership sought a transfer of power that would leave property relations substantially intact, and that independence in 1947 was therefore both a real emancipation from foreign rule and an incomplete social revolution. Every term of that sentence was fought over, inside the movement most bitterly of all, since it implied judgements about allies, about the party's wartime and postwar tactics, and about the character of the state the movement bequeathed. What the guide can say neutrally is that the reading organised the tradition's historiography of the national movement, from Sumit Sarkar's studies of popular movements to Bipan Chandra's work on the nationalist leadership's economic thought, and that rival historiographies, liberal, Hindu nationalist and subaltern, defined themselves partly against it. Even its enemies concede the reading a coherence that made the argument worth having.

### How did the tradition judge the achievement of independence?

As a double event: a genuine emancipation from foreign rule, won by mass struggle, and an incomplete social revolution, since the transfer of power left land, capital and caste substantially where colonial rule had placed them. The whole strategy of the succeeding decades was built inside that doubleness.

## Peasant movements and the making of the rural base

The rural base on which the governing record rests was built in struggles that preceded the state governments by a generation, and two of them entered the tradition's permanent memory. The Tebhaga movement of 1946 and 1947 in Bengal demanded that sharecroppers retain two thirds of the harvest rather than surrendering half to the landlord, and spread through the districts of north and central Bengal with a speed that revealed how shallow the old deference ran. The Telangana struggle of the late 1940s, in the princely state of Hyderabad, went further, as peasants under communist leadership resisted the Nizam's feudal order and then the authority that succeeded it, redistributing land and governing villages in the liberated zones before the movement was withdrawn under military pressure. Both struggles were defeated or contained in their immediate aims, and both deposited the deposits on which later politics drew: organisers trained, villages politicised, and a demonstration, visible to every tenant in eastern and southern India, that the relations of the countryside could be confronted collectively and survive the confrontation.

The tradition's literature on its own movements deserves the same scepticism the field applies elsewhere, and its historians have not spared it. Party histories long wrote the struggles as the unfolding of a line, whereas the archival scholarship shows peasants pursuing their own grievances, sometimes ahead of the party, sometimes against its instructions, with caste, kinship and local enmity shaping the fronts as surely as any programme. The mature view in the field treats the movements as encounters between an organised doctrine and a countryside with its own politics, in which the doctrine's categories were educated by the encounter. That education is one source of the tradition's later sophistication about caste and region: it learned, in Tebhaga's villages and Telangana's, that the Indian peasant did not arrive as the blank proletarian of theory.

## What kind of state? The debate over the republic's character

A movement that contests elections inside a state must decide what the state is, and the tradition debated the class character of the Indian republic with an intensity outsiders find surprising until they see the strategic stakes. Party documents characterised the state, in successive formulations, as the organ of the bourgeoisie and the landlords, led by the big bourgeoisie, whose compromises with imperialism and landlordism explained both the republic's democratic forms and its social conservatism. Academic writers complicated the formula. Hamza Alavi's analysis of the postcolonial state, developed for Pakistan and applied widely to India, argued that the military and bureaucratic apparatus inherited from colonial rule was overdeveloped relative to the society it governed, and mediated among the propertied classes, metropolitan capital, landlords and indigenous bourgeoisie, rather than serving as the simple instrument of any one of them. Pranab Bardhan's political economy of the republic proposed a coalition of dominant proprietary classes, industrial capital, rich farmers and the professional salariat, whose competition for subsidies and rents explained the state's characteristic blend of ambition and paralysis. Each account carried a different strategy inside it: Alavi's implied a state with dangerous autonomy, Bardhan's a state bargainable with class by class, the party formula a state to be confronted as a bloc.

The debate mattered beyond strategy because the governing experience fed it evidence no other communist movement possessed. A party that runs state governments learns the state's grain from inside: which of its parts answer to elected ministers and which to the judiciary, the finance system, the police tradition and the centre's fiscal levers. The Bengal and Kerala experiences were mined, in the tradition's journals, for exactly this knowledge, and the resulting literature on Indian federalism from the left remains among the most concrete writing on how the republic actually allocates power. Later sections return to the state's limits when the strongest objection is heard; the point here is that the question what kind of state was not a scholastic preliminary for this tradition. It was the operating problem of its daily politics, debated with the documents of its own governments on the table.

## The wider movement: unions, women and the cultural front

The streams table excluded three theatres of the tradition's life, and a guide that excluded them from the text as well would misrepresent how the movement reproduced itself. Trade unionism came first historically: communist organisers built some of the earliest durable unions in the Bombay textile mills, the Bengal jute belt and the railways, and the great strikes of the interwar years gave the infant party its first mass presence. The union federations aligned with the parties remained, through the century, among the largest in the country, and the union hall, like the reading room, functioned as a school in which the categories of the theory arrived as explanations of the member's own working life. Labour historiography written within the tradition, on the mill districts and their communities, added a further correction to any picture of Indian Marxism as a purely agrarian affair: the industrial working class was small by European measure but strategically concentrated, and the movement's imagination was formed as much in Bombay's mills as in Bengal's villages.

The women's movement within the left posed the caste question's twin. Organisations of communist women, from the self-respect and mahila associations of the 1940s to the large women's federation aligned with the larger party, mobilised women as workers, peasants and citizens while arguing, sometimes against their own leaderships, that patriarchy was not a residue the revolution would sweep up unattended. The tradition's social theory absorbed the pressure unevenly, and the intersection of gender with caste and class became, in the later literature, one of the sites where the Indian field's characteristic move, refusing to dissolve one structure into another, was practised most rigorously. Feminist historians working in dialogue with the tradition rewrote the history of the national movement and of labour from the women's side, and the field's syllabi changed accordingly.

Culture carried the movement furthest beyond its membership. The Progressive Writers' movement and the Indian People's Theatre Association of the 1940s gathered writers, actors and musicians of genius, many of them not communists, into a cultural front whose songs, plays and films entered the permanent repertoire of Indian popular culture. A peasant in Bengal or a millhand in Bombay might never read a page of theory and still carry the movement's account of the world in a chorus learned at a street performance. Intellectual histories of the period credit the cultural front with naturalising the vocabulary of exploitation and dignity in the vernacular public sphere, a precondition of the electoral politics that followed. The tradition's enemies understood this perfectly, which is why its cultural workers were so often the first targets of repression; its historians understand it too, which is why the cultural front, though absent from the streams table's four rows, belongs in any honest anatomy of how the anomaly became possible.

## India in the world of Marxism

Return the case to the world, since the anomaly claim is finally a claim about the world's literature. The international movement's Indian entanglements began at its summit: the colonial debate at the Comintern's Second Congress in 1920, where Roy's supplementary theses stood beside Lenin's, made the Indian question one of the places where the doctrine of world revolution was first forced to specify itself for societies unlike Europe's. Thereafter the traffic ran both ways at unequal rates. Indian communists translated, adapted and sometimes quietly discarded the international movement's directives; the international movement, for its part, rarely studied the Indian experience it claimed to guide, and its general histories of communism allotted India a paragraph where the anomaly deserved a chapter. The same asymmetry marked the academy. European and American scholarship on Marxism proceeded for decades as if the tradition's only significant twentieth-century lives were Russian, Chinese and western European, while the second largest communist experience under democracy accumulated unexamined in journals its authors assumed nobody abroad would read.

The costs of the asymmetry were intellectual, not merely reputational. The Indian case carries evidence for questions the international literature argues about in the abstract: whether communist parties can operate pluralist institutions without ceasing to be themselves, whether land reform administered by an organised party differs in kind from land reform decreed by a developmental state, whether a doctrine of class can survive translation into a civilisation organised by birth. For each question the Indian record offers decades of documented experience, in English, in a scholarly literature of high quality. The field's marginality in world scholarship is therefore best explained by the organisation of that scholarship, its area studies divisions and its regime-centred habits, rather than by any deficiency in the material. This guide, written for a general reader, can perform one service against the asymmetry: to state the case in the terms the world's literature uses, so that the anomaly becomes visible to the readers that literature trained.

### In what sense is India the control experiment of twentieth-century communism?

It makes India the control experiment of twentieth-century communism: the one large case where the party variable was present for decades while the variables of coercion, one party rule and insulation from voters were absent. Whatever the Indian parties did cannot be explained away by the usual machinery of communist power.

The verdict the anomaly supports is deliberately narrower than the uses partisans make of it. The Indian case shows that communist parties could govern democratically, redistribute land and security, build welfare institutions and accept dismissal, within the limits of a federal capitalist state. It does not show that those parties found a road to socialism through state governments, a destination their own programmes located beyond anything a province could attempt, and it does not show that the experience is exportable at will. A test case establishes what is possible under specified conditions; it does not issue promises. Holding the claim at exactly that strength is what distinguishes the anomaly as an analytical instrument from the anomaly as a slogan, and the distinction governs the assessment with which this guide approaches its close.

## The rival accounts: how other traditions explain the same record

A guide earns its authority by stating the opposing explanations as their holders state them, and Indian development has been explained from at least four standpoints that owe Marxism nothing. Each is presented here at full strength, attributed to its tradition, because the Indian Marxist literature defines itself against them and cannot be understood in isolation from the argument.

The first is the liberal account of markets and reform. In the version developed by economists such as Jagdish Bhagwati and Arvind Panagariya, India's post-independence poverty was substantially self-inflicted: an interventionist state throttled enterprise with industrial licensing, import controls and punitive taxation, producing decades of the slow growth that Raj Krishna famously christened the Hindu rate of growth, while the East Asian economies that liberalised raced ahead. Growth accelerated, on the influential argument of Dani Rodrik and Arvind Subramanian, when the state changed its attitude toward business in the 1980s, and decisively after the 1991 reforms dismantled licensing and opened trade. Poverty fell as growth rose, which the liberal account reads as the central datum of the whole period: it was the market's release, not any class politics, that improved the material condition of the average Indian, and the communist governed states, taxing and regulating inside the same federation, either hindered that process or survived by quietly accommodating it. The tradition's economists answer that the liberal narrative confuses a growth acceleration with a development strategy, neglects the public investment and institutional groundwork the planning era laid beneath the later boom, and reads distribution out of the story. Both versions cannot be wholly right, and any student of the subject has to know that the argument, not the verdict, is the field's actual content.

### How do market liberals explain the same development record?

By the state's grip and its relaxation. Licensing, controls and autarky kept growth at the slow pace economists dubbed the Hindu rate; the pro-business turn of the 1980s and the liberalisation of 1991 released enterprise, growth quickened, and poverty retreated as output expanded.

The second account is institutional, and it cuts across the first. Its proponents, in the line of work associated internationally with the new institutional economics and in India with scholars of state capacity, hold that neither markets nor plans deliver anything without institutions: courts that enforce contracts, a bureaucracy that implements without capture, property rights legible enough to invest against, and a state able to raise revenue and spend it on purpose. India's uneven development, on this reading, tracks the uneven quality of its institutions across states and sectors, and its democratic politics, with its competitive pressure on governments to distribute patronage, systematically degrades the institutions growth requires. The account has an explanation ready for the anomaly itself: Kerala's performance reflects an unusually capable and literate society pressing any government, of whatever colour, toward service delivery, while the communist states' failures reflect the same institutional ceiling that binds their rivals. Class, on this view, is one interest among many, and the Marxist tradition's elevation of it into the master key is a category error that the comparative evidence of Indian states refutes, since states with similar class structures and different institutions diverge, and states with different class structures and similar institutions converge.

A third account, the most consequential in Indian political science, makes caste and community the master variables and class the derivative one. Kanchan Chandra's theory of ethnic parties argues that in a patronage democracy, where the state controls a large share of valued goods and voters have limited information, counting heads by ethnic category is a rational strategy for elites and voters alike, which explains the rise of caste parties without any reference to economic structure. Christophe Jaffrelot's account of India's silent revolution documents how the lower castes captured political power in the northern states through organisation and franchise, transforming representation while leaving the language of class marginal to the actual competition. M. N. Srinivas had earlier supplied the vocabulary, dominant caste and Sanskritisation, that taught generations to see caste as a political and cultural system in motion rather than as an economic residue. On the strongest version of this account, the Indian Marxists' difficulty was not tactical but ontological: they brought a theory of a society stratified by production to a country stratified by birth, and their regional successes occurred precisely where caste mobilisation had already been absorbed into class organisation by historical accident, in the anti-caste movements of the south and the peculiar class formation of Bengal. The tradition's reply, that the caste account cannot explain why some caste blocs press material demands and others do not, carries weight; but the burden of the exchange lies on the Marxists, and this guide has treated the caste problem at a length proportioned to that burden.

### What do scholars of caste politics claim that class analysis misses?

That political identity in India is counted in castes, not classes, and rationally so: where the state distributes scarce goods and information is costly, voters and elites organise by the categories that predict who will deliver. Representation can therefore be transformed, as the north's silent revolution transformed it, without class politics leading.

The fourth account is federal and comparative. It begins from the plainest fact of the Indian data, that development outcomes diverged dramatically among states sharing one constitution, one currency and one national policy regime, and it explains the divergence by state level politics and policy: the quality of a state's leadership, the coherence of its ruling coalition, its administrative inheritance and its political settlements. Scholars in this line, comparing Kerala with states of similar income, or Bengal's trajectory with Maharashtra's, distribute credit and blame among governments of every ideology and treat ideology itself as a weak predictor beside competence and coalition management. The Kerala model debate belongs to this account's terrain. Defenders of the model, among them Richard Franke and Barbara Chasin, argued that public action, organised popular pressure and left governments produced health and education outcomes at low income that income based theories could not explain. Critics answered that Kerala's foundations were laid earlier, by princely states that invested in schooling and health, by missionary activity, by matrilineal traditions and by anti-caste mobilisation, and that the model's economics rested increasingly on migration and remittances rather than on any productive transformation the left could claim. Amartya Sen and Jean Dreze, whose account of public action is adjacent to this debate rather than inside the Marxist tradition, credited the political mobilisation of the state's public, in which the left was central but not alone. The debate remains open in the literature, and a concept guide serves its readers by reporting it as open: the foundations were substantially pre-communist, the consolidation and universalisation under elected governments were substantially the left's work, and the proportions are argued by serious people on both sides.

None of the four accounts is a straw man, and the tradition's serious writers engage them as peers. What the Marxist account claims, against all four, is the connective tissue: that the distribution of land and assets explains why institutions were captured, why markets under planning and after it served the propertied first, why caste mobilisation took the material forms it did, and why state governments diverged along lines that track their agrarian settlements and movement histories. Whether that claim to connection is a strength or an imperialism of one variable over a plural world is the deepest methodological argument in Indian social science, and it will not be settled here. It is enough that the reader sees the Indian Marxist tradition as one contestant in a crowded explanatory field, distinguished by the length of its record, the depth of its institutions and the anomaly at its centre, rather than as a relic awaiting a verdict from outside.

## The strongest objection: social democracy under a communist label

Every claim this article has made now faces the objection the brief for it requires to be stated at full strength, and it will be stated in its strongest available form, attributed to the scholarly assessments that developed it, before any answer is attempted. Communist parties governing Indian states operated within a capitalist national economy and a constitutional framework. They taxed, borrowed and competed for investment like any other state government. They could not socialise the means of production, which lay outside their jurisdiction; they administered a welfare and regulatory state inside a market economy, protected by a constitution whose property clauses their own lawyers invoked. What occurred in Kerala and Bengal, on this assessment, was social democratic administration conducted under a communist label: competent, sometimes exemplary government of a reformist character, land reform partial, no socialisation anywhere, and a programme indistinguishable in its operative content from what a determined social democratic party would have attempted. The label survived, the critics argue, because it organised loyalty and distinguished the brand, while the content converged with the European social democracy that communist parties historically defined themselves against.

The assessment is associated, in the scholarly literature, with the comparative study of the Indian states, where Atul Kohli's work on the politics of poverty treated the Bengal Left Front as the subcontinent's most effective reformist regime, a status he explained by its organisation and discipline while classing its programme as redistributive reform within capitalism rather than as any transition beyond it. Pranab Bardhan's analyses of the Bengal experience similarly weighed real achievements in tenancy reform and rural democratisation against the constraints of a state government inside a national capitalist economy, and found the experience's meaning in the quality of its reformism. The objection draws strength from the comparison its proponents press hardest: set the Left Front's Bengal beside the social democratic governments of postwar Europe and the family resemblance is operational, in budgets, in instruments, in the limits each accepted. What the Indian parties added, the critics concede, was organisation in the countryside and a rhetoric of transformation; what they did not add, across more than three decades of continuous opportunity in one state, was any alteration in the property relations of the economy they governed.

### Where does reformist administration end and a distinctively Marxist government begin?

Its relation to property. Social democratic administration redistributes income and services while leaving ownership of land and capital where it stands. A distinctively Marxist government would move against the property relations themselves, and the critics ask where, in the Indian record, that movement occurred.

The answer the tradition gives, and which this guide reports without endorsing, begins by relocating the comparison. Land reform in the Indian setting was not an income transfer; tenancy abolition, sharecropper registration and homestead rights altered property relations in the one asset that structured rural life, land, and they altered the power relations through which every other village institution operated. Decentralisation to elected panchayats, staffed by the landless and the smallholders the reforms had secured, changed who staffed the state at the level where most citizens meet it. The defenders argue that judging a state government by its failure to socialise an economy it had no jurisdiction over applies a test no one set: the parties' own programme assigned state governments the tasks of democratic reform and the defence of the working people's gains, within a strategy that located socialisation at the level of the whole economy and the whole union. On that self-understanding, the record is not a failed socialism but a performed reformism, and the charge dissolves into a disagreement about what the parties were ever for.

The concession the brief requires, and honesty, come last in this section and stand without qualification. The constraint the objection identifies is real, and it is the central problem of the whole experience. A communist party that governs a province inside a capitalist federation discovers that the instruments of transformation lie elsewhere, in the centre it cannot win, while the instruments it does hold suffice for reform and defence. Over decades, reform and defence become the party's habit, its personnel's skill and its electorate's expectation, and the distance between the programme's horizon and the government's practice widens into the condition critics describe. Whether that condition is a refutation of the party's strategy or a demonstration of the constitutional problem any such strategy must solve is, this guide judges, the genuine open question of the Indian experience, the one the anomaly poses to the world. The objection has been given its full strength here because the tradition's own best writers, in the debates after the Bengal defeat of 2011 in particular, took it up themselves, and a literature arguing with its own hardest critics in print is a literature still alive.

One further turn of the screw should be recorded, since the critics press it and the defenders have no settled reply. If the content was reformist, the rhetoric of the parties continued to promise transformation, and the gap between promise and practice was borne by the cadre and the voter, who were asked to sustain a revolutionary identity on a diet of municipal achievement. Some of the tradition's internal literature treats that gap as the seed of the defeats that eventually came, when electorates weary of the machine asked what the label now meant. Others treat the identity as the very thing that kept the reform honest, the horizon that prevented a reformist government from becoming merely a management team. The argument is internal, continuing, and unresolved, and the assessment section gathers it with the rest of the evidence.

## Misreadings to correct

Five errors recur in summaries of this subject, in the press and in textbooks, and the guide corrects them in one place. The first holds that Indian communism is a single movement. It never was after 1964, and arguably never was before: two major parties, a revolutionary current outside parliament, and a penumbra of formations divide the inheritance, dispute its meaning and compete, sometimes bitterly, for the same constituencies. The second error makes the movement's strength national. Its strength was concentrated, in a few states whose specific agrarian and social histories this article has described, and its weakness across most of the country is as much a datum as its strongholds. The third error, the most consequential for a general reader, holds that Indian Marxism produced no theory of its own and merely applied imported doctrine. The mode of production debate, the feudalism controversy in historiography, the rebuilt drain analysis and the caste and class literature are the refutation: this was a tradition that generated its own problems and argued them at a level the international field has yet to absorb.

The fourth error reports that Indian Marxists treat caste as a merely economic phenomenon, destined to dissolve into class as development proceeds. The correction has a history worth repeating: an early economism did hold something like that view, and the serious literature of the tradition abandoned it under criticism, much of it from within the wider left and from the anti-caste movements, settling instead on the articulation of caste and class as distinct, interacting structures. Attributing the abandoned position to the mature field is the kind of error that survives because it flatters every party to the quarrel except the record. The fifth error equates the tradition with its electoral presence and reads a lost election as the tradition's obituary. The institutions section has made the reply: journals, publishing houses, university centres and syllabi carried the intellectual tradition across every electoral trough, and its presence in Indian intellectual life has been larger, and more durable, than its presence in legislatures. A reader who holds the five corrections together possesses the guardrails within which the detailed literature can be read without the standard accidents.

## Where to start: a reading route through the tradition

New readers ask where to begin, and the field rewards starting from its classics of exposition rather than from party documents or polemics. Begin with Kosambi's introduction to the study of Indian history, still the most bracing single demonstration of the method, a book that teaches its reader to ask of any period what was produced, by whom, and who took the surplus. Continue with Bhaduri's essay on semi-feudalism and the replies it provoked in the Economic and Political Weekly, the quickest route into the mode of production debate in its original voices, and with Irfan Habib's essays on the colonial economy for the drain analysis in its mature form. For the movement's own self-understanding, the memoirs and histories written by participants should be read against the scholarly histories, since the distance between the two is itself one of the field's lessons. The exportable summary, for readers whose interest is comparative, is the pair of companion studies this guide has relied on: the Kerala and Bengal articles for the governing record, and the historiography article for the school that rewrote the past.

A word about order, since the route can also be run backwards with profit. Readers who start from the present shape of Indian politics and work back through the state cases to the debates will find the theory illuminated by the practice at every step, while readers who start from Kosambi and the classification debates will find the politics legible as applied theory. Either direction respects the article's central contention, that the two histories explain one another. What a reader should not do is start from the caricatures, either the hostile summary in which the tradition is a foreign implant that failed, or the commemorative one in which it is an unblemished saga of the people's cause. The literature itself, contentious, evidentiary and self-critical, is more interesting than both, and it is unusually accessible: the central journal is in every serious library, the classics are in print in cheap editions, and the debates were conducted in English for any reader to audit.

### Where should a newcomer's first week of reading be spent?

On the method in action. Kosambi for history, Bhaduri and his critics for the agrarian debate, one scholarly history of the movement for the narrative, and the Kerala and Bengal studies for the record in office. A week across those four shelves orients a reader in the whole field.

## The subaltern challenge: the critique that came from inside the house

No account of the intellectual tradition is complete without the rebellion that arose in its own seminar rooms. In the early 1980s a group of historians associated with Ranajit Guha launched the Subaltern Studies project, with a manifesto charging that the historiography of Indian nationalism had been dominated by elitism, colonialist and bourgeois nationalist alike, and that the politics of the subaltern classes, peasants, workers, tribals and the lower castes, constituted an autonomous domain that elite narratives, including the narratives of the organised left, had failed to register. Guha's own major work reconstructed peasant insurgency in colonial India from the records of its suppression, reading the counter-insurgency prose of the state against itself to recover the insurgent's consciousness. The debt to the Marxist school was visible on every page: the questions were its questions, the archives its archives, the category of the subaltern itself adapted from Gramsci. The rebellion was therefore a family quarrel of the most productive kind, and it changed the terms in which the tradition's history had to be written.

The quarrel's substance deserves statement at the strength its participants gave it. Elite historiography, Guha argued, treated the nation as the achievement of its leaders and the masses as material to be mobilised; the party histories of the left, for all their celebration of peasant struggle, reproduced the structure by measuring movements against the line of the organisation that claimed to lead them. Against this, the subaltern historians proposed that popular politics had its own idioms, religious, communitarian and kin based, which class categories translated badly and vanguard narratives erased. The subsequent volumes ranged across the whole terrain of the tradition's competence, caste, gender, communal violence, labour and the colonial state, and the project's later migration toward postcolonial theory carried the argument into the international academy with a force no previous Indian historiography had achieved abroad. Critics from within the Marxist tradition answered in kind. Sumit Sarkar, himself an early contributor, later charged the project with surrendering material analysis to a discourse theory that could no longer explain change, and with romanticising the subaltern's consciousness into an essence. Vivek Chibber's later polemic pressed the case that the subaltern theorists had misdescribed both capitalism and the interests of the labouring poor. The argument, like the mode of production debate before it, has no referee and no final whistle; its importance for this guide is what it reveals about the tradition's metabolism, its capacity to generate critics armed with its own methods and to be renewed by the wound.

### What did the subaltern historians charge against the tradition's own history writing?

That it was elitist in structure even when radical in sympathy: it credited the nation and its movements to leaders and organisations, and measured popular politics by its distance from the correct line, thereby erasing the autonomous idioms in which peasants, workers and tribals actually acted.

Notice how the episode bears on the democratic anomaly with which this article began. The international fame of Subaltern Studies accomplished, for the historiography, what the communist governments never accomplished for the politics: it placed an Indian product of the Marxist milieu at the centre of global scholarly conversation. The irony is structural and the tradition's historians are aware of it. The school's categories travelled because they were formed in argument against a metropolitan Marxism from a subcontinental archive, which is precisely the operation this guide has described in the agrarian debate, in the drain analysis and in the caste literature. India's contribution to the world of Marxist thought has repeatedly taken that form, a correction issued from the periphery that the centre first resisted and then absorbed, and the pattern may be the field's most generalisable feature.

A second internal front opened on the question of the nation itself. Partha Chatterjee's work on nationalist thought argued that anti-colonial nationalism, for all its defiance of empire, remained a derivative discourse, reasoning within categories the colonial order had supplied, and the argument implicated the Marxist historiography of the freedom struggle in the same derivation. The tradition's historians replied that the charge confused the vocabulary of a discourse with the interests it served, and the exchange refined both positions. What matters here is cumulative: by the century's end, Indian Marxist scholarship had been challenged on its treatment of popular consciousness, of nationalism, of caste and of gender by critics who had learned their trade inside its institutions, and it had answered, conceded ground, and continued. A field that survives its own best students is not in decline; it is in the condition the sciences call normal.

## The vernacular life of the tradition: languages, regions and translation

English carried the field's scholarship, but the movement lived in the languages of its regions, and any account confined to English misses the tradition's actual social depth. The classics of Marxism were translated, early and repeatedly, into Bengali, Malayalam, Telugu, Tamil, Hindi, Marathi and a dozen other languages, often by organisers working under ban or in prison, and the party publishing houses maintained translation as a permanent department rather than an afterthought. In Kerala, a dense culture of reading rooms and lending libraries, many of them movement built, made Malayalam translations of political economy and history part of ordinary village intellectual life. In Bengal, a Bengali Marxist literature of real sophistication, historical, philosophical and literary critical, grew beside the English one and sometimes ahead of it. The theoretical consequences were not trivial. Concepts acquire different resonances in different tongues: class in a language whose word for it carries caste associations, exploitation in a language rich in vocabulary for forms of customary extraction, each translation an act of interpretation that fed back into the tradition's self-understanding.

Regional variation followed the languages. The Andhra movement grew through the Telugu countryside's struggles against landlordism and the Nizam; the Maharashtra movement through the Bombay mills and a distinctive engagement with the anti-caste thought of the region; the Punjab movement through canal colony peasant grievances and the diaspora radicalism of the Ghadar generation. Each regional tradition maintained its own martyrology, its own canonical struggles and its own internal balance between class and community appeals, and the all-India parties functioned, in practice, as federations of these regional cultures united by programme and discipline. Historians of the movement increasingly write its history regionally for exactly this reason, and the concept guide's streams table should be imagined as drawn over a map, each stream running at different depths in different linguistic soil.

### Why did translation matter to the Indian tradition's theory?

Because it forced the categories to survive in languages whose vocabularies carried caste, kinship and customary extraction inside the very words for class and exploitation. Translation was applied theory: every rendering chose an equivalence, and the choices, argued over in the movement's journals, sharpened the concepts themselves.

## Generations: how the tradition renewed and changed itself

The tradition's century divides, for analytical purposes, into generations whose questions differed. A founding generation, formed in the 1920s and 1930s under colonial illegality, asked how a communist movement could exist in a colony at all, and answered with organisation: unions, peasant associations, cultural fronts and a party. A second generation, formed by independence and the first decades of the republic, asked how the movement should judge and use the new state, and answered with the electoral road, the land reform campaigns and the first state governments. A third generation, formed in the universities of the 1960s and 1970s, asked what Indian society actually was, and answered with the great scholarly constructions this guide has surveyed: the agrarian debate, the feudalism controversy, the drain rebuilt, and the beginnings of the caste and class literature. A fourth generation, formed after liberalisation, asked what remained of the framework when its policy world had dissolved, and answered variously, with critiques of the new economic order, with the subaltern and postcolonial turns, and with a historiography increasingly detached from any party's needs.

Periodising in this way clarifies the tradition's present shape without pronouncing on any living controversy, which this guide is barred from doing and does not need. The scholarly generations reproduced themselves through institutions that outlasted the political conjunctures, while the political generations reproduced themselves through organisations whose fortunes varied by state and decade. The two reproduction systems ran at different speeds, which is the deep reason the intellectual presence outlasted the electoral one through every trough. It also explains a feature of the field that puzzles newcomers: the coexistence of a sophisticated, self-critical scholarly literature with party documents that can read, by comparison, as catechism. The two registers answer to different reproduction systems, one rewarded for novelty and evidence, the other for continuity and clarity, and the tradition is unintelligible until both are seen operating at once.

The generations also bequeathed different Indias to one another. The founders' India was a colony to be liberated; the second generation's, a republic to be democratised in substance; the third generation's, a society to be deciphered; the fourth's, a formation inside a global capitalism whose centre of gravity had shifted toward Asia. Each bequest reframed the founding texts. Marx on India was read by the founders as a call to complete the destruction of the old order, by the scholars as an early, flawed draft of the colonial analysis their own work replaced, and by later readers as a document in the history of European thought about Asia, to be criticised as such. A tradition that can reread its own scripture at that range of angles is not suffering from an excess of dogma, whatever the justified criticisms of its political practice, and the rereading habit is among the habits this guide most recommends to students meeting the field for the first time.

## The Kerala model as an argument

Kerala's development experience attracted an international literature out of proportion to the state's size, and the argument over it is the best single doorway into the whole field's habits of mind. The puzzle, as development economists posed it, was the divergence between the state's modest income and its social indicators: literacy, life expectancy and infant survival at levels associated, in cross country comparison, with far richer economies. The Marxist and left literature proposed an explanation in terms of public action: land reform that broke the old agrarian order, mass literacy and health campaigns, food distribution, and above all an organised and demanding citizenry, formed by the movements this article has described, which pressed successive governments of both fronts to deliver services. Sen and Dreze gave the explanation its canonical non party form, crediting the politics of public action across the state's parties and movements rather than communism alone, and Franke and Chasin documented the model's local operation in a study village over time.

The critics developed three lines of answer, each of which the field's honest literature registers. The foundations objection holds that the model's preconditions, princely investment in education and health, missionary schooling, matrilineal property traditions among some communities and powerful anti-caste movements, predated communist government and would have told under any administration. The finance objection holds that the model's later decades rested on remittances from the state's workers in the Gulf economies, an external subsidy no policy created, and that its public finances ran chronic strain, with high commitments to salaries, pensions and welfare against a narrow productive base. The production objection holds that the state conspicuously failed to industrialise, that its labour relations and politics deterred investment, and that a development pattern strong in distribution and weak in production contains the date of its own exhaustion. Defenders reply that the foundations objection proves too much, since other regions enjoyed fragments of the same inheritance without the same outcomes; that remittances followed the education the model produced, so the subsidy was partly self-made; and that the production objection measures the model against an industrialisation standard its architects explicitly subordinated to human development. The literature's mature state, as this guide reported in the rival accounts section, is an unsettled argument with agreed facts, which is the condition of most serious arguments about development everywhere.

For the concept guide, the Kerala debate's function is diagnostic. It shows the tradition's characteristic strengths in a single case: the insistence on land and class as the substrate of social outcomes, the appeal to organisation as the mechanism, and the willingness to treat a state's whole history as the evidence. It also shows the tradition's characteristic vulnerability: a template temptation, the slide from a conjunctural achievement, produced by a unique inheritance and a specific movement, toward a model commended for export. The field's better writers police that slide themselves, and the assessment toward which this article moves will count the policing as part of the record.

### Did communism cause Kerala's social outcomes?

Not alone, on the literature's own showing. Princely reform, missionary schooling, matrilineal traditions and anti-caste movements laid foundations before any communist ministry; elected governments, pressed by an organised citizenry, universalised and defended the gains. The proportions remain the substance of an honest, unfinished argument.

## Religion and community: the framework's open file

The caste section treated the first of the two great non class structures of Indian life; the second is religious community, and the tradition's handling of it is the least settled part of its inheritance. The classical expectation, that communal allegiance would fade as class organisation advanced, failed in India as it failed elsewhere, and failed bloodily, in the Partition massacres that accompanied independence and in recurring communal violence across the republic's history. The tradition's serious social theory responded by abandoning the expectation in its simple form. Community, in the mature literature, is analysed as a material structure in its own right: it organises marriage and inheritance, charity and credit, neighbourhood and occupation, and it commands loyalties that economic interest does not dissolve because they are partly constitutive of the persons who hold them. A materialism that cannot say why would be a poor materialism, and the field's best work on communalism therefore attends to its political economy, the competition for state resources among community elites, the electoral arithmetic that rewards polarisation, the colonial enumeration that hardened community boundaries into administrative fact, without reducing the phenomenon to those mechanisms.

The practical politics followed the theory with a lag the literature does not hide. Communist parties organised across community lines where their movements were strong, and built durable multi community bases in Kerala and Bengal, but the movement's national weakness left it a spectator in many of the arenas where communal politics was decided. Its analysis of secularism, defended in its journals as the necessary condition of any class politics in India, remained one of its most consistent public positions, and the defence drew on its whole framework: communal division as the solvent of every solidarity the labouring poor had managed to build. Whether secularism as the republic practised it, an equidistance among communities administered by the state, or secularism as the movement conceived it, a politics that organises citizens across community on material demands, better describes the available road remains an open argument in the literature, conducted with the historical record rather than with the controversies of the moment, which this guide does not enter.

What the open file teaches a student is the field's rhythm of correction. On caste, an early reduction was abandoned; on community, an early expectation of dissolution was abandoned; on agriculture, a confident classification dissolved into regional specification. In each case the correction came from the tradition's own encounter with evidence and from critics who shared enough of its premises to be heard. The pattern is the strongest available answer to the charge that Marxist thought in India was a catechism applied to a country it declined to study. The opposite is nearer the record: it is a literature whose central doctrines bear the marks of repeated revision under Indian evidence, and whose unrevised remnants are visible precisely because the revisions around them show what revision looks like.

## What the Indian case contributes to Marxist theory

A concept guide should end its analytical work by saying what the case teaches the theory it belongs to, and the Indian experience makes four contributions that general accounts of Marxism have yet to absorb at their proper weight. The first concerns modes of production. European theory inherited its categories from a transition that could be narrated, however contentiously, as one formation succeeding another across a continent. Indian scholarship confronted formations laminated together: capitalist relations in one district, interlocked dependence in the next, family production persisting inside both, and a state whose revenue systems descended from precolonial apparatuses through a colonial refashioning. The debate that resulted taught the wider theory a discipline it still needs: a mode of production is identified by the relations through which surplus is extracted and the dynamic those relations impose, not by a checklist of institutional forms, and a social formation may house several such relations in articulated combination for generations without any of them being a mere survival awaiting dissolution. Later theorists of articulation, working on other continents, arrived at similar conclusions by other roads; the Indian debate had reached them, with data, in a weekly journal, by the middle of the 1970s.

The second contribution concerns the party and democracy. Classical theory bequeathed two images of the communist party: the vanguard of a revolution that would dissolve the old state, and, in the social democratic degeneration the communists defined themselves against, the party as an electoral machine administering capitalism. Indian practice produced a third thing that neither image contains: a party of cadre organisation and transformative programme that submitted itself, permanently, to electoral arbitration, governed where it won, accepted removal where it lost, and reproduced its identity across generations of that alternation. Whether the third thing is a discovery or a slow surrender is the argument of the objection section, and it remains genuinely open. What is not open is that the thing existed, at scale, for decades, and that theories of the party which cannot describe it are incomplete in a way their authors rarely noticed, because the case sat outside the literatures they read.

The third contribution concerns caste and the architecture of oppression. European class theory assumed that the relations of production organise social hierarchy more or less directly, and that other hierarchies are commentary. The Indian literature, forced by its evidence, built a more general instrument: an account of distinct structures of domination, each with its own mechanism and history, which articulate with one another so that the operation of each is conditioned by the others, without any being reducible to a mask of the rest. That instrument, developed for caste and class, turned out to be exactly what the wider theory needed for gender, for race and for community, and its Indian construction site deserves recognition in the general history of the method. The fourth contribution is quieter: a demonstration, across the historiography and the agrarian studies alike, of how a research programme renews itself by losing arguments in public. The feudalism thesis modified under criticism, the classification debate dissolving into regional specification, the economism on caste abandoned under fire from the movements the theory claimed to serve: in each case the literature's capacity to record its own defeats and continue is the phenomenon a philosophy of social science should want to explain, and the Indian field supplies unusually clean material for the explanation.

### What single sentence captures the tradition's distinctiveness?

It is the Marxism that evidence kept correcting. Forced to govern under a constitution, to classify a laminated countryside, and to face caste and community as structures no formula dissolved, it revised its categories in public and built institutions where the revisions could be argued with data.

## Strategy argued in the open: fronts, alliances and the road not taken

The parties' strategic literature forms a sub field of its own, and its recurring questions reveal the tradition's operating dilemmas more honestly than its commemorative histories do. Should the movement ally with the non communist left and democratic forces against its principal adversary, and at what price in independence? Should state governments be used as showcases of alternative administration, accepting the constraints of office, or as platforms of mobilisation against the centre, accepting the risk of dismissal? Should the party ever join or support a government at the centre in which it cannot set the terms? Each question generated documents, dissents and eventually schisms, and each was argued in the vocabulary of the theory: the character of the state, the stage of the revolution, the identity of the principal enemy. The most famous episode is also the most instructive. In 1996, when coalition arithmetic after a national election offered the larger party's Bengal chief minister the premiership of a united front government, the party's central committee declined the offer, a decision its most respected leader, Jyoti Basu, later described on the record as a historic blunder. The episode is now part of the settled historical record and the party's own self criticism, and it crystallises the anomaly's inner tension: a party organised to transform the state hesitating at the threshold of the only office from which the whole state could be addressed, because its doctrine told it the office, on those terms, would transform the party instead.

The alliance question had a class theory inside it, which the academic literature formalised. Alliances presuppose a judgement about which classes and parties can be trusted to carry a democratic programme against landlordism and monopoly capital, and the successive party documents read, to a scholarly eye, as applied versions of the agrarian and state debates: the characterisation of the big bourgeoisie as compromising or as progressively national, of the rich peasantry as ally or as adversary, decided the front's architecture each time it was rebuilt. When the characterisations changed, the strategy documents changed with them, sometimes more slowly than the scholarship that undermined the old wording, and the lag between the field's research front and its political documents is a documented feature of the tradition's internal life, criticised by its own intellectuals in its own journals. A reader tracing any single strategic formula through the decades will find the whole tradition in miniature: doctrine, evidence, institutional inertia and the occasional dramatic rupture, all in public print.

## The agrarian question's second life

Scholarly programmes rarely die; they change their questions, and the agrarian inquiry's evolution after the classification debate illustrates the pattern. Village studies continued, but their centre of interest migrated: from the identity of the mode of production to the workings of labour markets, the feminisation of agricultural work as men migrated, the caste composition of the new rural employers, the politics of water and electricity pricing, and the effects of the liberalised economy on small producers exposed to world prices. The surveys associated with the Foundation for Agrarian Studies, working in the tradition's empirical line, applied the old instruments, household schedules recording labour use, assets and incomes, to the new countryside, and found differentiation continuing, employment scarce and precarious, and the small peasantry surviving by combining cultivation with wage work in proportions the classical alternatives had both foreseen and missed. The semi-feudal question itself returned in altered dress whenever interlocked credit reappeared in new forms, in contract farming arrangements and in the advances of commission agents, and the literature's habit of reaching for Bhaduri's model in such cases, and arguing about whether it fitted, testifies to a model's proper afterlife: not as a verdict on a subcontinent but as an instrument for specifying a relation wherever it recurs.

Agrarian distress in the liberalisation era posed the tradition's economics its hardest test, and its response divided along lines this guide has met before. One wing read the distress as the predictable consequence of the state's retreat from credit, extension and price support, and of agriculture's exposure to volatile world markets, a reading continuous with the drain analysis's suspicion of integration on unequal terms. Another wing, including voices inside the tradition, pressed the uncomfortable question whether the small farm structure the earlier reforms had consolidated could sustain the countryside's population at rising standards, and whether the movement's defence of that structure had become a defence of the trap its own theory once diagnosed. The argument was conducted, characteristically, in the Weekly and in Social Scientist, with village data deployed by all parties, and it remains open in the form most useful to students: as a set of specified mechanisms and specified evidence, rather than as a contest of loyalties. The classification debate's deepest bequest was that style, and it survived the debate's own dissolution.

### How did the agrarian inquiry change after the classification debate?

It moved from naming the whole to measuring the parts: labour markets, women's work, migration, water, prices and the household's patchwork of cultivation and wages replaced the single label as the object of study, while the old models survived as instruments for specifying relations case by case.

## The evidentiary base: village studies, archives and restudies

Underneath every debate this guide has reported sits a distinctive evidentiary culture, and describing it explains why the arguments carried the weight they did. Indian Marxist scholarship built its cases from two kinds of ground work practised at unusual intensity. The first was the village study: a researcher, often a team, residing in a settlement and recording, household by household, the land held, the labour hired and sold, the debts owed and to whom, the crops grown and their disposal, until the village's relations stood revealed as a system. The tradition inherited exemplars from the colonial surveys and from Daniel Thorner's generation, multiplied them through the universities, and made the restudy, returning to a surveyed village after decades, a genre of its own, in which the earlier schedules served as a baseline against which differentiation, migration and commercialisation could be measured rather than asserted. The mode of production debate's data, the land reform literature's implementation records and the later agrarian studies all stand on this base, and its disciplines, patience, enumeration and suspicion of the informant's and the investigator's categories alike, are the field's methodological signature.

The second ground work was archival, and the historians owned it: the revenue records of the Mughal and colonial states, the settlement reports in which British officers described the tenures they were taxing, the inscriptions and grant plates of the early medieval kingdoms, the censuses whose categories taught Indians to count themselves by caste and community. Habib's Mughal agrarian system was rebuilt from Persian fiscal documents; the feudalism controversy turned on the interpretation of land grant inscriptions; the colonial drain was recomputed from the trade and budget statistics the empire published about itself. A tradition whose theory inclined it toward abstraction disciplined itself, in India, by an almost antiquarian respect for the document and the schedule, and the respect shows in the texture of its classics, which are dense with the grain of their sources. Students are advised to notice this when they read Kosambi or Bhaduri: the polemic rides on enumeration, and the enumeration is the argument's real body.

### Why do Indian Marxist arguments so often turn on village schedules and fiscal records?

Because the field built its authority from enumeration. A household schedule can decide whether a peasantry is differentiating; a revenue record can decide how an empire extracted its surplus. The tradition learned to make classifications answer to documents that hostile readers could inspect.

## The tradition and Indian democracy: a mutual education

Close the analytical sections with the relationship that frames all the others, the one between this tradition and the democracy that housed it. The debt runs in both directions, and stating each direction plainly is the surest guard against the caricatures. Indian democracy gave the communist movement what no other large democracy gave any communist movement: the time and the legal space to become a governing tradition, to train generations of administrators and legislators, to lose power and return to it, and to discover, in the only laboratory available, what its programme became under constitutional constraint. The movement's acceptance of that arbitration, maintained through dismissals, bans in its early decades and the long temptation of the insurgent alternative, is a fact about the tradition that its critics in the revolutionary camp count as betrayal and its historians count as its definition. Either way, the Indian case stands in the comparative record as the demonstration that the arbitration could be accepted, durably, by parties of this family.

The tradition's repayment to the democracy is also part of the record, and it is not small. Communist and left movements were among the builders of the republic's democratic substance in the countryside: the tenancy movements that made the rural poor into citizens with rights a court would recognise, the literacy and library movements that made them readers of the ballot and the newspaper, the panchayat institutions through which village India practised self government, and the insistence, carried into every constituent debate the left could reach, that political democracy without land and livelihood was a form awaiting content. Political scientists outside the tradition, studying why democratic institutions took root in some Indian regions more deeply than others, repeatedly find the movement's organisational legacy among the causes. The anomaly and the democracy, in short, made one another: the world's principal case of elected communism exists because a poor, hierarchical society sustained a competitive democracy, and that democracy is partly unintelligible without the movements that taught its poorest citizens to use it.

## The tradition meets liberalisation

The economic reforms of 1991 form the watershed of the tradition's later history, and they can be treated here in the historical register this guide applies to everything after the events themselves settled into the record. Faced with a balance of payments crisis, the government of the day dismantled industrial licensing, cut tariffs, welcomed foreign investment and began the long retreat of the state from the commanding positions the planning era had built. The left's economists responded with the most sustained body of critique produced by any Indian school: the reforms, they argued, subordinated national policy to the preferences of foreign capital and domestic finance, exposed agriculture and small industry to volatile world markets, thinned the fiscal capacity on which welfare depended, and mistook an acceleration of growth driven by services and speculation for a development strategy. Prabhat Patnaik's theoretical work gave the critique its architecture, arguing that the contemporary imperialism operated through finance and the threat of capital flight rather than through territorial control, so that even elected governments of large democracies found their policy space disciplined by asset holders abroad. Whatever the verdict on the thesis, it represented the tradition doing what this guide has repeatedly shown it doing: rebuilding an inherited category, imperialism, under the pressure of a changed world, in argument with evidence.

The political record of those years supplied the anomaly with new chapters. The left's parliamentary strength, resting on its state bases, made it a necessary partner in the coalition arithmetic of the hung parliaments that followed the reforms, and in 2004 the left parties supported a Congress led government from outside, negotiating a common programme that slowed privatisation and expanded rural employment guarantee legislation, before withdrawing support in 2008 over the question of a civil nuclear agreement with the United States, which the parties opposed as a strategic subordination. The episode belongs to settled history now, and the literature treats it as the parliamentary road's fullest national test before the state defeats that closed the period: influence without office, exercised for a programme, surrendered on a point of principle whose wisdom the tradition's own commentators continue to dispute. Governments in Bengal in the same years attempted to court industrial investment for a state whose agriculture their reforms had transformed, and the collision between the industrial drive and the agrarian base, over land acquisition for factories, became the defining crisis of the Left Front's final period, analysed in the companion article on Bengal.

The reforms also rearranged the field's internal map. The planning establishment the economists had addressed dissolved as an audience; the journals' debates shifted to employment, agrarian distress, food security and the fiscal politics of welfare; and a younger scholarship, trained in the tradition's methods, dispersed into the study of labour informalisation, urban informality and the political economy of the new services economy. Critics of the tradition, including sympathetic ones, charged that its economics had become a defence of a vanishing policy world, better at explaining why reforms would fail the poor than at designing a successor programme for an economy of services, migration and global supply chains. Defenders answered that the predicted failures, in agriculture, employment quality and regional balance, had largely arrived on schedule, and that a critique vindicated by events is not refuted by being unfashionable. The exchange continues in the journals, and a concept guide's duty is discharged by recording its shape: the tradition after liberalisation is smaller in the state's counsels and undiminished in its argumentative habits, a combination its whole history should have taught readers to expect.

## The international conversation: dependency, unequal exchange and the colonial mode

Indian Marxist economics was never a provincial literature, and its exchanges with the world's debates form one of its claims on the general reader. The drain analysis converged, from an independent direction, with the Latin American dependency school and with Samir Amin's theory of accumulation on a world scale, and Indian economists argued with both as peers: sharing the premise that underdevelopment was produced by integration into the world economy rather than by insulation from it, while disputing the dependency school's neglect of internal class structure, the very variable the Indian village studies existed to measure. Utsa Patnaik's work on the export of surpluses and on unequal exchange placed Indian evidence inside the Emmanuel debate about trade between high and low wage economies, and her later reconstruction of the colonial drain gave the old nationalist charge a quantitative architecture that travelled far beyond the academy. Alavi's colonial mode of production, examined in the debate section, belonged to the same conversation, an attempt to name what a periphery's formation is when neither feudalism nor capitalism describes its motion.

The conversation also ran through the theory of the postcolonial state, where Alavi's overdeveloped state thesis, first framed on Pakistan, was taken up across the developing world as the starting point for analysing bureaucracies and armies that towered over the classes they governed. Indian scholars refined and resisted it in equal measure, Bardhan's coalition model offering a more plural, bargaining picture of the same state, and the tension between the two pictures, state as autonomous arbiter against state as arena of class compromise, structured a generation of comparative political economy far beyond South Asia. When this guide claims that India is a test case the world has underused, the claim rests on such exports as much as on the electoral anomaly: concepts forged on Indian materials demonstrated their portability by organising research on other continents, even where the exporters' names dropped out of the citations.

A reciprocal debt should be recorded with equal plainness. The Indian tradition renewed itself repeatedly from abroad: from Lenin's agrarian studies in the classification debate, from Gramsci in the subaltern turn and in reflections on hegemony in a caste society, from the British Marxist historians in the feudalism controversy, and from the world systems literature in the economics of empire. Its distinctiveness never lay in autarky. It lay in the discipline with which imported instruments were broken against Indian evidence and rebuilt, a discipline visible to any reader who compares the questions asked in Delhi and Kolkata with the questions asked in the metropolitan journals of the same years and notices how often the Indian questions were the harder ones, because the society under study refused the categories more instructively.

## The canon: the texts the tradition argues from

Every living tradition is held together by a set of texts its members are expected to have read and licensed to dispute, and the Indian field's canon is unusually legible. At its head stand the two scholarly monuments of the 1950s and 1960s: Kosambi's introduction to Indian history and Habib's agrarian system of Mughal India, books whose methods are still the syllabus's answer to the question of what Marxist history looks like when it is done well. Beside them stand the debate texts: Bhaduri's semi-feudalism essay and the numbers of the Weekly that carried the replies, Rudra's search for the capitalist farmer, Utsa Patnaik's studies of differentiation, and Bagchi's history of private investment under colonialism. The movement's own classics form a parallel shelf: the histories and memoirs of participants, Namboodiripad's writings on Kerala and the national question, the documents of the party congresses, and the journalism of the party press. A canon of this kind does quiet organisational work. It gives strangers in different universities a common set of references, it transmits the field's standards by example, and it supplies the fixed points against which novelty must register its difference.

Canons also embalm, and the field's critics, internal and external, have noted what this one excludes. Women's writing entered late and partially, though the feminist historians of nationalism and of women's work are now assigned reading; the anti-caste tradition's own classics, Phule and Ambedkar above all, were for decades read as adjacent rather than as central, a marginalisation the caste and class literature has been repairing; and the insurgent current's texts circulated outside the canon's institutions altogether. The repair work is visible in the syllabi of the present century's courses, where Ambedkar is read with Marx rather than after him, and where Guha's insurgency stands beside the party histories it criticised. A canon under repair by its own custodians is the institutional form of the rhythm of correction this guide has described at the level of doctrine.

### The teachers' shortlist

Kosambi's introduction for historical method, Habib on the Mughal agrarian order for the archive in action, Bhaduri and his critics for the classification debate, and Bagchi on colonial investment for the drain made analytical. Together they exhibit the field's standards better than any summary of them.

## Assessment: the balance sheet of a century

The guide has assembled the evidence under the discipline its subject taught it, and it remains to weigh that evidence in the open, with the verdicts graded as the tradition's own practice grades them. Begin with what the record establishes beyond serious dispute. A communist movement of the classical type governed Indian states through free elections for decades, administered consequential land reforms where its village organisation could enforce them, built and defended public systems of health, education and food distribution, and surrendered office when voters withdrew their mandate, repeatedly and without constitutional crisis. An intellectual tradition arising from that movement produced the most influential school of historiography in the country's universities, an agrarian literature of international standing, a rebuilt analysis of colonial exploitation, and a body of theory on caste and class that corrected its own founding simplifications under the pressure of evidence and of its critics. Both halves of the record are documented in literatures open to any reader, much of them written by the tradition's opponents, which is the strongest form of documentation there is.

Against that, the debits are equally part of the record, and this guide has refused to soften them. The movement never approached national power and never solved the problem of its regional confinement. Its governing practice converged, over long incumbency, with the reformist administration its doctrine defined itself against, and the objection that names that convergence has been stated here at full strength because the tradition's own writers state it so. Its categories, for all their revision, arrived late and partially at caste and later still at gender and community, and the movements of the oppressed conducted some of their most important arguments against the left rather than within it. Its economics, formidable in critique, did not produce a successor programme for the economy liberalisation made. And the anomaly itself carries an ambiguity no advocacy dissolves: the same constitutional fidelity that made the Indian parties the world's democratic test case also ensured that the test would measure reform under constraint rather than transformation attempted, so the case answers a narrower question than the one the classical theory asked.

The assessment this guide defends, against both the celebratory and the dismissive summaries, is that the Indian experience is the most instructive failure and the most instructive success in the modern history of Marxism, and that the two descriptions attach to different parts of the record with unusual cleanliness. As a strategy for achieving the classical aims of the movement, it failed on its own terms and its honest literature concedes the failure's shape. As a demonstration of what the tradition's methods could discover about a society, and of what its organisations could deliver to the poorest citizens of a democracy by way of land, security, schooling and standing, it succeeded on terms its rivals' literatures confirm. The world's scholarship, which has largely ignored both the failure and the success because the case fitted neither of its stories about communism, is the poorer for the omission, and repairing that omission has been this article's purpose from its first paragraph.

### How should a reader finally judge the Indian tradition?

By separating its two records and grading each on its own evidence: the political strategy against the aims it set itself, the scholarship against the standards of the fields it entered. Conflating the grades, in praise or in condemnation, reproduces the errors this guide exists to correct.

One refinement of the verdict is owed to the evidence about scale. Judged as a national movement, the tradition is a regional story with national influence; judged as a set of state level experiments, it is among the most consequential political experiences of the democratic century anywhere; judged as a scholarship, it is a world class literature that its home country produced in its own languages and journals. The three judgements use different denominators, and much of the confusion in the secondary chatter about Indian communism comes from silently switching among them. Fix the denominator before reaching for the verdict, and most of the famous paradoxes of the subject, the mighty weakling, the ruling opposition, the dogmatic empiricists, resolve into the ordinary complexity of a large thing seen from several sides. That discipline of denominators is the last methodological gift the field offers its students, and it is fitting that a tradition which began by asking what kind of society India was should end by teaching its readers to ask what kind of question they are asking.

The guide's own denominator should be declared with the same candour. This article has treated the tradition as a field of evidence and argument, because that is the treatment a concept guide owes, and it has deliberately declined the two easier genres, the commemoration and the prosecution, between which the public memory of Indian communism oscillates. Readers who want either genre will find shelves of both elsewhere, much of it written with passion and some of it with evidence. What has been attempted here is the third thing the subject deserves and rarely receives outside the field's own journals: an account in which every claim carries its mechanism, every mechanism its evidence, and every verdict its conditions, so that a reader who finally disagrees with the assessment can locate the exact paragraph where the disagreement begins and the exact observation that would settle it. A tradition that taught its students to argue that way about everything else can hardly complain at being argued about that way itself. The sections that remain after this assessment are written for the article's three working readers, the student, the teacher and the researcher, and they convert the survey into routes: what to prepare, what to set, and where the unworked archives lie waiting for the next generation of the argument.

## Before the republic: the battle of the plans

The tradition's engagement with economic strategy began before the state it would argue with existed, in the extraordinary season of the 1940s when the shape of an independent Indian economy was debated in print by every organised interest in the country. The National Planning Committee, convened under Congress auspices with Jawaharlal Nehru in the chair, gathered industrialists, economists and political workers into an official anticipation of planning. The Bombay Plan of 1944, authored by the country's leading industrialists, accepted state direction and a measure of redistribution as the price of a stable capitalism, startling its contemporaries and instructing historians ever since about the Indian bourgeoisie's distinctive formation. Against both stood M. N. Roy's People's Plan, drafted for the Radical Democratic Party he had founded after his break with communism, which proposed to invert the priorities: agriculture and consumer goods first, employment and purchasing power in the countryside as the engine, heavy industry thereafter. The communist movement's own economic documents of the period pressed land abolition as the precondition of any plan at all.

The battle of the plans matters to this guide because it fixed the terms of the next half century's argument. Every question the planning era debated, the priority of heavy industry against agriculture, the role of foreign capital, the treatment of the village as market or as burden, was posed in the 1940s in forms the later literature inherited. The tradition's economists cut their teeth on these documents, and the agrarian primacy that distinguishes Indian Marxist economics from its Soviet model, the insistence that no industrialisation could outrun the countryside's relations, descends directly from the People's Plan controversy and from the famines and food crises that gave the controversy its urgency. When independence delivered the planning apparatus into Congress hands, the left became the opposition that had already written its critique, which is one reason the critique carried an authority out of proportion to the movement's parliamentary weight in the republic's first decades.

## The Kisan Sabha and the peasant as political subject

Between the founding of the party and the governing era stands the making of the peasant movement, and its central institution was the All India Kisan Sabha, founded in 1936, which organised tenants and smallholders across the provinces in the decade before independence. Its most famous early leader, Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, a sanyasi from Bihar who built the Bihar Kisan Sabha into the strongest provincial peasant organisation in the country, embodied the encounter this guide keeps describing: a religious renouncer turned agrarian organiser, fluent in the idioms of the countryside, who arrived at a politics of tenant rights and zamindari abolition through the grievances of the Bihar tenantry and argued, in his writings, that the peasant question was the Indian question. His relations with both Congress and the communists were stormy, since he resisted every attempt to subordinate the peasant organisation to another movement's priorities, and the Kisan Sabha's internal battles over whether peasants should be organised as a class across their internal differences, or whether poor peasants and agricultural labourers needed their own organisations against rich peasants as well, rehearsed in the 1940s the entire agenda the mode of production debate would formalise a generation later.

The Kisan Sabha's legacy in the tradition is double. Institutionally, it trained the cadre and mapped the constituencies from which the later state movements grew; the Bihar and Andhra countrysides it organised remained reference points for the agrarian literature long after. Intellectually, it settled one question early: the Indian peasant was not a residue to be waited out but the central political subject of the country, whose organisation would decide every other question the tradition posed itself. The parties that forgot the settlement, in periods of urban or parliamentary absorption, were reminded of it by their own historians, and the governing fronts' agrarian first programmes, in Kerala and Bengal alike, were written by men whose political education had run through the Sabha's world.

## Science for the people: the Kerala Sastra Sahitya Parishad

One Kerala institution deserves rescue from the footnotes, because it shows the tradition's method migrating into a domain no other communist movement entered as deeply. The Kerala Sastra Sahitya Parishad, founded in the early 1960s by science writers and teachers in the movement's orbit, set out to carry scientific knowledge into Malayalam public life: publishing science books and magazines in the language, organising village science forums, and campaigning on questions from public health to the environment with the same organisational patience the movement brought to tenancy. Its literacy work in the districts fed directly into the total literacy campaigns for which Kerala became famous, and its activists staffed the people's planning experiment's technical volunteer corps. The Parishad's premise was the tradition's premise applied to knowledge itself: that the monopolies worth breaking included the monopoly of certified expertise, and that a society's scientific temper, like its land, could be redistributed by organisation.

The people's science movement, as the Parishad's example inspired imitations across the country, supplied the Indian left with a second institutional legacy beside the unions and the parties: a network of volunteer science organisations whose members included many with no party allegiance, and whose campaigns, on drugs policy, on appropriate technology, on environmental questions, carried a materialist style of public argument into arenas the parties could not reach. Intellectual historians treat the movement as evidence for this article's larger claim, that the tradition's Indian distinctiveness lies in the density of the civil society it built around its politics, a density that explains both the governing record's administrative competence and the intellectual tradition's durability. Reading rooms, theatre troupes, science forums and publishing houses are not decorations on the anomaly. They are a large part of its mechanism.

## The tradition's literary criticism: realism, progress and after

Aesthetics was the one field where the tradition's Indian career began in glory and continued in permanent argument. The Progressive Writers' Association, launched in the 1930s with a manifesto drafted in London and a first conference at Lucknow in 1936 presided over by Premchand, declared that literature's business was with hunger, poverty and the fight against reaction, and gathered into its orbit writers in Urdu, Hindi, Bengali and a dozen other languages whose work remade Indian fiction's sense of its subject. Premchand's own late fiction, Sajjad Zaheer's criticism, the Urdu short story's astonishing generation, the Bengali novel's turn to the village and the mill: the progressive moment supplied modern Indian literature with much of its conscience and many of its classics. The movement's aesthetics were contested from birth, between those who held that commitment judged a work by its service to the struggle and those, often the best writers themselves, who held that reality honestly rendered was the only service literature could perform without ceasing to be literature.

The argument never ended, which is the sign of its life. Marxist literary criticism in India developed, in Bengali and Malayalam especially, into a serious scholarly practice, arguing about realism and modernism, about the novel's relation to the nation, about popular forms and their audiences, with the party's cultural functionaries sometimes disciplining and sometimes merely irritating the critics. The tradition's literary historians have been candid about the costs of the disciplining episodes, and the candour belongs to the same pattern of self correction this guide has tracked through economics and historiography. What survives every verdict is the premise the progressives installed in Indian literary culture and no subsequent school has dislodged: that literature answers to the society that produces it, that the writer's materials are social facts, and that criticism is a branch of the study of those facts. A premise can conquer a culture while its party loses every election in the region, and in Indian letters something like that conquest is what the record shows.

## Labour in the republic: unions, law and the great strike

The industrial working class, small beside the peasantry but concentrated at the economy's nerve points, gave the republic's labour politics its form, and the tradition's role in that form is a study in both achievement and limit. The union federations aligned with the left organised the mill districts, the mines, the ports and the railways, and the labour law of the republic, with its machinery of adjudication and its protection of the organised minority, bore the imprint of decades of union pressure from all federations. The climactic episode of the post independence labour movement, the railway strike of 1974, when the country's train workers struck for a bonus regime equal to other public employees and were met with mass arrests and military deployment on the railways, stands in the tradition's memory as the demonstration of both terms of its labour politics: the capacity of organised labour to stop the country's circulation, and the readiness of the state, in the years before the Emergency that followed, to answer organisation with coercion. The strike's defeat, and the Emergency's broader assault on union and civil liberties in 1975 and 1976, formed a generation of the movement's organisers as surely as any victory.

The labour literature the tradition produced in these decades is among its most concrete: studies of the Bombay mill workforce and its neighbourhoods, of the jute workers of the Hooghly, of miners and plantation labour, which together constitute an archive of Indian working class life unmatched by any other school. Its findings repeatedly embarrassed simple theory. Workers' loyalties ran through caste, language and village networks as much as through class organisation; the mill district's politics fused union, neighbourhood and community leadership; and the boundary between the formal working class and the vast informal economy of casual labour was crossed by individual workers weekly. The scholarship absorbed these findings into the tradition's characteristic late style, in which class remained the organising question but never again the only one.

## The informal economy: the framework's hardest Indian object

That boundary became, by the century's end, the central problem of Indian labour studies, and the tradition's handling of it closes this survey of its research fronts. The overwhelming majority of Indian workers labour without contracts, protection or fixed employers, in construction, domestic work, street trade, home based manufacture and casual agriculture, and a theory of the working class formed on the factory faced the question whether its central category described the country's labouring reality at all. The literature's response divided instructively. One line extended the classical analysis: informal workers were a proletariat in substance, separated from the means of production and selling labour power, whose informality was a form of exploitation's organisation rather than an exception to it, subsidising formal capital through cheap services and flexible supply. Another line argued that the informal economy's internal differentiations, of caste, gender, asset and patronage, required the articulation approach the caste debate had built, and that organising such workers, as the unions of domestic workers, waste pickers and construction labourers that grew in these decades attempted, demanded methods the factory union model did not supply. The argument remains open, and its openness is the point for this guide: on the most important empirical question in its home territory, the tradition's literature is a live research programme with competing mechanisms and shared evidence, not a settled catechism, and students meeting it there meet the field as it actually is.

## Marx on India, and the Asiatic mode argued over

Standing behind every part of this tradition is an embarrassment it learned to use: the founder wrote about India himself, briefly, brilliantly and in part wrongly, and his Indian readers had to decide what to do with the texts. The 1853 newspaper articles described the Indian village community as the stagnant foundation of an unchanging society, credited British destruction of that order with a brutal historical necessity, and looked to the railway as the forerunner of an industrial transformation the British themselves would not complete. Indian Marxists inherited the whole complicated legacy. The village community analysis entered their historiography as a question rather than a doctrine: Kosambi's work treated the village and its caste order as historical products with dates and mechanisms, not as the timeless essence the articles implied, and later scholarship, Indian and international, reconstructed how much of Marx's picture derived from the colonial reports on his desk. The double mission attributed to British rule, destructive and regenerating at once, was argued over for a century, with the mature school settling on a formulation the founder's Indian critics and defenders could both work from: the destruction was real and documented, the regeneration was the empire's accident rather than its gift, and the task of completing the transformation had passed, by the logic of the analysis itself, to Indians.

The related theoretical relic, the Asiatic mode of production, generated a scholarly industry of its own. Marx's scattered remarks had suggested a formation distinct from slavery and feudalism alike: self sufficient village communities beneath a centralised state that organised irrigation and took the surplus as tax, a structure some readers treated as India's deep past and others as a category error produced by thin information. Indian historians argued the question on their evidence. The dominant view in the school, associated with Habib's reconstructions of the Mughal revenue system, found the Indian formations better described by the general categories, suitably specified: a peasantry of small producers subjected to a surplus extracting state and to local hierarchies of caste and land, in arrangements whose dynamics the feudalism and capitalism debates could capture without a special Asiatic drawer. Defenders of the category replied that the state's role as organiser of production and the village's corporate tenures marked a genuine difference of structure. The argument, like the others this guide has followed, ended without a referee's decision and with the evidence greatly enriched, and it taught the same lesson: in the Indian field, even the founder's own categories were treated as hypotheses, which is perhaps the most unorthodox orthodoxy a Marxist tradition has practised.

### How did Indian scholars treat Marx's own writings on their country?

As evidence and as inheritance, not as verdict. They used the questions, tested the claims against the archive, corrected the village community picture and the double mission thesis where the record required, and treated the correction itself as fidelity to the method rather than disloyalty to the man.

## Tribe, frontier and the limits of the agrarian map

The agrarian map this guide has drawn has a margin its makers acknowledged late: the tribal peoples of the hills and forests, whose societies stood partly outside the caste and tenancy orders of the plains. Kosambi's history had given tribes a central theoretical place, as the material from which caste society itself had been formed by absorption, and the tradition's ethnographic historians continued to read tribe and caste as a continuum rather than as opposites. Political practice pressed harder questions. Tribal lands were alienated to plains settlers, moneylenders and mining and forest interests across the century, and the movements that resisted, some led by the left, some by tribal organisations of their own, posed the question whether the adivasi was to be organised as peasant, as worker or as a people with prior rights in territory. The Jharkhand movement for a tribal state, in which left parties participated without ever leading, and the Tripura movement's fusion of class and tribal defence, described earlier, mark the poles of the tradition's experience: where it subordinated the tribal question to the agrarian one, it was outflanked by movements that reversed the priority; where it learned to carry both, it built some of its most durable rural bases.

The theoretical yield matches the pattern of the caste case. Tribe, in the mature literature, is treated as another structure with its own logic, of kinship, territory and customary right, articulating with class and caste rather than dissolving into either, and the state's forest and mining regimes are analysed as the mechanism by which tribal India was integrated into the national economy on the harshest terms. A scholarship formed in the plains had to travel to the hills to complete its map, and the journey, like the journey to the caste question before it, was made under pressure from movements of the people concerned, which the tradition's honest historians record as the source of the correction.

## Silent Valley and the tradition's environmental education

The environment arrived late on the tradition's agenda, as it did on most, and its Indian education can be dated to a forest in Kerala. The Silent Valley, a tract of undisturbed rainforest in the Western Ghats, was proposed in the 1970s as the site of a hydroelectric dam, and the campaign to save it drew the Kerala Sastra Sahitya Parishad into the centre of Indian environmental politics. The Parishad's scientists argued the ecological case in Malayalam, in pamphlet and village meeting, against a project supported by the state's development establishment and initially by much of the left itself, for which electricity for a poor state was a self evident good. The campaign's victory, when the project was abandoned in the early 1980s, taught the movement a lesson it generalised slowly: that development's costs could fall on the irreplaceable, that its own cadres could be found on the wrong side of such a question, and that scientific organisation, the Parishad's whole method, was the instrument by which the correction was made from within the tradition's own civil society.

Later environmental argument in the field, over large dams, mining in tribal territories and industrial pollution, proceeded inside the framework the episode built: development interrogated by its distribution of costs, expertise democratised rather than merely denounced, and the state's developmentalism, including that of governments of the left, treated as a position to be argued with rather than a loyalty to be enforced. The ecological Marxism treated elsewhere in this series found, in the Indian literature, a case study rather than a theory: a movement discovering, in a single valley, that its account of progress had a hole in it, and repairing the hole with its own tools. Few episodes better illustrate the rhythm of correction on which this guide's assessment rests.

## The road argued over: the Chinese path debate inside the movement

No episode displays the young tradition's seriousness, and its vulnerability to imported templates, like the internal struggle of 1948 to 1951 over the road the Indian revolution should take. In the first years of the republic, with the Telangana struggle in progress and the Chinese revolution victorious, a powerful current in the party argued the Andhra thesis: that India's conditions, a vast peasantry, a weak and compromised bourgeoisie, a countryside ripe for agrarian revolution, matched China's, and that the strategy of protracted people's war from rural base areas should be adopted as the movement's line. Opposing currents argued for an urban and insurrectionary reading, and the party oscillated between lines with a violence of internal rhetoric that its historians now treat with candour. The settlement of 1951, reached with the counsel of the international movement, withdrew the Telangana struggle, committed the party to the parliamentary and mass organisational road within the new republic's constitution, and thereby chose, in effect, the path whose consequences this entire article describes. Every later feature of the tradition, the electoral anomaly, the state governments, the reformist convergence and the revolutionary splinter that refused the settlement in 1967, descends from that choice, and the documents of the debate remain the most instructive in the movement's archive for any reader who wants to see doctrine, evidence and circumstance collide in real time.

The historiography of the episode carries the field's signature strengths and weaknesses together. Party histories long compressed the debate into a morality of correct and incorrect lines; the scholarly literature restored its substance, showing serious people reasoning from real evidence about a society in upheaval, and erring, where they erred, by submitting Indian conditions to a foreign template's authority. The lesson the tradition's teachers draw from the episode now is the lesson this guide has drawn from the classification debate and the caste correction alike: the categories must be broken against the country's evidence before they are trusted, and the moments when the Indian movement forgot the discipline, in 1948 as in the economism on caste, are the moments its own historians mark as its defeats within its victories.

## The movement press: newspapers as daily institutions

A word, finally among the institutions, for the daily press, the least scholarly and most continuous of the tradition's carriers. The principal parties built daily newspapers in the regional languages, Deshabhimani in Malayalam, Ganashakti in Bengali and their fellows elsewhere, which at their height were among the largest circulation dailies in their states and functioned as the movement's daily school: reporting the world through its framework, training generations of journalists, and binding a readership's habits of interpretation to the organisation across a lifetime. In Kerala and Bengal the party daily was a feature of the morning landscape at tea stalls and reading rooms, and its editorial line was an event in the state's politics in itself. The sociology of the institution repays attention: a newspaper that must sell copies daily cannot live by catechism alone, and the party dailies developed professional newsrooms whose standards their rivals acknowledged, so that the tradition's interpretation of events arrived carried by competent journalism, a combination of doctrine and craft that purely academic traditions rarely achieve. The decline of print has thinned this carrier as it has thinned all print, but through the period this article covers, the daily paper was the anomaly's quiet machinery, reproducing the movement's common sense one morning at a time.

## Federalism: the left's second constitutional doctrine

Experience taught the movement a constitutional doctrine beside its social one, and the doctrine deserves record because it altered the republic's own argument about itself. Parties that governed states while opposing the centre learned federalism from the receiving end: the dismissal of Kerala's ministry in 1959, the use of governors, fiscal dependence and central investigative and planning levers all instructed them in the gap between the constitution's federal text and its unitary practice. The left's state governments became, from the late 1970s, the organisers of the wider opposition's federal campaign, convening conclaves of non Congress chief ministers that pressed for restructured centre state relations, greater fiscal devolution and the restraint of the dismissal power. The doctrine they articulated drew on the tradition's theory of nationalities: a multinational union, they argued, is held by consent renewed through genuine state autonomy, and centralisation is not strength but the brittleness of a state that must administer what it cannot comprehend. The campaign's partial victories, in the restraint that later practice placed on dismissals and in the permanent elevation of devolution in national debate, belong to the anomaly's wider consequences, and they complicate any account in which the communist parties figure only as supplicants of the constitutional order. They were among its working co authors in the federal chapter, and the comparative literature on Indian federalism, whatever its authors' politics, writes them into that chapter as principals.

## Who wrote the tradition: teachers, journalists and full timers

The field's demography explains its texture, and a concept guide should describe the people as plainly as the doctrines. The tradition's intellectuals were rarely a professoriate alone. Its characteristic figure was the teacher organiser: the college lecturer who built the union in his district, the schoolteacher whose reading room became a village institution, the journalist of the party daily who wrote the district's history between editions, the full time party worker whose education was the movement's own schools and whose library was the publishing house's catalogue. The universities supplied the field's peaks, the Kosambis and Habibs, but its base was this broad stratum of the educated provincial middle class, for whom Marxism was simultaneously an analysis, a vocation and a ladder of self education out of the narrowness of small town life. The bhadralok of Bengal, the schoolteacher castes of Kerala, the Telugu country's scribal communities supplied the stratum disproportionately, and its social narrowness left marks the literature acknowledges: the tradition's recurring difficulty in hearing the lowest castes and the tribal peoples in their own voices, corrected late and under pressure, tracks the composition of its writing class as faithfully as it tracks any doctrine.

Autodidactism was the stratum's glory and its method. A movement that printed the classics cheaply and organised study circles in union offices created readers who arrived at the texts without the academy's mediation and argued with them accordingly, sometimes naively, sometimes with a freshness the universities envied. The field's institutions existed to connect the two publics, the seminar and the study circle, and the Weekly's correspondence columns, where professors and full timers disputed the classification of a Bihar village on equal typographic terms, remain the emblem of the connection. When later sections of this series treat the tradition's examination presence, the connection matters practically: the Indian state's competitive examinations test a canon that this demography built, and generations of aspirants from the same provincial stratum have entered the services carrying its categories with them, a diffusion of the field's influence into the administration itself that no electoral statistic records.

## The border war and the education of a split

The split of 1964, summarised in the parties' section, has a wound at its centre that a concept guide should name, because it explains the emotional register of Indian communist polemics for a generation. The border war between India and China in 1962 placed the undivided party in an impossible position: its international loyalties pulled toward a socialist neighbour, its national existence toward the country whose soldiers were dying in the Himalayas. The leadership's public stance supported the defence of the country while calling for a negotiated settlement, and the state, doubting the distinction, imprisoned much of the left leadership under emergency regulations. Inside the prisons and outside them, the argument over the war fused with the argument over the Sino Soviet dispute and over the party's whole strategy, and the organisation that emerged in 1964 was two organisations, each persuaded that the other's international allegiance explained its domestic errors. The episode belongs to settled history, and the literature's treatment of it has matured into the field's usual pattern: the documents published, the positions reconstructed in their own terms, and the judgement distributed with a care the participants could not afford at the time. Its long consequence was structural. The Indian movement thereafter thought in the plural about the socialist world, and its intellectuals' habit of judging foreign models against Indian evidence, rather than importing them as lines, dates in its modern form from that education.

## The tradition's sociology: A. R. Desai and the social background

Sociology was the last of the social sciences the tradition entered in force, and its entry produced one of the field's most assigned books. A. R. Desai's Social Background of Indian Nationalism, published in 1948, applied the materialist method to the freedom struggle itself, tracing the rise of nationalism to the new classes and economic relations colonialism had created, the dispossessed artisans, the new intelligentsia, the commercial and industrial bourgeoisie, and reading the movement's phases as those classes entered and shaped it. The book's appearance at the moment of independence made it the template for a Marxist sociology of India, and Desai's subsequent career, building the sociology department at Bombay and training generations of students, institutionalised the approach. His later work on the Indian state and on rural sociology pressed the framework against the republic's development, characteristically insisting that the categories prove themselves against the transformation actually under way.

The dialogue between this sociology and its great rival, the tradition of M. N. Srinivas, organised Indian sociology's central argument for decades. Where the Marxist school began from production, property and class, Srinivas began from caste as lived hierarchy, from the village as a moral community and from processes like Sanskritisation by which castes rose within the ritual order; where the first saw the village's unity as the ideology of its dominant landholders, the second saw solidarities the class lens flattened. The mature state of the discipline absorbed both, as this guide's caste sections have shown: village studies in the Marxist line learned to record ritual rank with an ethnographer's patience, and the Srinivas tradition's students learned to ask who owned the land beneath the moral community. A student who reads the quarrel whole acquires Indian social science's essential education, and the quarrel's existence, two research programmes correcting one another across fifty years in the same journals, is the strongest institutional evidence for the assessment this article has been building: the tradition's contribution was never only its conclusions, it was the argument it made unavoidable.

## How the tradition remembers itself: documents, memoirs and the open archive

A final feature of the field deserves notice before the guide turns to its readers, because it conditions everything a researcher can do with the subject. The Indian movement documented itself obsessively. Party congresses published their documents; factions published their polemics; the state published its surveillance; and the participants, in retirement, published memoirs in English and the regional languages in numbers no comparable movement matched. Muzaffar Ahmad, a founding figure, wrote the movement's early history from inside; the documentary collections assembled by scholars placed the Comintern correspondence, the trial records of the great conspiracy cases and the internal circulars of the underground years into print; and the conspiracy trials themselves, Meerut above all, in which the colonial state prosecuted the young movement's leadership in 1929 and thereby gave it a courtroom platform for four years, survive as inadvertent archives of the accused's own account of their politics. The result is a self documentation so dense that the historian's problem is selection rather than scarcity, and the tradition's history has consequently been rewritten several times as archives opened and generations changed: from commemoration, to documentary reconstruction, to the critical scholarly histories that now treat the party's past with the same evidentiary discipline the party's historians applied to everyone else's.

The memoir literature carries a particular evidential charm the guide should register. Communists who had spent years underground or in prison wrote about those years with a concreteness that corrects the abstractions of strategy documents: the safe houses and the reading, the prison study circles in which a generation educated itself on the classics, the marriages and estrangements the work exacted. Social historians of the movement mine the memoirs for the texture of commitment, and the texture matters to the concept of the tradition itself, since a movement's capacity to reproduce itself across defeats is a fact about the lives it made livable for its members, not only about the correctness of its line. The anomaly, read at this depth, rests on thousands of such lives organised into institutions durable enough to outlast them, which is why this article has returned so often from doctrine to carriers: libraries, dailies, journals, study circles, unions and fronts. Doctrines are cheap in the history of ideas. Carriers are the expensive part, and India is where this tradition built them best.

## The literacy campaign: the tradition in miniature

The total literacy campaign that began in Ernakulam district at the end of the 1980s and spread across Kerala compresses the whole tradition into a single episode, and it is worth narrating at the pace it deserves. A district administration, a Left Front state government and the Kerala Sastra Sahitya Parishad combined to attempt what the state's statistics said should already have been true: teaching every remaining non literate adult in the district to read. The method was the movement's method, organisation applied to knowledge. Volunteers, drawn heavily from the Parishad and the youth and student movements, were trained in cohorts; the district was mapped household by household; learners were gathered in small groups at hours their work allowed; primer materials carried content from the learners' own lives, wages, health, rights, rather than the alphabetic pieties of official textbooks; and the campaign was run with the festivity of the cultural front, songs, street theatre and processions converting a bureaucratic target into a popular festival in which the newly literate marched as its heroes. Ernakulam was declared totally literate, the campaign was extended across the state, and the national government adopted the district model for its own literacy missions, with the Parishad's activists in demand as trainers far beyond Kerala.

The episode rewards the guide's analytical habits. Every element of the anomaly's mechanism is present in miniature: a cadre organisation's patience, a scientific society's expertise, a state government's sponsorship held at the distance that left the campaign's popular character intact, and a social demand, the dignity of the signature over the thumbprint, that the community itself supplied. The critics' cautions belong to the record too: literacy measured at the campaign's threshold is a fragile attainment without continuing education behind it, and the follow up, libraries, continuing classes and the neo literate press, determined what the achievement finally meant. Both the achievement and the caution are the tradition: the organisation that could mount the campaign and the literature that audited its results without mercy were products of the same world.

## The tradition in the industrial city

The industrial city tested the tradition differently from the countryside, and its urban record completes the map. Bombay's mill districts, the girangaon of the Marathi working class, formed the classic site: an immense textile workforce in the movement's early decades, housed in chawls around the mills, organised first by the communist led Girni Kamgar Union whose great strikes of the late 1920s announced the party's arrival as a mass force. The literature the city produced, union histories, memoirs, and later a rich social history of the mill neighbourhoods, recorded a working class culture of theatre, festival and argument in which the union hall was one institution among several competing for the worker's loyalty, alongside the caste association, the village network and the gymnasium. When, in 1982, the mill workers struck again in one of the largest industrial actions in the country's history, the strike was led from outside the established federations, by an independent leader whose rise itself testified to the workers' impatience with the old organisations, and its long defeat across the following years closed the mills and dissolved the world that had sustained the city's labour politics for a century. The tradition's urban historians wrote the episode as an ending: the industrial proletariat of the classical texts deindustrialised in place, its children dispersing into the informal economy the agrarian section has already met.

The urban lesson parallels the rural one. In the city as in the village, the tradition's strength was never the automatic product of exploitation's severity; it was built, institution by institution, in the chawls and reading rooms, and it decayed when the material basis of those institutions, the mill, the stable tenancy, the mass meeting, decayed beneath them. A materialism that cannot explain its own rise and decline in material terms would fail its own test, and the field's urban literature passes the test with the same unsparing enumeration it brought to the Bihar village: counting the looms as they fell silent, and asking what organisation could mean when the counted world dissolved.

## The periodisation wars: ancient, medieval and the early medieval

The historians' stream deserves one more descent into its workshop, because the periodisation argument displays the school's method at its most granular and its influence at its widest. Colonial historiography had divided the Indian past by the religion of its rulers, a Hindu period, a Muslim period and a British period, and nationalist historiography had largely inverted the valuations while keeping the frame. The Marxist school proposed to divide by social formation instead, and the proposal forced a generation of argument about where the joints of Indian history actually lie. Was there a slave owning formation in the ancient world, as some applied the classical sequence? Did the centuries after the Gupta empire constitute a feudal formation, as Sharma argued from the land grants, or an early medieval order of a different kind, as B. D. Chattopadhyaya proposed from the same inscriptions read for state formation, local cult integration and expanding settlement rather than for decline? Did the Sultanate and Mughal centuries form a distinct formation, centralised and extractive, or a continuation of the early medieval order, as different wings of the school maintained against one another?

Chattopadhyaya's intervention is the instructive case, since it came from within the materialist camp and against its reigning thesis. Reading the early medieval evidence for expansion rather than collapse, agrarian settlement spreading, local chiefs becoming kings, regional cultures crystallising, he argued that the period's integrating processes had been misread as fragmentation by a feudalism model that equated the absence of empire with decay. The debate that followed, conducted across the Indian History Congress and the journals, refined every term it touched: what a land grant proves, what urban decline would even look like in the record, how a state forms at the locality. A reader from outside the discipline might ask what hangs on the answer, and the participants' own reply is the school's reply everywhere: the periodisation is the theory of the past in compressed form, and getting the joints right decides where change is thought to come from, whether from the collapse of orders or from their internal growth. That the school's own students performed the correction on the school's own founder generation is the pattern this guide has by now established as the tradition's signature, and the early medieval debate is its cleanest historiographical instance.

## The bourgeoisie argued over: national, comprador or something else

Every strategy document in the tradition presupposed a verdict on one class, and the argument over the Indian bourgeoisie's character ran through the century like a ground bass. Colonial categories offered two ready made figures: the comprador, the native agent of foreign capital whose interests were imperialism's, and the national bourgeoisie, the indigenous capitalist whose growth imperialism stunted and whose interest therefore ran toward independence and industrialisation. The party's successive characterisations divided the class between these figures, locating the big bourgeoisie's leadership in a compromising dual role, tied to imperialism and landlordism yet capable of conflict with both, a formulation elastic enough to justify every alliance the movement ever made and criticised, by its own theorists, for exactly that elasticity. The academic literature brought the question to the evidence. Bagchi's investment history showed an Indian bourgeoisie neither simply comprador nor heroically national: enterprising within grooves the colonial system cut, accumulating in trade, finance and the interstices of the managing agency system, and pressing the colonial state, with increasing confidence, for the conditions of its own expansion. The Bombay Plan's industrialists, accepting planning and state enterprise as the scaffolding of their future, displayed a class that intended to use the independent state rather than merely to staff it, and the post independence settlement, in which the public sector built the heavy industry private capital would later inhabit, bore out their expectation.

Gramsci's notion of passive revolution gave the argument its most influential later frame. Applied to India by scholars working in and around the tradition, the concept described a transformation accomplished from above, by a state managing the transition to capitalism while containing the popular forces that a fuller revolution would have unleashed: land reform enough to break the old intermediaries but not the rural order, industrialisation enough to found a national economy but not to disturb its hierarchies, democracy enough to legitimate the order but administered so that its poorest citizens' demands arrived filtered through patronage. The frame's power is that it explains the republic's characteristic blend, genuine transformation and genuine containment in the same institutions, and its critics' counter, that it explains everything and therefore risks explaining nothing, has been pressed from within the field with equal force. The argument, at the time of this guide, stands where the field's best arguments stand: as the organising dispute of a literature, with the evidence richer for its conduct.

## Public systems: health, education and the welfare argument

The governing record's most durable monuments are systems rather than statutes, and the tradition developed a distinct political economy of public systems that a guide should state in its own right. Its premise came from the record itself: in Kerala and Bengal, the goods that changed ordinary life most measurably, the clinic that functioned, the school that taught, the ration shop that stocked grain, were delivered by public systems whose performance depended less on their budgets than on the organised vigilance of their users. The literature generalised the observation into a theory of accountability. Public systems decay, on this account, when their users are unorganised and their staff answer only upward; they perform when unions of teachers and health workers, parent and patient committees, panchayats and an attentive press convert the user into a monitor. The communist governed states' contribution was less the money, which every Indian state lacked, than the surrounding organisation, the same civil society density this article has credited in the literacy campaign and the science movement. Economists of the tradition formalised the argument against both the marketisers, who proposed to discipline public systems by exit into private provision, and the budget maximalists of their own camp, for whom every failure was a failure of funds: the comparative evidence across Indian states, they argued, showed systems with similar budgets performing at wildly different levels, and the variance tracked organisation and political competition more closely than expenditure.

Education carried the argument's hardest case, because the tradition's own governments presided over systems whose quality the tradition's own intellectuals criticised in print: schooling universalised in access while its content and its teacher politics fell short of the movement's professions. The self criticism is of a piece with the field's pattern and is recorded here in that spirit. What the public systems literature establishes for the assessment is a mechanism, specified and testable, linking the political anomaly to the social outcomes its defenders cite: not virtue in office, but organisation around office, the permanent mobilisation of the governed as the quality control of their own services. Where that mobilisation thinned, in the fronts' later years of machine politics, the systems' performance furnished the critics' evidence, and the tradition's analysts, in the post mortem literature after the state defeats, said so before their opponents did.

## The Gandhian inheritance read by its rivals

The tradition's historiography had to digest the most successful peasant politics of the century, which was not its own: the Gandhian movements that drew the Indian countryside into nationalism a generation before the Kisan Sabha. Champaran, Kheda and Bardoli, the campaigns in which Gandhi organised peasants against indigo planters, revenue demands and tax enhancements, were studied by Marxist historians with a respect their polemics did not always show, because the campaigns posed the field's central question in an unfamiliar form. Here was a politics that mobilised peasants massively, disciplined them rigorously, and directed their energy toward the colonial state while carefully containing their demands against Indian landlords and creditors, a politics of class harmony in the village under national unity against the foreigner. The Marxist reading, in its mature form, neither dismissed the achievement nor accepted the harmony: it showed, from the campaign records, how the containment was organised, which demands were ruled out of order and whose interests the rulings served, while conceding that Gandhi had solved the problem of peasant mobilisation on a scale the left of the time could not approach, and had solved it by speaking to the peasant's moral world in an idiom the class vocabulary lacked.

The argument's yield for the tradition was the template of its best historical work: take the rival movement's archive seriously, reconstruct its social logic from its own documents, and let the criticism arise from the reconstruction rather than from denunciation. The subaltern historians inherited the template and turned it on the left itself; the national movement's historiography, in all schools, still works in its shadow. For the concept guide, the episode completes the portrait of the scholarly tradition at its characteristic best and connects the historiography back to the politics: a movement competing for the peasantry's allegiance was forced, by the quality of the competition, to understand the peasantry better than any doctrine it started with required, and its understanding outlasted the competition.

## What counts as evidence: the programmes compared

The rival accounts section stated what each tradition claims; a shorter comparison of what each counts as proof will serve the student who has to choose among them in an essay. The Marxist literature's characteristic evidence is relational: the household schedule that shows who hires and who is hired, the grant inscription that shows who received the village's surplus, the budget that shows where the transfer went. Its claims fail, when they fail, by the standard it sets itself, when the relations it names are shown not to govern the outcome it explains, as the feudalism thesis was wounded by evidence of trade and the classification by evidence of regional variety. The liberal account's characteristic evidence is aggregative: growth rates, poverty headcounts, the before and after of a reform, and its claims fail when the aggregates are shown to conceal the distribution the Marxists insist on, or when the acceleration is redated to causes the narrative omitted, as the argument over the 1980s turn showed. The institutional account works from comparison across jurisdictions sharing a policy and differing in outcome, and fails when the residual it calls institutions is shown to be the deposit of the class settlements it bracketed. The caste account works from the ethnography and the electoral geography of identity, and fails where identity's material errands cannot be specified, where the bloc that voted as a caste divided, on inspection, along the lines of land and occupation after all.

No referee stands above these standards, which is why the field's arguments persist and why this guide has reported them as a field of live disputes rather than as a museum. The practical counsel for students follows from the comparison: state which programme's question is being answered before answering it, since most bad essays on this subject confuse the questions and then grade one programme by another's evidence. The scholarly literature at its best does the opposite, borrowing a rival's datum and showing what its own framework makes of it, Bhaduri conceding the capitalist farmer's existence and disputing his typicality, the liberal narrative absorbing the land reform evidence and disputing its weight. Readers who learn to spot that move, the concessive absorption, possess the key to the whole literature's quality, and to its difference from the polemics that surround it in the public prints.

## Language: the question the tradition answered early

The reorganisation of the Indian union into linguistic states, accomplished in the 1950s after struggles in which the demand for a Telugu state cost a fast unto death and set the subcontinent's internal map alight, found the communist movement on the demand's side from the beginning, and the alignment is theoretically instructive. A doctrine whose theory of nationalities treated language communities as nations entitled to self organisation within a union had a ready answer where the Congress leadership hesitated: the linguistic province was democracy's form in a multilingual country, the condition on which administration, education and politics could reach citizens in their own tongue. The movement's own organisation prefigured the doctrine, since its regional cultures were already linguistic, its dailies already Malayalam and Bengali and Telugu, its classics already translated. When the states were redrawn, the left's strongholds emerged as coherent linguistic homelands, Kerala and Bengal among them, and the tradition could plausibly claim that the federal map the republic settled into bore its argument's imprint alongside the national movement's.

The language question also disciplined the tradition in the opposite direction. The anti Hindi agitations in Tamil Nadu, where a Dravidian movement built its politics on resistance to the northern language's imposition, confronted the left with an identity mobilisation it neither led nor could dismiss, and its Tamil organisation's survival depended on absorbing the linguistic sentiment into its own programme rather than opposing it in the name of class unity. The pattern is by now familiar enough to state as a rule of the Indian field: every supra class identity the tradition met, caste, community, language, tribe, first appeared to it as an obstacle and was then incorporated, under pressure and with losses, as a structure its politics had to organise rather than an error it could correct. The incorporation is the tradition's real syllabus, and the reader who has followed it across the sections of this guide has the measure of both its adaptability and its cost.

## The famine and the making of a generation

Behind the postwar tradition stands a catastrophe that formed its founding generation as surely as any text: the Bengal famine of 1943, in which a province of the empire starved on a vast scale while the war economy boomed around it. The communist party, legal for the period because of its support for the anti fascist war, threw its young organisation into relief work and into the Grow More Food campaign, and its cadres learned the countryside's relations, the hoarding trader, the indebted tenant, the absent state, in the school of the relief queue. The famine entered the tradition's memory as the conclusive demonstration of its colonial analysis: an empire that could move grain for armies let its subjects starve for want of purchasing power, in a market functioning exactly as designed. The cultural front made the famine its defining subject, in plays, reportage and art that fixed the event in Bengal's conscience, and the IPTA's famine drama toured the province while the dying continued.

The famine's analytical afterlife matters as much as its memory. When Amartya Sen rebuilt famine theory a generation later around entitlements, arguing that famines characteristically occur amid food availability when the entitlements of the labouring poor collapse, the Indian Marxist literature recognised a framework continuous with its own relief workers' reports, and argued with it on the shared ground: over the relative weight of market mechanism and state policy, of war inflation and procurement failure, in the Bengal case and in the general theory. Sen's related observation, that independent India's press and electoral politics have prevented a comparable famine since, while scarcity and malnutrition persisted unphotographed, entered the tradition's democratic argument as evidence for the anomaly's other half: the same publicity and organisation that made elected communism possible also made mass starvation politically impossible, and a literature that prizes mechanisms should credit the mechanism wherever it operates, in its opponents' hands as in its own. The famine generation's lesson, absorbed into the field's bones, is that food is the first test of every Indian politics, and the governing fronts' later investment in public distribution systems was written by men and women for whom 1943 was not history but biography.

## The collapse elsewhere and the reckoning at home

The fall of the European socialist states and the dissolution of the Soviet Union between 1989 and 1991 struck every communist party on earth, and the Indian parties' response belongs to this guide because it displayed, under maximum stress, the difference the anomaly had made. Parties whose identity rested on a ruling state's example faced extinction or reinvention; the Indian parties, whose identity rested on their own electoral and scholarly record, convened congresses, published extended ideological documents, and argued the catastrophe out in their press over several years. Their settled assessment, reached by the larger party in documents of the early 1990s, held that the Soviet system's deformation, the substitution of the party for the class, the suppression of inner party and soviet democracy, the command economy's ossification, explained the collapse without invalidating the framework the Indian movement applied at home; the assessment conceded errors in the movement's own past judgements of the socialist states with a frankness the earlier documents had lacked. Critics, including the tradition's own dissenters, found the reckoning too convenient, an autopsy that acquitted the pathologist, and the argument joined the field's standing stock of open disputes.

The episode's analytical interest is comparative. The Indian parties survived the collapse of their doctrine's homeland with their organisations, their state governments and their publishing worlds intact, and the explanation the record suggests returns to this guide's central mechanism: institutions rooted in a domestic society's life, in unions, libraries, dailies and state administrations answerable to voters, outlast the foreign exemplar that parties without such roots had leaned on. The scholarly tradition's parallel survival has the same shape, since the Indian field's classics were arguments about India, vulnerable to Indian evidence and to nothing in Moscow's archives. A tradition whose centre of gravity was always its own country's material could lose the century's largest argument abroad and continue its arguments at home, diminished in confidence, enlarged in the one asset this guide has tracked throughout: the obligation, now unavoidable, to stand on its own record.

## The small hours: study circles and the making of readers

The tradition's most characteristic institution was also its smallest: the study circle, a handful of members meeting after work with a text, a teacher drawn from their own ranks and a discipline of reading that treated a pamphlet with the seriousness a university reserves for a classic. Party schools extended the form, taking organisers out of their districts for weeks of political economy, history and the movement's own documents, and the prison system, in the decades when the leadership was so often inside it, became the form's involuntary university, where the classics were read in the only leisure the state provided. The memoir literature returns to these scenes with unmistakable gratitude, and the gratitude identifies a mechanism the anomaly's sociology requires. A movement reproduces itself not by agreement but by literacy in a shared set of texts and arguments, renewed in each recruit who works through them under a teacher's eye, and the Indian movement's investment in that reproduction, in translations priced for a worker's wage, in libraries at the end of village lanes, in examiners of its own cadre as exacting as any university's, is the deepest layer of the institutional density this article has described. The scholarly tradition and the study circle are the same fact at different scales: a civilisation of readers, arguing from common books, in a country where the movement had taught a significant part of the poor to read in the first place.

## Open questions: where the field's own agenda points

A guide that has reported a century of argument should close its survey by marking the questions the literature itself leaves open, since those are the doors through which new readers enter as contributors. Four stand out in the field's own journals. The first is archival and regional: the synthesis of the movement's history still rests disproportionately on Bengal and Kerala, while the records of the Andhra, Punjab, Maharashtra and Tamil movements, much of them in regional languages, await the integrated treatment the two heartlands have received. The second is the gender archive: the women's organisations of the left kept records and produced memoirs that the general histories cite more often than they absorb, and the rewriting of the movement's social history from those sources has begun without yet reaching the textbooks. The third is the rural restudy: the village schedules of the classification debate's generation constitute a baseline whose systematic restudy, village by village, would convert an argument about the past into a measurement of half a century's change, and the agrarian studies foundations have shown the work is possible. The fourth is conceptual, and it is this article's own: whether the articulation framework built for caste and class can organise the field's newest objects, the platform economy's labour relations, the climate stressed countryside, the politics of the city that no longer has mills, with the same discipline its builders applied to the tenant and the millhand.

To these the guide adds the observation that the school's public position has itself become an object of study. In successive decades the historians' textbooks and the economists' policy standing made the tradition a party to public controversies over what Indian students should be taught and what Indian governments should do, controversies conducted in the press and in parliament as much as in the journals. The field's literature registers those episodes as part of its conditions of existence: a scholarship installed in a nation's syllabi will be fought over by the nation's politics, and the school's survival through the fights, with its classics still assigned and its questions still set, is itself evidence for the institutional argument of this article. The next synthesis, whenever it comes, will be written by scholars formed in those conditions, and it will find, as this guide has found, that the tradition's most reliable product is not any of its answers but the quality of the arguments its institutions make unavoidable.

## The Emergency: the tradition's democratic stress test

Between 1975 and 1977 the republic suspended its own democracy, and the episode belongs in this guide because it stress tested the anomaly's premises in the harshest available conditions. The government of Indira Gandhi, citing internal disturbance, proclaimed an Emergency: elections postponed, press censored, opposition politicians and activists imprisoned in large numbers, and constitutional amendments carried by a captive parliament. The larger communist party's predecessor formations divided under the pressure in ways the literature records without flinching. The Communist Party of India supported the Emergency in its first phase, reading it as a blow against the right reaction its analysis had cast as the principal danger, a judgement its own later documents revisited with regret. The party formed at the 1964 split opposed the Emergency, saw its cadres imprisoned and its organisations driven underground, and emerged in 1977 as a principal of the democratic restoration, winning Bengal in the election that followed the Emergency's lifting and beginning there the long tenure this article has described.

The historiography extracts from the episode the lesson the anomaly would predict. The party whose instincts had been formed by decades of constitutional opposition read the authoritarian turn as the emergency it was, and paid for the reading in prison; the party that subordinated the democratic question to its characterisation of the government's enemies misread the moment and spent years repairing the error in its own documents. Democratic practice, the school's writers concluded, was not a tactic to be suspended when the analysis pointed elsewhere but the medium in which the movement's Indian life was lived, and the conclusion hardened into doctrine precisely because it had been learned at cost. For the comparative record the episode adds the missing cell in the anomaly's table: the Indian parties were tested not only by winning and losing elections but by the abolition of elections themselves, and the principal tradition among them chose, in the event, the restoration of the ballot over every advantage the suspension offered its rivals' enemies.

## The field goes global: provincialising Europe and the ironies of reception

The tradition's widest international influence arrived, characteristically, under another flag. Dipesh Chakrabarty, trained in the Calcutta world this article has described and a contributor to Subaltern Studies, published at the century's end a work that carried the Indian argument into the centre of global humanities debate. Its proposal, condensed in its title's gerund, was to provincialise Europe: to treat the categories of European political thought, the citizen, the state, capital, modernity itself, as local products of a particular history that had been universalised by empire, indispensable for thinking about India and inadequate in the same gesture. Chakrabarty's two histories, the history of capital's universalising drive and the history of the lifeworlds that drive could not digest, grew directly from the soil this guide has surveyed: from the labour history of the jute mills he had written earlier, from the subaltern quarrel with elitist narrative, and behind both from the old Indian insistence that the categories must be broken against the evidence of a society they were not made for. The book was received abroad as postcolonial theory, its author as a founder of that field, and its Marxist formation, visible on every page to a reader of this tradition, went largely unremarked in the reception.

The irony generalises, and the guide records it as the final form of the absence with which it began. Again and again, products of the Indian Marxist milieu have entered world scholarship stripped of their provenance: the subaltern category without the party histories that provoked it, the critique of historicism without the classification debates that practised it, the drain's magnitudes quoted in metropolitan polemic without the school that computed them. Provenance is not vanity. A concept detached from the argument that produced it loses the evidence that disciplines its use, and the world literature's habit of importing Indian conclusions without Indian arguments is the scholarly form of an old extractive pattern the tradition's economists named in another context. The remedy available to a guide is the one attempted here: to restore the arguments, the journals, the villages and the votes, so that the concepts can be met in the company that explains them.

A closing word on method, since the article has practised one and should state it. The tradition treated in these pages has been described throughout by its formations and positions rather than by its fortunes at any present moment, its parties by what they built and argued rather than by the controversies of a season, and its present by structure rather than by headline. That discipline is not squeamishness. A field whose deepest feature is duration, a century of organisation and argument carried across the most turbulent century in its country's history, is misread at the tempo of the news cycle, and the reader who has absorbed the structural picture owns the instrument with which any season's headlines can be weighed when they come. The anomaly will be tested by the future as it was by the past; the literature will be corrected by its students as it was by their teachers; and the questions of the streams table, power, period, production and caste, will still be the questions when the next synthesis is attempted, by hands this guide cannot foresee, in journals whose names, if the pattern holds, the tradition itself will have built.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the history of Marxism in India?

It begins with a party founded in the 1920s under colonial rule, built through unions, peasant associations and cultural fronts, and tested by bans and prison. Independence brought legality and a choice, settled by 1951, for the parliamentary road. Kerala elected a communist led ministry in 1957. The movement split in 1964 and again at the decade's end, when the revolutionary current broke away. Left Fronts then governed Bengal for over three decades and Tripura for long stretches, while Kerala alternated. Across the same century, the movement's scholars rebuilt Indian history, economics and social theory in its journals and universities.

### Q: Why has communism succeeded in some Indian states?

Because several conditions met there and not elsewhere. A tenancy burdened countryside gave land politics its material, as in Bengal and Malabar. Organisation preceded opportunity: unions, reading rooms, peasant associations and libraries made the party part of everyday life before it asked for votes. Anti caste and social reform movements had already loosened the old hierarchies, so egalitarian appeals found prepared ground. And the grievances kept renewing themselves, refugees in Bengal, tenants in Kerala, tribal land in Tripura. Where landlord power fused early with caste authority and a rival machine absorbed peasant discontent, the same appeals found no purchase.

### Q: What are the main communist parties in India?

Two principal parties descend from the undivided movement. The larger, formed at the 1964 split, led the governing fronts in Bengal and Tripura across their long tenures and shares office in Kerala's rhythm of alternation; it defines itself against both revisionism and adventurism. The older party, continuing the pre split organisation's name, works in the same states and nationally in alliance politics. A revolutionary current broke away at the end of the 1960s after the Naxalbari rising, rejecting elections altogether, and its descendants form a third family. Around these stand smaller formations, splinters and regional parties of the broader left.

### Q: What is distinctive about Indian Marxism?

Three things together. Its parties governed for decades through free elections inside a capitalist federation, a record without parallel and largely missing from world accounts of communism. Its scholars produced original theory under Indian evidence, above all the debate over whether agriculture was semi feudal or capitalist, which matched the European transition debate for rigour. And its categories had to survive caste, community, language and tribe as structures no formula dissolved, so the tradition learned to articulate distinct oppressions rather than reduce them. A doctrine corrected repeatedly by its own country's facts is the shortest honest description.

### Q: What was the mode of production debate?

An argument carried on mainly in the Economic and Political Weekly from the late 1960s into the 1980s about the character of Indian agriculture after independence. Amit Bhaduri's semi feudal thesis held that a landlord who combined letting with lending and crop buying profited from his tenants' dependence and blocked innovation, dragging cultivators into forced commerce. Ashok Rudra and Utsa Patnaik answered with evidence of capitalist farmers, wage labour and peasant differentiation. Others complicated both poles, asking how any mode of production is identified from observable relations. The exchange produced models, village data and the field's most original theoretical work.

### Q: Which Indian Marxist thinkers matter most?

Start with the historian D. D. Kosambi, whose study of Indian history founded a school and a method. Irfan Habib follows for the Mughal agrarian order and the colonial economy, with R. S. Sharma for the feudalism thesis. Among economists, Amit Bhaduri for the semi feudal model, K. N. Raj for planning and the agrarian constraint, Amiya Kumar Bagchi for colonial investment, and Prabhat Patnaik for imperialism and macroeconomic argument. M. N. Roy matters as the founding generation's most original and most heretical mind. Gail Omvedt anchors the caste and class literature that corrected the tradition from within its wider world.

### Q: Has any communist party governed democratically in India?

Yes. Communist led governments have won state elections repeatedly since Kerala first returned one in 1957, and they have governed under a constitution, before independent courts and hostile central governments, and have left office when voters dismissed them. The Bengal Left Front held its state across successive elections over more than three decades, the longest run of democratically elected communist government anywhere. Kerala developed a rhythm of alternation between fronts in which the left returned to office as regularly as it left. The anomaly is not survival by coercion. It is survival by consent, renewed and withdrawn at the ballot box.

### Q: How does Indian Marxism handle caste?

Badly at first, then with increasing honesty. The early movement treated caste as a feudal survival that class organisation would dissolve, and practice refuted the confidence: caste allocated land, credit, water and dignity, and voters mobilised by caste more readily than by class. Kosambi recast caste as a real structure of exploitation rooted in production's history. Later work, Omvedt's above all, arrived at articulation: caste and class as distinct orders, each conditioning the other's operation, neither reducible to the other. The detailed argument, including the confrontation with Ambedkar's critique, fills the companion article on the caste and class debate.

### Q: Where is Indian Marxist work published?

In an institutional world the tradition built. The Economic and Political Weekly, which published the agrarian and planning debates across two decades, remains a journal of record for the social sciences. Social Scientist, founded in 1972, carries the theoretical and political economy work. The Indian History Congress gives the historians their annual parliament. Party publishing houses in Delhi and Kolkata issue the classics and the scholarship in English and the regional languages at student prices, and party dailies such as Deshabhimani and Ganashakti carry the interpretation into everyday reading. University presses and the movement's bookshops in the university towns complete the circuit.

### Q: Where should you start with Indian Marxism?

With the method in action rather than with summaries. Take Kosambi's 1956 volume first, for the historian's craft, then Bhaduri's essay on semi feudalism together with the replies it provoked, which between them teach the agrarian debate in its original voices. Add a scholarly narrative of the movement itself for the spine, and the companion studies of Kerala and Bengal for the record in office. Keep the streams table beside you as the map: parties, historians, economists, social theorists, four questions that organise a century. A week across those shelves orients a reader in the entire field.

### Q: How does the democratic anomaly grade the Indian case?

It treats India as the closest thing the century produced to a controlled comparison for communism in office. Elsewhere in the family's history, rule arrived with censorship, a single legal party and the abolition of any route by which voters could dismiss the rulers. In the Indian states, a communist movement held office for decades with none of those instruments, exposed throughout to courts, newspapers, rivals and elections it could and did lose. Results obtained under those conditions cannot be credited to repression, just as failures under them cannot be blamed on it. What the comparison proves is possibility within limits: redistribution and welfare delivered constitutionally. What it cannot prove is that the road continued past those limits toward the destination the programme named.

### Q: What should a student separate between governing a state and transforming an economy?

The jurisdictions. A state government commands land, agriculture, education, health, police and local administration, which is enough to reform tenancy, redistribute ceiling surplus land and build welfare systems. It does not command currency, banking, foreign trade, industrial licensing in the planning era or the fiscal transfers its budget depends on, all of which sit with the centre. Governing a state therefore tests a party's administrative and redistributive capacities under another government's economy. Transforming an economy would require the commanding heights no Indian state ministry ever held. Confusing the two tests produces both the inflated claim and the inflated refutation.

### Q: Why did communist parties win state power but not national power?

Their strength was depth in a few states rather than breadth across the country, and the electoral system rewards exactly that profile at provincial level while starving it nationally: concentrated votes capture states, while a minority scattered thinly across hundreds of constituencies converts into almost no seats at the centre. The strongholds, moreover, were linguistically and culturally distinctive places, which limited the export of their appeal. Office then absorbed the organisers a national campaign would have required, and incumbency softened the edge an opposition must keep. The parties shaped national politics at coalition high points, but shaping is not holding, and their own strategists understood the problem without solving it.

### Q: How does the streams table order the tradition?

By question rather than by fame. Each of its four rows is a stream defined by the problem it refused to drop: the parties by power inside a parliamentary federation, the historians by the periodisation of the Indian past, the economists by the character of the agrarian and colonial economy, the social theorists by the articulation of caste and class. The columns add the period of defining work, the principal figures and the companion article carrying the full treatment. Read across a row to learn a stream; read down the questions column to learn the field, since the four questions together describe what Indian Marxism was for.

### Q: What separates social democratic administration from a distinctively Marxist government in this debate?

The treatment of property. Social democratic administration moves money rather than ownership: it taxes, transfers and funds services while the ownership of land and capital stays exactly where it stood. A distinctively Marxist government, on the critics' test, would move against the property relations themselves. The Indian debate turns on where the record falls. The defence points to tenancy abolition, sharecropper registration and homestead rights as alterations of property in the countryside's decisive asset, and to decentralisation as a change in who staffs the state. The critics answer that a province inside a capitalist federation could do no more than reform, which is precisely their point about the label.
