An election verdict is usually read for what it ends. The 2014 election, the inflection that returned Narendra Modi and the Bharatiya Janata Party with the first single-party Lok Sabha majority since 1984, has been read that way for years, as the closing entry of the coalition era. The reading defended here looks in the opposite direction, at what the verdict made possible. Within twenty months of the counting of votes on 16 May 2014, the new government had rebuilt the machinery through which every later decision would travel: a Prime Minister's Office configured as the coordination centre of the executive, a NITI Aayog installed where the Planning Commission had allocated resources among governments for six decades, a cabinet committee system stripped of the intermediary layers that coalition bargaining had deposited, and a personnel configuration across the secretariat, the regulatory bodies and the constitutional commissions that carried the arrangement into daily administration. Demonetisation in November 2016, the goods and services tax in July 2017 and the Article 370 decisions of August 2019 were policies chosen by a political leadership. Each was also an operation executed through an architecture that the 2014 verdict had enabled and that no coalition-era government possessed. That enablement, rather than the mandate alone, is the substantive consequence of the 2014 inflection.

## The 2014 Election as Two Inflections: Closure and Enablement

The two readings of the 2014 verdict begin from the same count. The Election Commission of India, in its Statistical Report on the General Election of 2014, recorded 282 Lok Sabha seats and 31.0 percent of the valid vote for the Bharatiya Janata Party, 336 seats for the National Democratic Alliance, and 44 seats with 19.3 percent of the vote for the Indian National Congress, on a turnout of 66.4 percent. Narendra Modi took the oath as Prime Minister on 26 May 2014. A companion analysis in this series reads those figures as a closure: the verdict's meaning lies in [the 2014 verdict's structural analogue to 1971](/2019/07/03/2014-inflection-election-reanalysed/), the Garibi Hatao landslide of Indira Gandhi, read against the post-Mandal electoral arithmetic that had fragmented every Lok Sabha between 1989 and 2014. On that reading, 2014 is an inflection because a twenty-five year structure of coalition government ended, and the reading is correct as far as it goes.

This analysis takes the same verdict and asks what it opened. The distinction matters because the two readings explain different evidence. The closure reading explains the election: the collapse of the Congress organisation, the concentration of the anti-Congress vote, the personal authority of the winning candidate. The enablement reading explains the government: the sequence of institutional constructions undertaken between May 2014 and the middle of 2015, and the operational character of the decisions that followed from 2016 onward. An inflection is a point at which a curve changes direction. The coalition-era curve changed direction twice in 2014, once in the electoral arena and once in the architecture of the executive, and the second change is the one that governed how policy was made for the remainder of the decade.

The claim can be stated with precision. The 2014 verdict conferred three assets that no government since 1984 had held together: a single-party majority in the Lok Sabha, a Prime Minister whose authority within his party was unmediated by coalition partners or by a rival organisational centre, and a parliamentary position in which the executive's legislative programme faced no veto player inside the ruling arrangement. Those assets were converted, in the government's first year, into a durable configuration of offices, committees and appointments. The configuration is the architecture of this analysis, and it had four dimensions: the centralisation of coordination in the Prime Minister's Office, the replacement of the Planning Commission by NITI Aayog, the restructuring of the cabinet committee system together with a new layer of coordination among secretaries, and a personnel configuration that staffed the configuration with chosen officeholders. Each dimension is documented below from dated instruments: cabinet resolutions, Appointments Committee decisions, Cabinet Secretariat notifications and the Department of Personnel and Training's posting orders.

The architecture is also the mechanism that connects the verdict to [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) through which the Modi era renegotiated the founding settlement's permanent tensions over the religion-state boundary, the centre-state distribution of authority and the claims of social justice. Renegotiation of that order requires decisions that cross ministries, override settled departmental positions and hold a single line through implementation. The coalition-era executive, organised for bargaining among partners, was structurally unsuited to produce such decisions at speed. The post-2014 architecture was built to produce them, and the three decisions examined in this analysis, demonetisation, the goods and services tax and the Article 370 orders, are the decisions in which the architectural advantage showed itself most plainly.

One clarification governs everything that follows. To call the architecture an enabling condition is not to call it a cause of the policies it carried. The architecture did not decide that banknotes would be withdrawn in November 2016 or that Article 370 would be rendered inoperative in August 2019; political leadership decided those questions. The architecture determined the form the decisions took: the secrecy perimeter inside which they were prepared, the absence of intermediary forums in which they could be diluted, the speed of the interval between decision and execution, and the direct line from the Prime Minister's Office to the officials charged with delivery. Electoral analysis that stops at the mandate cannot see that form. Institutional analysis that ignores the mandate cannot explain where the authority to build the form came from. The integrated reading holds both, and it is the reading defended here.

The second asset deserves its own chronology, because the party's reconstruction is the least institutional-looking and most consequential of the three. On 9 July 2014 Amit Shah, the general secretary who had run the Uttar Pradesh campaign, became president of the Bharatiya Janata Party, succeeding Rajnath Singh on his move into the government. The party then ran a membership drive through 2014 and into March 2015 at the end of which it claimed a membership of one hundred million, a figure the party presented as making it the largest political party in the world; the number is the party's own and is attributed here to its claim. The organisational meaning of the drive was to convert a cadre-based party with an election-season apparatus into a standing mass-membership organisation whose booth-level structures persisted between elections, and to concentrate the command of that organisation in a presidency held by the Prime Minister's closest political associate. The verdict supplied the legitimacy for the conversion; the conversion supplied the discipline that made the executive's internal unanimity durable. Electoral analysts writing on the period, Milan Vaishnav of the Carnegie Endowment among them, placed 2014 in the sequence of India's critical elections, verdicts that reorganise the party system itself; the argument of this analysis is that the critical character of the election showed at least as clearly in the reorganisation of the executive as in the reorganisation of competition.

The construction window is itself a datable sequence, and laying it out exposes how little of the architecture was left to drift. The ministerial groups were abolished on 31 May 2014. The cabinet committees were reconstituted in June 2014. The Planning Commission's replacement was announced on 15 August 2014 and effected on 1 January 2015. The PRAGATI review platform began on 25 March 2015. The Groups of Secretaries reported in January 2016 and were renewed in sectoral form in October 2016. Lateral entry was advertised in June 2018. The committee system was reconstituted with two new bodies on 6 June 2019. Five years, eight dated instruments, four dimensions: the sequence has the rhythm of a programme rather than a series of improvisations, and it is the rhythm that a pure drift account of the period has to explain away.

The vocabulary of enabling conditions comes from the institutional literature rather than from electoral commentary. Devesh Kapur's work on the Indian state, including the research programme gathered in Public Institutions in India (2005) with Pratap Bhanu Mehta, insists that the performance of a government is set less by its intentions than by the capacity and configuration of the institutions through which intentions must pass. A verdict changes intentions at the top overnight; it changes configuration only if the winners spend their first months rebuilding it. The Modi government spent its first months doing exactly that, ahead of its first full budget and ahead of every major policy it is remembered for. That ordering, architecture before policy, is the factual spine of the enablement reading, and it is what distinguishes the reading from the standard narrative in which 2014 matters because a strong leader arrived. Leaders had arrived before. The rebuilding, in this sequence and at this speed, had not.

The verdict's internal composition is part of the enablement, and the Election Commission's figures state it precisely. The Bharatiya Janata Party won its 282 seats on 31.0 percent of the national vote, the lowest vote share ever to produce a single-party majority in the Lok Sabha, and the alliance total of 336 seats rested on the concentration of that vote in the states where the party contested most strongly. The 2014 verdict was therefore a manufactured majority of exceptional efficiency: the electoral system converted a plurality into command of the House, and the command, not the plurality, was the asset the architecture consumed. Political scientists who describe the verdict as a mandate speak of its direction; the enablement reading speaks of its mechanics. A party that had won 282 seats on a dispersed 40 percent would have held the same legal capacity with a different internal politics, since a broader base usually carries broader internal claims. The concentrated verdict left the leadership unusually free of internal creditors, which is one more reason the first twenty months could be spent on construction rather than on payment.

A counterfactual test disciplines the claim, and the series' own chronology supplies the controls. The minority and coalition governments of 1989 to 1991 and 1996 to 1998 disposed of the same constitutional instruments, the same ordinance power, the same Appointments Committee and the same Planning Commission, and their institutional construction was close to nil: V. P. Singh's government is remembered for a policy decision, the implementation of the Mandal Commission's recommendations in August 1990, not for any rebuilding of the machinery that took it, and the United Front governments of 1996 to 1998 governed through a steering committee of coalition partners that replicated, outside the cabinet, the bargaining the system was designed to contain. The contrast isolates the variable. Where the verdict fragments authority, the executive's energy is consumed in holding itself together and none remains for construction; where the verdict concentrates authority and the leadership holds a design, construction is the first use the surplus is put to. The enablement reading of 2014 is the application of that generalisation to the strongest case of concentration since 1984, and the controls of 1989 and 1996 are what entitle it to claim the verdict, rather than the decade or the leader alone, as the enabling cause.

## Dimension One: The Prime Minister's Office as Coordination Centre

The first dimension was assembled in the government's first week, and its founding appointments carry dates that repay attention. Narendra Modi took office on 26 May 2014. On 28 May 2014, two days later, the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet named Nripendra Misra, a retired Uttar Pradesh cadre officer of the 1967 batch of the Indian Administrative Service and a former chairman of the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, as Principal Secretary to the Prime Minister. On 30 May 2014 the same committee named Ajit Doval, a Kerala cadre officer of the 1968 batch of the Indian Police Service and a former director of the Intelligence Bureau, as National Security Advisor. In June 2014 Pramod Kumar Mishra, a Gujarat cadre officer of the 1972 batch who had served as principal secretary to Modi in Gandhinagar between 2001 and 2004, joined the office as Additional Principal Secretary. Bhaskar Khulbe, an officer of the 1983 batch, served as a Secretary to the Prime Minister within the same configuration. Four appointments, each made inside the government's first month, placed a complete coordination team above the ministries before the government's first budget had been presented.

### Why did appointing a Principal Secretary require an ordinance in May 2014?

Section 5(8) of the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India Act of 1997 barred a former chairperson of the regulator from further government employment, and Misra had chaired the authority from 2006 to 2009. The government promulgated an ordinance on 28 May 2014 amending the Act to relax the bar, and Parliament replaced it with an Amendment Act in July 2014.

The ordinance episode is the earliest documentary evidence for the dimension. Governments reveal their institutional priorities in the friction they are willing to absorb. The Modi government absorbed a parliamentary controversy, and criticism from the Congress and the Left parties that the law was being amended for one individual, in order to place its chosen officer at the head of the Prime Minister's Office on its own timetable. The episode also established a pattern that recurs across the architecture's construction: where an existing rule obstructed a configuration the leadership wanted, the rule was amended, replaced or routed around by a dated instrument, rather than allowed to veto the configuration by default.

The office's working relationship with the permanent administration was set in the same first fortnight. On 4 June 2014 the Prime Minister met the secretaries of all the ministries and departments together, an address to the assembled heads of the permanent executive that the press of the day recorded as the first of its kind in years, and set before them a charter of priorities that included time-bound decision-making, the simplification of rules and direct accountability for outcomes. The secretaries were also given to understand that they could approach the Prime Minister directly on questions stuck in process, a channel that bypassed the ministerial layer and was understood across the secretariat as an instruction about where the executive's effective centre lay. The meeting of 4 June 2014 is the soft instrument that accompanied the hard instruments of appointment and committee design, and its message was architectural rather than exhortatory: the flow of decision was being re-routed, and the re-routing was announced to the people through whom decisions flow.

What the assembled office did with its position is the substance of the centralisation claim, and the claim should be stated in its documented form. The Prime Minister's Office under Misra operated as the point at which inter-ministerial questions were settled before they reached the cabinet. Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India (2021), describes cabinet meetings in this period as bodies that ratified decisions substantially settled in advance through the Prime Minister's Office, with ministers' offices in direct and frequent transaction with the principal secretary's staff rather than with each other. That characterisation is an interpretation, and it is attributed here to its author; the underlying documentary pattern is not in dispute. The office supervised ministries through review meetings chaired by the Prime Minister, monitored flagship programmes through the PRAGATI platform from March 2015, and received the reports of the Groups of Secretaries from January 2016, two coordination instruments examined in the cabinet committee dimension below.

The configuration was renewed rather than improvised at the end of the government's first term. After the 2019 election returned the Bharatiya Janata Party with 303 seats, the Appointments Committee upgraded Ajit Doval to cabinet rank on 3 June 2019 for a further five-year term, and reappointed Nripendra Misra and Pramod Kumar Mishra with the rank of cabinet minister in the table of precedence, with effect from 31 May 2019. The entity record of the series registers the succession that followed within the brief's documentary scope: Misra's tenure as Principal Secretary ran from 2014 to 2019, and Pramod Kumar Mishra succeeded to the principal secretaryship in 2019. A coordination team that is carried across an election, upgraded in rank and renewed in tenure has crossed the line from a leader's personal staff to an institution, which is the conversion that [the personality-as-institution framework](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/) was built to describe in the case of Indira Gandhi. The parallel is analytical, not decorative: the framework holds that a leader's personal authority becomes politically durable when it is deposited in offices that survive the occasions of their creation, and the post-2014 Prime Minister's Office is the clearest deposit of that kind in the period.

Two cautions discipline the dimension. The first is comparative. The office of Principal Secretary to the Prime Minister was not invented in 2014; P. N. Haksar had run a powerful secretariat for Indira Gandhi from 1967, and the antecedent question is addressed in full later in this analysis. The second is evidential. Much of the Prime Minister's Office's coordination work leaves no public record, and the strongest claims about its reach rest on the accounts of participants and scholars rather than on published minutes. The documentary floor of the dimension is nonetheless solid: the appointments, the ordinance, the cabinet-rank upgrades and the review instruments are all matters of dated public record, and the interpretation built on them in this analysis stays within what that record supports.

The security side of the office acquired institutional form of its own, and the dates show the same pattern of construction. In April 2018 the Ministry of Defence notified a Defence Planning Committee, chaired by the National Security Advisor, to prepare a national security strategy and review capability development across the armed forces: a permanent planning body for the security establishment, placed under the Prime Minister's principal security official rather than under the defence ministry's own hierarchy. In October 2018 the Cabinet Secretariat reconstituted the Strategic Policy Group, the apex inter-ministerial body on national security, with the National Security Advisor as its chairman in place of the Cabinet Secretary who had chaired it since its creation in 1999. Ajit Doval thus came to preside over the two bodies through which long-horizon security planning and inter-agency coordination ran, an accumulation of chairmanships in one officeholder that mirrors, inside the security domain, the accumulation the Prime Minister's Office performed across the whole executive. Brajesh Mishra had combined the posts of principal secretary and National Security Advisor under Atal Bihari Vajpayee between 1998 and 2004, and that dual-hatting is the recognised antecedent; the post-2014 arrangement went further, separating the posts and then building standing institutions under the security advisor alone.

Beneath the named principals, the office was staffed as an institution rather than a household. Rajeev Topno, a Gujarat cadre officer of the 1996 batch, served as private secretary to the Prime Minister from 2014, one of a layer of joint secretaries and directors drawn substantially, though not exclusively, from officers the leadership had worked with in Gujarat or in the ministries of the first year. The staffing pattern matters for the same reason the principals' dates matter: a coordination centre is a flow of paper and access, and the post-2014 office arranged the flow so that a ministry's route to a decision ran through officials whose tenure depended on the Appointments Committee, whose calendar was set by the office's review platforms, and whose reporting habit pointed toward the centre. Students of the period who describe a Gujarat model transplanted to Delhi overstate the personnel share and understate the design, but the perception itself, recorded across the journalism of 2014 and 2015, registers the speed with which the office's configuration became the executive's common knowledge.

The office's review practice reached down to the project level in the first hundred days, and the record of those months shows the pattern before it had a platform. Through June and July 2014 the Prime Minister took presentations from the infrastructure ministries in sequence, power, coal, highways, railways, and set targets in the room, with the ministries' secretaries present and the decisions minuted through the Cabinet Secretariat. The coal and power reviews of that season addressed the fuel linkages and stranded generation capacity that the outgoing government had left to ministerial groups; the questions that the Empowered Groups of Ministers had been constituted to settle were settled in the review room instead. The method was regularised the following March as PRAGATI, but its logic was fixed in the first summer: the centre would hold the calendar, the ministries would report against dates, and the chain of accountability would run from the secretary's desk to the Prime Minister's screen without an intervening chamber of negotiation.

## Dimension Two: NITI Aayog and the End of Allocation Planning

The second dimension removed an institution before it built one. The Planning Commission had been created by a resolution of the Government of India dated 15 March 1950, with Jawaharlal Nehru as its first chairman, and for six decades it performed two functions that no other body performed together: it wrote the five-year plans that framed public investment, and it allocated central assistance to the states, a flow governed from 1969 by the Gadgil formula and its later revisions. Allocation made the Commission a bargaining counter in centre-state relations. A chief minister travelled to Delhi to negotiate a plan size, and the negotiation, repeated annually, was one of the few structured occasions on which the Union government had to answer state governments collectively about money. The National Development Council, constituted on 6 August 1952 and composed of the Prime Minister, Union ministers and every chief minister, approved each plan and gave the bargaining a constitutional-adjacent stage.

Narendra Modi announced the Commission's replacement in his Independence Day address of 15 August 2014, arguing from the Red Fort that the institution belonged to an economic structure that had passed. The instrument followed on 1 January 2015, when a resolution of the Union Cabinet established the National Institution for Transforming India, NITI Aayog, with the Prime Minister as chairperson, Arvind Panagariya, a Columbia University economist, as its first vice chairman, and a Governing Council of chief ministers and lieutenant governors in place of the National Development Council. The Governing Council held its first meeting on 8 February 2015. Rajiv Kumar succeeded Panagariya as vice chairman on 1 September 2017, and Amitabh Kant served as chief executive officer from 2016. The Twelfth Five-Year Plan, covering 2012 to 2017, ran out its term on 31 March 2017 and was not succeeded by a Thirteenth; NITI Aayog instead issued a three-year action agenda in August 2017, covering 2017-18 to 2019-20, as the first instalment of a longer vision document sequence that carried no allocations.

### What happened to the National Development Council after 2014?

The National Development Council was never abolished by a formal instrument, and it never met again. It had approved the Twelfth Five-Year Plan at its fifty-seventh meeting on 27 December 2012. After the Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015 created the NITI Aayog Governing Council with substantially the same membership, the older body lapsed into desuetude.

### What did the Fourteenth Finance Commission change in February 2015?

The Fourteenth Finance Commission, chaired by Y. V. Reddy, reported in December 2014, and the Union government accepted its central recommendation in February 2015: the states' share in the divisible pool of union taxes rose from 32 percent to 42 percent, the largest increase in tax devolution in the Commission's history.

The architectural significance of the dimension lies in what the Governing Council cannot do. It meets at the Prime Minister's summons, deliberates on strategy documents and passes no allocations, because the institution it serves holds no funds to allocate. The Planning Commission's successor is a think tank with a federal council attached, and the council's composition, which looks like a concession to federal consultation, should not be confused with a share in fiscal decision. State governments retained channels of influence: the Finance Commission's constitutional cycle, the goods and services tax Council examined later in this analysis, and bilateral negotiation with Union ministries. What they lost was the annual, institutionalised, all-states-together negotiation over the size and shape of central assistance, a loss that Pratap Bhanu Mehta and other contributors to the institutional literature had anticipated in a different register: Devesh Kapur and Pratap Bhanu Mehta, in their edited volume Public Institutions in India (2005), documented the long decline of the Commission's technocratic authority, and the post-2014 sequel essays in that research programme record the completion of the decline by abolition rather than reform.

Yamini Aiyar, president of the Centre for Policy Research, argued in essays of the period that the post-2014 settlement recentralised initiative in the Union executive even where it enlarged state fiscal space, because the design of flagship programmes, the monitoring of their delivery and the credit claimed for them all ran through the Prime Minister's Office and its platforms. Defenders of the change answer on the same evidence: the plan-era bargaining forum had degenerated, in the description of the Cabinet resolution's own preamble logic, into a one-size allocation exercise ill-matched to states at very different levels of economic structure, and competitive federalism among states required a strategy body rather than a disbursing one. Both positions are rendered here at their strongest because the architectural fact beneath them is the same, and it is the fact that matters for the decisions of 2016 to 2019: after January 2015, no institution stood between the Union executive and the states with an independent allocative function of its own. When the goods and services tax required a centre-state bargaining forum in 2016, one had to be invented inside the Constitution, and the terms of its invention, a council in which the Union holds one-third of the vote and decisions require three-fourths, bear the mark of the vacuum it filled.

The Governing Council's own calendar records the body's consultative rather than decisional character. After the first meeting of 8 February 2015, the Council met again on 15 July 2015, on 23 April 2017, on 17 June 2018 and on 15 June 2019: five meetings in more than four years, each convened by the Prime Minister, each organised around strategy documents and presentations prepared in Delhi. The Council's one experiment in delegated work came early. In 2015 three sub-groups of chief ministers were constituted under its umbrella, and the sub-group on centrally sponsored schemes, convened by the Madhya Pradesh chief minister Shivraj Singh Chouhan, reported in October 2015 in favour of reducing the number of schemes, raising the states' share of flexible funds and respecting state choice in design. The recommendations were absorbed selectively into the Union budget's scheme architecture. The episode shows the forum functioning as the design intended: a place where states could be heard in formation of policy, on questions framed by the centre, with the decision reserved to the Union executive.

Where the states needed a forum of their own, they built one outside the architecture, and the occasion is diagnostic. In April 2018 the finance ministers of the southern states met at Thiruvananthapuram, on the initiative of Kerala's finance minister Thomas Isaac, to concert opposition to the terms of reference of the Fifteenth Finance Commission, which had directed the Commission to use the population figures of the 2011 census and which the southern governments argued would penalise states that had succeeded in population control. A conclave of state finance ministers organising against a Union-appointed commission's mandate is the plan-era bargaining instinct surviving in exile: the collective negotiation that the National Development Council had housed until 2012 and the Governing Council did not house after 2015 migrated to ad hoc coalitions convened by the states themselves. The migration confirms the dimension from the outside. The bargaining did not disappear from Indian federalism; it lost its address inside the Union executive's building.

The institution's own succession and fiscal context complete the dimension. Arvind Panagariya resigned as vice chairman in August 2017 to return to Columbia University, and Rajiv Kumar, an economist from the Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry's research system, succeeded him on 1 September 2017, a change of the institution's head executed by the same appointing centre without parliamentary involvement, exactly as the resolution form permits. The fiscal ground shifted in the same season: the Union Budget for 2017-18 abolished the distinction between plan and non-plan expenditure that had structured Indian budgeting since the First Plan, following the recommendation of the committee the government had appointed on expenditure management. With the plan category went the last bookkeeping trace of the Commission's world, in which the size of a state's plan had been the measure of its relationship with Delhi. The resolution of 1 January 2015 created NITI Aayog with a provision for regional councils of states on specific issues, convened by the Prime Minister for fixed tenures; the provision existed on paper through the period, and its sparing use belongs to the same pattern, the forum held in reserve by the centre rather than owned by the members.

The institution also served, once, as the operational forum the design had kept in reserve, and the occasion connects the second dimension to the first operationalisation. When demonetisation disrupted payments in November 2016, NITI Aayog constituted on 30 November 2016 a committee of chief ministers on digital payments, chaired by the Andhra Pradesh chief minister Chandrababu Naidu, with the chief ministers of Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Maharashtra and other states as members, to recommend measures for expanding digital transactions. The committee reported in January 2017. The episode matters beyond its subject. At the moment of maximum administrative stress, the federal consultation the government needed was convened through the consultative body's machinery, under the centre's framing, on the centre's timetable: the Governing Council form, dormant for allocative purposes, proved available for operational ones. The announcement of the Commission's end had been made in the Independence Day address of 15 August 2014, in which the Prime Minister also spoke of Team India, a phrase the new institution's cooperative federalism language would formalise; the 2016 committee is where the language met a crisis.

## Dimension Three: Cabinet Committees, and the Layer Beneath Them

The third dimension restructured the forums in which the executive deliberated, and its first act was a subtraction. On 31 May 2014, five days after taking office, the Prime Minister abolished the nine Empowered Groups of Ministers and twenty-one Groups of Ministers inherited from the United Progressive Alliance, directing ministries to decide at their own level the questions the groups had been holding. The Prime Minister's Office statement of that date presented the abolition as a measure for faster decision and greater accountability. Institutionally, it removed the coalition era's characteristic device: the standing sub-cabinet forum in which allied parties and senior ministers negotiated a question to a standstill or a settlement before the cabinet saw it. The groups had been chaired in the preceding government by figures of independent weight, Pranab Mukherjee, P. Chidambaram and A. K. Antony among them, and their abolition announced that inter-ministerial disagreement would be resolved inside ministries, or escalated to the Prime Minister's Office, rather than composed in a ministerial marketplace.

The standing committees of the cabinet were reconstituted under the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules of 1961 in June 2014 and again after each reshuffle, with the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet, the Cabinet Committee on Security and the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs at the system's centre. The composition pattern of the period is the evidence for the dimension: the senior committees were kept small, chaired by the Prime Minister, and populated by the holders of the great offices, with the Prime Minister's Office servicing the flow of papers. Jaffrelot's account in Modi's India (2021) of cabinet government in this period, in which full cabinet meetings were frequently brief and decisions arrived at them substantially prepared, is the scholarly form of a description that serving officials of the period also gave to journalists: the committee system functioned as a ratifying layer over a coordination process that ran through the Prime Minister's Office and the Cabinet Secretariat, the inheritance, in altered form, of [the coalition-era renegotiation framework](/2019/05/15/coalition-era-1989-2014-explained/) inverted, bargaining displaced from the centre of the system to its margins.

Beneath the committees, the government built an instrument with no coalition-era precedent. In January 2016 the Prime Minister constituted eight Groups of Secretaries, drawn from the senior civil service and charged with producing transformative proposals across themes that included accelerated growth with inclusion, employment generation, energy conservation and farmer-centric agriculture. The groups presented their reports to the Prime Minister in a series of meetings in January 2016, in the presence of the full body of secretaries, and the exercise was institutionalised in October 2016 when ten further sectoral groups were formed to review the work of ministries and report by the end of November. The Groups of Secretaries matter architecturally because they created a channel through which the political executive set the civil service to generate policy in formations that cut across departmental boundaries, reporting to the Prime Minister in person. A departmental secretary's first audience for a new proposal was no longer the secretary's own minister alone.

The PRAGATI platform, launched on 25 March 2015, completed the layer. PRAGATI, the Pro-Active Governance and Timely Implementation platform, is a monthly video conference chaired by the Prime Minister on the fourth Wednesday of the month, in which Union secretaries and the chief secretaries of the states answer for stalled projects, pending programmes and public grievances drawn from the government's databases, with the Prime Minister's Office preparing the flagged items a week in advance. Its architectural function is disciplinary: the entire senior administration of the Union, and the chief secretaries of every state, are answerable in one room to one chair on a fixed calendar. State chief secretaries attend as respondents in a Union-chaired forum, a fact about the federal balance of the architecture that sits alongside the devolution settlement of 2015 and qualifies it.

The second term renewed the committee architecture with two additions that date the dimension's maturity. On 6 June 2019, a week after the second swearing-in, the government reconstituted eight cabinet committees and constituted two new ones: a Cabinet Committee on Investment and Growth, chaired by the Prime Minister with Amit Shah, Nirmala Sitharaman, Nitin Gadkari and Piyush Goyal as members, and a Cabinet Committee on Employment and Skill Development, a ten-member body also chaired by the Prime Minister. The reconstitution also displayed the personnel principle of the system in its purest form: the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet, the body that approves the senior staffing of the state, consisted of two members, the Prime Minister and the Minister of Home Affairs, Amit Shah, and Shah sat on all eight committees. A structure in which one minister is present at every table and the Prime Minister chairs the tables that decide is a structure designed for a single line of decision, and it was in place, in that form, eight weeks before the Article 370 decisions of August 2019 used it.

The 2014 reconstitution set the pattern that 2019 completed. The Cabinet Committee on Security as constituted in June 2014 consisted of the Prime Minister with Rajnath Singh at Home Affairs, Arun Jaitley at Finance and Defence in the government's first months, Sushma Swaraj at External Affairs and Manohar Parrikar at Defence from November 2014: the holders of the four great offices, and no one else. The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs was kept comparably tight around the finance and infrastructure portfolios. Small committees chaired by the Prime Minister have a double property that the period exploited. They decide quickly, because their members carry the portfolios that execution requires; and they leave no minutes of dissent in wider circulation, because the circle of the informed is the circle of the responsible. The coalition-era committee system had optimised for the opposite properties, breadth of assent first and speed second, which is why the 2014 reconstitution belongs in an analysis of the verdict's consequences rather than in a footnote on procedure.

The Cabinet Secretariat's part in the layer deserves its sentence, because the Secretariat is where committee government becomes administration. Pradeep Kumar Sinha became Cabinet Secretary in June 2015 and was extended repeatedly, holding the office across the 2019 election, the longest continuous tenure at that post in the period covered by this series. Through his Secretariat the decisions of the committees were circulated, their implementation was chased across ministries, and the Groups of Secretaries process was serviced: it was Sinha who presented the implementation review of the eight groups' recommendations to the Prime Minister on 27 October 2016, when the sectoral second round was commissioned. A coordination architecture needs a memory and a chasing mechanism as much as it needs a centre; the Secretariat under an extended-tenure Cabinet Secretary supplied both, and its continuity across the 2019 transition is one more instance of the period's preference for renewing its instruments rather than rotating them.

The follow-up meeting of 27 October 2016 shows the mechanism's second half, the conversion of proposals into administrative assignments. The Prime Minister's Office record of that meeting states that the follow-up action taken by the ministries and departments on the eight groups' suggestions was reviewed, with the Cabinet Secretary presenting the position, and that ten new groups were then constituted on sectoral themes, each named for a cluster of ministries, to report within a month. The cycle thus closed: generation of proposals outside the departmental line, presentation to the political head, assignment back into the departments with the centre monitoring execution, and renewal of the generating body for the next round. Administrative systems scholars describe such cycles as the difference between a committee and a routine; the groups of secretaries had become a routine by October 2016, with a cadence, a reporting line and a secretariat, which is to say they had become part of the architecture rather than an episode inside it.

The legislative record of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha measures the system's throughput. PRS Legislative Research, in its review of the Sixteenth Lok Sabha's term, recorded that Parliament passed legislation at a pace comparable to earlier full-term Houses, with the government's major economic bills, the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016, the goods and services tax amendment of 2016 and the real estate regulation Act of 2016 among them, carried through a bicameral legislature in which the ruling alliance lacked a Rajya Sabha majority for the entire term. Legislative management under those conditions ran through the Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs, through floor coordination by the parliamentary affairs ministry, and through the sequencing of bills behind the ordinances the executive issued when a session's arithmetic failed. A committee system that ratifies internally and an ordinance power exercised without hesitation are the two halves of one arrangement, and the throughput of 2014 to 2019 is unintelligible without both.

The June 2019 reconstitution also redistributed the chairmanships that the Prime Minister did not keep, and the distribution is informative. The Cabinet Committee on Parliamentary Affairs was reconstituted under the chairmanship of Rajnath Singh, the defence minister and the government's senior-most figure after the Prime Minister and the Home Minister, a placement of the legislature's management with the member of the government whose seniority the opposition benches most readily acknowledged. The committee system's lower tier thus ran on seniority and trust, while its apex, security, appointments and the two new economic bodies, stayed with the Prime Minister and the Home Minister. The map of chairs in June 2019 is a miniature of the whole architecture: breadth where breadth cost nothing, concentration where decisions were irreversible.

## Dimension Four: The Personnel Configuration

Institutions are staffed before they are anything else, and the fourth dimension is the pattern of staffing that turned the other three dimensions from paper into practice. Its documentary base is the least read of the four: the appointment notifications of the Department of Personnel and Training, which record, date by date and name by name, the movement of secretaries, the empanelment of officers for senior posts, the selection of chairpersons and members of regulatory bodies, and the composition of the commissions. The notifications are public records, and they are the primary source a researcher must master to see the architecture whole; journalists who document personnel trails of this kind describe the craft of keeping such records safe and ordered in accounts such as [the VaultBook account of how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), and the personnel record of 2014 to 2019 rewards the same patience, because no single notification announces a configuration. The configuration is the accumulation.

The mechanism of accumulation was the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet, chaired by the Prime Minister with the Home Minister as its other member, through which every secretary-level appointment in the Government of India and every appointment to the great regulatory and constitutional offices passed. The committee's two-member form was not new; its use in this period was systematic in a specific way. Tenures at the top of ministries were kept short enough, and transfers frequent enough, that the secretariat's senior layer was repeatedly re-sorted by the centre; empanelment, the process by which officers are cleared for secretary rank, acquired from 2015 a multi-source feedback input, reported in the press of the period as a 360-degree appraisal, which widened the discretionary element in promotions that had previously run on seniority and annual reports alone. Critics inside and outside the services read the pattern as a loyalty filter; the government's stated rationale, in the Department of Personnel and Training's own descriptions, was performance assessment and the breaking of a seniority queue that rewarded survival over delivery. The dispute is noted here as a dispute; the architectural fact is the discretion itself, concentrated in a two-member committee serviced by the Prime Minister's Office.

The regulatory appointments show the configuration reaching beyond the secretariat. At the Reserve Bank of India, Raghuram Rajan's term as governor ended in September 2016 and Urjit Patel, a deputy governor, succeeded him on 4 September 2016; Patel resigned on 10 December 2018, after a public season of disagreement between the Bank and the government over reserves and regulatory forbearance, and the Appointments Committee named Shaktikanta Das, a retired finance secretary who had served as the government's economic affairs secretary during demonetisation, as governor on 11 December 2018. At the Securities and Exchange Board of India, Ajay Tyagi succeeded U. K. Sinha as chairperson on 1 March 2017. At the Election Commission, the chief election commissionership passed from Nasim Zaidi to Achal Kumar Jyoti in July 2017, to Om Prakash Rawat in January 2018 and to Sunil Arora on 2 December 2018. Each appointment was lawful, each was made through the constituted channel, and the pattern across them is the dimension's point: the offices that referee the economy and the electoral process were staffed, on a rolling basis, by a single appointing centre working to its own calendar.

The boldest personnel instrument of the period was lateral entry. In June 2018 the Department of Personnel and Training advertised ten joint secretary posts, in departments that included revenue, financial services, economic affairs, agriculture, road transport and civil aviation, open to specialists from outside the civil services on contract. The advertisement drew 6,077 applications, the Union Public Service Commission conducted the selection, and on 12 April 2019 the Commission recommended nine candidates for appointment. The numbers are small and the significance is structural: joint secretary is the rank at which policy files are framed for decision, and opening it to contract appointments from the market announced that the personnel configuration was not bound by the career service's own reproduction. The recommendation of the Second Administrative Reforms Commission era had finally been executed, by the government with the parliamentary strength to absorb the services' displeasure.

A comparison sharpens the dimension. The post-1980 reconstruction of the Prime Minister's office under Indira Gandhi, traced in the account of [the post-1980 office-rebuilding completed](/2019/05/04/1980-indira-return-power/) in this series, rebuilt a leader's secretariat after a return from opposition; the post-2014 configuration did that and more, because it extended the appointing centre's reach into the regulatory bodies and the election machinery on a published, rolling schedule, and because it was preceded by the removal of the forums, the ministerial groups and the allocation commission, in which alternative centres of staffing influence had sat. Personnel was the dimension that made the other three self-reproducing: committees decide as their members are chosen to decide, and an office that coordinates the choosers coordinates the system.

The configuration reached the constitutional commissions on the same calendar. The Fifteenth Finance Commission was appointed on 27 November 2017 under the chairmanship of N. K. Singh, a former revenue and expenditure secretary and member of the Rajya Sabha, with terms of reference that became, as recorded earlier, a federal controversy in themselves. The friction the configuration generated is also part of its record, and it surfaced most sharply inside the Reserve Bank: on 26 October 2018 the Bank's deputy governor Viral Acharya delivered a public lecture in Mumbai warning that governments which undermine the independence of a central bank incur the wrath of markets, a speech widely reported as a signal of the disagreement that ended, six weeks later, in Governor Urjit Patel's resignation. The episode is the personnel dimension's stress test. The appointing centre prevailed, a successor was installed within a day, and the cost was paid in the currency of institutional reputation, which the critics of centralisation banked and the defenders discounted. Both readings presuppose the configuration's existence, which is the point at issue in this analysis.

The personnel frontier kept moving to the end of the brief's scope. On 15 August 2019, in his Independence Day address, the Prime Minister announced the creation of a Chief of Defence Staff, a single-point military adviser to the government, a reform that committees had recommended since the Kargil Review Committee reported in 2000 and that no intervening government had executed. The announcement is personnel architecture in its purest form: a new apex office, created by executive decision, designed to end a committee-era dispersion of advice among three service headquarters. Its placement in the sequence of this analysis, in the same month as the Article 370 decisions, is not a coincidence of the calendar. It is the same governing method, applied to the last great unconsolidated secretariat of the Indian state.

The audit offices tell the quieter version of the same story. Rajiv Mehrishi, who had been home secretary and finance secretary, was appointed Comptroller and Auditor General in September 2017 and held the office through the period; the Election Commission's senior membership turned over on schedule through Appointments Committee selections, with Sunil Arora taking office as Chief Election Commissioner on 2 December 2018, in time to conduct the 2019 general election. None of these appointments breached a convention or a statute, and that regularity is the analytically interesting fact. The personnel dimension did not work by violation; it worked by the continuous, lawful exercise of a discretion that the Constitution and the service rules had always vested in the political executive, exercised without the coalition-era habit of distributing the choices among partners, factions and regional claims. What changed after 2014 was not the location of the appointing power but the number of hands on it.

The empanelment process supplied the configuration with its quietest and most pervasive instrument. Press reporting of 2015 and 2016, in The Hindu and The Indian Express, described the government's introduction of a 360-degree appraisal into the empanelment of officers for joint secretary and secretary positions, under which the assessments of peers, subordinates and stakeholders supplemented the confidential reports on which empanelment had rested, and described panels returning smaller lists than the services proposed. The Department of Personnel and Training defended the change as a widening of the evidence base for selection; the services' associations regarded it, in the reporting, as a discretion widened at the centre. The attributed dispute is noted here for what it shows about the dimension's reach: the personnel configuration extended below the named appointments, into the promotion machinery through which every senior officer's prospects pass, and it is at that depth, rather than at the level of the famous names, that a configuration becomes a climate.

The map also states the analysis's limit in tabular form. The right-hand column records enablement, not authorship: the decisions were chosen by the political leadership, and a different leadership with the same architecture could have chosen otherwise. What the architecture foreclosed was not choice but friction, the delays, leaks, dilutions and vetoes through which the coalition-era machinery had processed every hard decision between 1989 and 2014.

The map repays a slow reading, because each row records the same substitution performed in a different material. In the first row the material is attention: a centre that reviews projects monthly, reads ministry files directly and receives the secretaries' reports in person converts the Prime Minister's attention from a scarce favour into a scheduled administrative resource, and departments reorganise themselves around its calendar. In the second row the material is money's forum: a Union that distributes no plan funds has no annual negotiation to lose, and state governments learn that their relationship with Delhi runs through tax devolution formulas, council votes and scheme guidelines rather than through the plan holiday of the old Delhi season. In the third row the material is agenda: a committee system small enough to ratify and serviced by the Cabinet Secretariat, supplemented by secretary groups that generate the papers the committees ratify, relocates the deliberation of the executive to rooms the centre convenes. In the fourth row the material is careers: a two-member Appointments Committee, an empanelment shield relaxed for the office's own head, and a lateral door opened in 2018 together make the senior service's prospects legible as a function of the centre's confidence. Attention, the forum for money, the agenda and careers: a verdict that changed all four within twenty months changed the government, and the three decisions examined next are the proof of use.

## The Architecture at Work: Demonetisation, 8 November 2016

The demonetisation of 8 November 2016 is the cleanest test of the enablement claim, because its operational form was impossible under the preceding architecture and routine under the new one. At 8 p.m. that evening the Prime Minister addressed the nation and announced that the 500-rupee and 1,000-rupee banknotes of the Mahatma Gandhi series would cease to be legal tender at midnight, withdrawing, on the Reserve Bank's figures, about 86 percent of the currency in circulation by value. The legal sequence was compressed into the day itself. The Reserve Bank has stated, in reply to applications under the Right to Information Act, that the recommendation to withdraw the notes was made at a meeting of its Central Board in New Delhi on 8 November 2016; Urjit Patel repeated the substance of that account in his evidence to the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance in January 2017. The Union cabinet was informed on the same evening, in a meeting that preceded the broadcast. A decision to extinguish the greater part of a currency between breakfast and midnight, carried through a central bank board, a cabinet and a broadcast inside one day, is a fact about machinery before it is a fact about policy.

The machinery's features are each traceable to a dimension. The secrecy perimeter was a product of the first dimension: preparation ran through the Prime Minister's Office and a small circle in the finance ministry and the Reserve Bank, with the Economic Affairs Secretary, Shaktikanta Das, as the government's operational face in the weeks that followed; the governor who chaired the board that recommended withdrawal had been appointed two months earlier under the fourth dimension. The absence of any intermediary forum was a product of the third: no Empowered Group of Ministers existed to which a question of this sensitivity would earlier have been referred, and the cabinet's role was confirmatory on the day. The precedent for a single executive stroke of this scale in Indian economic administration is the bank nationalisation of July 1969, executed by ordinance under Indira Gandhi, and the series' account of [the 1969 nationalisation as executive method](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/) shows the same preference for the ordinance and the fait accompli; the difference is that in 1969 the stroke was an improvisation against the party's own old guard, and in 2016 it was the designed output of a standing architecture.

The merits of demonetisation are disputed, and the dispute is marked here rather than settled, because the substantive adjudication belongs to the analysis of the decision itself. Raghuram Rajan, Patel's predecessor, stated publicly in September 2017 that the Reserve Bank had been consulted in February 2016 and had conveyed orally that the short-term costs of withdrawal could outweigh the longer-term benefits, a statement the government side answered by pointing to the board's formal recommendation of 8 November. The Reserve Bank's Annual Report of August 2018 recorded that 99.3 percent of the withdrawn notes had returned to the banking system, a figure each side of the argument reads in its own favour. What is not disputed by either side is the procedural record: the decision was prepared in secret, taken in a day and implemented through the banking system without prior parliamentary or cabinet-committee deliberation. That record is the architecture's signature, and it is legible whatever verdict history returns on the policy.

The execution instruments that followed the announcement show the same compression. The exchange and deposit window ran to 30 December 2016 under a sequence of Reserve Bank and finance ministry notifications that the Economic Affairs Secretary defended in near-daily public briefings, a communication structure in which one secretary, rather than a committee of ministers, carried the government's case. On 30 December 2016 the President promulgated the Specified Bank Notes (Cessation of Liabilities) Ordinance, extinguishing the Reserve Bank's liability on the withdrawn notes, and Parliament converted the ordinance into an Act in February 2017. The legal tail of the largest administrative operation of the decade was thus closed by the same instrument, ordinance first and ratification after, that had opened the architecture's own construction with the TRAI amendment of May 2014.

One element of the demonetisation record remains disputed, and the dispute is itself evidence about the architecture. The opposition parties, led in Parliament by the former Prime Minister Manmohan Singh, who called the measure organised loot and legalised plunder in the Rajya Sabha on 24 November 2016, alleged that a decision of this scale must have been prepared over months and demanded to know who had been consulted and when. The government's account held that preparation was confined to a small circle for reasons of secrecy inherent in the measure, and the Reserve Bank's replies placed the formal recommendation on the day of the announcement itself. Whether the preparation ran for weeks or for months is contested on the record; what neither side's account disputes is that the circle was small, that the cabinet's involvement was terminal rather than deliberative, and that no institution outside the Prime Minister's Office and the central bank possessed the information in time to obstruct. A system in which the only arguments about a decision concern how long the centre held it, and not which forum released it, is a system the 2014 construction built.

The delivery infrastructure through which the operation's social costs were managed was also a construction of the period, and the chronology joins the sections of this analysis together. The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, launched on 28 August 2014, had opened zero-balance bank accounts on a mass scale; the finance ministry's figures recorded more than 240 million such accounts by the months preceding November 2016. The Economic Survey of 2014-15 had named the combination of Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar identification and mobile telephony the JAM trinity and proposed it as the state's new delivery rail for subsidies. In November 2016 the same accounts became the banking system's intake channel for the deposits of a cash economy, and the direct-benefit-transfer machinery built on the trinity carried the compensating payments and the publicity of the exchange period. Whether the deposit surge represented inclusion or distress is part of the disputed merits; the institutional fact is that the architecture of 2014 had built, for other purposes, the only channel through which a currency withdrawal could be administered at all, and the decision's feasibility is partly a function of that prior construction.

The fiscal aftercare of the operation ran through the same executive channel. The Taxation Laws (Second Amendment) Act of December 2016 carried the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Yojana, a disclosure window under which unaccounted cash could be declared at an effective charge of about half, with a quarter of the declared sum locked into an interest-free deposit for four years. The scheme was drafted in the revenue department, piloted through Parliament in the winter session's closing days and administered by the same secretaries who had run the exchange operation, a legislative tail executed in five weeks. Whatever judgement is passed on the operation's economics, its administrative biography is uniform from announcement to aftercare: a small authoring centre, statutory instruments produced at speed, and a secretariat that carried the scheme from the cabinet's information to the tax department's counters without a deliberative chamber intervening at any stage.

## The Architecture at Work: The Goods and Services Tax and the Council Form

The goods and services tax tested the architecture in the opposite direction: not a decision taken without bargaining, but a bargain that had to be rebuilt inside a new institution because the old bargaining machinery had been dismantled. The Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act received the President's assent on 8 September 2016, after ratification by the legislatures of more than half the states, and inserted Article 279A, under which the President constituted the Goods and Services Tax Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister, in September 2016. The Council held its first meeting on 22 and 23 September 2016 under the chairmanship of Arun Jaitley, and the tax was launched at midnight on 1 July 2017.

### Who chaired the Goods and Services Tax Council at its first meeting in September 2016?

Arun Jaitley, the Union Finance Minister, chaired the Council from its constitution, as Article 279A requires the Union Finance Minister to preside. Jaitley chaired the first meeting on 22 and 23 September 2016 and the rate-setting sessions of May and June 2017 that preceded the launch of the tax on 1 July 2017.

The Council's voting arithmetic is the architectural core. The Union government's vote carries one-third of the total weight, the states together carry two-thirds, and a decision requires three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting. The arithmetic gives the Union a permanent veto, since no three-fourths majority can be assembled against its third, and gives the states collectively the same protection against the Union. In practice the Council of 2016 to 2019 decided by consensus, and the consensus was real negotiation: state finance ministers, among them Kerala's Thomas Isaac, argued rates, exemptions and compensation in the open and extracted concessions, including the statutory guarantee of compensation for revenue loss that the states had demanded as their price. The contrast with the plan era is exact. Under the Planning Commission, the states bargained with a disbursing body whose allocations the Union controlled administratively; under the Council, the states bargain inside a constitutional body whose rules the Union cannot alter alone, and the Union's security lies in its veto rather than in discretionary control of the purse. The architecture did not abolish centre-state bargaining; it relocated bargaining into a form the 2014 settlement designed, on terms set by the member with the blocking share. That relocation, executed within thirty months of the verdict, is the strongest single evidence that the enablement reading captures something the mandate reading cannot: a disposition of institutional design, exercised consistently across unlike cases.

The Council also had an ancestor, and the ancestor's fate measures the change. The Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers, constituted in 2000 and chaired for a decade by West Bengal's finance minister Asim Dasgupta, had carried the states' side of the tax reform negotiation through the value added tax settlement and into the goods and services tax design. The Empowered Committee was a creature of the finance ministry's convenience, without constitutional standing, and its work stalled whenever the Union finance minister of the day chose delay, as the reform stalled between 2011 and 2014. The 101st Amendment constitutionalised the forum: the Council cannot be dissolved by the member it most constrains, its voting weights are in the constitutional text, and its secretariat sits in Delhi under the Union finance minister's chairmanship. The states traded the informality of the Empowered Committee for the permanence of the Council, and the Union traded the discretion of delay for the security of the veto. The amendment's own passage displayed the new bargaining at full stretch: the Rajya Sabha passed the amendment bill on 3 August 2016 only after the Congress, the largest opposition party, was brought inside the settlement, Assam became the first state to ratify on 12 August 2016, and the ratifications of more than half the states were completed in time for the presidential assent of 8 September 2016, a ratification campaign run by the finance ministry in five weeks.

The compensation settlement completes the picture of the Council as designed bargaining. The states' price for surrendering their independent indirect tax powers was a statutory guarantee: the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act of 2017 assured every state fourteen percent annual growth in its protected revenue for five years, funded by a compensation cess on demerit goods. A guarantee of that generosity is not the product of a forum the Union dominated at will; it is the product of a forum whose rules forced the Union to buy consent it could not command. The architecture reading accommodates the fact without strain. The post-2014 state was built to remove vetoes over the executive's own decisions, not to abolish negotiation where the Constitution itself required joint decision; and in the one domain where joint decision was constitutionally entrenched, the executive designed the joint body, chaired it, held its blocking share, and paid the asking price of the other members. Control of the forum, rather than exemption from forums, is the period's consistent institutional signature. The Council's cadence confirms the routinisation: it met for the thirty-fifth time on 21 June 2019, the first meeting chaired by Nirmala Sitharaman as finance minister, and the transfer of the chair with the portfolio, without disturbance to the body's calendar or voting practice, is the mark of an institution that had outgrown the individuals who negotiated its birth. A forum designed in 2016 was, by 2019, simply the way indirect taxation was governed in India, which is the fate the architecture's builders intended for all four of its dimensions.

The Council's operating troubles were themselves processed inside the design. When the goods and services tax network's filing systems faltered in the first filing season of 2017, the Council constituted a Group of Ministers, chaired by Bihar's deputy chief minister Sushil Kumar Modi, to oversee the technology remediation, and the group reported back to the Council through the autumn of 2017. A ministerial group inside the Council's structure, chaired by a state finance-side leader of the ruling party's Bihar unit, is the coalition-era device resurrected for a technical purpose and subordinated to the constitutional forum: evidence that the post-2014 executive's objection to the ministerial group was never to the form itself, but to the form as a chamber of political veto. Where a group advised, it could live; where it could block, it had been abolished in May 2014.

## The Architecture at Work: Article 370 and the Citizenship Amendment

The third operationalisation came after the locked date of this article's series position, and it is included because the brief governing this analysis fixes its evidence scope on the decisions the architecture enabled; the chronology is stated plainly so that the reader can place it. The 2019 election, whose counting took place on 23 May 2019, returned the Bharatiya Janata Party with 303 seats. The second Modi government took office on 30 May 2019, with Amit Shah entering the cabinet as Minister of Home Affairs on 1 June 2019. The cabinet committees were reconstituted, with the two additions recorded in the third dimension, on 6 June 2019.

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act's reception supplies the operationalisation sequence with its necessary counterweight. The Act, which made religion a criterion in a fast track to citizenship for specified minorities from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan who had entered India by 31 December 2014, was met from 15 December 2019 by the sit-in at Shaheen Bagh in Delhi, led substantially by Muslim women, and by protests across university campuses and in Assam, where the state's own movement organisations feared a settlement of the foreigners question contrary to the Assam Accord of 1985. The rules under which the Act would operate had not been notified within the period this analysis covers, and the Home Minister's repeated statements linking the Act to a subsequent register of citizens kept the measure's full design in dispute. The architectural reading takes no side in that dispute; it records that the same executive which moved the measure through both Houses in four days in December 2019 faced, in the street, the one forum its construction could not redesign, and that the ensuing months of the brief's aftermath belong to the analysis of the citizenship question itself.

Jammu and Kashmir had been under central rule since the Bharatiya Janata Party withdrew from Mehbooba Mufti's coalition government on 19 June 2018; Governor's rule followed, and President's rule was imposed on 19 December 2018 and extended in July 2019, which placed the powers of the state legislature with Parliament. On 5 August 2019 the President issued the Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order of 2019, numbered C.O. 272, which superseded the Order of 1954, applied the entire Constitution of India to the state, and, through an amendment of the interpretation Article 367, read the reference to the state's Constituent Assembly in Article 370 as a reference to its Legislative Assembly. The Rajya Sabha adopted the statutory resolution and passed the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Bill the same day, with Amit Shah piloting both; on 6 August 2019 the President issued the declaration under Article 370(3), numbered C.O. 273, rendering Article 370 inoperative except as applied with modifications, and the Lok Sabha passed the Reorganisation Bill by 370 votes to 70. The Reorganisation Act received presidential assent on 9 August 2019 and took effect on 31 October 2019, constituting the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir with a legislature and the Union Territory of Ladakh without one.

Every step of that sequence ran on rails the architecture had laid. A personnel configuration placed the party's principal organiser at the Home Ministry eight weeks before the move. A committee system reconstituted in June 2019 put the same minister on every standing committee and the Appointments Committee. A parliamentary position built in 2014 and enlarged in 2019 supplied the majorities in both Houses on consecutive days, including, through President's rule, the concurrence of a state legislature that did not sit. The procedural route, the use of Article 367 to reinterpret Article 370 and of President's rule to substitute Parliament for the state assembly, was contested at the time by constitutional scholars and opposition parties, and petitions challenging the August decisions were filed in the Supreme Court; the contest is recorded here as a contest, and the merits of the Kashmir settlement belong to the dedicated analysis of the abrogation in this series. The architectural point survives any verdict on the merits: between the decision and its execution there stood no forum, no coalition partner, no allocation body and no committee in which the August sequence could have been stalled, and that absence was not an accident of August 2019. It was the design of 2014, operating in 2019. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, passed by Parliament on 11 December 2019 and assented to on 12 December 2019, extended the same mode into the religion-state tension, and its treatment likewise belongs to its own analysis; it is named here to complete the brief's sequence of operationalisations.

The parliamentary divisions of those two days quantify the position the architecture disposed of. In the Rajya Sabha the statutory resolution was adopted and the Reorganisation Bill passed on 5 August 2019 by 125 votes to 61, in a House where the ruling alliance lacked a majority of its own and assembled one from regional parties that were not its allies; in the Lok Sabha the Bill passed on 6 August by 370 votes to 70. A two-thirds-adjacent majority in the directly elected House, and a constructed majority in the federal House, for the most consequential centre-state measure since the reorganisation of 1956: the figures belong in an architectural account because assembling them was the work of the parliamentary management machinery the verdict had built and five years of committee and personnel practice had kept in order.

The personnel dimension closed the sequence on the ground. The National Security Advisor was present in Srinagar in the days following 5 August, touring the valley and meeting residents in Shopian and Srinagar in the second week of August in images the government released as evidence of calm; the security grid through which the decisions were implemented reported through the configuration his office chaired. When the Reorganisation Act took effect on 31 October 2019, Girish Chandra Murmu, a Gujarat cadre officer who had served as principal secretary to Modi in Gandhinagar, took office as the first Lieutenant Governor of the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir, and Radha Krishna Mathur as the first Lieutenant Governor of Ladakh. The staffing of the successor administrations by officers of the appointing centre's longest acquaintance is the fourth dimension operating at the exact point where the third operationalisation landed, and it completes the demonstration the architecture map tabulates.

## Antecedents: Drift, Acceleration and Consummation

The strongest objection to the enablement reading must be met in its strongest form. The objection holds that the architecture was not created in 2014 at all: strong prime ministerial offices existed under Indira Gandhi, whose principal secretary P. N. Haksar ran a secretariat of formidable reach from 1967 to 1973; the Planning Commission had been losing authority since the liberalisation of 1991 reduced the share of investment the state directed; cabinet committees and ministerial groups were Vajpayee-era and United Progressive Alliance instruments, reconstituted by every government; and the United Progressive Alliance itself built an extra-cabinet centre in the National Advisory Council of 2004, chaired by Sonia Gandhi, which drafted legislation outside the cabinet system entirely. On this account, 2014 accelerated a drift toward executive centralisation that runs across parties and decades, and the enablement reading mistakes the acceleration for a creation.

The objection is correct about antecedents and wrong about what they prove, and the distinction is the one the brief of this analysis prescribes: antecedent presence is not architectural consummation. Haksar's secretariat was powerful because Indira Gandhi's political position after 1971 was commanding, and it decayed with her position; it was never renewed across an election as an upgraded institution, and the office she built did not survive her in its strong form, as the series' account of [the Emergency's institutional high-water mark](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/) demonstrates in the negative. The National Advisory Council proves the same point from the other side: it was an alternative centre created because the Prime Minister of the day did not command his own government's centre, a device of a divided executive. The post-2014 construction differs from both in the properties that define an institution rather than a court: it was built by dated public instruments in the government's first year, it integrated four dimensions that earlier centralisations had held separately, it renewed itself across the 2019 election with upgraded ranks, and it processed, between 2016 and 2019, three decisions of a scale that no antecedent arrangement since 1971 had processed at comparable speed. Drift names a direction; the years 2014 and 2015 supplied the destination, staffed and minuted.

The deeper antecedent is the constitutional one, and it returns the analysis to the series' founding frame. The Constitution of 1950 built a strong Union executive and left its internal organisation to convention and to the Transaction of Business Rules, which is why the architecture of 2014 could be constructed almost entirely without constitutional amendment: a cabinet resolution here, an appointments order there, a reconstitution notification, a single amendment of the Goods and Services Tax article aside. The asymmetry that [the federal design of the founding settlement](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) embedded, a Union stronger than the states in every concurrent field, waited sixty-four years for a parliamentary position capable of using it to the full. That is the final form of the enablement claim: the 2014 verdict did not write new constitutional powers; it supplied, for the first time since 1984, the political conditions under which the Constitution's existing centralising capacity could be organised into a system.

The United Progressive Alliance decade supplies the control case, because it centralised and dispersed at once. Sanjaya Baru, who served as media advisor to Manmohan Singh, published The Accidental Prime Minister in 2014, arguing from inside the office that the Prime Minister's authority was systematically shared with, and on political questions subordinated to, the president of the Congress party, with files and appointments moving between the two centres. The book was contested by the government it described, and its thesis is attributed here to its author; the institutional residue is not contested. The National Advisory Council, the ministerial groups chaired by heavyweights with their own followings, the Planning Commission under a deputy chairman of independent standing in Montek Singh Ahluwalia: the UPA executive was a federation of centres, each with a constituency, and its decisions bore the marks of composition among them. The post-2014 executive's single-centre design is legible only against that federation. Where the UPA multiplied centres to hold a coalition together, the post-2014 construction abolished the subsidiary centres because no coalition required them, and concentrated the proceeds in an office whose holder had no partner to compose with.

The Planning Commission's long decline makes the same point on a slower clock. After 1991 the share of investment in the economy that public plans directed fell steadily, the Commission's own role narrowed toward the allocation of central assistance and the writing of documents whose horizon private capital ignored, and the institution's last deputy chairman served a full decade, from 2004 to 2014, without the abolition that economists across the spectrum periodically recommended. Decline, in other words, was a thirty-year condition and abolition was a one-day act. The enablement reading's claim is precisely that the distance between a condition and an act is where verdicts matter: the condition was available to every government from 1991, and the act required the parliamentary position, the internal party authority and the design preference that converged only in 2014. The drift objection, pressed to its conclusion, explains why the materials for the architecture lay ready; it cannot explain the date on the Cabinet resolution.

The 1970s contribute one more antecedent that sharpens the adjudication, because it isolates the variable of design intent. The Indira Gandhi years hosted a public debate about a committed bureaucracy, a doctrine associated with her government's rhetoric and defended by ministers such as Mohan Kumaramangalam, holding that the civil service should be committed to the elected government's programme rather than neutral between programmes. The debate announced an intention to politicise the permanent executive, and the intention was widely condemned and imperfectly executed: the Emergency's administrative excesses, later catalogued by the Shah Commission, showed commitment pursued through coercion rather than through institutional redesign. The post-2014 period inverted the formula. No doctrine of commitment was announced; the civil service's formal neutrality was never questioned in the government's public language; and the personnel configuration was rebuilt through appointments, tenures and empanelment, instruments that required no doctrine at all. Critics who reach for the 1970s analogy therefore miss the design difference: the earlier period declared a politicisation it could not routinise, and the later period routinised a configuration it never declared. The enablement reading holds the difference to be the substance of the 2014 inflection.

## What the Architecture Enabled, and What It Did Not

### Did the post-2014 architecture depend only on the size of the majority?

The majority was necessary and not sufficient. A majority of 282 seats conferred the legal capacity to legislate, appoint and amend, and the 2019 figure of 303 renewed it; capacity alone sat unused by earlier governments that possessed it, including the Congress government of 1984 with its larger majority. The construction converted capacity into architecture through deliberate instruments.

The limits of the architecture are as instructive as its reach. It enabled speed, secrecy and a single line of decision; it did not enable competence, consent or correctness, and the record supplies the counterweights in the government's own terms. The Reserve Bank governorship crisis of December 2018, in which Urjit Patel resigned after a public disagreement that included the government's unprecedented invocation of consultations under Section 7 of the Reserve Bank of India Act of 1934, showed a referee institution asserting a boundary the appointing centre had to negotiate rather than command. The goods and services tax, for all the Council's consensus, required the compensation guarantee and repeated rate corrections through 2017 and 2018, evidence that bargaining had been relocated rather than abolished. The Article 370 decisions were followed by litigation whose outcome lay in a court the architecture does not staff. An enabling condition widens the space of the possible for a leadership; it does not choose within the space, and the adjudication offered by this analysis claims no more.

The federal limits of the design were analysed within the period itself. Louise Tillin of the King's India Institute, in her work on Indian federalism under the Modi governments, treats the period as a renegotiation of the federal bargain conducted through fiscal instruments, the devolution award, the restructuring of centrally sponsored schemes and the tax council, rather than through constitutional amendment of the Seventh Schedule or the state list. That characterisation matters to the adjudication because it locates the federal change in the same register as the rest of the architecture: the constitutional text of centre-state relations survived intact, and the operating system beneath it was rewritten. A future government with different preferences inherits the text unchanged and the operating system transformed, and the distance between those two inheritances is the precise measure of what the election of 2014 enabled.

The architecture's domain was the Union executive, and the electoral record marks the boundary with unusual clarity. In February 2015, nine months after the verdict, the Aam Aadmi Party won sixty-seven of the seventy seats in the Delhi assembly election, reducing the Bharatiya Janata Party to three; in November 2015 the Grand Alliance of Janata Dal (United), Rashtriya Janata Dal and Congress defeated the Bharatiya Janata Party in Bihar; in December 2018 the Congress took Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Chhattisgarh from the Bharatiya Janata Party months before the parliamentary verdict of May 2019 enlarged the same party's Lok Sabha majority. No configuration of committees and secretaries in Delhi could process a state election, and none was asked to. The enablement claim is a claim about the machinery of the Union government, and the state results are its proper outer fence: inside the fence, the architecture's effects are documented and large; outside it, Indian politics continued on its own plural logic, returning the architects to office in 2019 for reasons the architecture alone cannot explain.

The two readings of 2014 can therefore be integrated without remainder. The closure reading is right that the verdict ended the coalition era and that its structural analogue is 1971: a personalised majority displacing a fragmented field. The enablement reading is right that the verdict's substantive consequence was the architecture: the four dimensions built in 2014 and 2015, renewed in 2019, through which demonetisation was processed in a day, the goods and services tax was rehoused in a constitutional council, and the Article 370 settlement was executed in a week. The mandate explains who governed; the architecture explains how governing was done, and the second explanation is the one that survives the election cycle, because the architecture, unlike the mandate, was still in place, upgraded and staffed, when the next verdict arrived. Inflection, in the end, is the correct word for 2014 in both registers: the curve of Indian politics changed direction at the ballot box, and the curve of Indian government changed direction in the secretariat, and the second change is the one the following decade was governed by.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the 2014 election enable in Indian governance?

The 2014 election enabled a single-party executive to rebuild the machinery of government in its first twenty months. The Bharatiya Janata Party's 282 seats, recorded in the Election Commission's Statistical Report of 2014, removed every veto player inside the ruling arrangement. The government used that freedom to centralise coordination in the Prime Minister's Office from May 2014, to replace the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog by the Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015, to abolish the coalition-era ministerial groups, and to staff the secretariat and regulatory bodies through a two-member Appointments Committee. Demonetisation in 2016, the goods and services tax in 2017 and the Article 370 decisions of 2019 were later run through that architecture.

### Q: What is the post-2014 PMO architecture?

The post-2014 PMO architecture is the configuration of offices through which the Prime Minister's Office coordinated the executive after May 2014. Its core team was appointed inside the government's first month: Nripendra Misra as Principal Secretary on 28 May 2014, Ajit Doval as National Security Advisor on 30 May 2014, and Pramod Kumar Mishra as Additional Principal Secretary in June 2014, with Bhaskar Khulbe serving as a Secretary to the Prime Minister. Around that team stood instruments rather than conventions: the monthly PRAGATI review of projects and grievances from 25 March 2015, the Groups of Secretaries reporting to the Prime Minister from January 2016, and direct review of ministries by the Prime Minister's Office, a pattern Christophe Jaffrelot documented in Modi's India (2021).

### Q: What is NITI Aayog and how does it differ from the Planning Commission?

NITI Aayog, the National Institution for Transforming India, was established by a resolution of the Union Cabinet on 1 January 2015, with the Prime Minister as chairperson and Arvind Panagariya as first vice chairman. The Planning Commission, created by resolution on 15 March 1950, wrote five-year plans and allocated central assistance to the states, which made it a bargaining counter in centre-state relations. NITI Aayog holds no funds and allocates nothing; it is a strategy and policy body whose Governing Council of chief ministers deliberates without distributing resources. The Commission's five-year plans ended with the Twelfth Plan on 31 March 2017, replaced by action agendas and vision documents that carry no allocations.

### Q: How did the 2014 verdict change Cabinet operations?

The verdict replaced bargaining with ratification as the cabinet's working mode. On 31 May 2014 the government abolished the nine Empowered Groups of Ministers and twenty-one Groups of Ministers through which coalition-era questions had been pre-negotiated, directing ministries to decide at their own level. The standing cabinet committees were reconstituted in smaller, Prime Minister-chaired form, and inter-ministerial questions were settled increasingly through the Prime Minister's Office and the Cabinet Secretariat before reaching the cabinet table. Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India (2021), describes cabinet meetings of the period as frequently brief and confirmatory. In June 2019 the system was extended by two new committees, on Investment and Growth and on Employment and Skill Development.

### Q: Who are the key PMO officials under Modi?

The founding team of the post-2014 Prime Minister's Office was appointed in the government's first month. Nripendra Misra, a retired officer of the 1967 batch and a former chairman of the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, became Principal Secretary on 28 May 2014, an appointment that required an ordinance amending the TRAI Act. Ajit Doval, a former director of the Intelligence Bureau, became National Security Advisor on 30 May 2014. Pramod Kumar Mishra, who had served Modi in Gujarat, became Additional Principal Secretary in June 2014 and succeeded Misra as Principal Secretary in 2019. Bhaskar Khulbe served as a Secretary to the Prime Minister. Misra, Mishra and Doval were all renewed at cabinet rank in 2019.

### Q: Did the 2014 election change Indian federalism?

The 2014 election changed the operating balance of Indian federalism without amending its constitutional text. The Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015 replaced the Planning Commission, the forum in which states had negotiated plan assistance since 1950, with NITI Aayog, whose Governing Council of chief ministers holds no allocative power. Against that centralising move, the government accepted the Fourteenth Finance Commission's award in February 2015, raising the states' share of union taxes from 32 to 42 percent. The goods and services tax then placed centre-state taxation inside a constitutional council under Article 279A, in which the Union holds one-third of the weighted vote and decisions need three-fourths. States gained untied money and lost negotiated forums.

### Q: How is the Modi PMO different from earlier PMOs?

Earlier strong prime ministerial offices rested on the leader's political position and faded with it. P. N. Haksar's secretariat served Indira Gandhi from 1967, and its reach contracted as her position did; the United Progressive Alliance period added the National Advisory Council of 2004, an alternative centre that existed because the Prime Minister's own centre was divided. The post-2014 office differs in construction and durability: it was assembled by dated instruments in the first month of the government, integrated with the committee system, the secretaries' layer and the appointments process, and renewed across the 2019 election with its principals upgraded to cabinet rank. Precedent supplied the parts; the post-2014 period assembled them into a standing system.

### Q: What is the Group of Secretaries mechanism created after 2014?

The Group of Secretaries mechanism is a coordination device the Prime Minister built beneath the cabinet committee system. In January 2016 eight groups of senior civil servants were constituted and charged with producing transformative proposals on themes that included accelerated growth with inclusion, employment generation, energy conservation and farmer-centric agriculture. The groups presented their reports to the Prime Minister in person during January 2016, before the full body of secretaries. In October 2016 the exercise was repeated in sectoral form, with ten groups reviewing the work of ministries and reporting by the end of November. The mechanism gave the political executive a channel for generating policy across departmental boundaries, with the Prime Minister as the reporting point.

### Q: What is PRAGATI, the Prime Minister's project-review platform?

PRAGATI, short for Pro-Active Governance and Timely Implementation, is a monthly video conference launched on 25 March 2015 and chaired by the Prime Minister, conventionally on the fourth Wednesday of the month. Union secretaries and the chief secretaries of the states attend, and the agenda is drawn from the government's databases of stalled infrastructure projects, pending programmes and public grievances, with flagged items circulated by the Prime Minister's Office a week in advance. Officials answer for delays directly to the chair. The platform is an instrument of the post-2014 architecture rather than a mere technology: it fixes a calendar on which the senior administration of the Union and the chief secretaries of every state are answerable in one forum.

### Q: What happened to the Groups of Ministers after May 2014?

The Groups of Ministers were abolished in the new government's first week. On 31 May 2014 the Prime Minister wound up the nine Empowered Groups of Ministers and twenty-one Groups of Ministers inherited from the United Progressive Alliance, and ministries were directed to process the pending questions at their own level. A statement from the Prime Minister's Office presented the decision as a measure for faster decisions and greater accountability in the system. The groups had been the coalition era's standing device for pre-negotiating contentious questions among senior ministers and allied parties, chaired in the preceding government by figures such as Pranab Mukherjee, P. Chidambaram and A. K. Antony. Their abolition removed the executive's intermediary negotiating layer.

### Q: What replaced the Five-Year Plans after the Planning Commission closed?

Nothing replaced the Five-Year Plans in their allocative function. The Twelfth Five-Year Plan, covering 2012 to 2017, was allowed to run to its term on 31 March 2017, and no Thirteenth Plan was written. NITI Aayog, which had succeeded the Planning Commission by the Cabinet resolution of 1 January 2015, issued instead a three-year action agenda in August 2017, covering 2017-18 to 2019-20, as the first component of a fifteen-year vision and seven-year strategy sequence. The substitution is the architectural point in miniature: the plan had carried allocations of central assistance to the states, and the action agenda carries recommendations only, because the institution that issues it holds no funds to distribute.

### Q: What was the lateral entry scheme for senior bureaucrats announced in 2018?

The lateral entry scheme opened senior civil service posts to specialists from outside the services. In June 2018 the Department of Personnel and Training advertised ten joint secretary posts, in departments including revenue, financial services, economic affairs, agriculture, road transport and civil aviation, to be filled on contract by candidates with domain expertise. The advertisement drew 6,077 applications, and the Union Public Service Commission conducted the selection, recommending nine candidates on 12 April 2019. Joint secretary is the rank at which policy files are framed for decision, so the scheme's importance exceeded its numbers: it established that the personnel configuration of the post-2014 state was not bound by the career service's internal succession.

### Q: Why was NITI Aayog created by Cabinet resolution instead of legislation?

NITI Aayog was created by Cabinet resolution because its predecessor had been created the same way, and because a resolution sufficed for the institution the government intended. The Planning Commission rested on a Government of India resolution of 15 March 1950, not on a statute, so a resolution of the Union Cabinet on 1 January 2015 could replace it without a parliamentary bill, a debate the government might lose control of, or a statutory definition of the new body's powers. The choice also matched the design: NITI Aayog was to be a policy think tank of the executive, holding no funds and exercising no statutory authority, so no legislation was needed to empower it. Speed and revocability were the resolution's advantages.

### Q: How did PMO centralisation shape the way the demonetisation decision was taken in 2016?

PMO centralisation determined the decision's secrecy, speed and shape. Preparation for the withdrawal of 500-rupee and 1,000-rupee notes ran through the Prime Minister's Office, a small circle in the finance ministry and the Reserve Bank, rather than through any cabinet committee or ministerial group; the ministerial-group layer that might earlier have received such a question had been abolished in May 2014. The Reserve Bank has stated that its Central Board recommended the withdrawal at a meeting on 8 November 2016, the cabinet was informed that evening, and the Prime Minister announced the decision in a broadcast at 8 p.m., effective at midnight. A months-long preparation and a one-day legal sequence are the operational signature of the post-2014 architecture.

### Q: What do critics of post-2014 PMO centralisation argue, and how do defenders answer?

Critics argue that coordination hardened into subordination. Christophe Jaffrelot, in Modi's India (2021), presents a Prime Minister's Office through which decisions were substantially settled before the cabinet met, reducing ministers to executants of lines set elsewhere; Yamini Aiyar's essays for the Centre for Policy Research add that programme design, delivery monitoring and political credit all converged on the same office. Defenders of the arrangement answer that the preceding system had produced the delays the abolition of ministerial groups was meant to cure, that a Prime Minister elected on a personal mandate is entitled to a secretariat that can enforce his programme across ministries, and that the 2019 verdict, which enlarged the majority to 303 seats, ratified the arrangement. The factual record beneath both arguments is shared.

### Q: How does the architecture-enablement reading of 2014 differ from the mandate reading?

The mandate reading explains the election; the enablement reading explains the government. The mandate reading treats the 282 seats of May 2014 as the event, and interprets the verdict through its structural analogue to the Garibi Hatao landslide of 1971 and the collapse of the Congress organisation. The architecture-enablement reading treats the verdict as a threshold: the asset that mattered was the freedom to rebuild the executive, exercised between May 2014 and January 2016 through the Prime Minister's Office configuration, the NITI Aayog resolution, the committee restructuring and the personnel pattern. The readings answer different questions, who won and why, against how governing was reorganised, and the integrated account defended in the scholarship of the period holds both: closure of the coalition era and enablement of a new governing architecture.

### Q: What is the Appointments Committee of the Cabinet and what does it approve?

The Appointments Committee of the Cabinet is the committee of the Union cabinet that approves the senior staffing of the Indian state. Its composition in the post-2014 period was two members: the Prime Minister as chair and the Minister of Home Affairs, a membership displayed in the reconstitution of 6 June 2019, in which the committee consisted of Narendra Modi and Amit Shah alone. The committee's writ runs over secretary-level appointments in the ministries, the heads of the regulatory bodies and the members of the constitutional commissions, processed through the Department of Personnel and Training. Because every senior appointment in the executive passed this one table, the committee was the gearbox of the personnel configuration: the mechanism by which the appointing centre re-sorted the secretariat on a rolling basis.

### Q: How did the post-2014 government approach appointments to regulatory bodies?

The post-2014 government staffed the regulatory bodies through the Appointments Committee on a rolling calendar, and the Reserve Bank sequence shows the pattern at full strength. Raghuram Rajan's governorship ended in September 2016; Urjit Patel, a deputy governor, succeeded him on 4 September 2016 and chaired the board that recommended demonetisation two months later. Patel resigned on 10 December 2018, after a public disagreement between the Bank and the government, and Shaktikanta Das, a retired finance secretary, was named governor the following day. At the Securities and Exchange Board of India, Ajay Tyagi succeeded U. K. Sinha on 1 March 2017, and the chief election commissionership changed hands three times between July 2017 and December 2018. Each appointment followed the constituted channel; the pattern is the concentration of the choosing.

### Q: What role did the Cabinet Secretary play in the post-2014 coordination system?

The Cabinet Secretary, the senior-most civil servant and head of the Cabinet Secretariat, worked in the post-2014 system as the administrative hinge between the Prime Minister's Office and the ministries. Pradeep Kumar Sinha held the office from 2015 through the period, receiving extensions that carried him across the 2019 election. The Cabinet Secretariat services the cabinet committees, circulates their decisions and monitors their implementation across departments; the Cabinet Secretary also presented the follow-up reports of the Groups of Secretaries, as at the meeting of 27 October 2016, at which the implementation of the eight groups' recommendations was reviewed and ten sectoral groups were commissioned. In an architecture where committees ratified and the Prime Minister's Office coordinated, the Cabinet Secretary's machinery kept the paper moving between the two.

### Q: How did the second Modi government of 2019 adjust the cabinet committee structure?

The second Modi government reconstituted the committee system within a week of taking office and extended it in the direction of the first term's design. On 6 June 2019 eight standing cabinet committees were reconstituted under the Transaction of Business Rules, and two new committees were constituted: a Cabinet Committee on Investment and Growth, chaired by the Prime Minister with Amit Shah, Nirmala Sitharaman, Nitin Gadkari and Piyush Goyal as members, and a Cabinet Committee on Employment and Skill Development with ten members, likewise chaired by the Prime Minister. The Home Minister sat on all eight committees, and the Appointments Committee consisted of the Prime Minister and the Home Minister alone. The adjustments were made on 6 June 2019, eight weeks before the committee system so arranged processed the Jammu and Kashmir decisions of August 2019.
