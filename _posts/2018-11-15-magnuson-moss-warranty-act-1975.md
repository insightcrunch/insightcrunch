## Introduction

The operative question answered by the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act is narrow. When a seller or manufacturer chooses to make a written promise about a consumer product, what must that promise disclose, how must the promise be labeled, what conditions may not be attached, and what happens when the promisor refuses to perform? Congress in 1974 legislated against a market in which the written warranty had become a selling device first and an enforceable commitment second. A warranty could be advertised in large type as comprehensive protection and then qualified in dense language that few purchasers read before sale and fewer could enforce after a defect appeared. Terms varied without a common vocabulary, so comparison rested on slogans rather than on coverage, duration, exclusions, and remedy. A seller could give a written warranty with one hand and disclaim the implied warranties arising under state law with the other, leaving a document that looked like protection and functioned as a limitation. For most individual purchases, suit over a breached warranty cost more than the loss, and that arithmetic made many written promises practically unenforceable regardless of the merits of a claim.

The House report that accompanied the legislation, House Report Number 93-1197 of the Ninety-third Congress, described the purpose in functional terms. The statute was meant to make warranties on consumer products more readily understood and more readily enforceable, and to give the Federal Trade Commission better means of protecting consumers against deceptive warranty practices. Hearings and reports before enactment returned repeatedly to complaints from owners of motor vehicles who alleged that manufacturers and dealers had failed to perform in accordance with the warranties issued at sale. The problem, as Congress framed the problem, was not an absence of warranties, which American firms issued in enormous numbers after the Second World War. The problem was asymmetry. The warrantor drafted the document, controlled the service network, and possessed the technical information, while a purchaser encountered the full terms often only after payment. Federal legislation answered that asymmetry by imposing disclosure, labeling, anti-conditioning, and enforcement rules on any warrantor that entered the written warranty market voluntarily.

## Formal Identity and the Two Halves of the Statute

The formal name of the statute is the Magnuson-Moss Warranty and Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act. Congress enacted the measure as Public Law 93-637, and President Gerald Ford signed the act on January 4, 1975. The name honors Senator Warren G. Magnuson of Washington, who carried the legislation in the Senate, and Representative John E. Moss of California, who carried the legislation in the House of Representatives. Title I, the warranty title, created a new chapter of Title 15 of the United States Code. The warranty provisions begin at section 2301 of Title 15 and continue through section 2312 of Title 15, although the operative core runs from the definitions in section 2301 through the enforcement provisions in section 2310. Title II addressed the Federal Trade Commission directly. Among other changes to the Federal Trade Commission Act, Title II enacted section 57a of Title 15, which became Section 18 of the Federal Trade Commission Act and which prescribes how the Commission may promulgate trade regulation rules defining unfair or deceptive acts or practices with specificity.

The two halves share a consumer protection purpose, and the two halves operate through different instruments. The warranty half regulates private documents and private conduct, attaching to written warranties, to implied warranties supplied by state law, to service contracts, and to settlement and litigation mechanisms. The rulemaking half regulates an agency. Section 57a grants the Commission authority to issue industry wide rules defining particular acts or practices as unfair or deceptive, and section 57a simultaneously surrounds that authority with procedures more elaborate than the notice and comment process of the Administrative Procedure Act. A profile faithful to the statutory identity has to hold both halves together, because Congress enacted both halves in a single law on a single day.

The warranty half also sits inside a larger structure. State law, principally Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code as adopted in each state, continued after 1975 to supply the underlying law of sales warranties, including the implied warranty of merchantability. The federal statute layered disclosure and enforcement rules over state warranty law, without codifying a complete federal law of sales. A defective appliance could raise a safety question under [the Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972](/2018/10/01/consumer-product-safety-act-1972/) as the parallel product regime, a deception question under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, and a warranty question under the Magnuson-Moss provisions. Safety law asked whether a product presented an unreasonable risk, deception law asked whether a representation misled purchasers, and warranty law asked whether a voluntary written promise had been disclosed honestly, labeled accurately, and performed as written.

## A Statute That Requires No Warranty

The most consequential limit in the statute is a negative proposition. The Magnuson-Moss provisions impose no obligation on any manufacturer or seller to provide a written warranty, or any warranty at all, on any product. A manufacturer may sell a consumer product with no written warranty, and the statute, standing alone, is not violated by that choice. Section 2302 states the limit expressly. Nothing in the warranty chapter, apart from a provision on extension of a warranty period during loss of use, authorizes the Commission to prescribe the duration of a written warranty given or to require that a consumer product or any component be warranted. The architecture is conditional throughout. Duties arise because a warrantor has given a written warranty, because a supplier has sold a product accompanied by a written warranty or a service contract, or because an implied warranty under state law has arisen and the federal statute then limits disclaimer of that warranty. Absent one of those triggers, the federal warranty rules do not compel a firm to promise repair, replacement, refund, or any other remedy.

That conditional design explains both the reach and the restraint of the warranty half. Congress chose disclosure and enforceability over mandated coverage. A firm remained free after January 4, 1975 to compete without a written warranty, with a short written warranty, or with an extensive written warranty, and the statute policed the honesty and clarity of whichever course the firm selected. The statute established no federal minimum quality standard, and enforcement followed the promise and the state law warranty, not a federal judgment about how durable a toaster, a television set, or an automobile ought to be.

Within that conditional frame, the statute deploys distinct mechanisms, summarized here and treated in depth in later sections. Designation comes first. Section 2303 requires a warrantor to designate a written warranty clearly and conspicuously as either a full warranty, with a statement of duration, or a limited warranty, and section 2304 supplies the federal minimum standards for the full designation. The label is therefore a regulated representation rather than a marketing choice, and most written warranties issued after 1975 carried the limited label because the standards for the full label were demanding. Disclosure and pre-sale availability form the second mechanism. Section 2302 requires full and conspicuous disclosure of warranty terms in simple and readily understood language, to the extent required by Commission rules, and directs the Commission to prescribe rules requiring that warranty terms be made available before sale. Commission rules in Part 701 and Part 702 of Title 16 implemented those duties, and advertising guides in Part 239 of Title 16 addressed warranty claims in advertising.

Implied warranty protection forms the third mechanism. Section 2308 provides that a supplier may not disclaim or modify an implied warranty where the supplier gives a written warranty or enters into a service contract within ninety days of the sale, subject to a limited permission to restrict the duration of an implied warranty to the duration of a written warranty of reasonable duration where the restriction is conscionable, appears in clear and unmistakable language, and is prominently displayed on the face of the warranty.

The tie-in prohibition forms the fourth mechanism, in section 2302, subsection (c). No warrantor may condition a written or implied warranty on the consumer using, in connection with the warranted product, any article or service identified by brand, trade, or corporate name, other than an article or service provided without charge under the warranty. The Commission may waive the prohibition only after the warrantor satisfies the Commission that the product will function properly only if the identified article or service is used, and only after the Commission finds that a waiver serves the public interest, with applications and dispositions published in the Federal Register. The provision is the statutory basis for the proposition that use of an independent repair shop or an independently manufactured part does not, without more, void a warranty. A warrantor remained able to deny coverage for damage that an unauthorized part or an improper repair actually caused, because causation presents a different question from conditioning.

Fee shifting, private enforcement, and informal dispute mechanisms complete the warranty half. Section 2310 authorizes a consumer damaged by failure of a supplier, warrantor, or service contractor to comply with an obligation under the warranty chapter, or under a written warranty, an implied warranty, or a service contract, to bring suit in a court of competent jurisdiction. Where a consumer finally prevails, section 2310 permits a court to award reasonable attorneys' fees based on actual time expended, together with costs, unless the court determines that such an award would be inappropriate. The fee provision altered the economics described at the opening of the profile, because a claim too small to justify a fee paid in advance could support representation where a prevailing consumer could recover fees from the warrantor. Section 2310 also encourages informal settlement, authorizes the Commission to prescribe minimum requirements for informal dispute mechanisms under Part 703 of Title 16, and permits a warrantor to require resort to a qualifying mechanism before legal remedies where the warranty incorporates that requirement in accordance with Commission rules.

The final mechanism belongs to the second half of the statute. Section 57a authorizes the Commission to prescribe rules defining with specificity acts or practices that are unfair or deceptive, and conditions that authority on procedures that include a notice of proposed rulemaking, an opportunity for oral presentation, an opportunity for cross examination on disputed issues of material fact, a statement of basis and purpose addressing prevalence and economic effect, and judicial review under a substantial evidence standard. Congress in the Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980 added an advance notice stage. The combined procedure, often described as hybrid rulemaking, made each trade regulation rule slower and more expensive than a rule issued under the ordinary Administrative Procedure Act process. Between 1975 and November 2018, that procedural weight formed part of the explanation offered in oversight hearings and Commission statements for the limited number of trade regulation rules completed under section 57a. The warranty rules changed private documents within months of enactment. The rulemaking procedures changed agency behavior for decades, and the suppression operated through process rather than through any repeal of the underlying authority.

## Definitions, Scope, and the Boundaries of Coverage

Coverage under the warranty half begins with the definitions in section 2301. A consumer product is any tangible personal property distributed in commerce and normally used for personal, family, or household purposes, including property intended to be attached to or installed in real property regardless of whether attachment or installation has occurred. The test turns on normal use. A product of a type ordinarily used for personal, family, or household purposes falls within the definition based on the character of the product, and a product of a type ordinarily used only for commercial or industrial purposes falls outside the definition on the same inquiry. The definition reaches components and systems intended for installation in a house, such as heating equipment or built in appliances. A transaction for services alone, with no consumer product at its center, is not a sale of a consumer product, and the warranty chapter does not convert every consumer service transaction into a covered warranty transaction. Repair services enter the statute through defined connections to a product, principally through a service contract, through warranty obligations concerning a product, and through the tie-in prohibition.

A consumer, under section 2301, is a buyer of a consumer product other than a buyer purchasing for purposes of resale. The definition extends beyond the original purchaser. A person to whom a product is transferred during the duration of an applicable implied warranty, written warranty, or service contract is a consumer, and any other person entitled by the terms of a warranty or service contract, or under applicable state law, to enforce the obligations of the warranty or service contract is likewise a consumer. The resale exclusion places commercial purchasers outside the consumer frame, and the regulatory definition in Part 701 of Title 16 states the related boundary for disclosure purposes by defining a consumer as a buyer other than a buyer purchasing for resale or for use in the ordinary course of the business of the buyer.

A written warranty, under section 2301, takes either of two forms, and both forms require writing and a connection to a sale. The first form is a written affirmation of fact or written promise made in connection with the sale of a consumer product by a supplier to a buyer, relating to the nature of the material or workmanship, that affirms or promises that the material or workmanship is free of defects or will meet a specified level of performance over a specified period of time. The second form is an undertaking in writing, in connection with the sale by a supplier, to refund, repair, replace, or take other remedial action where the product fails to meet the specifications set forth in the undertaking. In either form, the affirmation, promise, or undertaking must become part of the basis of the bargain between supplier and buyer for purposes other than resale. An oral assurance does not satisfy the statutory definition, although state law may give an oral statement independent effect. Sections 2302, 2303, and 2304 also do not apply to expressions of general policy concerning customer satisfaction that are not subject to any specific limitations.

An implied warranty, under section 2301, is an implied warranty arising under state law, as modified by section 2304, subsection (a), and by section 2308, in connection with the sale of a consumer product by a supplier. The definition points outward to state law for content and inward to two federal sections for limits on disclaimer and duration. A service contract, under section 2301, is a contract in writing to perform, over a fixed period of time or for a specified duration, services relating to the maintenance or repair, or both, of a consumer product. A written warranty ordinarily accompanies the product as part of the sale, while a service contract is a separate written agreement for future maintenance or repair services, frequently sold for a separate price. Commission interpretations in Part 700 of Title 16 addressed the boundary among written warranties, service contracts, and agreements sold and regulated under state law as contracts of insurance.

A supplier is any person engaged in the business of making a consumer product directly or indirectly available to consumers, a functional definition reaching manufacturers, distributors, and retailers without requiring that a supplier have manufactured the product at issue. A warrantor is any supplier or other person who gives or offers to give a written warranty, or who is or may be obligated under an implied warranty. The definitions identify the party answerable for a written promise by the act of giving or offering the promise, and allow a single firm to be supplier and warrantor in the same transaction.

Price thresholds complete the scope picture, and the thresholds differ by provision. Section 2302, subsection (e), provides that the provisions of section 2302 apply only to warranties pertaining to consumer products actually costing the consumer more than five dollars, so the contents rules, the disclosure authority, and the tie-in prohibition in section 2302 carry a statutory floor of more than five dollars. Section 2303, subsection (d), provides that subsections (a) and (c) of section 2303, the designation duty and the provision for Commission exemptions from designation, apply only to warranties pertaining to consumer products actually costing the consumer more than ten dollars and not designated as full warranties with a statement of duration, so the designation regime carries a floor of more than ten dollars.

The fifteen dollar figures are regulatory in origin for the disclosure and pre-sale rules, rather than a restatement of the section 2303 designation floor. Part 701 of Title 16 states in section 701.2 that the regulations in Part 701 establish requirements for disclosing the terms and conditions of written warranties on consumer products actually costing the consumer more than fifteen dollars, and section 701.3 imposes the single document disclosure duty at the same figure. Part 702 of Title 16, the pre-sale availability rule issued under section 2302 on December 31, 1975, states in section 702.3 that the requirements of Part 702 apply to consumer products actually costing the consumer more than fifteen dollars, and Part 702 assigns duties to sellers to make the text of a written warranty readily available to a prospective buyer and duties to warrantors to supply sellers with the necessary materials. Part 239 of Title 16, the guides for the advertising of warranties and guarantees issued on May 1, 1985, adds no independent dollar threshold. Section 239.2 applies only to advertisements for written warranties on consumer products covered by Part 702, which imports the more than fifteen dollars scope of Part 702 for that section, while the other sections of Part 239 apply to the advertising of any warranty or guarantee. Designation attached under section 2303 at more than ten dollars, the section 2302 rules attached at more than five dollars, and the disclosure duties of Part 701 and the pre-sale availability duties of Part 702 attached at more than fifteen dollars.

Below every threshold, and outside every definition, the statute leaves the transaction to state law and to Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act. The definitions, the exclusions, the tangible property requirement, and the graduated dollar floors together mark the class of transactions Congress selected in 1975, household and personal purchases of tangible goods in commerce, accompanied in the covered cases by a written promise, a state law implied warranty, or a written service contract. Later sections take each mechanism in turn.

## Origins in a Marketplace of Unread Promises

The Magnuson-Moss Warranty and Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act grew out of a simple and repeated experience in American retail life during the 1960s and the early 1970s. A household bought a refrigerator, a washing machine, a television set, or an automobile on the strength of a warranty described in advertising and in the showroom as generous protection, and then discovered, after a breakdown, that the printed document said something narrower, stranger, and far less helpful than the sales pitch had suggested. The warranty might cover parts but not the labor needed to install the parts. The warranty might promise repair while assigning the cost of shipping a heavy appliance to the buyer. The warranty might exclude the very component that failed most often, or it might impose conditions buried in dense print that few purchasers had read and fewer had understood. In case after case reaching Congress and the Federal Trade Commission, the paper promise and the lived remedy failed to match, and the gap became a defining grievance of the consumer movement.

That movement supplied the political conditions for federal warranty legislation. President John F. Kennedy sent the first presidential message on consumer interests to Congress on March 15, 1962, and federal consumer protection acquired a durable place on the legislative agenda during the decade that followed. Consumer organizations testified, agency staff investigated, and committees accumulated complaint files. House Report 93-1107, the report that accompanied the bill in 1974, recalled a rising tide of complaints beginning in the late 1950s from motor vehicle owners who reported that manufacturers and dealers were not performing in accordance with automobile warranties. Automobiles mattered because of cost and dependence. A family denied the promised repair lost transportation to work and school, together with a repair bill that the warranty had appeared to promise to absorb.

Appliances supplied a second body of evidence, and in some respects a cleaner one, because appliance warranties could be collected and compared side by side. Warranties for refrigerators, freezers, ranges, washers, and dryers varied enormously in duration, in the parts covered, in the treatment of labor charges, and in the service arrangements behind the promise. A seal or certificate format, decorative borders, and formal language gave many documents an official appearance, while the operative terms often limited coverage under conditions that a purchaser had little practical ability to evaluate at the point of sale. Warranty competition therefore rewarded the appearance of coverage. A warrantor offering honest but modest terms could appear stingier than a competitor offering sweeping language with quiet exclusions, because purchasers had no reliable way to compare documents before money changed hands.

## Findings That Turned Complaints Into a Federal Record

Federal investigators converted the complaints into findings that gave Congress a record for legislation. In 1968, President Lyndon B. Johnson created the Task Force on Appliance Warranties and Service, a body that included the Chairman of the Federal Trade Commission and the Secretary of Commerce, with the Commission, the Department of Commerce, the Department of Labor, and the Special Assistant to the President for Consumer Affairs preparing its work. The task force set out to encourage improvements in the quality of service and repairs, to assure that warranties and guarantees said what warranties and guarantees meant, to inform consumers about how long a product could be expected to last under proper use, and to determine whether federal legislation was needed. Each aim pointed at a documented failure in service networks, in warranty language, or in durability information, and voluntary improvement had left the core practices in place.

The Report of the Task Force on Appliance Warranties and Service, issued on January 8, 1969, examined more than 200 warranties used by more than 50 appliance manufacturers. The picture drawn in that report, as later summarized in House Report 93-1107, was dismal in a specific and legislatable way. Consumers often misunderstood the scope of warranties, manufacturers and sellers often failed to honor the warranties that existed, and terms were difficult to obtain before purchase and difficult to read when obtained. Parallel Commission work reinforced the appliance evidence from the automobile side. Commission staff produced a 1968 staff report on automobile warranties, and 1969 testimony, including testimony associated with Consumers Union, kept the repair problem before legislators.

A further task force effort in 1969 examined the need for federal warranty legislation in the household appliance industry, and in 1971 President Richard Nixon proposed a Fair Warranty Disclosure Act. The administration proposal sought to prevent deceptive warranties and to prohibit the improper use of a written warranty to avoid implied warranty obligations arising under state law. The 1971 proposal did not become law, and the failure mattered to the shape of the eventual statute in two respects. Disclosure had gathered support across administrations of both parties, strengthening the case that the problem was structural rather than partisan, and the collapse of narrower proposals left room for a broader measure that paired disclosure with federal minimum standards for any warranty advertised as full, with protections for implied warranties, and with a strengthened Federal Trade Commission. Within [the history of US consumer protection law](/2019/02/15/us-consumer-protection-law-history/), the warranty statute therefore belonged to a sequence in which agency fact finding preceded legislation, and in which each failed bill transferred its evidence and much of its language to a successor.

## Passage After a Long Multi-Congress Effort

The statute took the names of the legislators who carried the measure through the final Congresses, Senator Warren G. Magnuson of Washington and Representative John E. Moss of California, and neither name attached to a sudden initiative. Congressional attempts at consumer warranty protection had begun in 1967, and more than a dozen versions of warranty and consumer protection bills were introduced, heard, and debated in the two chambers during the years between the first Senate bills of the Ninetieth Congress and enactment in the Ninety-Third Congress. In February 1971, Senator Magnuson introduced S. 986 in the Ninety-Second Congress, a two-part bill that combined a title on warranties with a title enlarging the consumer protection authority of the Federal Trade Commission. The pairing survived into the enacted law and explained much about the long delay. Warranty rules raised questions about federal intrusion into sales contracts governed largely by state law, and Commission authority raised questions about agency reach. Combining the subjects gave each title a constituency and a set of opponents, and bills died at successive Congresses with hearings held and floor action incomplete.

The committee path in the Ninety-Second Congress illustrated the pattern. The Senate Consumer Subcommittee, chaired by Senator Frank E. Moss of Utah, a different lawmaker from Representative John E. Moss, held hearings on S. 986 and on administration proposals, and the Senate Committee on Commerce ordered an amended version reported favorably. A unanimous consent order then referred the bill to the Senate Judiciary Committee until October 15, 1971, and the Judiciary Committee took no action before returning the bill. In the House, the Subcommittee on Commerce and Finance held hearings on companion proposals during the Ninety-First Congress without reporting a bill, and full committee action lapsed as two Congresses ended. The hearings of one Congress became the drafting material of the next.

The successful vehicle was S. 356 of the Ninety-Third Congress, sponsored by Senator Magnuson and introduced on January 12, 1973. Congress.gov records committee consideration in the Senate by the Committee on Commerce and by the Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs, and in the House by the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce. Representative Moss sponsored the House companion, H.R. 7917, introduced on May 17, 1973 and referred to the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce, whose Subcommittee on Commerce and Finance conducted the House hearings on consumer warranty protection during 1973. The Senate passed S. 356 on September 12, 1973. The House took more than a year longer and passed S. 356 on September 19, 1974, passing the Senate bill in lieu of H.R. 7917 and laying the House bill on the table. A joint conference committee reported an agreed version on October 8, 1974. The Senate agreed to the conference report on December 18, 1974, and the House agreed to the conference report on December 19, 1974. President Gerald Ford signed the measure on January 4, 1975, and the act became Public Law 93-637, published at 88 Stat. 2183, with the warranty provisions codified at sections 2301 and following of Title 15 of the United States Code and the Commission rulemaking procedures addressed elsewhere in the same public law at section 57a of Title 15.

The lopsided final votes recorded a consensus built by the long committee process. By December 1974, the task force reports, the Commission staff work, and seven years of hearings had narrowed the range of argument, and the conference version answered objections based on federalism, compliance cost, and Commission power through thresholds, exemptions, and procedural limits. The designation and disclosure rules carried the marks of that compromise, regulating warranties that warrantors chose to give and leaving the initial choice about giving a warranty with the warrantor.

## The Full or Limited Designation

Section 103 of the enacted law, codified at section 2303 of Title 15, attacked the misleading headline directly by forcing a choice of label. A warrantor warranting a consumer product by means of a written warranty had to designate the warranty clearly and conspicuously in one of two ways, unless the Federal Trade Commission exempted the warranty from designation by rule under subsection (c) of section 2303. A warranty meeting the federal minimum standards in section 2304 of Title 15 had to be designated a full warranty, with a statement of duration inserted where the statutory formula placed parentheses, so that a compliant label read in substance as a full warranty for a stated period. A warranty failing to meet the standards had to be designated a limited warranty. The statute supplied no third label and no permission to use the word full for a warranty that fell short in any respect covered by section 2304.

### What does a full warranty designation mean?

A full designation signals that a written warranty meets the federal minimum standards in section 2304 of Title 15. The warrantor must remedy defects without charge within a reasonable time, impose no unreasonable conditions, leave implied warranty duration unlimited, and offer refund or replacement after reasonable repair attempts fail.

The depth behind the label explains why Congress treated the single word as a regulated claim. Remedy without charge meant that the warrantor could not assess the consumer for costs incurred in connection with the required remedy. The bar on unreasonable conditions, expressed in section 2304 as a prohibition on duties beyond notification as a condition of securing remedy, prevented a warrantor from converting a repair promise into an obstacle course of registrations, retained packaging, or repeated trips unrelated to diagnosis. The prohibition on limiting the duration of an implied warranty meant that a full warrantor could not use a short written term to cut off the implied warranty of merchantability that state law supplied. The bar on excluding or limiting consequential damages, unless an exclusion appeared conspicuously on the face of the warranty, forced a full warrantor to surface a major reservation where a reader encountered the warranty first. The refund or replacement election after a reasonable number of failed attempts gave the label an ending, and section 2304(e) of Title 15 provided that a warranty designated as full was deemed, in an action under the act or under state law, to incorporate at least the minimum requirements of section 2304.

A limited designation carried the opposite signal without carrying a judgment that coverage was worthless. Limited was the residual category for a written warranty that departed from the federal minimum standards in one or more respects, whether by charging for labor, by limiting implied warranty duration in a manner permitted for limited warranties, by excluding consequential damages, by covering selected parts for differing periods, or by omitting the refund or replacement election after repeated failures. The label told a shopper that the detailed terms contained at least one departure from the full federal template. The absence of a required designation on a warranty subject to section 2303 was a failure of a federal labeling duty rather than evidence that no warranty existed. Section 2303(b) kept the duty within bounds by excluding statements or representations similar to expressions of general policy concerning customer satisfaction and subject to no specific limitations, so that a general satisfaction slogan without specific terms did not become a written warranty for designation purposes. Section 2303(d) tied the designation provisions to the statutory price floor stated in the scope discussion earlier in this profile, and the Commission exemption power in section 2303(c) allowed designation to be excused by rule for classes of warranties in which the label served no useful purpose.

## Disclosure of Terms and Availability Before Purchase

Designation supplied the signal, and section 2302 of Title 15 supplied the text behind the signal. Section 2302(a) declared three purposes, improving the adequacy of consumer information, preventing deception, and improving competition in the marketing of consumer products, and required a warrantor, to the extent required by Commission rules, to disclose warranty terms fully and conspicuously in simple and readily understood language. The statute listed the items that Commission rules could require in the written warranty itself. The list began with the clear identification of the names and addresses of the warrantors and the identity of the party or parties to whom the warranty was extended, so that a document identified both the promisor and the persons entitled to enforce the promise. The list continued with the products or parts covered, with a statement of what the warrantor would do in the event of a defect, malfunction, or failure to conform with the written warranty, at whose expense performance would occur, and for what period of time performance would be given. A companion statement set out what the consumer had to do and what expenses the consumer had to bear, and a further item required the exceptions and exclusions from the terms of the warranty to appear as part of the disclosure rather than as material a reader encountered only after a claim failed.

The remaining items in section 2302(a) turned a warranty into an operating instruction. The statute contemplated a step by step procedure for obtaining performance, including identification of any person or class of persons authorized to perform warranty obligations. Information about an informal dispute settlement procedure offered by the warrantor belonged in the disclosure, together with a recital, where the warranty so provided, that resort to the procedure could be required before legal remedies in court were pursued. A brief and general description of legal remedies followed, together with the time at which the warrantor would perform and the period within which, after notice of a defect, performance would occur. The characteristics or properties of products or parts that were not covered completed the picture from the negative side, and a closing item required the elements of the warranty to be stated in words that would not mislead a reasonable and average consumer about nature or scope. The statutory price floor for the section, lower than the designation floor, reflected a legislative judgment that basic disclosure reached further down the price scale than the formal full or limited label, as the scope discussion earlier in this profile sets out in full.

Disclosure locked inside a sealed carton after purchase would have defeated each stated purpose of section 2302, and section 2302(b) therefore addressed timing. Section 2302(b)(1)(A) directed the Commission to prescribe rules requiring that warranty terms be made available to the consumer, or to a prospective consumer, prior to sale. Section 2302(b)(1)(B) authorized rules governing the manner and form of clear and conspicuous presentation, so as not to mislead a reasonable and average consumer, in advertising, labeling, point of sale material, and other written representations. The same subsection withheld two powers. Nothing in the chapter, apart from a provision on extending warranty periods during loss of use, authorized the Commission to prescribe warranty duration or to require that a product or component be warranted, and the pre-sale rule consequently governed access to chosen terms rather than the existence of an offer.

The Commission issued the implementing rules on December 31, 1975, publishing the Disclosure Rule at Part 701 of Title 16 of the Code of Federal Regulations and the Pre-Sale Availability Rule at Part 702 of the same title, with Part 702 resting on authority in sections 2302 and 2309 of Title 15. Under Part 701, at the regulatory price figure stated in that same scope discussion, a warrantor for a covered product had to disclose specified items clearly and conspicuously in a single document in simple and readily understood language, preventing the information from being scattered across a manual, a carton, and a registration card. Under section 702.3 of Part 702, a seller of a covered product had to make the text of the warranty readily available for examination by a prospective buyer, either by displaying the text in close proximity to the warranted product or by furnishing the text upon request before sale. A seller choosing the request method had to place signs reasonably calculated to elicit attention in prominent locations in the store or department, advising prospective buyers that warranties were available upon request. Warrantors had to supply sellers with compliance materials, through copies collected in a binder, through text printed on packaging, or through stickers, tags, signs, or posters disclosing the text, and a copy of the warranty accompanied the product after sale under the prescribed methods.

Catalog and mail order sales received separate treatment in section 702.3, because a catalog buyer had no shelf and no binder to consult. A catalog seller had to disclose clearly and conspicuously, in close conjunction with the product description or in a clearly referenced information section, either the full warranty text or the fact that the warranty could be obtained free upon specific written request, together with the address for a request, and a seller electing the request method had to provide a requested copy promptly. Door to door sales received a parallel rule requiring disclosure, before consummation of a sale, that a representative had warranty copies available for inspection during the presentation. A later statutory amendment, treated in full in the amendments discussion later in this profile, added an internet method for making terms available, altering the medium without altering the sequence fixed in 1975. Terms came first, and purchase came after an opportunity to read the terms.

## Implied Warranties Preserved Against Disclaimer

The Magnuson-Moss Warranty and Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act, Public Law 93-637, signed on January 4, 1975, and codified at sections 2301 and following of title 15, United States Code, divided responsibility between federal and state law. Congress left the substantive content of implied warranties to state law, regulated disclosure and designation of written warranties federally, and built a bridge between the two systems in section 2308. A supplier that offered a written warranty, or that sold a related service contract close to the sale, lost the power under state sales law to erase the implied warranties that accompanied the transaction.

Section 2308(a) stated the central restriction directly. No supplier could disclaim or modify any implied warranty to a consumer with respect to a consumer product if the supplier made any written warranty to the consumer with respect to that product, or if, at the time of sale or within 90 days thereafter, the supplier entered into a service contract with the consumer that applied to that product. The prohibition operated except as provided in subsection (b), which addressed duration alone. Subsection (c) declared a disclaimer, modification, or limitation made in violation of section 2308 ineffective for purposes of the federal chapter and for purposes of state law.

An implied warranty was not a term printed in a warranty booklet. The warranty arose by operation of law when state law conditions were satisfied. Section 2301 defined an implied warranty as an implied warranty arising under state law, as modified by sections 2308 and 2304(a)(2), and defined a supplier as a person engaged in the business of making a consumer product directly or indirectly available to consumers. Federal law borrowed the state law right, identified the transactions in which that right deserved protection against contractual elimination, and disabled the private language that would have removed the right. A breach claim remained grounded in state law concepts of merchantability or fitness, while the federal statute determined whether a disclaimer could be raised as a defense and supplied a federal enforcement path through section 2310(d).

### Can a written warranty cancel the implied warranty?

No. When a supplier gives a written warranty on a consumer product, section 2308 forbids that supplier from disclaiming or modifying the implied warranties that state law provides. A limited warranty may shorten implied warranty duration only to the written warranty period, under strict conditions.

The duration exception in section 2308(b) required exact attention because a permission to limit duration could be mistaken for a general permission to disclaim. Subsection (b) provided that, for purposes of the federal chapter other than section 2304(a)(2), implied warranties could be limited in duration to the duration of a written warranty of reasonable duration, if the limitation was conscionable and was set forth in clear and unmistakable language and prominently displayed on the face of the warranty. The written warranty itself had to have a reasonable duration. The limitation had to be conscionable, a standard that invited examination of bargaining context and surprise. The words had to be clear and unmistakable, so a general exclusion buried in broad language about all other warranties did not satisfy the provision. The limitation also had to appear prominently on the face of the warranty, rather than in small print in a separate sales document.

Subsection (b) permitted a ceiling tied to the written warranty period. A one year limited written warranty could, if every statutory condition was met, support a limitation of implied warranty duration to that same one year period. Subsection (b) did not permit a shorter implied warranty period than the written warranty period, and the subsection did not permit elimination of the implied warranty during the written warranty period. During that period, the implied warranty survived as a separate source of obligation measured by state law standards.

The service contract trigger in section 2308(a)(2) closed an alternative route around the prohibition. A supplier could not avoid the restriction by withholding a written warranty while selling, at the time of sale or within 90 days thereafter, a service contract that applied to the product. A contract entered into on the eighty-ninth day after sale fell within the stated window. A contract first entered into after that window closed did not trigger section 2308(a)(2) on timing alone, although section 2308(a)(1) applied whenever the supplier made a written warranty. Section 2306 separately recognized a service contract in addition to or in lieu of a written warranty, provided the contract fully, clearly, and conspicuously disclosed the terms and conditions in simple and readily understood language. Such disclosure did not restore a power to disclaim implied warranties when the contract fell within the section 2308 timing rule.

## The State Law Foundation Behind the Federal Bar

The implied warranty of merchantability supplied the principal background protection that section 2308 preserved. Section 2-314 of the Uniform Commercial Code, adopted in state versions with local variations, provided that a warranty that goods would be merchantable was implied in a contract for sale if the seller was a merchant with respect to goods of that kind, unless excluded or modified under section 2-316. Merchantable goods had to pass without objection in the trade under the contract description, be fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods were used, run of even kind, quality, and quantity within permitted variations, be adequately contained, packaged, and labeled as the agreement required, and conform to promises or affirmations of fact made on the container or label. For fungible goods, fair average quality within the description was also required. A household refrigerator satisfied the ordinary purpose test by cooling food reliably under normal conditions, while a refrigerator that could not maintain safe temperatures or leaked repeatedly under ordinary use presented a different question.

The implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose, stated in section 2-315 of the Uniform Commercial Code, rested on a narrower foundation. That warranty arose where the seller at the time of contracting had reason to know a particular purpose for which the buyer required the goods and that the buyer relied on the seller skill or judgment to select or furnish suitable goods, unless excluded or modified under section 2-316. Merchantability asked whether goods were fit for ordinary purposes, while fitness asked whether goods were fit for a specific purpose communicated in circumstances that supported reliance. Paint that performed adequately as ordinary interior paint could breach a fitness warranty after a seller recommended the paint for a particular exterior use known to the seller.


Section 2-316 explained why a federal anti-disclaimer rule had practical force. Under state sales law, implied warranties could be excluded or modified by an as is clause, by a conspicuous writing mentioning merchantability for a merchantability disclaimer, by examination of the goods in specified circumstances, or by course of dealing and usage of trade. A seller could place an express promise in prominent marketing material and place a disclaimer in formal documents that, under section 2-316, removed the implied warranty that would otherwise have supplied a baseline of quality. Section 2308 intervened at exactly that junction, and the state law disclaimer route closed for a triggered supplier apart from the limited duration rule in subsection (b).

Privity added a separate state law layer that section 2308 did not erase. Privity described the direct contractual relationship between a buyer and a seller or manufacturer. Some states required vertical privity between a consumer and a remote manufacturer for particular implied warranty claims, some states relaxed that requirement for consumer goods, and some states distinguished economic loss from personal injury or damage to other property. Horizontal privity concerned extension of protection to persons other than the immediate buyer, such as family members or later transferees. Federal courts applying the act frequently looked to state law to determine whether an implied warranty existed and whether a particular claimant could enforce that warranty against a particular defendant.

Congress had structural reasons for preserving state law rather than writing a federal code of merchantability in 1975. The Uniform Commercial Code represented a developed body of sales law, enacted state by state and administered by state courts in ordinary contract litigation. A federal substantive standard would have displaced or duplicated that body of law for consumer goods while leaving commercial sales under state rules, creating boundary disputes over products, buyers, and uses. Preservation allowed merchantability to remain connected to trade usage, product categories, and state decisions that had given concrete meaning to ordinary purpose. The federal contribution targeted the pairing of an affirmative written promise with a formal elimination of background protection, and the use of technical language that consumers had little practical ability to negotiate.

## Federal Minimum Standards for a Full Warranty

Section 2304 stated the federal minimum standards that a written warranty had to meet before a warrantor could designate the warranty as full under section 2303. Section 2303 required a warrantor to clearly and conspicuously designate a written warranty as a full warranty with a statement of duration, if the warranty met the section 2304 standards, or as a limited warranty, if the warranty failed to meet those standards. A product could also carry a multiple warranty that was part full and part limited under section 2305, provided the components were clearly and conspicuously differentiated. The designation rule transformed section 2304 into a public signal at the point of sale, and a warrantor that used the word full assumed the obligations that gave the word that meaning.

Section 2304(a)(1) required the warrantor, as a minimum, to remedy a consumer product within a reasonable time and without charge in the case of a defect, malfunction, or failure to conform with the written warranty. Remedy without charge reached beyond supplying a replacement part while charging for labor, shipping, removal, or installation, and section 2304(a)(4) provided that replacement of a component part included installing the part without charge. Section 2304(a)(2) addressed duration and deliberately overrode section 2308(b). A warrantor seeking full status could not impose any limitation on the duration of any implied warranty on the product, notwithstanding section 2308(b). A limited warranty could use section 2308(b) to tie implied warranty duration to a reasonable written warranty period under the stated conditions, while a full warranty could not use that device at all.

Section 2304(a)(3) governed consequential damages. A full warrantor could not exclude or limit consequential damages for breach of any written or implied warranty on the product, unless the exclusion or limitation conspicuously appeared on the face of the warranty. The provision imposed a visibility condition rather than an absolute ban, and a term in a separate manual or in inconspicuous boilerplate did not meet that condition. Consequential damages could include losses beyond repair cost, such as spoiled food after a refrigerator failure, subject to governing law on foreseeability and proof. Section 2304(a)(4) supplied a decisive endpoint. If a product or a component part contained a defect or malfunction after a reasonable number of attempts by the warrantor to remedy defects or malfunctions, the warrantor had to permit the consumer to elect either a refund for the product or part, or replacement without charge of the product or part. The Federal Trade Commission could specify by rule what constituted a reasonable number of attempts for particular defects under different circumstances. The election belonged to the consumer, and continued repair attempts could not become an indefinite substitute for refund or replacement after the statutory threshold was crossed.

Section 2304(b) limited the duties that a full warrantor could impose as a condition of securing a remedy. The warrantor could not impose any duty other than notification as a condition of remedy for a product that malfunctioned, was defective, or failed to conform, unless the warrantor demonstrated in a rulemaking proceeding, or could demonstrate in an enforcement or informal dispute settlement proceeding, that the additional duty was reasonable. A registration card or a mandatory inspection schedule could each become a condition that needed such justification. Section 2304(b)(2) preserved a narrower power to require, as a condition of replacement or refund, that the product be made available free and clear of liens and other encumbrances, except as otherwise provided by Commission rule or order where such a requirement would be impracticable. Section 2304(b)(4) extended the subsection (a) duties to each person who was a consumer with respect to the product, supporting coverage for a qualifying transferee during the warranty period. Section 2304(c) provided that subsection (a) duties were not required if the warrantor showed that a failure was caused by damage, not resulting from defect or malfunction, while the product was in the possession of the consumer, or by unreasonable use, including failure to provide reasonable and necessary maintenance.

Few written warranties qualified as full because the standards accumulated rather than offered alternatives. A warrantor had to satisfy every condition at once. Free and timely remedy, unrestricted implied warranty duration, a tightly confined approach to consequential damages, a consumer election of refund or replacement after a reasonable number of failed attempts, and a near prohibition on consumer side conditions combined to create exposure that a manufacturer could estimate only with difficulty. Long product service lives magnified the exposure from section 2304(a)(2), and products installed in a home magnified repair cost because removal, transportation, and reinstallation formed part of real expense. A single weakness converted the designation to limited, and most warrantors after January 4, 1975, drafted limited warranties that disclosed restrictions plainly and avoided the full label.

The scarcity of full warranties did not make section 2304 unimportant. The standards disciplined the far larger limited market by defining the benchmark against which the word limited acquired meaning. A consumer comparing two appliances could treat limited as a statutory admission that the warranty failed at least one federal minimum standard. A warrantor drafting a limited warranty had to decide which standard to depart from, whether by charging for labor, covering only named parts, limiting implied warranty duration under section 2308(b), or withholding a refund election after failed repairs. Disclosure rules then required the chosen departures to be stated, and competition could occur over movement toward the full benchmark even when a warrantor stopped short of full status.

## Disclosure, Designation, and Implied Protection in a Single Transaction

A major appliance purchase showed the three provisions working as a single system. A consumer bought a washing machine for household use from a retail seller in 2018, and the manufacturer supplied a written warranty made available before purchase. Section 2302(a) described the contents that Federal Trade Commission rules could require in a written warranty, stated in simple and readily understood language and disclosed fully and conspicuously. The listed contents included clear identification of the names and addresses of the warrantors, the identity of the party or parties to whom the warranty was extended, the products or parts covered, a statement of what the warrantor would do in the event of a defect, malfunction, or failure to conform and at whose expense and for what period of time, a statement of what the consumer had to do and expenses the consumer had to bear, exceptions and exclusions, and the step by step procedure for obtaining performance, including identification of the persons authorized to perform warranty obligations. The list also included information about an informal dispute settlement procedure, a brief and general description of legal remedies available to the consumer, the time and period for performance after notice of a defect, the characteristics of products or parts that were not covered, and wording that would not mislead a reasonable and average consumer as to the nature or scope of the warranty.

The washing machine warranty covered the motor for five years and all other parts for one year, charged for labor after the first year, and bore the conspicuous designation limited warranty. That designation followed from section 2303 because the warranty failed section 2304 in more than one respect, including the labor charge, which conflicted with remedy without charge. The face of the warranty also contained a sentence stating that implied warranties were limited to one year, and section 2308(b) supplied the test for that sentence. The sentence had legal effect only if the one year period was a reasonable duration, the limitation was conscionable, and the sentence appeared in clear and unmistakable language prominently displayed on the face of the warranty.

Language in the sales receipt stating that the seller and the manufacturer disclaimed all implied warranties, including merchantability and fitness, conflicted with the written warranty given for the machine. A disclaimer made in violation of section 2308 was ineffective under section 2308(c) for federal and state law purposes, and the disclaimer did not remove the implied warranty of merchantability arising under the state version of section 2-314. If the machine could not complete a wash cycle or leaked under normal loads during the first year, the implied warranty supplied a claim measured by fitness for ordinary purposes alongside any claim under the written promise to repair covered parts.

The retailer also offered, for a separate price at the checkout counter, a three year service contract covering maintenance and repair after the written warranty period. Because the offer occurred at the time of sale, the timing fell within section 2308(a)(2). A form in the service contract packet purporting to disclaim implied warranties encountered the same federal bar as a disclaimer in the warranty booklet. Section 2306 required service contract terms to be disclosed fully, clearly, and conspicuously in simple and readily understood language, and section 2308 prevented the disclosed contract from serving as a vehicle for eliminating background implied protection.

The word limited on the washing machine warranty signaled that the implied warranty sentence, the labor charge, the parts schedule, and the exclusions deserved close reading under section 2302(a)(5) and (a)(6). A competing machine offered with a warranty designated full for a stated duration would have promised a different legal package under section 2304, including remedy without charge, no limitation on implied warranty duration, and a refund or replacement election after a reasonable number of failed repair attempts. Section 2302 forced the details into a disclosed document, section 2303 forced the summary label to match the section 2304 benchmark, and section 2308 preserved the state law floor beneath both documents.

## The Tie-In Prohibition in the Text of the Statute

Section 102(c) of the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 2302(c), governs a large share of everyday warranty disputes in the United States. In paraphrase faithful to the enacted language, no warrantor of a consumer product may condition a written or implied warranty of that product on the consumer using, in connection with the product, any article or service identified by brand, trade, or corporate name. The parenthetical inside the sentence qualifies the prohibition in one direction, and the exception clause that follows qualifies the prohibition in another, and an account that omits either qualification misstates the law in a way that matters in practice.

A warrantor, as section 2301 defines the term, is a supplier or other person who gives or offers to give a written warranty or who is or may be obligated under an implied warranty. The verb in section 2302(c) is condition, and the object of the conditioning is the warranty itself. A warrantor breaks the rule by making the existence, the continuation, or the honoring of coverage depend on a specified purchasing or servicing choice, and the dependence does not require an express term that uses the word condition. Warranty language, promotional material, a technical service bulletin directed to dealers, or a seal placed across a product housing could each communicate the condition, and the Federal Trade Commission treated implication as well as express statement as a problem in the interpretations and enforcement correspondence described later in this section.

The choice that a warrantor may not compel is an article or a service used in connection with the warranted product and identified by brand, trade, or corporate name. An article covers a replacement part, a filter, or a cartridge installed in or applied to the product. A service covers maintenance and repair labor, whether described by the name of a manufacturer, by the name of a dealer network, or by a corporate designation for authorized providers. The identifying feature is the brand link. A warrantor may state a specification, a viscosity grade, or a performance standard in neutral technical terms, and a requirement stated in those terms raises a different question from a requirement that the part carry one corporate name or that the work occur at a facility operating under one corporate name.

The parenthetical qualification concerns price. The prohibition does not apply to an article or service provided without charge under the terms of the warranty. A tie extracts a second payment from a consumer who has paid for the warranted product and diverts that payment away from competing sellers of parts and competing providers of service. Where the warrantor supplies the named article or performs the named service free of charge under the warranty, the consumer pays nothing extra for compliance. The qualification is narrow. Free provision for one repair does not license a brand condition for other repairs that the warranty does not cover without charge, a point that the Federal Trade Commission made in public guidance issued in April 2018.

The exception clause concerns waiver by the Federal Trade Commission, and the statutory standard is demanding. The prohibition may be waived only if the warrantor satisfies the Commission that the warranted product will function properly only if the article or service so identified is used in connection with the warranted product, and the Commission finds that a waiver is in the public interest. The word only carries the weight of the first test. A showing that the branded article performed better or lasted longer does not satisfy a standard written in terms of exclusive functional necessity, and functional necessity alone does not compel a waiver, because the Commission must also weigh the effect on consumers and on competition. The Commission must identify in the Federal Register, and permit public comment on, all applications for waiver, and must publish the disposition of any such application, including the reasons for the disposition.

### Does using aftermarket parts void a warranty?

Federal law prohibits a warrantor from voiding a warranty or denying coverage solely because a consumer used an aftermarket part or independent service, unless the branded item was provided free of charge or the Federal Trade Commission granted a waiver after the statutory showing.

The interpretation that the Federal Trade Commission adopted at 16 C.F.R. section 700.10 gave the statutory sentence a concrete illustration. The interpretation states that no warrantor may condition the continued validity of a warranty on the use of only authorized repair service or authorized replacement parts for nonwarranty service and maintenance, apart from an article or service provided without charge or a waiver obtained under section 2302(c). A provision declaring a warranty void if service is performed by anyone other than an authorized dealer of a named brand, and requiring genuine parts of that brand, violates the ban on tying arrangements and is deceptive under section 2310, because a warrantor cannot avoid liability where a defect is unrelated to the use of unauthorized articles or service. Language that implies to a consumer acting reasonably that coverage requires purchase of a branded article or service is deceptive on the same reasoning.

## The Burden of Proving That an Independent Part Caused the Failure

The tie-in prohibition answers the question of what a warrantor may demand in advance, and the burden of proof answers the question of what a warrantor must show after a failure has occurred. A warrantor that may not require a branded part in advance does not lose every right to consider the part that a consumer in fact used. The interpretations and the consumer guidance of the Federal Trade Commission preserved a causation defense with clear boundaries. A warrantor could deny liability where the warrantor demonstrated that the defect or damage was caused by an unauthorized article or service. The verb in the interpretation is demonstrate, and the placement of that obligation on the warrantor is the operative point. The consumer does not bear the burden of disproving causation, and the mere presence of a nonbranded part or the mere fact of service outside an authorized network does not, without more, carry the warrantor burden.

An aftermarket oil filter illustrates the allocation in the setting that generated the most documented controversy before November 2018. Suppose an engine developed a knocking noise after an oil change at an independent business that installed a filter made by a company other than the vehicle manufacturer, and suppose the owner sought warranty coverage for engine damage. The filter brand alone supplies no lawful ground for denial under Commission guidance. The warrantor would need to show, through inspection or testing specific to the vehicle and the filter, that the filter was defective, that the filter was incorrect for the application, or that the installation was improper, and that the identified defect or error caused the engine damage. A competing explanation rooted in a defect in the engine itself would defeat a denial that rested only on the filter brand. In February 2012, Kia Motors issued Technical Service Bulletin 114, stating that Kia did not test or approve any aftermarket filters and recommending genuine Kia parts, and directing attention to filter replacement at the customer expense when a noise condition followed a recent oil change and an aftermarket filter was present. The Automotive Aftermarket Industry Association, the Automotive Oil Change Association, and the Tire Industry Association complained to the Federal Trade Commission that the bulletin treated presence as causation.

An independent shop brake repair presents the burden question where the safety stakes are higher. Brake service involves pads, rotors, fluid, and labor that affects stopping performance directly, and a manufacturer concern about improper parts or improper installation in that system is not fanciful. The legal allocation does not change because the system is safety critical. Suppose an independent shop replaced worn pads and rotors with parts from an independent supplier, and a braking failure followed, leading to a claim under the vehicle warranty for a related component. Denial would require a demonstration tied to the actual work and the actual parts, such as measurements showing a rotor outside the applicable specification, evidence that an incorrect pad compound damaged a caliper, or a finding that a technician omitted a required procedure and that the omission produced the failure. If inspection showed that a warranted caliper had seized because of a manufacturing defect and that the independent pads and rotors met specification and were installed correctly, the brand of the pads and the identity of the shop would provide no basis for denial, and the defense would not convert one demonstrated failure into a cancellation of the entire warranty.

A third-party ink cartridge in a home printer extends the same analysis beyond the automobile market, and the extension matters because the statutory text covers consumer products generally rather than vehicles alone. Suppose a printer stopped printing cleanly after installation of a compatible cartridge from an independent supplier, and the owner sought coverage for a failed print head. The warrantor burden would require evidence that the cartridge caused the print head failure, through leakage, electrical incompatibility, or an ink formulation that clogged or corroded the head, demonstrated in the particular machine, rather than evidence only that the cartridge lacked the manufacturer brand. A warrantor that carried that burden could deny the print head claim, and a warrantor that could show only a coincidental brand difference could not treat the cartridge as a basis for refusing coverage of a paper feed defect or another unrelated failure. Brand is not causation, and suspicion is not a demonstration.

## The False Claim and the Repeated Federal Corrections

Measured against that legal structure, the most repeated false claim in American consumer life before November 2018 was the claim that use of independent parts or independent repairs voids a warranty. The claim appeared in warranty booklets, on manufacturer websites, in dealer service departments, in technical bulletins addressed to technicians, and on seals and stickers placed across the housings of electronic products, where breaking a seal to open a device was represented as an act that ended coverage. A consumer facing a failed engine or a broken game console had a strong incentive to avoid any step that a warrantor might later characterize as fatal to coverage, and a warning that overstated warrantor rights could steer servicing decisions even when no claim was ever denied. The warning redirected business toward branded parts and authorized service and burdened the small businesses that offered competing products and services, in the phrasing that Thomas B. Pahl used on April 10, 2018, as Acting Director of the Bureau of Consumer Protection.

The Federal Trade Commission corrected the claim in public, in writing, at intervals across the decades after enactment. A prominent correction was a consumer alert on automobile warranties, routine maintenance, and repairs, issued in connection with a press release dated January 18, 2011, and based on an alert first published in December 2010, under the question whether using the dealer is a must. The alert answered that question in the negative and stated that the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act makes illegal a claim by a manufacturer or dealer that a warranty is void or a denial of coverage simply because someone other than the dealer performed the work. The alert gave the belt illustration that became the standard teaching example. If a consumer or an independent mechanic replaced a belt improperly and engine damage resulted, a manufacturer or dealer could deny responsibility for the engine repair, but only after demonstrating that the improper replacement, rather than another defect, caused the damage, and the warranty would remain effective for other parts of the vehicle. The alert also stated that simply using an aftermarket or recycled part does not void a warranty.

On August 20, 2010, American Honda issued position statements for the Honda and Acura brands recommending genuine parts purchased through authorized dealers, stating that the quality, performance, and safety of other parts and the compatibility of other parts with a particular vehicle were unknown, and stating that only purchase through an authorized dealer assured authenticity, reliability, and compatibility. The statements also declared that the new vehicle warranty and the replacement parts warranty did not apply to any part not purchased from an authorized dealer. On August 25, 2010, the Automotive Aftermarket Industry Association complained to the Federal Trade Commission that the Honda statement conflicted with the statute and misled owners about the ability to choose service and parts without loss of warranty protection. Hyundai had issued a statement in August 2010 warning that aftermarket, imitation, or recycled collision repair parts could compromise vehicle performance in a crash. The Federal Trade Commission ultimately took the position, as reported in proceedings involving the Automotive Oil Change Association, that the Honda position statement did not violate the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, a disposition that independent associations criticized on the ground that implication rather than formal conditioning caused consumer confusion.

The correction with the widest product reach before the November 2018 article date arrived in April 2018, when Federal Trade Commission staff sent warning letters dated April 9, 2018 to six major companies that marketed and sold automobiles, cellular devices, and video gaming systems in the United States. The Commission press release of April 10, 2018 did not name the six companies, and the release stated that, unless warrantors provide the parts or services for free or receive a waiver, statements that consumers must use specified parts or service providers to keep warranties intact generally are prohibited by the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act and may also be deceptive under the Federal Trade Commission Act. Examples of questionable provisions in the release included a requirement of company parts to keep warranties intact and a provision excluding coverage where a warranty seal had been altered, defaced, or removed, the language associated with void if removed stickers. In May 2018, reporting based on a Freedom of Information Act request by Motherboard identified the recipients as Hyundai, Asus, HTC, Sony, Microsoft, and Nintendo. The letters were staff warning letters rather than adjudicated findings of violation.

## Quality Control, Competition, and the Arguments on Both Sides

Original parts are designed and built as components of a tested system, and a manufacturer that has engineered tolerances, materials, and procedures for that system has information that an outside parts maker may lack. American Honda made the quality control argument in the August 20, 2010, position statements, asserting that original parts were designed and built to work together within respective systems to provide optimum performance, safety, and system integrity, and that the quality, performance, safety, and compatibility of aftermarket, counterfeit, or gray market parts were unknown. Kia Motors made a narrower version in Technical Service Bulletin 114 in February 2012, stating that Kia did not test or approve any aftermarket filters and that genuine Kia parts were designed to operate at the specifications established during engine lubrication design and testing. Hyundai Motor Company made a collision specific version in August 2010, as summarized in the association complaints, by warning that aftermarket, imitation, or recycled collision repair parts could compromise vehicle performance in a crash. The contested question in each episode was whether the practical message exceeded a recommendation and operated as a condition.

Safety and liability supplied a second manufacturer argument. The Security Industry Association voiced a version of that argument in opposition to state right to repair legislation in Vermont in reporting published in 2018, through representative John Hoellerer, who raised unaddressed liability concerns about whether responsibility for a malfunction after independent reinstallation of a security system would lie with the independent provider or with the original equipment manufacturer, and who also raised concern about publication of proprietary information. The argument was directed to proposed legislation rather than to section 2302(c) itself, but the underlying concern matched the concern that Honda and Kia expressed in parts language, namely that control over parts and procedures reduces uncertainty about performance and about responsibility when performance fails.

The independent sector answered with competition, price, and convenience arguments advanced by named organizations. The Auto Care Association, which operated as the Automotive Aftermarket Industry Association before a renaming completed in 2014, argued through president and chief executive Kathleen Schmatz in January 2011 that vehicle manufacturers such as Honda put out misleading information that confused owners about the ability to choose service location and parts while protecting a new vehicle warranty. The Automotive Oil Change Association, through president Pat Wirth in proceedings reported after a meeting with Federal Trade Commission officials concerning the Honda statement, argued that Honda language implied that a consumer would void a new car warranty by choosing a nondealer provider and described that implication as patently untrue. The Repair Association, founded in 2013 and led by executive director Gay Gordon-Byrne, argued in public statements summarized in 2016 reporting that manufacturers had eroded the ability to repair a widening class of products over two decades and that restrictions served in part to force purchases of new products or expensive warranties rather than to protect intellectual property, and Gordon-Byrne stated in the Vermont debate in 2018 that state repair measures did not ask manufacturers to reveal source code or trade secrets.

## The Limits of the Prohibition and the Questions Left Outside It

The first limit is the causation defense, and the limit is substantial rather than technical. A warrantor may deny a claim where an independent part was defective, where an independent part was incorrect for the application, where independent service was performed improperly, or where the part or the service caused damage to a warranted component, provided that the warrantor demonstrates the causal link. The second limit is scope. Section 2302(c) governs conditioning of a written or implied warranty on use of a branded article or service, and the provision does not itself create a freestanding entitlement to obtain parts, manuals, tools, diagnostic software, or service information from a manufacturer. A manufacturer could comply with the tie-in prohibition while pairing a replacement component to a particular unit through software or withholding a diagnostic function from independent providers, and each practice would present a legal question under some other body of law or under state legislation. A digital lock that prevents access to software necessary for a repair restricts repair without conditioning a warranty, and the principal federal statute addressed to circumvention of such measures is [the Digital Millennium Copyright Act](/2017/08/01/digital-millennium-copyright-act-1998/) as the other principal federal obstacle to repair, operating through anticircumvention rules and through periodic exemptions granted by the Librarian of Congress rather than through warranty law.

The third limit concerns the difference between a warranty dispute and a repair market dispute. The prohibition protects the coverage that a warrantor has promised or that the law implies, for the duration and on the subjects that the warranty covers, and the prohibition loses direct application when a warranty has expired or when a consumer seeks affirmative access rather than preservation of an existing promise. Right to repair proposals debated in state legislatures in 2017 and 2018, including the Vermont measure discussed in 2018 reporting, sought to move beyond the warranty boundary by requiring manufacturers to make documentation, parts, and tools available on fair and reasonable terms. Enacted as part of the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, signed on January 4, 1975, the provision answered a specific abuse, the use of a coverage promise as leverage over later purchases of parts and service. A reader who treats the provision as a settlement of every repair dispute possesses a misunderstanding of the opposite kind from the false claim that the provision was written to destroy.

## The Informal Dispute Settlement Mechanism Before Suit

Section 2310 of Title 15 built a deliberate sequence into warranty enforcement, and litigation occupied the last position in that sequence rather than the first. Subsection (a)(1) declared a congressional policy of encouraging warrantors to establish procedures through which consumer disputes could be settled fairly and expeditiously through informal dispute settlement mechanisms. Subsection (a)(2) directed the Federal Trade Commission to prescribe rules setting forth minimum requirements for any such procedure incorporated into the terms of a written warranty, and the statute required those rules to provide for participation by independent or governmental entities. The Commission discharged that direction in Part 703 of Title 16 of the Code of Federal Regulations, first promulgated on December 31, 1975, and the resulting framework determined when a warrantor could insist that a consumer pass through a private process before entering a courtroom.

The prior resort consequence appeared in subsection (a)(3), and the text attached that consequence to three cumulative conditions. A warrantor had to establish a procedure, the procedure and its implementation had to meet the requirements of the Commission rules, and the warrantor had to incorporate into a written warranty a requirement that the consumer resort to the procedure before pursuing a legal remedy under section 2310 respecting that warranty. When all three conditions held, an individual consumer could not commence a civil action under subsection (d) without initially resorting to the procedure. A class could not proceed under subsection (d), beyond the extent a court determined necessary to establish the representative capacity of the named plaintiffs, unless the named plaintiffs, after notifying the defendant that they were named plaintiffs in a class action respecting a warranty obligation, initially resorted to the procedure. Representative capacity in a federal class action was to be established through Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. Prior resort operated as a prerequisite rather than as a surrender of the judicial claim. A decision rendered in the procedure did not bind the consumer, and the statute provided that in any civil action arising out of a warranty obligation and relating to a matter considered in the procedure, a decision in the procedure would be admissible in evidence.

Part 703 supplied the operational content that the statute left to rulemaking. Section 703.2 forbade a warrantor from incorporating a mechanism that failed to comply with sections 703.3 to 703.8, and section 703.2 imposed disclosure duties that made prior resort a matter of advance notice rather than surprise. The warrantor had to disclose clearly and conspicuously on the face of the written warranty a statement of the availability of the mechanism, the name and address of the mechanism or its name and a telephone number that consumers could use without charge, and a statement of any requirement that the consumer resort to the mechanism before exercising rights or seeking remedies created by Title I of the Act. The same disclosure had to explain that resort would not be required under the Act if the consumer pursued rights and remedies not created by Title I. The warranty or accompanying materials also had to supply a form addressed to the mechanism or a toll free telephone number, the name and address of the mechanism, a brief description of its procedures, its time limits, and the types of information the mechanism could require.

Independence formed the center of the organizational rules, because a mechanism financed by warrantors had to be insulated from the parties that paid for the program. Section 703.3 required the mechanism to be funded and competently staffed at a level sufficient to ensure fair and expeditious resolution of all disputes, and the mechanism could not charge consumers any fee for its use. The warrantor and any sponsor other than the warrantor had to take all steps necessary to ensure that the mechanism, its members, and its staff were sufficiently insulated from the warrantor and the sponsor, including committing funds in advance, basing personnel decisions solely on merit, and refraining from assigning conflicting duties to mechanism staff. Section 703.4 addressed the qualifications of the members who decided disputes. Members could not be employees of a warrantor or of a sponsor, subject to a narrow allowance for persons whose sole duty was administering the mechanism, and members had to be persons interested in the fair and expeditious settlement of consumer disputes.

Operation under section 703.5 proceeded on a short clock and under duties of candor to both parties. The mechanism had to establish written operating procedures, make copies available upon request, and inform both the warrantor and the consumer immediately of receipt of a dispute. The mechanism had to investigate, gather, and organize all information necessary for a fair and expeditious decision, including issues concerning the number of repair attempts, the length of repair periods, the possibility of unreasonable use of the product, consequential damages, and any other remedy under the Act. Information that tended to contradict facts submitted by the other party had to be disclosed clearly, accurately, and completely to both parties, with an opportunity to explain or rebut the information, and the mechanism could not require information beyond what was reasonably necessary to decide the dispute. If the dispute had not been settled, the mechanism had to act as expeditiously as possible and within forty days of notification. The decision had to include remedies appropriate under the circumstances, including repair, replacement, refund, reimbursement for expenses, and compensation for damages, and had to state a specified reasonable time for performance. The mechanism had to disclose the decision and its reasons to the warrantor, determine whether the warrantor would abide by a decision requiring action, and disclose to the consumer the decision, the reasons, the warrantor intended actions, and warnings that dissatisfaction left legal remedies available, including small claims court, and that the decision was admissible in evidence under section 2310(a)(3). Delay beyond forty days was permitted only within narrow limits, including a period attributable solely to a consumer failure to supply basic identifying information and a seven day period where the consumer had made no attempt to seek redress directly from the warrantor.

Accountability continued after a decision through recordkeeping, audit, and openness requirements. Section 703.6 required the mechanism to maintain records on each dispute, including the identities of the consumer and the warrantor, the brand and model of the product, the dates of receipt and disclosure, the evidence collected, the decision and the identity of members voting, and the warrantor statement of intended action. The mechanism had to maintain indexes by warrantor, brand, and model, compile semiannual statistics tracking resolution, compliance, noncompliance, decisions adverse to the consumer, lack of jurisdiction, and delay beyond forty days, and retain the records for at least four years after final disposition. Those semiannual statistics were open to any person for inspection and copying under section 703.8, which also opened records of oral proceedings to public observation on reasonable conditions, and section 703.7 required an audit at least annually, with the audit report submitted to the Federal Trade Commission and made available to any person at reasonable cost. Subsection (a)(4) added Commission oversight. The Commission could act on its own initiative, and was required to act upon a written complaint filed by any interested person, to review the bona fide operation of a procedure that a written warranty stated to be a prerequisite to a legal remedy, and a finding of noncompliance empowered the Commission to take appropriate remedial action under any authority available under the Act or any other provision of law.

The automobile sector produced the most visible application of the model in BBB AUTO LINE, a program offering mediation and arbitration of automobile warranty disputes, including disputes under the Act and under state lemon laws. During 2018 the program was administered by the Council of Better Business Bureaus, and local Better Business Bureau offices supplied hearing sites and logistical support while participating manufacturers committed funding in advance. The program charged consumers no fee, and annual audits measured the program against the federal requirements in the manner that section 703.7 prescribed. For a vehicle owner, the practical path ran from notice to the manufacturer, through a staff effort at resolution, and, where settlement failed, to a hearing and a decision that the manufacturer had agreed in advance to accept in qualifying cases while the consumer retained the option of rejecting the outcome and filing suit. That asymmetry captured the statutory bargain.

## The Private Cause of Action in State and Federal Court

Subsection (d)(1) created the private action that gave the rest of section 2310 its force. Subject to subsections (a)(3) and (e), a consumer damaged by the failure of a supplier, warrantor, or service contractor to comply with any obligation under the Act, or under a written warranty, an implied warranty, or a service contract, could bring suit for damages and other legal and equitable relief. The action could be brought in any court of competent jurisdiction in any State or the District of Columbia, or in an appropriate district court of the United States subject to the limits in subsection (d)(3). The reference to subsection (e) preserved a cure principle, under which a person obligated under a warranty or service contract generally had to be afforded a reasonable opportunity to cure a failure to comply before suit, with named plaintiffs in a class action supplying that opportunity through notice that they acted on behalf of the class.

Forum selection turned on thresholds that Congress set high for federal court and left absent for state court. Subsection (d)(3) provided that no claim would be cognizable in a federal suit under subsection (d)(1)(B) if the amount in controversy of any individual claim was less than the sum or value of twenty five dollars, if the amount in controversy, computed on the basis of all claims to be determined in the suit, was less than the sum or value of fifty thousand dollars, exclusive of interests and costs, or if the action was brought as a class action and the number of named plaintiffs was less than one hundred. The three figures worked together as a filter. The twenty five dollar floor excluded trivial individual claims, the fifty thousand dollar aggregate floor confined the federal forum to suits of substantial combined value, and the one hundred named plaintiff requirement prevented a small group from using the class form to enter federal court under the Act. No comparable floor restricted the state court route, and the structure therefore directed the ordinary single product dispute toward state court, including small claims court, while reserving the federal forum for large individual losses, aggregated claims of sufficient value, and class actions with at least one hundred named plaintiffs.

## Fee Shifting as the Enforcement Engine

### Can a consumer recover attorney fees after winning a warranty case?

A consumer who finally prevails may recover costs and expenses, including attorney fees based on actual time expended, if the court finds the amount reasonably incurred. The court retains discretion to deny fees as inappropriate, so recovery is authorized but not automatic in every case.

Subsection (d)(2) supplied that rule in a single sentence with four working parts. The consumer had to finally prevail in an action brought under subsection (d)(1). The court could allow the consumer to recover, as part of the judgment, a sum equal to the aggregate amount of cost and expenses, including attorney fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the plaintiff for or in connection with the commencement and prosecution of the action. The court retained discretion to determine that an award of attorney fees would be inappropriate, and that reservation made the award discretionary rather than absolute. The actual time expended standard tied the fee to hours demonstrably spent rather than to a percentage of the recovery, and the reasonableness determination gave the court control over rates, duplication, and effort disproportionate to the task.

Settlement offers interacted with the fee rule through that discretion and through the reasonableness inquiry, rather than through a separate formula stated in subsection (d)(2). The statutory text contained no provision that reduced a fee by a stated fraction because a consumer had rejected an offer, and no provision that awarded costs to a warrantor because a judgment fell below an offer. Dufresne v. DaimlerChrysler Corp., 975 So. 2d 555, decided by the District Court of Appeal of Florida, Second District, in 2008, illustrated the related prevalence question that an accepted offer raised. The consumer accepted a proposal for settlement under Florida procedure, exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney fees, and the trial court denied fees on the theory that acceptance meant the consumer had not finally prevailed. The appellate court reversed, holding that a settlement entered under the Florida offer of judgment statute was judicially enforceable and therefore the functional equivalent of a consent decree, so acceptance did not preclude prevailing party status, with the amount left to the trial court on remand.

The economic significance of subsection (d)(2) exceeded its length. The background rule in American litigation left each side to bear its own attorney fees regardless of outcome, absent a statute, a contract, or a recognized equitable doctrine. Warranty losses sat at the wrong end of that rule. A refrigerator that failed under warranty or a vehicle repair that had to be repeated could impose losses measured in hundreds or a few thousand dollars, while competent representation consumed fees that exceeded the loss. A rational consumer facing unrecoverable fees abandoned meritorious claims, and a rational warrantor pricing the risk of denial could discount the chance of suit accordingly. Fee shifting reversed both calculations. Counsel could accept a small warranty case on the prospect of payment by the defendant after success, a consumer could enforce a written promise without wagering more on fees than the product was worth, and a warrantor evaluating a denial confronted exposure that included the other side litigation expense and grew with delay. That private enforcement model required no agency appropriation, and the model explains why a statute addressed to household goods produced a substantial body of reported litigation.

Class litigation tested the boundary between fee shifting and the older equitable common fund doctrine, and the leading appellate exposition came from the Seventh Circuit. In Skelton v. General Motors Corp., 860 F.2d 250, decided in 1988, consumers pursued a class action under the Act concerning defective automobile transmissions, and the case resolved through a settlement fund that released the defendant from liability both for damages and for statutory attorney fees. The district court awarded fees from the fund under a calculation that reduced the lodestar and denied any enhancement for the risk of nonpayment. The court of appeals held that when a settlement fund was created in exchange for release of the defendant liability both for damages and for statutory attorney fees, equitable fund principles governed the court award of attorney fees, and the court reversed the denial of a risk enhancement and remanded.

## Government Enforcement by the Commission and the Attorney General

Private suits operated alongside two public channels, and the first channel ran through the general prohibition on unfair or deceptive acts or practices. Subsection (b) provided that a failure to comply with any requirement imposed by the Act, or by a rule under the Act, or a violation of any prohibition contained in the Act, or in a rule under the Act, constituted a violation of section 45(a)(1) of Title 15, the provision of the Federal Trade Commission Act condemning unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce. The linkage mattered because the Commission enforced section 45(a)(1) through the machinery of the Federal Trade Commission Act, including administrative complaint proceedings that could end in a cease and desist order and federal court proceedings available under that Act. A warranty violation therefore exposed a warrantor to agency action without proof that an individual consumer had sued.

The second public channel was injunctive and appeared in subsection (c), which the statutory heading described as injunction proceedings by the Attorney General or the Commission for a deceptive warranty, noncompliance with requirements, or violation of prohibitions. The authority sat in subsection (c)(1). The district courts of the United States had jurisdiction over an action brought by the Attorney General, acting in that capacity, or by the Commission through attorneys designated for the purpose, to restrain a warrantor from making a deceptive warranty respecting a consumer product, or to restrain any person from failing to comply with a requirement imposed by or under the Act or from violating a prohibition contained in the Act. Upon a proper showing that weighed the equities and considered the likelihood of ultimate success, and that the action would be in the public interest, a court could grant a temporary restraining order or a preliminary injunction without bond after notice to the defendant. In a Commission action, failure to file a complaint under section 45 within a period specified by the court, not exceeding ten days after issuance of the order or injunction, resulted in dissolution of the order. Subsection (c)(2) defined a deceptive warranty to include a written warranty containing a representation that was false or fraudulent or that would mislead a reasonable individual exercising due care, a written warranty that omitted information necessary to make the warranty not misleading, and a written warranty created through terms such as guaranty or warranty where the terms and conditions so limited its scope as to deceive a reasonable individual.

## Litigation Over the Prerequisite and the Reach of the Mechanism

The nonbinding character of the mechanism produced recurring litigation when a warrantor attempted to convert an informal procedure into binding arbitration that would end a dispute without a court. In Wilson v. Waverlee Homes, Inc., 954 F. Supp. 1530, decided by the United States District Court for the Middle District of Alabama in 1997 and affirmed by the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit at 127 F.3d 40 in the same year, the district court concluded that the Act did not permit a warrantor to compel binding arbitration of warranty claims through a predispute agreement. The analysis rested on the text and structure of section 2310, which encouraged informal mechanisms meeting Commission requirements and preserved a consumer suit for damages and other legal and equitable relief, and on legislative history stating that an adverse decision in an informal proceeding would not bar a civil action on the warranty. The Commission rejection of a proposal to authorize binding arbitration as a qualifying mechanism reinforced the conclusion. Prior resort delayed suit until a fair and fast procedure had run and left the decision admissible rather than conclusive, while binding arbitration displaced the court altogether.

## The Rulemaking Half of the Act

The statute that reached President Ford in January 1975 carried two titles pulling against each other. Title I dealt with warranties themselves, setting disclosure standards, defining full and limited warranties, restricting disclaimers of implied warranties, and creating private remedies for consumers. Title II, styled as a set of improvements to the Federal Trade Commission Act, did something quite different. Section 202 of Title II added a new section 18 to the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57a, and that section governed how the Federal Trade Commission could write trade regulation rules defining unfair or deceptive acts or practices. One half of the act therefore widened the path for individual consumers with defective products, while the other half narrowed and obstructed the path for the agency charged with policing deception across whole industries. The consequences of the rulemaking half flowed less from what the section forbade than from what the section made expensive.

Section 57a authorized the Commission to prescribe rules that define with specificity acts or practices that are unfair or deceptive in or affecting commerce, and to include requirements designed to prevent those acts or practices. A violation of a finished rule counted as a violation of section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, which exposed the violator to the enforcement machinery and civil penalties that attended rule violations. The procedure attached to the grant, set out in subsections (b) through (e) of section 57a, consumed the generosity of the grant. Congress designed a hybrid form of rulemaking that borrowed the written comment process of ordinary informal rulemaking and then grafted onto that process the trappings of a trial.

### Why does the FTC so rarely issue trade regulation rules?

The hybrid procedure in section 57a explains much of the rarity. Each rule demands years of hearings, cross examination, economic analysis, and record building before promulgation, and each finished rule then faces substantial evidence review in a court of appeals. Faced with that price, the Commission has usually chosen case by case enforcement instead.

The sequence began, after amendments enacted in 1980, with an advance notice of proposed rulemaking published in the Federal Register and submitted to the commerce committees of the Senate and the House of Representatives. The advance notice had to describe the area of inquiry under consideration, state the objectives the Commission sought to achieve, and identify possible regulatory alternatives under consideration, and the notice invited responses and suggestions for alternative methods. Only after that preliminary round could the Commission publish a notice of proposed rulemaking, and the 1980 amendments required the proposed notice itself to go to the same two committees thirty days before publication. The proposal had to state the text of the rule with particularity, including any alternatives, and explain the reason for the proposed rule. Interested persons could then submit written data, views, and arguments, and the statute required the Commission to make those submissions publicly available.

Section 57a also required an opportunity for an informal hearing conducted by a presiding officer who answered to a chief presiding officer insulated from the rest of the Commission staff. At the hearing, an interested person could present a position orally, through documentary submissions, or through both. Where the Commission determined that disputed issues of material fact required resolution, the interested person could present rebuttal submissions and conduct cross examination, or have cross examination conducted on that person's behalf, to the extent the Commission judged appropriate and required for a full and true disclosure on those issues. The Commission could impose time limits, group persons with similar interests under a single representative, and channel cross examination through the Commission itself to avoid unnecessary cost or delay. A verbatim transcript entered the public rulemaking record. The hearing was called informal, yet the machinery of transcripts, presiding officers, recommended decisions, and contested factual issues gave the proceeding the tempo and expense of litigation.

The burdens continued after the hearing closed. The Commission could promulgate a final rule only on the basis of the rulemaking record, and the rule had to arrive accompanied by a statement of basis and purpose containing three specified elements. The statement had to address the prevalence of the acts or practices treated by the rule. The statement had to describe the manner and context in which those acts or practices were unfair or deceptive. The statement had to assess the economic effect of the rule, taking into account the effect on small business and consumers. A separate statute enacted in 1980, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57b-3, layered a regulatory analysis on top of the statement. A preliminary regulatory analysis accompanied the proposed rule, describing reasonable alternatives and offering a preliminary assessment of projected benefits, adverse economic effects, and effectiveness for the proposal and for each alternative. A final regulatory analysis accompanied the finished rule and explained why the Commission determined that the rule would attain its objectives. Judicial review then applied a demanding test. Any interested person had sixty days after promulgation to petition a court of appeals, either the D.C. Circuit or the circuit of residence or principal place of business. The reviewing court held the rule unlawful if the Commission's action lacked support in substantial evidence in the rulemaking record taken as a whole, or if a limitation of cross examination or rebuttal had precluded disclosure of disputed material facts necessary to a fair determination of the proceeding.

## Authority Confirmed and Then Burdened

The contrast with ordinary administrative practice explains why contemporaries treated the procedure as a deliberate brake. Under section 553 of title 5, the Administrative Procedure Act provision governing informal rulemaking, an agency ordinarily publishes notice of a proposed rule, accepts written comments, and issues the rule with a concise general statement of basis and purpose. No hearing is required unless another statute demands one, oral testimony and cross examination play no part, and judicial review proceeds under the arbitrary and capricious standard of section 706, a test asking whether the agency articulated a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made. Section 57a expressly directed the Commission to proceed in accordance with section 553, without regard to the formal rulemaking provisions of sections 556 and 557, and then imposed hearing rights, cross examination, transcript requirements, a structured statement of basis and purpose, and substantial evidence review that section 553 never contained. Substantial evidence review, borrowed from formal proceedings and from review of adjudicatory orders, obliged the Commission to build a record capable of surviving a court's examination of the whole record, including evidence detracting from the agency's findings. Procedure of that weight converted every trade regulation rule into a multi-year undertaking.

The background made the design choice sharper. In National Petroleum Refiners Association v. Federal Trade Commission, 482 F.2d 672, decided in 1973, the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit upheld the Commission's authority to issue substantive rules under section 6(g) of the Federal Trade Commission Act, the general power to make rules and regulations for the purpose of carrying out the statute. The case concerned a rule requiring the posting of octane ratings on gasoline pumps, and the court's holding confirmed that the Commission could define prohibited practices by rule rather than proceeding only through case by case adjudication. The Supreme Court denied certiorari in 1974, at 415 U.S. 951. Congress legislated against that backdrop when the act passed in late 1974 and took effect in January 1975. Rather than leaving the confirmed authority to operate under the ordinary Administrative Procedure Act framework that had governed the octane rule, Congress channeled rulemaking on unfair or deceptive practices into section 57a and surrounded the channel with procedural banks. Trade regulation rules could thereafter be issued only through the new section, and the price of the confirmed power rose sharply at the moment of confirmation.

## The Rules That Survived the Process

The output matched the design. Across the four decades after 1975, completed trade regulation rules under section 57a formed a short list, and the two examples most often cited show both the reach of a finished rule and the time required to finish one. The Commission issued the Funeral Industry Practices Rule, commonly called the Funeral Rule, on September 24, 1982, and the rule became fully effective on April 30, 1984. The Funeral Rule required funeral providers to give consumers itemized price information, barred misrepresentations, and protected the right of a consumer to select only desired goods and services, and the Fourth Circuit upheld the rule against industry challenge in Harry and Bryant Co. v. Federal Trade Commission, 726 F.2d 993, decided in 1984. The Used Motor Vehicle Trade Regulation Rule followed a longer road. The warranty title itself, at 15 U.S.C. 2309(b), directed the Commission to initiate a rulemaking on warranties and warranty practices in connection with the sale of used motor vehicles, and the Commission ran that proceeding through the section 18 procedures. A first final version appeared in August 1981, a reconsideration proceeding followed, and the Commission promulgated the revised final rule on November 19, 1984, with an effective date of May 9, 1985. The finished rule required dealers to display a Buyers Guide window sticker disclosing whether a warranty came with the vehicle and on what terms, a regime narrower than the version adopted in 1981, which had included disclosure of known major defects before that element was stripped during reconsideration.

Between the funeral and used car rules, the pattern of the period took shape. Proceedings opened with petitions, studies, and advance notices, consumed years in hearings and record building, and often ended without a rule at all. Commission staff drew the practical conclusion during the 1980s and 1990s, turning toward adjudication, consent orders, and industry guides that could shape conduct without the section 57a apparatus. A finished rule bound an entire industry and supported civil penalties against violators with actual knowledge, advantages that case by case enforcement could not match, yet a single rule could absorb the staff years that dozens of complaints and consent decrees required.

## The Children's Advertising Proceeding and the Legislative Response

No episode illustrated the political exposure of the rulemaking half more completely than the children's advertising proceeding that the press shortened to KidVid. Action for Children's Television petitioned the Commission in 1977 for rules against televised candy advertising aimed at children, and the Center for Science in the Public Interest filed a companion petition against advertising of sugared foods. The Commission staff presented recommendations in early 1978, and the notice published on April 27, 1978, at 43 Federal Register 17967, invited comment on options that included a ban on television advertising directed to children too young to understand selling intent, a ban on advertising of the most heavily sugared products to older children, and disclosure or counteradvertising requirements for other sugared products. The theories rested on unfairness as much as deception, on the premise that advertising addressed to an audience incapable of recognizing persuasive intent was unfair regardless of the truth of any particular claim. Hearings in early 1979 ran for six weeks and drew hundreds of witnesses, and the affected industries, including candy and cereal manufacturers, broadcasters, and advertising agencies, organized opposition inside the proceeding, in the courts, and in Congress. The Washington Post, ordinarily sympathetic to an activist Commission, editorialized against the proposal as an intervention that would turn the agency into a national nanny.

Beginning in 1978, Congress left the Commission operating on temporary appropriations while members debated restrictions on the agency. The confrontation produced a landmark in administrative practice on May 1, 1980, when the Commission closed for one day after its funding lapsed, an event that followed an April 1980 opinion of Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti holding that agencies could not operate during a lapse in appropriations. About sixteen hundred employees were furloughed in what amounted to the first shutdown of a federal agency over a funding dispute, and Congress approved funding that evening.

The Federal Trade Commission Improvements Act of 1980, Public Law 96-252, signed on May 28, 1980, gathered the restrictions into a single statute. The act added the advance notice requirement and the regulatory analysis requirement described earlier, subjecting future rules to additional front end procedure. The act subjected trade regulation rules to a two house legislative veto, under which a rule failed to take effect if both houses of Congress adopted a resolution of disapproval within the statutory review period, a provision codified at 15 U.S.C. 57a-1 that the D.C. Circuit held unconstitutional in decisions the Supreme Court summarily affirmed in 1983, in the wake of the legislative veto decision in Immigration and Naturalization Service v. Chadha. The act barred the Commission from promulgating any rule in the children's advertising proceeding pending on May 28, 1980, or in any substantially similar proceeding, on the basis of a determination that such advertising constituted an unfair act or practice, a prohibition codified at section 57a(h). The act confined the pending funeral industry rule by restricting the use of authorized funds for a final funeral rule unless the rule fell within specified categories centered on price disclosure and prohibitions of misrepresentation, boycotts, tying, and charges without prior approval. The act also barred trade regulation rules governing standards and certification activities. The statute that resulted from the KidVid fight therefore did more than end one proceeding. The episode entrenched a generation of caution inside the Commission about unfairness based rulemaking, and appropriations legislation extended restrictions on advertising rulemakings based on unfairness until 1994.

## Amendments, State Law, and the Division of Labor

The warranty title followed the opposite trajectory from the rulemaking title. Between 1975 and 2018 the substantive warranty provisions went largely unamended, and the stability itself forms part of the statute's history. The designations of full and limited warranties, the restrictions on disclaiming implied warranties, the refund and replacement remedy for products that could not be conformed to warranty after a reasonable number of attempts, and the fee shifting provision for prevailing consumers in section 2310(d) all operated in 2018 in substantially the form enacted in 1975. The clearest statutory amendment to the warranty title during the period was a narrow modernization of disclosure mechanics. The E-Warranty Act of 2015, Public Law 114-51, enacted on September 24, 2015, amended section 2302 to permit a warrantor to satisfy disclosure obligations by posting warranty terms on a website, provided that the product, its packaging, or its manual indicated the website location and an offline means of obtaining the terms, and the Commission revised the Disclosure Rule and the Pre-Sale Availability Rule in 2016 to carry the change into practice. An amendment that concerned the location of warranty text rather than the content of warranty obligations confirmed how settled the 1975 framework had become.

Administrative maintenance proceeded on the same modest scale. In August 2011 the Commission opened a regulatory review of the interpretations, rules, and guides under the warranty act, soliciting comments at 76 Federal Register 52596 on the interpretations at 16 C.F.R. part 700, the disclosure, pre-sale availability, and informal dispute settlement rules at parts 701 through 703, and the warranty advertising guides at part 239. The Commission completed the review in 2015 and retained the rules and guides in existing form, making limited modifications to the interpretations. One modification clarified that warranty language implying that coverage required the purchase of a branded article or service was deceptive under the tie-in interpretation at part 700.10. A review that began with broad questions about online availability and service contracts ended with the framework intact.

The relationship between the federal act and state law explained part of that durability, because the federal statute had never attempted to occupy the field. Section 2311 of title 15, captioned as a provision on applicability to other laws, contained the act's only significant preemption rule, and the rule was narrow. Under section 2311(c), a state requirement relating to labeling or disclosure with respect to written warranties, within the scope of the requirements of sections 2302, 2303, and 2304 and the rules under those sections, and not identical to the federal requirement, did not apply to written warranties that complied with the federal provisions. Section 2311(b) ran in the opposite direction, providing that nothing in the chapter invalidated or restricted any right or remedy of a consumer under state law or other federal law, and preserving state law on personal injury liability and consequential damages. The substantive content of warranty law therefore continued to come from the states. The implied warranties of merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose arose under Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code as enacted in each state, state law governed disclaimer and privity questions outside the federal restrictions, and a Magnuson-Moss action for breach of implied warranty borrowed the underlying state right and added federal procedure, forum, and fee shifting.

State legislatures built the most conspicuous supplement in the market for motor vehicles. Connecticut enacted the first state lemon law on June 4, 1982, Public Act 82-287, requiring a manufacturer to replace a new vehicle or refund its price when a defect could not be repaired after a reasonable number of attempts, and legislatures in other states followed Connecticut's model through the following decade. Lemon laws supplied the refund and replacement outcome that the federal act made available only through the general reasonable attempts standard of section 2304 and through litigation, and the state statutes added the same attorney fee recovery for prevailing consumers that the federal act had pioneered in 1975. Vehicle owners and the lawyers who represented them learned to plead state lemon law claims and federal Magnuson-Moss claims together, with the state statute supplying the substantive entitlement and the federal statute supplying an additional fee award and, in many cases, a federal forum. The division of labor kept the federal act central to warranty practice without making the federal act the source of most warranty rights.

## What the Record Measured

Measured against the ambitions of 1975, the warranty half delivered concrete and limited gains. Warranty terms became available before purchase in a standardized form, the full and limited designations gave consumers a vocabulary that sellers could not lawfully blur, and readable single document disclosure replaced the fine print practices that congressional hearings had documented during the early 1970s. Fee shifting sustained a genuine plaintiff side consumer bar, because a prevailing consumer recovered attorney fees based on actual time expended, which made representation economical in disputes where the amount in controversy alone would not support counsel. The limits were equally concrete. The act imposed no obligation to give a warranty at all, the full warranty standard set in section 2304 proved so demanding that full warranties remained rare in the marketplace, and the fifty thousand dollar amount in controversy threshold for federal court under section 2310(d) pushed smaller claims toward state courts. The tie-in prohibition of section 2302(c), which barred conditioning warranty coverage on the use of a branded article or service unless the article or service came without charge or the Commission granted a waiver, was strong in text and in the Commission's interpretations, and the 2015 clarification extended the prohibition to implied tying. Enforcement of that prohibition in routine retail transactions remained episodic, and the gap between the text and the service counter formed a persistent complaint in consumer comments filed in the 2011 review.

The rulemaking half measured differently. A procedure designed to guarantee deliberation guaranteed delay, and delay operated as a substantive outcome favoring the practices under examination. Between 1975 and 2018 the Commission completed only a small number of new trade regulation rules through the section 57a process, while building most consumer protection doctrine through adjudication, consent orders, policy statements, and guides that lacked the force of a rule and could not support civil penalties against nonparties. The durable lesson of Title II was that procedure decides substance in administrative law as reliably as any definition section, because a legislature unable to assemble a majority to repeal an agency power can achieve much of the same result by pricing the exercise of that power beyond the agency's budget of years and staff. The two halves of the 1975 act thus stand in permanent counterpoint. Title I trusted private litigation, armed individual consumers with fee shifting and standardized disclosures, and produced a working body of warranty practice that state legislatures extended through lemon laws. Title II distrusted administrative litigation, burdened the Commission with the forms of a trial, and produced a rulemaking record sparse enough to invite listing rule by rule. A reader tracing the consequences forward can follow [how the FTC writes consumer rules](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/) through later attempts to revive or bypass the hybrid procedure, and can weigh the federal and state allocation that section 2311 preserved through [federal and state consumer protection compared](/2019/02/01/federal-vs-state-consumer-protection/), where the same division between federal framework and state substance recurs across consumer law. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Does Magnuson-Moss require a warranty?

No. The statute imposes no duty on a manufacturer or a seller to give a written warranty, or any warranty at all, on a consumer product. Its duties are conditional. When a warrantor chooses to give a written warranty, the designation, disclosure, and pre-sale availability rules attach to that voluntary promise. When a supplier gives a written warranty or sells a service contract close to the sale, section 2308 bars disclaimer of the implied warranties that state law supplies. A product sold without a written warranty can therefore carry implied warranty protection under state law while standing outside most of the federal written warranty rules.

### Q: What is the difference between a full and limited warranty under Magnuson-Moss?

A full warranty meets the federal minimum standards in section 2304. The warrantor must remedy a defect without charge within a reasonable time, impose no unreasonable conditions on obtaining that remedy, place no limit on the duration of an implied warranty, and allow the consumer to elect a refund or a replacement after a reasonable number of failed repair attempts. An exclusion of consequential damages counts against full status unless the exclusion appears conspicuously on the face of the warranty. A limited warranty fails at least one of those standards, perhaps by charging for labor or by covering only named parts, and the warrantor must label the warranty limited.

### Q: Do aftermarket parts void a warranty under Magnuson-Moss?

No, not by brand alone. Section 2302(c) bars a warrantor from conditioning written or implied warranty coverage on the use of an article or service identified by brand, trade, or corporate name, unless the article or service is provided without charge under the warranty or the Federal Trade Commission has granted a waiver. A warrantor may deny a particular claim by demonstrating that an aftermarket part was defective, was incorrect for the application, or was installed improperly, and that the defect or error caused the failure. The presence of an independent part or service outside an authorized network, without that causal showing, supplies no lawful ground for denial.

### Q: What is the tie-in sales prohibition in Magnuson-Moss?

The tie-in prohibition in section 2302(c) provides that no warrantor may condition a written or implied warranty on the consumer using, in connection with the warranted product, any article or service identified by brand, trade, or corporate name. The prohibition does not apply to an article or service provided without charge under the terms of the warranty. The Federal Trade Commission may waive the prohibition only when the warrantor satisfies the Commission that the product will function properly only if the identified article or service is used, and the Commission finds that a waiver serves the public interest, with applications and dispositions published in the Federal Register.

### Q: Can you recover attorney fees under Magnuson-Moss?

Yes, when you finally prevail. Section 2310(d)(2) allows a court to award a prevailing consumer, as part of the judgment, costs and expenses, including attorney fees based on actual time expended, that the court determines were reasonably incurred in bringing and prosecuting the action. The award is discretionary rather than automatic, because the court may determine that an award would be inappropriate in a given case. That fee shifting rule changed the economics of warranty enforcement, since a claim worth hundreds or a few thousand dollars could support representation when a successful consumer could recover fees from the warrantor rather than bearing the full cost of counsel.

### Q: Did Magnuson-Moss change FTC rulemaking?

Yes. Title II of the act added section 18 to the Federal Trade Commission Act, codified at section 57a of title 15, and prescribed a hybrid procedure for trade regulation rules defining unfair or deceptive acts or practices. The procedure combined written comment under the Administrative Procedure Act with an opportunity for oral presentation, cross examination on disputed issues of material fact, a statement of basis and purpose addressing prevalence and economic effect, and judicial review under a substantial evidence standard. Amendments in 1980 added an advance notice stage and a regulatory analysis. The added procedure made each rule slower and more expensive to complete than an ordinary agency rule.

### Q: Does Magnuson-Moss cover used goods?

In part. The statute contains no general exclusion for used consumer products, and its written warranty duties attach when a seller or manufacturer gives a written warranty on a covered product, new or used, subject to the statutory definitions and price thresholds. The act imposes no duty to warrant a used product in the first place. Implied warranty protection for a used product depends on state law, on the character of the seller, and on whether section 2308 bars a disclaimer in the transaction. Federal rules for used motor vehicles separately required dealers to display a Buyers Guide disclosing whether a warranty came with the vehicle and on what terms.

### Q: How do state lemon laws relate to Magnuson-Moss?

State lemon laws supply a substantive refund or replacement entitlement for a defective vehicle that a manufacturer cannot repair after a reasonable number of attempts, with the attempts, the defect, and the vehicle age or mileage defined by each state statute. The federal act supplies a parallel claim for breach of a written or implied warranty, together with fee shifting for a prevailing consumer under section 2310(d)(2). Owners and counsel therefore pleaded state lemon law claims and federal Magnuson-Moss claims together in many vehicle cases. Section 2311 preserved rights and remedies under state law rather than displacing them, apart from a narrow rule for state labeling and disclosure requirements that differed from the federal requirements.

### Q: What is an informal dispute settlement mechanism and how does BBB AUTO LINE work?

An informal dispute settlement mechanism is a procedure a warrantor establishes for resolving consumer warranty disputes fairly and expeditiously outside court, under minimum requirements the Federal Trade Commission prescribed in Part 703 of title 16. When a written warranty incorporates a qualifying mechanism and requires resort to that mechanism before suit, a consumer must use the procedure first, although its decision does not bind the consumer and is admissible in a later action. BBB AUTO LINE applied the model to automobile disputes, offering mediation and arbitration without charge to the consumer, with participating manufacturers funding the program in advance and accepting qualifying decisions while the consumer retained the option of filing suit.

### Q: How is a service contract different from a warranty under Magnuson-Moss?

A written warranty is a promise made in connection with the sale of a consumer product, affirming that material or workmanship is free of defects or will meet a stated level of performance, or undertaking to refund, repair, or replace the product when the product fails to meet stated specifications. A service contract is a separate contract in writing to perform maintenance or repair services over a fixed period, frequently sold for a separate price. Section 2306 required service contract terms to be disclosed fully, clearly, and conspicuously. A service contract sold at the time of sale or within ninety days after sale also triggered the section 2308 bar on disclaiming implied warranties.

### Q: What dollar thresholds apply to Magnuson-Moss designation and disclosure rules?

The thresholds differ by provision. Section 2302 applies to warranties on consumer products actually costing the consumer more than 5 dollars. The designation duty in section 2303 applies to warranties on consumer products actually costing the consumer more than 10 dollars. The disclosure rule in Part 701 of title 16 and the pre-sale availability rule in Part 702 of title 16 applied at a regulatory figure of more than 15 dollars. Federal court access under section 2310(d) carried separate figures, including a 25 dollar floor for an individual claim, a 50,000 dollar aggregate floor, and a class action requirement addressed in a separate question in this section.

### Q: Can a seller shorten the duration of an implied warranty?

Only within a narrow permission. Section 2308(b) allowed a supplier to limit the duration of an implied warranty to the duration of a written warranty of reasonable duration, provided that the limitation was conscionable, was set forth in clear and unmistakable language, and was prominently displayed on the face of the warranty. The provision permitted a ceiling tied to the written warranty period. The provision did not permit a period shorter than the written warranty period, and the provision did not permit elimination of the implied warranty during the written warranty period. A warrantor offering a full warranty could impose no limit on implied warranty duration at all, under section 2304(a)(2).

### Q: Are consequential damages covered under a full warranty?

A full warrantor could not exclude or limit consequential damages for breach of a written or implied warranty on the product, unless the exclusion or limitation conspicuously appeared on the face of the warranty, under section 2304(a)(3). The provision imposed a visibility condition rather than an absolute prohibition. Consequential damages are losses beyond the cost of repair itself, such as food spoiled when a refrigerator failed, and recovery depended on governing law concerning foreseeability and proof of the loss. A term placed in a separate manual or in inconspicuous boilerplate failed the statutory condition, so placement and prominence decided whether an exclusion counted.

### Q: What are the requirements for a Magnuson-Moss class action?

A class action under section 2310(d) in federal court had to satisfy the general class action requirements of Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and the specific limits in section 2310(d)(3). The number of named plaintiffs could not be less than 100, the amount in controversy computed across all claims had to reach 50,000 dollars exclusive of interest and costs, and an individual claim below 25 dollars was not cognizable in the federal suit. The named plaintiffs also had to satisfy the informal dispute prerequisite by resorting to a qualifying mechanism, after notice to the defendant, before the class could proceed beyond representative capacity questions.

### Q: What does a consumer have to prove to win a Magnuson-Moss claim?

A consumer had to show that a supplier, warrantor, or service contractor failed to comply with an obligation under the act, under a written warranty, under an implied warranty, or under a service contract, and that the failure caused damage. In practice, the claim rested on the terms of the written promise or on the state law content of an implied warranty, such as merchantability under Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code. The consumer generally had to give the obligated party a reasonable opportunity to cure the failure before suit, under section 2310(e). A consumer who finally prevailed could then seek costs and attorney fees under section 2310(d)(2).

### Q: Does Magnuson-Moss cover an oral warranty?

No. The statutory definition of a written warranty in section 2301 requires a writing, either a written affirmation or promise about material, workmanship, or performance made in connection with the sale, or a written undertaking to refund, repair, replace, or take other remedial action. An oral assurance therefore falls outside the federal written warranty rules on designation, disclosure, and pre-sale availability. State law may give an oral statement independent effect as an express warranty or under other doctrines, and section 2311 preserved rights and remedies under state law. Expressions of general policy concerning customer satisfaction, without specific limitations, also fell outside sections 2302, 2303, and 2304.

### Q: Does warranty coverage transfer to a later buyer under Magnuson-Moss?

The statutory definition of a consumer extends beyond the original purchaser. A person to whom a product is transferred during the duration of an applicable implied warranty, written warranty, or service contract counts as a consumer, and any other person entitled by the terms of the warranty or service contract, or under applicable state law, to enforce the obligations also counts as a consumer. Section 2304(b)(4) likewise extended the duties of a full warrantor to each person who was a consumer with respect to the product. Whether a particular implied warranty claim survived a transfer against a remote manufacturer depended on state privity law, which varied by state.

### Q: How does a warrantor get an FTC waiver of the tie-in prohibition?

A warrantor had to apply to the Federal Trade Commission and satisfy two demanding tests in section 2302(c). The warrantor had to show that the warranted product would function properly only if the article or service identified by brand, trade, or corporate name was used in connection with the product, a standard written in terms of exclusive functional necessity rather than better performance or longer life. The Commission also had to find that a waiver served the public interest, weighing effects on consumers and on competition. The Commission had to identify waiver applications in the Federal Register, permit public comment, and publish the disposition of each application with reasons.

### Q: How does Magnuson-Moss apply to vehicle warranties?

Vehicle warranties formed much of the complaint record behind the act, and the federal rules applied to automobiles as consumer products. A written vehicle warranty had to be designated full or limited, disclosed before sale, and performed as written. Section 2302(c) barred a manufacturer or dealer from conditioning coverage on dealer service or branded parts, apart from free provision or a Commission waiver, and the Commission corrected claims that independent repair voided coverage in guidance and warning letters. Section 2310 supplied a private action with fee shifting, and BBB AUTO LINE supplied an informal mechanism for vehicle disputes. State lemon laws added refund and replacement entitlements pleaded alongside the federal claim.

### Q: How did the rulemaking procedures in Magnuson-Moss differ from ordinary agency rulemaking?

Ordinary informal rulemaking under section 553 of title 5 required notice of a proposed rule, an opportunity for written comment, and a concise general statement of basis and purpose, with judicial review under the arbitrary and capricious standard. Section 57a directed the Commission to proceed under section 553 and then added an advance notice stage after the 1980 amendments, an opportunity for oral presentation, cross examination on disputed issues of material fact, a verbatim transcript, a statement of basis and purpose addressing prevalence, manner of unfairness or deception, and economic effect, and judicial review under a substantial evidence standard. Each added element raised the time and record cost of a trade regulation rule.
