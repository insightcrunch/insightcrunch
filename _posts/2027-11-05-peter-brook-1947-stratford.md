Peter Brook's Stratford production of Romeo and Juliet, mounted at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre in the spring of 1947, was the work of a director who had turned twenty two only days before the first notices appeared, and it announced itself as a deliberate break with the reverent, pictorial Shakespeare that British audiences had been trained to applaud. Brook filled the public scenes with crowds, colour, and orchestrated brawling, played the lovers' scenes against gaunt and unsoftened settings, cut hard at the text, and removed the closing reconciliation of the two houses altogether, so that the tragedy ended without the handshake Shakespeare wrote for the fathers. The notices of 7 April 1947 split along a clean line. One camp saw a recklessly spectacular evening that sacrificed poetry to pictorial splendour. The other saw a fresh rendering, vigorous and untrammelled by tradition, that had found the living heart of the tragedy. Both camps were describing the same staging decisions, and the disagreement between them is the production's permanent value, because it forced a question that staging history had avoided for generations: whether the play belongs to its verse or to its world.

![Peter Brook's 1947 Stratford production of Romeo and Juliet at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict argued through the sections that follow is that Brook's production was the most consequential staging of the tragedy between John Gielgud's recovery of the full text in 1935 and Franco Zeffirelli's reinvention of its youth in 1960, and that its consequence rests on argument rather than on polish. The Stratford staging was not the best spoken Romeo and Juliet of its decade, and the hostile notices were right about the cost exacted from individual verse speaking when spectacle took the stage. The staging was, instead, the first modern production to treat the tragedy as a conflict between a public world and a private one, to build that conflict into scenery, crowd movement, and casting, and to refuse the consolation of the ending as Shakespeare's text offers it. A production that argues a reading of the play, visibly and at some cost to the poetry, changes what later directors believe a staging is for. Brook's Stratford evening did exactly that, and the record of its division, preserved in the notices and in Jill Levenson's performance history, shows the argument landing in real time.

## Peter Brook at Stratford: A Young Director's Wager

The wager can be stated plainly. Shakespeare's tragedy opens with a Chorus who promises that the deaths of two lovers will bury their parents' strife: "And the continuance of their parents' rage, / Which, but their children's end, nought could remove" (Prologue, lines 8 to 9). The promise frames the whole action as a transaction, in which private catastrophe purchases public peace. Brook's staging interrogated that transaction. The public world of Verona, its brawls, its feast, its watch, its ducal court, was staged at full scale and full colour, pressing in on every private encounter until the lovers' meetings looked like clearings in a hostile city. The private world, by contrast, was stripped of decorative comfort. The Manchester Guardian's notice of 7 April 1947 caught the design exactly, reporting that the warmth and colour sat in the brawling and crowd scenes while the balcony and Juliet's bed chamber stood hardly less gaunt and unsoftened than the Capulet tomb. A staging that gives the tomb's gauntness to the bedroom has made its claim before a line is weighed: in this Verona, private life is conducted in spaces the public world has already marked for death.

The claim extends to the ending, and the ending is where the wager becomes unmistakable. Shakespeare closes the tragedy with a civic settlement. Capulet offers his hand, "O brother Montague, give me thy hand" (5.3.296), Montague promises a statue of Juliet raised in pure gold (5.3.298 to 303), and the Prince gathers the survivors into a chastened community before the final couplet. Brook excluded the final reconciliation of the families from his performance text. The decision has been recorded in every serious summary of the production since, because it converts the staging's design argument into a textual one. A director who believes that the public world crushes the private one cannot honestly stage the public world's repentance as a warm settlement over the bodies. The cut told the audience that the feud's machinery, given the whole evening to display its energy, did not deserve the moral dividend Shakespeare's ending pays it. Whether the cut was legitimate is a genuine question, weighed in a later section. That it was the boldest interpretive act in British Shakespeare production in the immediate postwar years is difficult to dispute.

Set against the wager, the production's weaknesses were real and were named at once. The Times, in its notice of 7 April 1947, called the staging a recklessly spectacular version that sacrificed poetry and narrative to pictorial splendour, and complained that the director appeared to lose interest in humanity whenever humanity was not being treated in the mass. The Irish Times, on the same date, judged that the multitudes could swamp the principals and left an impression of too much production and too little play. These were not philistine objections. A tragedy whose lovers speak some of the most closely wrought lyric verse in English makes a legitimate demand on a director, and a staging that subordinates the speaking of that verse to crowd choreography must answer for the exchange. The defence, mounted most fully by Levenson four decades later, is that the exchange was the meaning: the text itself, in her reading, shows everyday existence drowning out a lyrical note through its changing diction, and Brook's version made that drowning visible. The notices noticed the effect and, in her phrase, did not understand it. The adjudication offered here sides with Levenson on the design and with The Times on part of the verse bill, and holds that the production survives the split verdict because its argument proved more durable than its deficiencies.

## Stratford in 1947: Barry Jackson's Young Company

The production cannot be separated from the institution that commissioned it, because the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre of 1947 was itself an argument about renewal. Sir Barry Jackson, the founder of the Birmingham Repertory Theatre and one of the most experienced theatre builders in Britain, had taken charge at Stratford in 1945, under a governing regime chaired by Lieutenant Colonel Fordham Flower, and had set about replacing an ageing festival company with a young ensemble. Jackson's policy, surveyed with approval in Shakespeare Survey after two seasons, was to throw the net wide: producers of different generations and habits, old hand and new, actor and academic, were engaged side by side, so that the company was constantly required to answer a fresh view of each play. The same survey noted the lavish, sometimes extravagant expenditure on scenery and costume that accompanied the policy. Money, youth, and directorial variety were the regime's three instruments, and Brook's Romeo and Juliet used all three.

Brook arrived inside that policy as its youngest instrument. He had directed Paul Scofield in King John at Jackson's Birmingham Repertory Theatre in 1945, while still short of his twenty first birthday, and in 1946 Jackson brought him to Stratford for Love's Labour's Lost, a staging remembered for its delicacy and pictorial wit, with Scofield as Don Armado. The 1947 festival then gave him the tragedy and a revival of the comedy in the same season, an act of institutional trust in a director of twenty two that has few parallels in the British theatre of the period. The season around him, running from April to September, included Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, Measure for Measure, Twelfth Night, The Merchant of Venice, and Pericles alongside the two Brook productions, and the company he inherited was stocked with young performers at the start of major careers. Donald Sinden, who would become one of the defining classical actors of the postwar stage, was in the Romeo and Juliet company. So was Scofield, returning to Brook as Mercutio. The programme preserved in the University of Kent's Ronald James collection lists, among the performers, Walter Hudd, Robert Harris, Beatrix Lehmann, Myles Eason, John Harrison, and Douglas Seale, a mixture of established character playing and new blood that matched the regime's design.

Postwar conditions pressed on the production from outside the theatre as well. Britain in the spring of 1947 was a country of rationing, bomb damage, and demobilised young men, and a festival staging that put a young company into a tragedy about a city consuming its own youth carried a charge that no programme note needed to spell out. The Stratford audience of 1947 did not need the parallel drawn. A Verona whose older generation presided over street violence, whose Prince could threaten and rebuke but not govern, and whose children died inside a settlement their parents negotiated too late, spoke to a playgoer who had spent six years watching states fail at exactly those tasks. Brook's instincts ran toward that charge rather than away from it. Where an older festival tradition might have cushioned the tragedy in period charm, the 1947 staging emphasised the crowd, the brawl, and the public square, the places where a society's failures are enacted in the open. The hostile notices complained about precisely the elements that carried the contemporary weight, which is one reason the production reads, in retrospect, as a document of its moment as well as an interpretation of the play.

The institutional frame matters for a further reason. Jackson's Stratford was not yet the Royal Shakespeare Company, which would not exist until 1961, and the Memorial Theatre's festival still operated as a seasonal gathering rather than a permanent ensemble with a London base. A young director's experiment there had consequences chiefly for reputation, not for a continuing company style, which made the wager cheaper to place and easier to abandon. Brook placed it anyway, and the London transfer of the production to Her Majesty's Theatre later in 1947, recorded in Scofield's stage biography among the credits for his Mercutio, carried the experiment out of the festival frame and into the commercial West End. A Stratford novelty that transfers has been judged twice, once by the festival audience and once by the market, and the transfer stands as evidence that the production's division of opinion was not a local scandal but a portable argument.

## The Jackson Gamble: Money, Youth, and a Director of Twenty Two

The appointment itself requires explanation, because festivals do not ordinarily hand their central tragedy to a director barely out of his teens in experience, whatever his age in years. Jackson's reasoning is recoverable from his policy. The Birmingham Repertory Theatre, which he had founded and run for three decades, had been built on the discovery of young talent, and its alumni included actors who became the defining classical performers of the century. Jackson read Stratford's postwar problem as a talent problem. The festival's prewar company had aged in place, its production habits had settled into routine, and the war had scattered a generation of performers across the services. His answer was a clean sweep: a young company, engaged fresh, and a roster of producers chosen for difference rather than for safety. Brook, who had already directed for him at Birmingham and had delivered the surprise success of the 1946 Stratford season in Love's Labour's Lost, was the policy's purest expression. To give such a director the tragedy was not an eccentricity within the policy. It was the policy, applied without flinching.

The money behind the policy came from the Flower family's theatre, and the Shakespeare Survey's observation about lavish and sometimes extravagant expenditure on scenery and costume deserves its weight in the production's story. Gérard's populated Verona, its feast, its crowds of supernumeraries in designed costume, was expensive theatre by festival standards, mounted in a country where clothes were still rationed and building materials licensed. The expenditure was itself a statement by the governors: the postwar festival would not apologise for scale. Brook's staging spent the money in a particular way that repaid study. The lavishness was public. It dressed the city, the feast, and the crowd, the elements an audience reads as the production's world, while the lovers' rooms stayed bare, so that the festival's wealth appeared on stage as Verona's wealth, the civic splendour of the houses whose feud consumed the play. A cheaper production could not have made the distinction, and a more conventionally lavish one would have spread the money evenly and lost it. The design budget, in Brook and Gérard's hands, became an interpretive instrument, allocating splendour to the world that the tragedy indicts.

The gamble's risk fell on Jackson as much as on Brook. A failed tragedy at the season's centre, directed by the regime's youngest producer, would have indicted the clean sweep itself, and the hostile notices of April 1947 did reach for exactly that larger target, treating the production as the new regime's recklessness made visible. The favourable notices answered in kind, treating the same evening as the regime's vindication. The production therefore carried a double burden from its first night: it had to succeed as an interpretation of the play and as a proof of an institution's direction. That it divided opinion so cleanly, along the line between the old settlement and the new, was in one sense the worst outcome for Jackson's diplomacy and the best for his policy, since a merely competent evening would have proved nothing about the need for change. The division proved the change was real.

## The Road to the Memorial Theatre: King John and Love's Labour's Lost

Brook's preparation for the tragedy ran through comedy and through a single actor. The King John at Birmingham in 1945 had given the twenty year old director a history play of processions, heralds, and a dying king, and had joined him to Scofield, whose presence would anchor his Stratford work across three seasons. The Love's Labour's Lost of 1946 then demonstrated the quality that made Jackson's trust intelligible. That comedy, a play of artificial courtesies and patterned speech that defeats most producers, was staged as a Watteau like entertainment of grace and melancholy, and it established Brook in critical memory as a director of visual intelligence who could make a design idea and a reading of the text coincide. When the same director turned to Romeo and Juliet a year later, the expectation formed by the comedy was for another exercise in pictorial refinement. What the audience received instead was scale, noise, and crowd pressure in the public scenes, held against bare exposure in the private ones. The shock recorded in the notices is partly the shock of a director refusing to repeat his own success.

The contrast between the two Stratford productions is instructive about Brook's developing method. In the comedy, design and text had been fused by elegance: the artificial world of Navarre was the play's subject, and an elegant staging simply showed the subject clearly. In the tragedy, the subject was a collision between two worlds, the public city and the private chamber, and a uniformly elegant staging would have concealed the collision by harmonising its terms. Brook therefore split the visual register. Gérard's scenery and costumes gave the public Verona high colour and populated movement, while the lovers' spaces were left gaunt. A director of twenty two had grasped a principle that many senior contemporaries missed, that a production's design must argue the play's structure rather than decorate its setting. The principle would govern his mature work for decades, through the famous white box of the 1970 A Midsummer Night's Dream and beyond, although the later career belongs to other articles and enters here only as context. In 1947 the principle appeared in embryo, and its embryo form is what the notices were struggling to describe.

There is a second element in the apprenticeship that the record supports and that interpretation should not skip. Brook had come up through wartime and immediate postwar repertory conditions, where companies were young because the profession had been scattered by war, where materials were short, and where a producer's resourcefulness was a practical necessity before it became an aesthetic. The Stratford regime of 1946 and 1947, with its young company and its readiness to spend on scenery while the country rationed food, was an unusual pocket of resource inside an austere theatrical economy. Brook's staging drew on both conditions at once. Its crowds and colour spent the festival's money in the open, where the audience could see the expenditure, while its bare private scenes carried the repertory virtues of economy and directness into the heart of a lavish production. Later legend, encouraged by the spare staging of Brook's maturity, sometimes repaints the 1947 evening as an early essay in emptiness, a first sketch of the empty space. The notices contradict the legend. The Times complained of pictorial splendour, the Irish Times of multitudes, the Guardian of warmth and colour in the brawling scenes. Whatever the production was, it was not empty. Its bareness was local and deliberate, reserved for the lovers, and it gained its force from the populated world around it.

## Casting Youth: Payne, Slater, and Scofield

The casting followed the regime's youth policy into the centre of the play. Laurence Payne, a leading man in his late twenties who had played brother Sebastian in the same season's Twelfth Night, took Romeo. Daphne Slater, who had turned nineteen in March 1947, weeks before the opening, took Juliet. Paul Scofield, twenty five and already the most discussed young actor in the company, took Mercutio. The ages matter because the tragedy had spent generations in the hands of performers in their thirties and forties, whose maturity lent the lovers a dignity the text does not ask for. Shakespeare's Juliet is thirteen, "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3.23), and the play insists on the disparity between her years and her experience at every turn. A Juliet of nineteen does not solve the disparity, and no casting could, but it changes the audience's computation of risk. Slater's Juliet looked, in the Theatre World notice's plain phrase, like Juliet, and the same notice found the ardour present in the playing. Against a Romeo still visibly a young man rather than a tragedian in training, the love scenes could register as an event between contemporaries of the feud's inheriting generation, not as a romance performed by the feud's survivors.

Scofield's Mercutio deserves separate attention, because the role is the production's hinge. Mercutio belongs to both of Brook's worlds. He is the Prince's kinsman and the street's wit, at home in the public square, yet his Queen Mab speech (1.4.53 to 95) opens the play's dream life, the private weather of fantasy that the lovers inhabit. A Mercutio played by the company's most magnetic young actor, in a staging that weighted the public scenes, could hold the balance between the worlds in his own person, brilliant among the crowds and dangerous in his intimacy with Romeo. His death under Romeo's arm, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1.99 to 106), is the moment the public world murders the private one's best friend, and the staging's whole argument passes through it. Scofield's presence in the part, between Payne's Romeo and the crowd scenes around them, gave the argument a human carrier. His Stratford seasons of 1946 and 1947, which also included the title role in Henry V, Don Armado, Malcolm, and Lucio, made him the emblem of Jackson's young company, and his Mercutio was the role in which the emblem met the tragedy's centre.

The youth of the casting carried a cost, and honesty about the production requires naming it. Young companies speak verse unevenly, and a director who weights crowds and movement will expose young speakers at exactly the moments when the verse makes its largest demands. The hostile notices' complaint about sacrificed poetry had a practical basis in the company's experience as well as in Brook's priorities. Slater was a RADA gold medallist fresh from a film contract, Payne a repertory leading man rather than a finished tragedian, and the balcony scene and the potion scene ask for a command of sustained lyric and rhetorical verse that few performers of any age possess entire. Brook's wager accepted the risk. The production bet that an audience would forgive uneven verse in exchange for a Verona that behaved like a real city and lovers who looked like the children the text describes. The division in the notices is, in part, the sound of that bet being settled differently by different judges.

### Did the young cast help or hurt the verse?

The young cast helped the tragedy's truth and hurt its finish, and the help outweighed the hurt. Slater at nineteen and Payne in his late twenties made the love legible as a generational event, while Scofield's Mercutio carried the public scenes. The verse, unevenly spoken in places, lost polish and gained urgency, which is the exchange the production intended throughout.

The exchange deserves one further turn. Verse speaking in the British theatre of 1947 was still dominated by an older ideal of musical delivery, in which the line's shape was honoured even at the expense of its pressure as speech. Gielgud, the presiding example of that ideal, had built the 1935 production around it. A young company directed by a young producer was unlikely to beat the older school at its own discipline, and Brook did not try. He redirected the company's energy toward situation, movement, and the crowd, accepting a plainer delivery of the verse as the price. Later British verse speaking would find a middle way, conversational yet metrically exact, and Brook's own mature productions would demand it. In 1947 the middle way was not yet available as a company skill, and the production's verse must be judged as the record presents it: the element on which the staging spent least, in a reading of the play that spent most on everything else.

## The Lovers Themselves: Slater's Juliet and Payne's Romeo

Slater's Juliet rewards a closer look, because her casting condensed the regime's whole theory into one performer. She had left the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art with its gold medal, the academy's highest mark for a graduating actor, and had been taken up at once by the film director Herbert Wilcox, who gave her a leading role in The Courtneys of Curzon Street in the same year as the Stratford season. A film contract of that kind, in the British cinema of 1947, was a machinery for manufacturing ingenues, and its presence in her biography might suggest a decorative Juliet cast for her face. The Stratford evidence points the other way. In the same festival she played Olivia in Twelfth Night, a part that punishes mere decoration with its shifts from mourning ceremony to erotic disorder, and Harold Matthews's season survey in Theatre World, which singles out her Olivia as young and high spirited, gives the Romeo and Juliet notice its weight when it finds that Slater looked the part of Juliet and that the ardour was present in her playing. Ardor, in a nineteen year old speaking some of the most demanding lyric verse in the language, inside a staging that stripped her scenes of scenic support, is not a compliment to appearance. It is a judgement about force.

The potion scene is the test the record lets a reader apply. Juliet's soliloquy before the draught, "Farewell! God knows when we shall meet again" (4.3.14), runs through imagined horrors, the vault's stench, the mad spirits, Tybalt's ghost hunting for Romeo, and ends in the toast that converts terror into willed action. The speech defeats performers far older than nineteen when it is treated as a set piece, because its logic is a mind arguing itself past fear in real time. A staging that leaves the bed chamber gaunt gives the speech no cover: no properties to handle, no picturesque gloom to hide in, only the voice and the argument. That Brook entrusted the scene, in that setting, to a performer of nineteen, and that the favourable notices record ardour rather than embarrassment, tells a reader where the production's confidence lay. The young company was not a limitation the staging endured. It was the instrument the staging chose, and its riskiest assignment was given to its youngest principal.

Payne's Romeo presents the complementary case. Romeo is the harder part to cast young, because the role's first act requires a performer to make Petrarchan convention legible as character, the sighing Rosaline worship that the play itself mocks, before the feast converts him. An older tragedian solves the problem with technique, signalling the convention's hollowness from outside it. A young leading man solves it by inhabiting it, playing the early scenes as a boy's sincere performance of a fashion he has not yet outgrown, so that the meeting with Juliet registers as the first unperformed feeling of his life. The production's design supported the second solution. Romeo's early appearances belong to the street and the crowd, Brook's strongest territory, where a young man's posturing reads socially, among companions who tease it. By the time the staging withdrew him into the gaunt private spaces, the audience had watched the posture fall away in public, and the verse of the orchard scene could be received as a new instrument rather than as a debut. The notices preserved no single famous phrase for Payne's Romeo comparable to the legend that would gather around Scofield's later roles, and the absence is itself informative: this was an ensemble production, remembered for its staging argument, in which even the title roles were subordinated to the whole. Subordination of the lovers to the production's argument is, once again, the Times complaint and the Levenson defence stated in a single fact.

## The Company Around the Principals: An Ensemble Before the Word

A festival production of this scale stands or falls on its ensemble, and the 1947 programme preserves an ensemble worth pausing over. Beyond the three principals, the cast list in the Kent programme record names Walter Hudd, Robert Harris, Beatrix Lehmann, Myles Eason, John Harrison, Douglas Seale, John Ruddock, John Blatchley, Michael Golden, Gwen Williams, Veronica Turleigh, and Donald Sinden among the performers, a roll that mixes the generations in the proportions Jackson's policy prescribed. Lehmann brought the authority of a major interwar career, formed at the Birmingham Repertory Theatre under Jackson himself a quarter century earlier, so that the young company played nightly alongside a living link to the institution's founding generation. Hudd and Harris supplied seasoned character weight of the kind a repertory season consumes in bulk. Sinden, at the very start of his career, stood at the roll's other end. A production remembered for its crowds was, in personnel, exactly what its staging argued: a city of mixed ages and standings, within which the young principals burned at the centre.

Repertory conditions shaped what such an ensemble could give a single production. The same performers were carrying roles across a season of seven or more plays, rehearsing by day and rotating performances by night, and the Shakespeare Survey's two season retrospect noted the tax that a varied roster of producers laid on players asked to answer a fresh directorial view in each production. Brook's demands, crowds composed to a pattern, movement orchestrated rather than improvised, fell on performers who were simultaneously serving other directors' contrary demands in the same week. That the notices converge on the crowd scenes' effectiveness, whether in praise or in complaint, indicates how fully the ensemble met the demand. Orchestrated crowd work is the first casualty of an overstretched company, since it cannot be faked by individual brilliance. Its success at Stratford in 1947 is the young company's collective credit line, and it complicates any account of the production as one young man's imposition on passive material. The argument was Brook's. Its execution belonged to a company that Jackson had assembled for precisely such tests.

The ensemble point also corrects a distortion in the production's later reputation. Because Brook's career became a sequence of famous collaborations and famous breaks, the Stratford evening is often narrated as an early solo, the prodigy against the institution. The programme tells a duller and better story: an institution that hired him, a governor who funded him, a designer and a composer who built his Verona, a senior actress formed by his employer's own theatre, and a company of peers who made his crowds move. Originality in the theatre is a property of teams before it is a property of names, and the 1947 record, read in its credits rather than in its legend, shows the team entire.

## Gérard's Verona: Colour in the Streets, Gauntness in the Chamber

Rolf Gérard, the German born designer who had become one of the most sought after stage artists in London, supplied the scenery and costumes, and Roberto Gerhard, the Catalan composer settled in England, supplied the incidental music. The two names, Gérard and Gerhard, have confused programme readers and later summaries ever since, and the production's documentation keeps them distinct: the eye of the evening was Gérard's, its ear Gerhard's. Gérard's Verona gave the festival its money's worth in the public scenes. Brawls, the Capulet feast, the street encounters, and the ducal interventions were staged with many supernumeraries, high colour, and the careful orchestration that Levenson notes in her account, the movement of crowds composed rather than improvised. The design made the feud visible as a social condition. A feud between two households, staged with principals only, reads as a private quarrel with unfortunate reach. The same feud staged through a populated city, where servants, citizens, watchmen, and guests fill the square, reads as a civic disease, which is what the Prince calls it when he charges the citizens with profaning their neighbour stained steel in the "pernicious rage" of the streets (1.1.77 to 86).

The feast scene shows the design argument at its sharpest. Shakespeare's ball is the one occasion when the public world and the private one occupy the same room on terms of ceremony: the lovers meet inside a ritual of the enemy house, "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5.137). A crowded, coloured, carefully danced feast gives that meeting its danger. Romeo and Juliet find each other not in a picturesque corner but inside the feud's own festival, watched by Tybalt, hosted by Capulet, surrounded by the machinery of alliance and display that intends Juliet for Paris. Brook's staging, by all accounts, made the feast a spectacle in its own right, and the spectacle was the point: the lovers' sonnet, the most formal and private utterance in the play, had to be carved out of a room full of public business. The Guardian's distribution of warmth, colour in the crowd scenes and gauntness in the chamber, describes a production that understood where the play keeps its heat. The heat is social before it is erotic, generated by a city that will not leave its children alone.

Against that colour, the private scenes stood bare. The balcony scene, which pictorial tradition had upholstered with moonlight, foliage, and masonry, was played in a space the Guardian found hardly less gaunt than the tomb. Juliet's bed chamber received the same treatment, and the tomb itself completed the sequence. The progression is the design's sentence: orchard wall, chamber, and vault rendered in one visual grammar of exposure, so that the audience watched the lovers' world narrow toward the grave by design rather than by plot alone. Brook and Gérard had, in effect, staged Nicholas Brooke's later critical observation avant la lettre, that the tragedy's form yokes lyric privacy to a public frame that finally crushes it, although the critical vocabulary for the observation did not yet exist. The notices that complained of the gauntness were registering the sentence without parsing it. Their complaint is the best evidence that the design registered at full strength.

### Why did Brook fill the stage with crowds for a love story?

The crowds were the argument. Brook staged Verona as a populated city whose brawls, feast, and watch pressed on every private meeting, so that the lovers appeared as clearings in hostile public space. The Guardian saw warmth and colour concentrated in those scenes. Their pressure made the feud a civic condition rather than a family quarrel.

Gerhard's music belongs in the same account, since a crowded stage in 1947 was also a sounding one. Incidental music in the postwar festival theatre still carried the burden of scene change and atmosphere that later practice would hand to lighting and sound design, and a composer of Gerhard's standing, a pupil of Schoenberg building a British career, signalled that the production's modernity extended beyond its visuals. The record preserves the credit more clearly than the score's character, and restraint is proper here: the music's presence in the programme is a fact, its effect recoverable only through the general impression of pace and occasion in the notices. What can be said is that Brook, at twenty two, was already assembling collaborators of independent reputation rather than house functionaries, a habit that would define his later companies. Gérard and Gerhard in 1947 are the first clear instance of the pattern.

## The Staging Scene by Scene: Brawl, Feast, Duel, Tomb

The design argument can be traced through the evening's four great public occasions, and the tracing shows how consistently the staging applied its grammar. The opening brawl sets the terms. Shakespeare begins not with the lovers but with servants trading insults over a bitten thumb, "Do you bite your thumb at us, sir?" (1.1.42), and escalates through Benvolio's attempted peace, Tybalt's contempt for it, the citizens' clubs, and the old men's absurd eagerness to join, before the Prince imposes order by decree. Most pictorial stagings treated the scene as colourful preamble. In a crowded staging it becomes the thesis in miniature: a whole city, from kitchen staff to ducal court, organised around a quarrel nobody present can explain, "bred of an airy word" (1.1.87). Brook's supernumeraries made the escalation social rather than anecdotal. The audience watched a brawl recruit a population, which is the tragedy's whole plot in its first ten minutes, and the Prince's rebuke, delivered to a stage full of citizens rather than to a pair of principals, landed as an address to a polity. Every later crowd scene inherited the charge of the first.

The duel in the street at the tragedy's centre extends the same logic to its catastrophe. The scene (3.1) is the play's most public event: Mercutio and Tybalt fight before witnesses, Romeo's intervention causes the fatal wound, and the Prince returns to find two corpses and a city demanding sentences. A staging strong in crowd work can make the scene's horror civic, the bystanders' fascination and paralysis forming part of the event, so that Mercutio's dying curse on both houses addresses a population that has stood and watched. Scofield's Mercutio, dying in a populated square rather than in a picturesque corner, gave the curse its full address. The scene also contains the production's boldest continuity of argument: the same crowd energy that made the feast delightful made the duel lethal, the city's vitality and its violence shown as one substance at two temperatures. Heat, in the brief's own word for the production's emphasis, names exactly that substance. Brook's Verona was hot not because its lovers burned in isolation but because its streets ran at a temperature at which any spark, a bitten thumb, a stepped between swords, could ignite the whole square.

The tomb scene, shorn of the reconciliation, closed the sequence with the grammar reversed. The churchyard is a public place invaded by private errands, Paris scattering flowers, Romeo breaking in with mattock and wrenching iron, the Friar arriving with lantern and spade, the Watch assembling the survivors. Shakespeare assembles the whole city at the vault, as if to insist that even this most private catastrophe must be transacted in public. Brook's staging, on the evidence of its design grammar, could hold the assembly and refuse its conclusion: the crowd present, the settlement withheld. The effect, for an audience that knew the play, would have been a withheld cadence, the musical phrase that declines to resolve. For an audience that did not, the ending simply stopped at the catastrophe and the Prince's grief, which is where the Chorus's own summary, "a pair of star crossed lovers take their life" (Prologue, line 6), had always located the story's end. The cut thus aligned the performance with the Prologue's bleaker contract against the final scene's more generous one, a choice between two endings Shakespeare himself wrote into the play's frame. Framed that way, Brook's cut is less an amputation than a siding with one of the text's own voices against another, which does not settle its legitimacy but does deny its critics the easy charge of ignorance.

## The Play's Own Heat: The Textual Warrant for Brook's Verona

The brief for any account of this production stresses Mediterranean heat, and the emphasis is not a publicist's colour. Shakespeare's text keeps its own weather report, and Brook's staging can be read as an attempt to stage the weather the verse describes. Verona's action runs through a summer pressure system. Benvolio opens the fatal scene with a forecast: "The day is hot, the Capels are abroad, / And if we meet we shall not scape a brawl, / For now, these hot days, is the mad blood stirring" (3.1.4 to 6). The play's erotics run on the same temperature. Juliet is the sun in Romeo's figuration (2.2.3), the feast is a night of lamps and torchlight, the wedding night is wished forward in the language of a Phaeton gallop toward darkness, and the city's violence and its desire share a vocabulary of fire, powder, and haste. "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6.9 to 11), the Friar warns, in the play's most exact statement of its thermodynamics. A director who stages the tragedy cool, as a moonlit romance with incidental swordplay, suppresses half the text's weather. Brook's crowds, colour, and pace restored the heat to both systems at once, desire and violence, and the restoration is the strongest textual defence of his method: the staging's temperature was not imposed on the play but read out of it.

The point deserves pressing, because it distinguishes Brook's Verona from the merely picturesque Italianate tradition it displaced. Nineteenth century stagings had made Verona warm in the decorative sense, sunlit backcloths, balconied facades, carnival colour as local charm, while playing the action at a temperate emotional register suited to mature performers and a consoling ending. Brook inverted the allocation. Gérard's colour belonged to the crowd scenes, where the text's heat is social and dangerous, and the lovers' scenes, which the decorative tradition had warmed with moonlight and foliage, went gaunt and cool in appearance while the verse supplied the temperature. The staging thereby separated the play's two heats: the city's, which kills, rendered in colour and mass; the lovers', which the city kills, rendered in exposure and voice. A spectator could watch the first heat surround the second all evening. The Guardian's weather report of the production, warmth in the brawling scenes, is the sound of that separation registering on a critic who had expected the warmth elsewhere.

Heat also governs the production's pace at the level of the scene sequence. The tragedy's four days are a heatwave's duration, and the play marks the pressure with a run of short scenes, messages, and near misses in its second half, a dramaturgy of no relief. A staging that lingers, changing scenery at leisure between picturesque tableaux, breaks the pressure and lets the audience cool. Brook's paraphrase, whatever it cost in cut passages, protected the pressure by keeping the public machine in motion around the lovers, so that the intervals themselves felt inhabited by the city. The method anticipates a principle of his mature practice, that a production's rhythm is a form of meaning, and it answers the standing question of why a director celebrated later for stillness and space began with crowds and speed. The crowd was the tragedy's weather system. Stillness, in 1947, would have been a lie about the climate of the play.

## The Private Scenes Problem: Exposure as Interpretation

The gauntness of the private scenes drew the sharpest single line in the hostile notices, and it deserves a section of its own, because the choice was the production's most misunderstood act of interpretation. Pictorial tradition treated the balcony scene as the tragedy's sanctuary, the place where staging lavished its tenderness. Brook and Gérard refused the sanctuary. Their reasoning, as Levenson reconstructs it from the evidence of the notices, was structural. Shakespeare's text keeps interrupting lyric privacy with public business. The balcony scene itself is framed by Mercutio's bawdy conjuring in the street outside the wall, and Romeo's first soliloquy begins from concealment among trees while his friends call through the dark. The wedding is arranged in a friar's cell under the shadow of the feud's politics, "In one respect I'll thy assistant be: / For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.90 to 92). The wedding night is broken by the lark and by the news of Tybalt's death and Romeo's banishment. The potion plot is a conspiracy conducted through messengers, letters, and a hired vault. Privacy in this play is never a possession. It is a series of raids on public space, and a staging that upholsters the raids as sanctuaries misrepresents the text's own distribution of safety.

Played bare, the private scenes acquired a different pathos. Juliet at a window in an unsoftened space, speaking "Gallop apace, you fiery footed steeds" (3.2.1) to a bare stage picture, becomes a figure of exposure rather than of romance, a girl addressing the night because the city offers no room built for her desire. The bed chamber scenes, staged in the same grammar, prepared the audience for the vault without a change of visual key, so that the tomb read as the last of the private rooms rather than as a new location. The design thereby solved a staging problem that defeats many productions, the fifth act's relocation of the action to Mantua and the churchyard, by making location matter less than condition. Everywhere the lovers met, the condition was the same: insufficient shelter. The Guardian's comparison of chamber and tomb, offered as a complaint, states the achievement exactly.

The cost, again, was verse. A bare stage throws the whole burden of atmosphere onto the speaking voice, and young voices in a festival company will not always carry it. The balcony scene in a gaunt setting with a Juliet of nineteen is a high wire act: the poetry must supply the moonlight that the design withholds. Where Slater's ardour, praised in Theatre World, met the demand, the scene could achieve a concentration that upholstered versions rarely reach. Where the speaking fell short, the scene had nothing to fall back on, and the Times critic's charge of sacrificed poetry found its evidence. Brook's wager was that concentration would occur often enough to justify the exposure. Retrospective judgement, which knows the production through its reputation rather than through a recording, cannot settle the nightly account. It can settle the interpretive one: the exposure was a reading, argued in space, and the reading is sound.

### Was the balcony scene really played bare, and was that a failure?

The Guardian's notice supports the bareness: the balcony stood hardly less gaunt and unsoftened than the tomb. The failure charge does not follow. Bareness was the staging's grammar for every private space, aligning orchard, chamber, and vault, and it threw the scene's burden onto the verse and the performer, where the play itself places it.

## The Verse Question: What the Hostile Notices Got Right

The adjudication promised earlier must now pay its debt to the hostile camp, because the verse question is not a misunderstanding to be dissolved but a loss to be entered in the ledger. British Shakespeare production in 1947 inherited a verse speaking culture of real sophistication. Its presiding intelligence, Gielgud, had demonstrated across the nineteen thirties that the tragedy's verse could carry a production almost unaided, its music and rhetoric organised by a single great instrument into an evening's architecture. University and repertory training fed the same ideal at lower altitudes: speak the line's shape, honour its rhetoric, let the poetry do the staging's work. Against that culture, a twenty two year old director who subordinated speaking to movement and crowd composition was not merely making a trade. He was demoting the art his audience's judges valued most, in the play where that art is most exposed. The Times critic's phrase about sacrificed poetry was the culture defending its treasury, and the treasury was real.

The loss can be specified more exactly than the notices specified it. What a paraphrase staging sacrifices first is not beauty but information. Shakespeare's verse carries the play's analysis of itself: the Friar's herb speech weighs human nature in its plant metaphors, "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, / And vice sometime by action dignified" (2.3.21 to 22), Mercutio's Mab fantasy diagnoses the dreaming city's appetites, and Juliet's epithalamion thinks through the erotics of night in images that a staging can illustrate but never replace. A production that trims such passages for pace, or plays them at crowd tempo, thins the audience's access to the play's own commentary on its events, and no quantity of choreographed brawling returns the loss. Levenson's defence, that the staging made the drowning of the lyrical note visible, answers the charge at the level of the whole evening. It does not answer it speech by speech, and a reader weighing the production should keep the two levels distinct. The whole evening argued something true about the play. Particular speeches, on the contemporary evidence, were spent to buy the argument.

Why, then, does the verdict of this article still side with the production? Because the alternative on offer in 1947 was not a staging that united Gielgud's verse with Brook's argument. No such staging existed, and the conditions for it, a company trained in both disciplines under a director fluent in both, would take the British theatre another generation to build. The choice the festival audience actually faced was between the inherited article, verse centred, pictorial, and consoling at the ending, and Brook's article, argued, youthful, and unconsoling. Judged as a choice between available goods rather than against an unavailable ideal, the Stratford wager bought more than it spent. It bought a reading of the play that scholarship would spend decades catching up with, at the price of verse finish that a different company on a different night might have supplied under the same design. The design was the production's bequest. The speaking was its circumstance. History keeps bequests and forgets circumstances, which is why the production's reputation has grown as its deficiencies have faded from living memory, and why the notices' hostility, preserved in the record, performs the useful office of remembering what the legend would prefer to drop.

## The Text on Brook's Stage: Paraphrase, Subtraction, and the Missing Handshake

Levenson titles her chapter on the production "Paraphrase, 1947", and the title is the most precise single word the record offers for Brook's textual practice. Her book's general thesis is that the decisive productions in the tragedy's stage history, Gielgud's excepted, subtracted a great deal from Shakespeare's text, each subtraction amounting to a version of the play restated in the production's own terms. Garrick subtracted to isolate pathos, Cushman to isolate Romeo, Zeffirelli would subtract to isolate youth and milieu. Brook's subtraction isolated the counterpoint of public and private worlds. Passages that fed neither the city's pressure nor the lovers' exposure were vulnerable, and the performance text that resulted was less a shortened Shakespeare than a restatement of the tragedy with its centre of gravity moved. The word paraphrase, in her usage, carries no sneer. A paraphrase is a text that has been thought through in another idiom, and her chapter treats Brook's idiom, the visual and choreographic language of crowds and bare rooms, as a genuine medium of interpretation.

The most consequential single cut was the ending. Shakespeare's final scene assembles the survivors in the churchyard and conducts a settlement: the Friar's narrative, the Prince's judgement, Capulet's offered hand, Montague's golden statue, and the distribution of pardon and punishment under the Prince's closing words, "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" (5.3.308). Brook excluded the families' reconciliation from his performance text, ending the tragedy without the settlement's central gesture. The cut must be understood against the Chorus's promise, quoted earlier, that only the children's end could remove the parents' rage. Brook's staging declined to cash the promise. The rage, in his Verona, was not a misunderstanding that grief could dissolve in an evening. It was the city's operating system, displayed for five acts in brawls, feasts, duels, and civic ceremony, and a handshake over two bodies would have priced the children's deaths as an acceptable fee for its suspension. The cut therefore argued that the tragedy's ending, as written, consoles too cheaply, and that a production honest about its own Verona must withhold the consolation.

The argument is strong, and its weakness is equally visible. Shakespeare's ending is not, in the text, a warm reconciliation. The Prince's tone is judicial and exhausted, the statues are a rich man's competitive grief, Capulet and Montague vie in endowment even as they clasp hands, and the final couplet calls the story one of woe without distributing comfort. A skilful production can play the settlement cold, as civic theatre performed by men who have run out of alternatives, and keep every line. Brook, at twenty two, chose the blunter instrument. He cut where a maturer director might have inflected. Yet the bluntness had a historical function that inflection could not have achieved. In 1947, the ending's settlement was still habitually played as the tragedy's moral, the moment where the audience's tears were certified as socially useful. Removing the settlement denied the certification, in a country then negotiating its own settlements after a war that had killed its young in industrial numbers. The cut's crudity and its timeliness are the same fact. A director's first duty, on the theory Brook would later articulate, is to the moment of the production's correctness, and in the spring of 1947 a reconciled Verona would have been a false note struck in gold.

Other cuts followed the same logic at smaller scale, and the record, which preserves the performance text's character better than its line by line contents, supports describing their tendency rather than inventing their inventory. Levenson's promptbook research for her Oxford edition, which assembled profiles of cuts and staging notes from some one hundred and seventy promptbooks across three centuries, places Brook's text among the century's decisive revisions, and her chapter's emphasis falls on proportion: elaborate public scenes expanded by choreography and numbers, lovers' episodes compressed by the pressure around them. The paraphrase was thus rhythmic as much as verbal. Brook re timed the tragedy so that public time, crowded and sequential, consumed private time, brief and raided. The play's own clock, four days from brawl to tomb against the nine months of Arthur Brooke's source poem, already runs at that pressure. The staging made the audience feel the clock in the body, which is what a paraphrase in the theatrical idiom is for.

### What did Brook cut from the ending, and what did the cut argue?

Brook removed the closing reconciliation between Montague and Capulet, the offered hand and the golden statue settlement that end Shakespeare's final scene. The cut argued that the feud, displayed all evening as the city's operating system, had earned no moral dividend from the children's deaths, and that the Chorus's promise of buried strife consoles too cheaply.

## The Notices of April 1947: A Division Adjudicated

The critical division is documented with unusual completeness, because the three notices of 7 April 1947 and the season survey in Theatre World between them stake out every position that later commentary would occupy. The Times led the hostile camp. Its critic saw a recklessly spectacular version, poetry and narrative sacrificed to pictorial splendour, and a director whose interest in humanity revived only when humanity arrived in the mass. The Irish Times supported the charge from a different angle, judging that the multitudes could swamp the principals and that the evening left an impression of too much production and too little play. The Manchester Guardian filed the most valuable notice of the three, divided against itself: warmth and colour in the brawling and crowd scenes, gauntness in the balcony and the bed chamber. The Guardian's critic had seen the design's distribution and reported it faithfully, while reading the distribution as imbalance rather than as argument. Harold Matthews, surveying the season for Theatre World in June 1947, led the favourable camp, calling the staging a fresh rendering, vigorous, colourful, and untrammelled by tradition, and finding in the young company, Slater above all, the ardour the tragedy requires. Levenson preserves one further favourable verdict, from a contemporary essay on style in Shakespearean production, judging that the production reached the essential living heart of the play, the poet's inner dream.

Adjudicating the division requires separating three questions that the notices run together. The first is empirical: what did the staging do? On this the camps agree completely. Crowds, colour, and orchestration in the public scenes; bare exposure in the private ones; a young company; a hard cut text ending without reconciliation. No notice disputes another's facts. The second question is evaluative: was the verse well served? Here the hostile camp has the better of the contemporary evidence, and the honest verdict is that the production's verse speaking was its weakest element, uneven in a young company and deliberately subordinated in the director's scale of priorities. The third question is interpretive: did the staging's distribution of emphasis amount to a reading of the play, or only to a young man's appetite for spectacle? This is the question on which the production's reputation turns, and on this question the favourable camp, completed by Levenson's retrospective analysis, wins outright. A spectacle appetite does not produce a consistent visual grammar across orchard, chamber, and tomb. It does not cut precisely the scene that contradicts its thesis. It does not align its casting, its design, its crowd choreography, and its textual practice on a single counterpoint. Brook's staging did all four, and the hostile notices, by describing the alignment accurately while deploring it, supply the strongest evidence that the alignment existed.

One further consideration belongs to the adjudication, the age of the judges. The daily press critics of 1947 had formed their expectations before the war, in a theatre where pictorial Shakespeare meant painted scenery in the tradition of the nineteenth century actor managers, and where a director's first obligation was understood as service to the verse. Brook's staging proposed a different obligation, service to the play's structure as the director read it, with the verse as one instrument among several. The notices' vocabulary betrays the transition. Pictorial splendour was the hostile camp's term for design that argued, since design that merely decorated had been familiar for a century and drew no such fire. Too much production and too little play assumes a ratio between production and play that the staging explicitly rejected, holding that the production, rightly conceived, is the play's meaning made visible. The division of April 1947 was therefore not a quarrel about one evening's competence. It was an early skirmish in the long postwar renegotiation of directorial authority in the British Shakespeare theatre, and Brook's side of that renegotiation is the side that history took.

## Brook Against Gielgud: Paraphrase After Recovery

The production's meaning sharpens against its great predecessor. Twelve years earlier, at the New Theatre in London, John Gielgud had staged the tragedy as an act of recovery, restoring passages that centuries of acting editions had cut, honouring the full complement of the text's comedy and bawdy, and sharing the roles of Romeo and Mercutio with Laurence Olivier in the famous alternation. The full story of that enterprise belongs to the account of [Gielgud and Olivier alternating as Romeo and Mercutio in the 1935 New Theatre production](/2027/10/15/gielgud-olivier-alternate-romeo-1935/), and its relevance here is the contrast of directorial faiths. Gielgud's production trusted the text to produce its own effects when given entire, and trusted verse speaking of the highest finish to carry those effects. Brook's production trusted the staging to produce a reading, and treated the text as material to be paraphrased in the staging's idiom. Levenson's book is built on the contrast: Gielgud's is the one production in her sequence that did not subtract a great deal, and Brook's follows it in her chapters as the return of subtraction at a higher level of self awareness.

Neither faith is simply refuted by the other, and the honest comparison must give each its due. Gielgud's recovery permanently raised the floor of textual expectation; after 1935, a British production that cut the tragedy casually, for convenience or for a star's comfort, stood exposed. Brook accepted that floor and cut anyway, for argument, which is why his cuts scandalised where an older manager's would merely have been noted. The scandal is the measure of Gielgud's achievement. At the same time, the recovery model left a question unanswered that the postwar theatre could not avoid: what a production is for, beyond fidelity. A complete text beautifully spoken can still decline to say what the play means, and audiences leaving the 1935 staging carried away performances rather than an interpretation. Brook's staging, whatever its verse cost, sent its audience home with an interpretation that could be disputed, and the dispute in the notices is the receipt. The two productions between them define the poles of the modern British staging of the tragedy, textual plenitude against interpretive paraphrase, and every serious production since has negotiated a position between them.

## The Zeffirelli Succession: One Comparison

Thirteen years after Stratford, Franco Zeffirelli's Old Vic staging would take Brook's two most publicised wagers, youth and milieu, and carry them further on a wave of Italianate naturalism, with a young company and a Verona of street level bustle; the full account belongs to [Zeffirelli's Old Vic production of 1960](/2027/11/12/zeffirelli-old-vic-1960/), and one comparative passage is all the present article claims. The comparison is this. Brook's youth was a casting policy inside a design argument, the young lovers exposed within an adult city's machinery. Zeffirelli's youth became the production's whole element, a Verona inhabited by the young at a pitch of physical life that made the older generation marginal. Where Brook's crowds pressed on the lovers from outside the argument's centre, Zeffirelli's crowds were the centre, and the lovers swam in their element rather than raiding it. The succession matters because it shows Brook's wagers becoming conventions: after 1947, and decisively after 1960, no major staging could return the lovers to middle aged tragedians or the city to painted backdrops without arguing the regression. Brook opened that road at Stratford without travelling its whole length, and the Old Vic production is best read as his inheritor rather than his rival.

## Pace, Cutting, and the Two Hours' Traffic

The Chorus promises "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, line 12), and the promise has served directors ever since as a licence and a taunt: a licence to cut toward speed, a taunt from a text that runs well past two hours entire. Brook's paraphrase took the licence seriously. A staging built on crowd pressure and raided privacy needs momentum the way a siege needs supply, since any slackening lets the audience settle into the private scenes as refuges and breaks the design's grammar. The cutting therefore served the tempo, and the tempo served the thesis. The lovers' story, in this Verona, had to feel hurried by the city around it, pursued from square to feast to street to vault by public business that never paused. The wider question of how running time governs the tragedy's staging, from the Chorus's promise to modern uncut marathons, is treated in the companion account of ['Two Hours' Traffic': running time on stage](/2028/02/04/two-hours-traffic-running-time/), and the Stratford production stands in that history as the postwar era's most argued attempt to buy speed with text.

The purchase deserves a cooler assessment than either camp gave it in 1947. Speed in this play is not a director's invention. The text compresses Arthur Brooke's nine months into four days, opens with a brawl already in progress, and lets the Friar state the tragedy's mechanics in a proverb, "Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast" (2.3.94). A production that dawdles contradicts its material. Yet speed bought by cutting has a compounding cost that Brook's paraphrase paid in a specific currency: the connective passages, the Prince's second intervention, the servants' and citizens' small change, the slower movements of the Nurse and the Friar, whose removal thins the very public world the staging exists to display. A paraphrase that cuts the city's minor voices to enlarge its crowds trades texture for scale. The trade was visible in 1947 and remains the standing technical objection to Brook's method, distinct from the moral objection to the missing reconciliation. Directors who learned from Stratford, Zeffirelli among them, kept the speed and restored some texture, which is the usual way of artistic succession: the pioneer's exchange rate improves in other hands.

The rhythmic point can be followed into the play's scene structure, where Brook's paraphrase found its warrant ready made. Shakespeare's tragedy is built from more than twenty scenes in five acts, alternating square and chamber, street and cell, feast and vault, at a tempo that forbids scenic ceremony. The Elizabethan stage, bare and continuous, played the alternation without pause, and the text's joints assume that fluidity: scenes end on exits that the next scene's entrance answers, and the rhyming couplet that closes one location is still sounding as the next location assembles. Nineteenth and early twentieth century pictorial staging had broken that fluidity with built scenery and transformation intervals, purchasing local beauty at the price of the alternation's meaning. A postwar director intent on the public and private counterpoint had therefore to solve a mechanical problem before an interpretive one, how to keep the stage moving between its worlds without dead air in which the counterpoint cooled. Brook's answer, on the evidence of the notices' emphasis on orchestration and pace, was choreographic: crowd movement that carried transitions inside the action, a populated stage whose reorganisations were themselves events. The paraphrase and the choreography were one policy, the text trimmed and the stage kept full, so that Verona never emptied long enough for the lovers to seem safe in it.

## The Production as Evidence: Promptbooks, Programmes, and Performance Criticism

The 1947 staging occupies a peculiar place in the tragedy's documentation. No film records it, the promptbook tradition preserves its cuts and staging notes as profiles among Levenson's assembled evidence, and its fullest surviving witnesses are paper: the programme, with its credits for Brook, Gérard, and Gerhard and its cast list of the young company; the notices of 7 April 1947; the season survey in Theatre World; and the production photographs that the Stratford archive holds. Performance criticism, the discipline that reads such paper as the residue of an event, takes this production as one of its founding cases, and the wider account of that discipline's methods belongs to the study of [performance criticism and the living text](/2027/05/07/performance-criticism-living-text/). The case is instructive precisely because the paper disagrees. A programme cannot record that the balcony was gaunt. A notice cannot record that the gauntness was intended. Intention must be reconstructed from the pattern of the whole, exactly as Levenson reconstructs it, and the reconstruction remains open to challenge, which is the discipline's honesty and its limit.

The practical moral for students of the production is archival. Work on a staging of this period proceeds by assembling scattered paper, programmes, notices, promptbook profiles, photographs, and memoirs, into a single ordered dossier before any claim is weighed. A private research vault such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) suits that work well, since the dossier's value lies in keeping unlike documents, a Kent programme record, a Guardian notice, a historian's chapter, in one retrievable order rather than in a pile of reproductions. Levenson's own practice is the model: her Oxford edition of the play grew from the assembly of some one hundred and seventy promptbooks into profiles, and her performance history grew from the same archive. The 1947 production's afterlife as evidence, canonised in her list of the five stagings that held the stage or remained influential across the tragedy's modern history, Garrick, Cushman, Gielgud, Brook, and Zeffirelli, rests on that archival labour. Canonisation by an editor is not a small honour. It means the production has passed from memory into the record by which the play itself is edited and taught.

### What did Brook mean by calling a production correct only at its moment?

Brook's later maxim holds that a production is only correct at the moment of its correctness, and only good at the moment of its success. The Stratford staging illustrates the doctrine before the doctrine existed: its choices answered the festival, the company, and the country of 1947, and claim no authority beyond that answering.

## The London Transfer: The Argument Leaves the Festival

The transfer to Her Majesty's Theatre in London later in 1947 changed the production's evidentiary status, and the change deserves a section because transfers are where festival experiments meet their second judgement. Scofield's stage biography records the sequence plainly in the credit for his Mercutio: the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre, then Her Majesty's Theatre, in the same year. A London transfer in the commercial theatre of 1947 was a business decision before it was an artistic one. West End managements did not import Stratford novelties out of cultural duty; they imported productions whose notoriety promised an audience. The division of the notices, hostile in the quality dailies and warm in the theatrical press, was in commercial terms an asset, since controversy of exactly that shape, respectable scandal about a classic, sells seats to playgoers who would not travel to Warwickshire for a festival experiment. The transfer therefore confirms what the favourable camp claimed and the hostile camp denied, that the production had an audience building power independent of critical blessing.

The transfer also fixed the production's personnel in the record at the moment of their greatest collective youth. Within a few seasons the company scattered along the trajectories that made the postwar British stage: Scofield toward the Hamlet and Lear that would define him, in Brook's own later productions among them; Sinden toward a long eminence in classical and modern repertory; Slater toward the screen career her Wilcox contract had promised, in which period drama and Shakespearean television work recur; Payne toward a leading man's career on stage and screen. Reading the 1947 programme with those trajectories in view produces a document richer than any single performance could have been, a company photograph taken at the instant before dispersal. Jackson's young company policy is vindicated by the photograph alone. Whatever the notices thought of the tragedy, the regime had assembled, in one season, a disproportionate share of the next generation's classical talent, and had given that talent, in Brook, a director of its own generation to answer to. The production's deepest institutional legacy may be that pairing, repeated across the postwar decades until it became the British theatre's normal method: young companies under young directors, judged by results rather than by seniority.

## What the Production Signalled: Postwar Shakespeare and a Career

The signals sent by the Stratford staging ran in two directions. Toward the British theatre, it signalled that the postwar Shakespeare would be a director's Shakespeare, in which a staging advanced a reading and accepted the casualties of the advance, and that the Memorial Theatre's young company policy could produce such work at the highest level of visibility. The signal was received. Within fifteen years the Royal Shakespeare Company had been founded on ensemble and directorial principles that Jackson's regime anticipated, Brook himself had become one of its co directors, and the argument of April 1947 had become the profession's common practice, so thoroughly that the hostile notices read as documents of a superseded settlement. Toward Brook's own career, the production signalled the pattern of a lifetime: a classic text treated as material for a present argument, collaborators of independent standing, crowds and emptiness held in one design grammar, and a willingness to cut a revered ending when the argument required blood. The Empty Space of 1968 and the Paris experiments that followed are context here, at passage length as the subject demands, but their line of descent from the Stratford wager is direct, and Brook's memoir writing returns to the Stratford years as the place where the pattern set.

### How much of the later Brook is visible in the Stratford staging?

The pattern is visible entire, in embryo. A classic paraphrased toward a present argument, a designer and a composer engaged as independent collaborators, public scale set against private exposure, and an ending cut for the argument's sake: the Stratford production contains the method of the mature Brook before the manifestos named it.

A final signal deserves recording, because it concerns the play rather than the profession. The Stratford staging demonstrated that Romeo and Juliet could bear the weight of a civic reading, that its feud, its Prince, its watch, and its crowds were not scaffolding around a love story but a subject in themselves, equal in claim to the lovers. Literary criticism would reach the same position by other roads in the decades that followed, in readings of the play's public world, its codes of violence, and its failed governance. The theatre arrived first, in April 1947, in a provincial festival town, under a director of twenty two. That priority is the production's strongest claim on the attention of anyone who studies how interpretations of this play are made.

## Brook's Retrospect: The Deadly Theatre and the Stratford Lesson

Brook's own later writing supplies the framework in which the Stratford production is most profitably judged, and the framework's central term is deadliness. In The Empty Space, published in 1968, he divided the theatre of his experience into categories, of which the Deadly Theatre, the theatre of habit, routine competence, and inherited gesture, became the most quoted. A deadly production of a classic, in his account, is not a bad one. It is a correct one, assembled from the approved moves, applauded by an audience that has agreed in advance what approval means. The Stratford staging of 1947 reads, against that later account, as a young director's refusal of deadliness before the word existed: the reverent pictorial Shakespeare of the festival tradition was precisely a deadly article, correct in every detail and inert in sum, and Brook's crowds, cuts, and refused reconciliation were instruments for breaking the agreement between stage and audience about what the evening was supposed to feel like. The hostility of the notices, on this reading, was a symptom the theory predicts. A deadly consensus, disturbed, complains of recklessness and sacrificed poetry, because recklessness and sacrifice are what the breaking of habit looks like from inside the habit.

The retrospect must be handled with one caution. Brook's memoir writing, in Threads of Time and in the essays gathered in The Shifting Point, treats his early career with the pattern making of an older artist arranging a life into a method, and the tidy descent from Stratford 1947 to the Paris centre and the white box Dream is partly a construction after the fact. The documentary record of 1947 shows a messier reality: a young director inside another man's institution, spending a governor's money, dependent on a young company's uneven skills, and reviewed by a press whose categories he had not yet learned to address. Both accounts are needed. The memoirs explain what the production meant to its maker across a lifetime. The notices explain what it did on its first nights. A reference article serves its readers by keeping the two explanations in separate columns, which is the practice followed throughout the present account, Brook's later formulations admitted as his retrospective testimony and never as evidence for the events of 1947 themselves.

One element of the retrospect, however, does independent work, the maxim that a production is only correct at the moment of its correctness. The sentence is usually quoted as a licence for directorial freedom, and it is that, but its edge cuts the other way as well. A production correct only at its moment cannot be revived as a monument, cannot be taught as a model to be copied, and cannot excuse its faults by its intentions. The Stratford staging passes its maker's own test with unusual completeness. Its moment, a young company in a festival town in a country emerging from war, a press divided between settlements, a play encrusted with consoling habit, was answered point for point by its choices. Removed from that moment, its specific devices, the crowds, the gauntness, the cut handshake, became other directors' conventions and then other generations' habits, deadly in their turn. The production's significance is therefore not a style to be inherited but a standard of answering to be met, which is a harder and more useful bequest.

## Legend and Record: Three Corrections

Brook's later fame has deposited a layer of legend over the Stratford production, and a reference article owes its readers the corrections in one place. The first correction concerns emptiness. Retrospective summaries, including low grade study materials, describe the 1947 staging as an early use of the empty space, the bare stage doctrine Brook would name in 1968. The notices of April 1947 disprove the description in the production's public half: a stage crowded with supernumeraries, high colour, and pictorial splendour is not an empty space, and the Guardian's gaunt private scenes were local effects inside a populated evening. The doctrine of 1968 grew from many sources, including Brook's wartime reading and his later work in small rooms. Projecting it back onto Stratford flatters the legend and falsifies the production, whose actual design intelligence lay in contrast, not in bareness.

The second correction concerns age. Brook is often described as the twenty one year old who revolutionised Stratford, and the figure is right for the invitation and the first Stratford season of 1946, when he was twenty one and newly arrived from Birmingham. By the opening of Romeo and Juliet in April 1947 he had turned twenty two, his birthday falling in March only weeks before the first notices. The difference of a year matters less as biography than as discipline: the production's daring needs no discount for prodigy, and inflating the prodigy cheapens the deliberateness of choices that were argued, rehearsed, and defended. He was a young director, not a boy wonder, and the production reads better, and more creditably, with the year restored.

The third correction concerns credit. The designer's name, Rolf Gérard, and the composer's, Roberto Gerhard, collide in print often enough that later summaries have merged them into a single phantom contributor or assigned the designs to the composer. The programme record is unambiguous: Gérard, the designer of scenery and costumes, a German born artist formed in the Berlin and Paris theatres; Gerhard, the composer of the incidental music, a Catalan modernist formed in Schoenberg's circle and settled in Cambridge. Keeping the credits distinct is not pedantry. The production's modernity ran through both channels, a Continental design intelligence and a modernist musical one, and merging the two names erases the evidence that Brook, at the start of his career, was already recruiting across the European arts rather than within the festival's house staff alone.

## What the Production Teaches: Lessons for Directors and Students

The production's study value divides into three lessons, each recoverable from the record without borrowing the legend's varnish. The first lesson is that design must argue. Brook and Gérard did not ask what Verona looked like. They asked what the tragedy claims about public and private life, and allocated colour, population, and bareness as the claim's evidence. A student who grasps the difference between those two questions holds the production's whole method, and can test any modern staging by it: whether the setting illustrates the play's setting or argues the play's structure. The Stratford evening passes its own test at every point the notices record, which is why hostile and favourable critics, describing the same distribution of warmth and gauntness, jointly certify the method even while disputing its price.

The second lesson concerns the ethics of cutting. Brook's paraphrase demonstrates that a cut is an interpretation with a body count, and that the only respectable defence of a cut is the reading it serves. The removal of the reconciliation survives scrutiny because the reading it serves, the feud as a civic system that has earned no dividend from its children's deaths, is coherent, textually grounded in the Chorus's own bleaker contract, and consistently executed across design, casting, and choreography. Cuts made for convenience, for a star's comfort, or for fear of an audience's patience fail the same scrutiny, and the record of the tragedy's staging is littered with them. A director studying Stratford learns to interrogate every proposed cut with the question the production answers so cleanly: what does the evening claim, such that this passage must go?

The third lesson is institutional, and it is the one the notices teach against their writers' intentions. Jackson's regime shows that a theatre's renewal is a personnel policy before it is an aesthetic: hire young, mix generations, spend on the visible world of the stage, and accept divided notices as the cost of work that matters. The hostile reviews of April 1947, read as a set, are the sound of an institution succeeding at its stated aim and being charged for the success. Students of theatre history who learn to read reviews that way, as evidence about the settlement a production disturbed rather than as verdicts from a neutral bench, hold the key to the whole archive of performance criticism, and the Stratford production, whose notices disagree so cleanly about everything except the facts, is the ideal first case on which to practise the reading.

## Verdict: The Production's Significance

One measure of the production remains to be entered, the simplest and the hardest to fake: it is still argued about. Garrick's staging survives as a textual fact, Cushman's as a biographical one, Gielgud's as an actors' legend of alternating genius. Brook's survives as an open question, whether the wager it placed was the tragedy's liberation or its betrayal, and open questions are the form in which stage history stays alive. Each generation of directors that takes up the play inherits the question in a practical form, since every staging must still decide how much of Verona to show, how young to cast, how fast to play, and whether the fathers may shake hands. The Stratford production did not settle those decisions for its successors. It made them unavoidable, and it priced them.

Weighed whole, the 1947 Stratford Romeo and Juliet stands as the hinge between the recovered text of the nineteen thirties and the reinvented youth of the nineteen sixties, and as the first fully argued directorial reading of the tragedy in the postwar British theatre. Its verse was uneven and its cutting sometimes crude, and the notices that said so were right on both counts. Its design argument was exact, its casting prophetic, its refusal of the reconciliation the boldest textual act of its decade, and its central perception, that the tragedy stages a public world crushing a private one, has passed into the common understanding of the play. A production is not required to be flawless to be decisive. It is required to change what can be thought and done with its play afterward. By that measure, the twenty two year old's Stratford wager won, and the division of April 1947 was the sound of it winning.

### Q: Who directed the 1947 Stratford Romeo and Juliet, and how old was the director at its opening?

Peter Brook directed the production, and he was twenty two years old when it opened in April 1947. Brook had been born in March 1925, so his twenty second birthday fell only weeks before the first notices of 7 April 1947. The age matters because later legend often makes him twenty one, the age at which Barry Jackson had invited him to Stratford and at which he had directed the previous season's Love's Labour's Lost. Restoring the correct year does not diminish the appointment's daring. A festival tragedy entrusted to a director of twenty two, in a season at the centre of a famous theatre's renewal, remains an act of institutional trust with few parallels in the postwar British theatre.

### Q: At which theatre and in which festival season was the production staged?

The production was staged at the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre in Stratford-upon-Avon, during the 1947 festival season that ran from April to September. The Memorial Theatre was then directed by Sir Barry Jackson, the founder of the Birmingham Repertory Theatre, who had taken charge in 1945 under governors chaired by Fordham Flower. Brook's tragedy stood in a repertory that also included Marlowe's Doctor Faustus, Measure for Measure, Twelfth Night, The Merchant of Venice, and Pericles, together with Brook's own revival of Love's Labour's Lost. The season's character, young companies under varied directors with generous spending on scenery and costume, framed the production from its first night.

### Q: Who played Romeo, Juliet, and Mercutio in the Stratford cast?

Laurence Payne played Romeo, Daphne Slater played Juliet, and Paul Scofield played Mercutio. Payne was a leading man in his late twenties who also played Sebastian in the same season's Twelfth Night. Slater had turned nineteen weeks before the opening, making her one of the youngest Juliets in the festival's history and a sharp contrast with the mature performers who had dominated the role for generations. Scofield, at twenty five, was already the most discussed actor of Barry Jackson's young company, and his Mercutio placed the tragedy's hinge role, the figure at home in both the street and the dream world, in the hands of the company's magnetic centre.

### Q: How old was Daphne Slater when she played Juliet, and what distinction had she won at drama school?

Daphne Slater was nineteen when she played Juliet, having been born in March 1928 and turned nineteen shortly before the April opening. She had won the gold medal of the Royal Academy of Dramatic Art, the academy's highest distinction for a graduating performer, and had been given a film contract and a leading screen role by the director Herbert Wilcox in the same period. The combination defined her casting's interest: a performer of certified technique and immediate public promise, young enough to make Juliet's age legible on stage. Theatre World's season survey found that she looked the part and that the ardour the tragedy requires was present in her playing.

### Q: Who designed the scenery and costumes, and who composed the incidental music?

Rolf Gérard designed the scenery and costumes, and Roberto Gerhard composed the incidental music. Gérard, a German born designer formed in the Berlin and Paris theatres, supplied the production's celebrated visual division: high colour, populated movement, and careful orchestration in the public scenes, against gaunt and unsoftened settings for the lovers' private scenes. Gerhard, a Catalan composer and pupil of Schoenberg who had settled in England, supplied a modernist musical frame unusual in the festival theatre of the period. The two credits, preserved distinctly in the programme record held in the University of Kent's programme collection, mark the production as an early instance of Brook recruiting collaborators of independent European standing.

### Q: Why are Rolf Gérard and Roberto Gerhard so often confused in accounts of the production?

The two men are confused because their surnames differ by a few letters and because both worked on the same 1947 production in different departments. Rolf Gérard was the designer of scenery and costumes; Roberto Gerhard was the composer of the incidental music. A reader who merges them invents a single phantom contributor and erases the production's most telling structural fact, that its modernity ran through two channels at once, a Continental design intelligence shaping what the audience saw and a modernist musical intelligence shaping what it heard. The programme record kept in the University of Kent's Ronald James collection credits them separately, and careful accounts of the production follow the programme.

### Q: What did The Times critic object to in the notice of 7 April 1947?

The Times critic objected that the staging was a recklessly spectacular version of the tragedy that sacrificed poetry and narrative to pictorial splendour. The notice added the sharper charge that the director appeared to lose interest in humanity whenever humanity was not being treated in the mass, a complaint aimed at the production's weighting of crowds over principals. The objection deserves its place in the record because it was partly just: the young company's verse speaking was the evening's weakest element, and Brook had deliberately subordinated it. Jill Levenson's later analysis answers the notice at the level of design, arguing that the critic noticed the staging's central effect without understanding it.

### Q: What did the Manchester Guardian notice praise, and what did it find gaunt?

The Manchester Guardian's notice of 7 April 1947 praised the warmth and colour of the production's brawling and crowd scenes, and found the balcony scene and Juliet's bed chamber hardly less gaunt and unsoftened than the Capulet tomb. The notice is the most valuable single document of the production's design, because its divided verdict records the staging's central device in the act of misunderstanding it. Brook and Gérard had distributed the visual register deliberately, splendour for the public world and exposure for the private one, so that orchard, chamber, and tomb shared one grammar. The Guardian's critic saw the distribution exactly and read it as imbalance, where Levenson later read it as the production's argument.

### Q: How did Harold Matthews describe the production in Theatre World?

Harold Matthews, surveying the Stratford season for Theatre World in June 1947, described Brook's staging as a fresh rendering, vigorous, colourful, and untrammelled by tradition. His notice led the favourable camp in the production's divided reception, and its judgements on the young company carry particular weight because the same survey assessed the whole season's work. Matthews found that Daphne Slater looked the part of Juliet and that the ardour was there in the playing, an assessment that answers the hostile notices' charge of sacrificed poetry at the point where the charge mattered most, the lovers themselves. Between the Times's recklessness and Matthews's freshness lies the complete contemporary argument about the production.

### Q: Why does Jill Levenson call her chapter on the production Paraphrase?

Jill Levenson calls her chapter on the production "Paraphrase, 1947" because Brook's performance text restated the tragedy in the staging's own idiom rather than merely shortening Shakespeare's words. Her book Shakespeare in Performance: Romeo and Juliet argues that the decisive productions in the play's stage history, Gielgud's excepted, each subtracted a great deal, and that each subtraction amounted to a version of the play thought through in new terms. Garrick's subtraction isolated pathos, Cushman's isolated Romeo, and Brook's isolated the counterpoint of public and private worlds, expressed through crowds, bare rooms, and a refused ending. The term carries no sneer in her usage. A paraphrase, for Levenson, is an interpretation that has found a medium.

### Q: Which productions stand with Brook's in Levenson's list of influential stagings?

Levenson's Oxford promptbook research and her performance history place Brook's 1947 staging among five productions that held the stage or remained highly influential across the tragedy's modern record. The five are David Garrick's production of 1748, Charlotte Cushman's of 1845 and 1846, John Gielgud's of 1935, Peter Brook's of 1947, and Franco Zeffirelli's Old Vic production of 1960 with its film of 1968. Inclusion in that sequence is the production's scholarly canonisation. It means that an editor preparing the play for Oxford, working from some one hundred and seventy assembled promptbooks, judged the Stratford evening one of the handful of stagings through which the tragedy's modern identity was made.

### Q: What company policy of Sir Barry Jackson made the appointment of so young a director possible?

Barry Jackson's policy of building a young company under a varied roster of producers made the appointment possible. Taking charge at Stratford in 1945, Jackson swept aside the festival's settled habits, engaged a company of young performers who had not worked at Stratford before, among them Paul Scofield and Donald Sinden, and employed producers of different generations and methods side by side, the old hand and the new, the actor and the academic. Brook, who had directed for Jackson at Birmingham and had succeeded with Love's Labour's Lost in the 1946 Stratford season, was the policy's logical product. The tragedy was not an exception to the system. It was the system applied at full strength.

### Q: Which Stratford production had Brook directed in 1946, and what had it established?

Brook had directed Love's Labour's Lost at Stratford in 1946, with Paul Scofield as Don Armado, and it had established him as a director of visual intelligence whose design ideas and textual readings coincided. The comedy, a play of artificial courtesies that defeats most producers, was staged with a delicacy and pictorial wit that made it the surprise success of Barry Jackson's first Stratford season. The establishment mattered in two directions. It earned Brook the tragedy of 1947, an act of trust that an untested director would not have received, and it formed the expectation his tragedy then refused, since audiences prepared for another exercise in elegance received crowds, pace, and a refused reconciliation instead.

### Q: What happened to the production after the Stratford season ended?

The production transferred to Her Majesty's Theatre in London later in 1947, carrying the festival experiment into the commercial West End. The transfer is recorded in Paul Scofield's stage biography in the sequence of credits for his Mercutio: the Shakespeare Memorial Theatre, then Her Majesty's Theatre, within the same year. A London transfer of that period was a business judgement as much as an artistic one, since West End managements imported Stratford productions whose notoriety promised an audience. The production's divided notices, hostile in the quality dailies and warm in the theatrical press, were in that market an asset. The transfer stands as evidence that the staging's argument travelled beyond the festival frame that had produced it.

### Q: Which other roles did Paul Scofield play at Stratford across the 1946 and 1947 seasons?

Across the 1946 and 1947 Stratford seasons Scofield played the title role in Henry V, Don Armado in Love's Labour's Lost, Malcolm in Macbeth, Lucio in Measure for Measure, Sir Andrew Aguecheek in Twelfth Night, and Mercutio in Brook's Romeo and Juliet. The range explains his standing in Barry Jackson's young company: a performer trusted with a king, a fantastic don, a revenger in waiting, a fantastical rake, a comic knight, and the tragedy's hinge within two seasons. His Mercutio gains context from the company around it. Brook was directing an actor he had already led in King John at Birmingham and in the previous season's comedy, and the collaboration's ease shows in the role's centrality to the staging's argument.

### Q: How does Brook's textual practice differ from the practice of Gielgud's 1935 production?

Brook's textual practice differs from Gielgud's as paraphrase differs from recovery. Gielgud's 1935 New Theatre production restored passages that centuries of acting editions had cut, gave Mercutio and the Nurse their full measures, and trusted the complete text, spoken at the highest finish, to produce its own effects. Brook's 1947 production cut hard, re timed the tragedy around its crowds, and removed the closing reconciliation, trusting the staging to produce a reading. Levenson's performance history is built on the contrast, treating Gielgud's as the one production in her sequence that did not subtract a great deal. The two stagings define the poles between which modern British production of the tragedy still negotiates.

### Q: What does the Chorus's phrase the two hours' traffic promise, and how did Brook's cutting answer it?

The Chorus's phrase promises that the story of the star crossed lovers will occupy the two hours' traffic of the stage, a duration Shakespeare's full text comfortably exceeds. Directors have treated the promise ever since as both licence and taunt, licence to cut toward speed and taunt from a text that resists the clock. Brook's cutting answered the promise in the service of his design: a staging built on crowd pressure and raided privacy required momentum, since any slackening would let the private scenes settle into refuges and break the production's grammar. The lovers' story had to feel hurried by the city around it, pursued from square to feast to vault by public business that never paused.
