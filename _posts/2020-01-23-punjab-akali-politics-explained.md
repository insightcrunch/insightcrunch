Punjab politics, organised around the Akali Dal for a century, is usually narrated as a passage through fire: a decade and a half of militancy between 1980 and 1995, sealed by the army assault on the Golden Temple complex in June 1984 and by the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi that October, after which the state's politics supposedly restarted on ordinary lines. That narrative is not false, and the evidence for its gravity is set out in full below. It is, however, a narrative that mistakes the loudest fifteen years of a hundred-year structure for the structure itself. The claim defended in this article is different. Punjab's politics operates through a religious-linguistic Akali architecture, built between 1920 and 1973 and still load-bearing after 2022: Sikh religious-political mobilisation through the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee and the Shiromani Akali Dal, a Punjabi-language commitment consolidated between the 1940s and 1966, a federal positioning towards the centre codified in the Anandpur Sahib Resolution of 1973, and, after 1992, a governance coalition with the Bharatiya Janata Party that lasted until September 2020. The militancy years tested that architecture and scarred it. The Aam Aadmi Party victory of March 2022, with 92 of 117 assembly seats, tests it again. Neither episode displaced it.

The militancy-era framing deserves its evidentiary due before it is set aside as an organising principle. Between June 1984 and August 1995, Punjab recorded an army operation inside its holiest shrine, the assassination of a serving Prime Minister by her Sikh bodyguards on 31 October 1984, organised killing of Sikhs in Delhi and other cities in November 1984 for which the Nanavati Commission later recorded an official Delhi toll of 2,733 dead, a peace accord signed on 24 July 1985 and abandoned in practice within eighteen months, an insurgency whose peak years from 1989 to 1992 killed, on the most cited official compilations, more than a thousand people a year, and the assassination of a serving Chief Minister, Beant Singh, by a human bomb on 31 August 1995. Any account that treats these events as surface disturbance would be unserious. The argument here is narrower and more exact: the events explain the trauma of a period, but they do not explain the pattern of the century, because the institutions that carried Punjab's politics into the crisis were the same institutions that carried it out, and because the questions that structured the crisis, Chandigarh, river waters, state autonomy and the political custody of Sikh religious institutions, were all posed by the architecture decades before the first shot of the insurgency was fired.

A second standard treatment, the party-narrative framing, organises Punjab's politics as a succession of party fortunes: Congress dominance to 1966, Akali alternation thereafter, a Congress restoration in 1992, the Akali and Bharatiya Janata Party order from 1997, and the Aam Aadmi Party irruption of 2022. This framing has the virtue of matching the election record, and the sections below use that record throughout. Its weakness is that it cannot explain persistence. It cannot explain why the Shiromani Akali Dal, reduced to 3 of 117 seats in 2022, still commands the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee electorate and remains the custodian of a programme, the federal programme of 1973, that no rival party has adopted or replaced. It cannot explain why the Aam Aadmi Party, a party with no Sikh religious inheritance at all, governed after 2022 by contesting the same ground the architecture marks out: the custody of Sikh institutions, the Punjabi language's official standing, the state's claims against the centre on water and finance, and the political inheritance of the farm protests of 2020 and 2021. Party fortunes rotate. The architecture decides what the rotation is about.

The architecture reading also disciplines the comparative question. Within [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/) that organises the state settlements examined across this series, Punjab is the case where the identity dimension is not a sentiment but a set of incorporated bodies: a statutory religious corporation elected by a confessional electorate, a party that grew out of that corporation's volunteer corps, a language inscribed as a state's official medium after a quarter-century of agitation, and a constitutional demand, state autonomy, written into a party resolution that outlived the insurgency fought partly in its name. Other states have linguistic movements, religious parties and federal grievances. Punjab alone fused all three into institutions founded in a single five-year span, from 1920 to 1925, and then ran its politics through them for a century.

## The Architecture in Outline: Four Components and a Century

The four components can be stated compactly before each is examined in its own section. The first component is the religious-political coupling built in 1920: the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, constituted at Amritsar on 15 November 1920 to take control of Sikh shrines from hereditary custodians, and the Shiromani Akali Dal, founded on 14 December 1920 as the political volunteer body of the same movement, with the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 then giving the Committee statutory form as an elected corporation administering the historic gurdwaras of Punjab. The second component is the linguistic commitment: from the language controversies of the 1940s, through the Punjabi Suba movement led first by Master Tara Singh and then by Sant Fateh Singh, to the Punjab Reorganisation Act of 1966, which on 1 November 1966 finally made Punjab a Punjabi-speaking state, at the price of a shared capital at Chandigarh and unresolved claims over territory and river waters. The third component is the federal programme: the Anandpur Sahib Resolution, adopted by the Akali Dal working committee at Anandpur Sahib on 16 and 17 October 1973 and endorsed in twelve resolutions at Ludhiana on 28 and 29 October 1978, which demanded that the centre's jurisdiction be confined to defence, foreign affairs, currency and communications, and that Chandigarh, Punjabi-speaking areas and the control of river headworks pass to Punjab. The fourth component is the coalition order of the post-militancy decades: the alliance between the Akali Dal and the Bharatiya Janata Party, sealed for the assembly election of February 1997 and renewed at every election until the farm laws of September 2020 broke it, which paired the principal Sikh party with the principal Hindu party of the state in a single governing combination.

The four components are set out as a citable map in the table below, which this article calls the four-component religious-linguistic Akali architecture map (Table 1). Each row names a component, the institution that carries it, the settlement that founded it, and the form its operation took across the four periods the article distinguishes: the pre-militancy decades from 1947 to 1980, the militancy years from 1980 to 1995, the coalition decades from 1995 to 2020, and the test years after 2020.

The table makes the article's central distinction visible. An electoral reversal strikes at the fourth component and leaves the other three untouched; that is what happened in 2017, when the Akali Dal fell to 15 seats and the coalition lost office, and again in 2022, when the Aam Aadmi Party won the state. A displacement of the architecture would require the first three components to lose their carriers: the Committee to cease functioning as a political prize, the language settlement of 1966 to cease structuring the state's claims, and the federal programme of 1973 to cease being the standing text against which every Punjab government's relations with Delhi are argued. No election since 1920 has done any of those three things. The insurgency of 1989 to 1992 came closer than any election, and even the insurgency was fought by factions claiming to defend the architecture's own programme against a party they accused of betraying it.

Two features of the architecture explain its durability, and both are institutional rather than emotional. The first is incorporation. The Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee is a body corporate created by statute, with an electorate of adult Sikhs who register on a confessional roll, an annual budget drawn on gurdwara offerings, and jurisdiction over the shrines that anchor Sikh collective life, from the Golden Temple at Amritsar to the historic gurdwaras of the state. A political movement whose religious wing is a statutory corporation with its own elections does not dissolve when its party loses an assembly election; it retreats into the corporation and contests the corporation's next election. The second feature is textual fixity. The Anandpur Sahib Resolution gave the federal component a document, short enough to be reproduced, precise enough to be argued clause by clause, and ambiguous enough, on the question of Sikh political sovereignty, to sustain two opposed readings for fifty years. Political programmes that live in documents can be shelved and revived; the Akali Dal shelved the Resolution between 1997 and 2020, governing in coalition with the party of the centre, and unshelved it in substance, if not in name, each time the coalition strained.

### How long did the Akali Dal and BJP alliance last?

The alliance sealed for the February 1997 assembly election endured for twenty-three years and seven months, breaking on 26 September 2020 when the Akali Dal quit the National Democratic Alliance over the farm laws. It covered five Punjab assembly elections, three Akali-led state governments and two spells of Akali ministers serving in Union cabinets at Delhi.

The longevity recorded in that answer is itself evidence for the architecture reading, because the alliance was never a mere seat-sharing device. The Bharatiya Janata Party's Punjab unit drew its strength from the state's urban Hindu trading electorate, concentrated in cities such as Ludhiana, Amritsar and Jalandhar, and the Akali Dal drew its strength from the rural Sikh peasantry of the Malwa, Majha and Doaba regions. The combination therefore governed as a treaty between the two communal electorates of a state whose religious demography, roughly 58 percent Sikh and 39 percent Hindu in the 2011 census, made single-community rule permanently contested. When the treaty broke in 2020, it broke on an architectural question, the farm laws' perceived transfer of agricultural regulation from the state to the centre, and not on a dispute about seats. The sections that follow take the components in order, and then return to the adjudication the brief of this subject demands: whether a century of evidence supports the architecture reading over the two framings it displaces.

## Component One: The Gurdwara Settlement of 1920 to 1925

The architecture's first component was built in five years of confrontation over a deceptively narrow question: who controlled the Sikh shrines? At the end of the First World War, the historic gurdwaras of Punjab were administered by mahants, hereditary custodians recognised by the British administration, many of whom treated shrine lands and offerings as personal property and some of whom had introduced practices the reforming Singh Sabha movement regarded as corruptions of the faith. The Singh Sabha movement, active since the 1870s, and its political offshoot the Chief Khalsa Diwan, founded in 1902, had spent decades arguing that Sikh identity required Sikh institutions to be governed by the Sikh collectivity, the Panth, through representative bodies. Baldev Raj Nayar, in Minority Politics in the Punjab published in 1966, treated this institutional demand as the seed of everything that followed: the Akali movement, on his account, began not as a party seeking office but as a corporate claim by a religious community to govern its own sacred property, and it carried that corporate form into politics permanently.

The claim was institutionalised in a single month of 1920. On 15 November 1920, a gathering of Sikhs at Amritsar constituted the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, a committee of 175 members, to take over the management of the shrines. On 14 December 1920, the movement raised its volunteer and political arm, the Shiromani Akali Dal, the army of the immortal, at Amritsar. The pairing was deliberate and it has never been undone: the Committee would hold the shrines, the Dal would hold the streets and, later, the ballot. Khushwant Singh, in A History of the Sikhs published in 1963 and revised in 1991, stressed the novelty of the arrangement in Indian politics: a religious community had built itself a parliament before it built itself a party, and the parliament, because it controlled sacred property and its revenues, gave the party a material base no election defeat could confiscate.

What followed was the Gurdwara Reform Movement, a sequence of morchas, disciplined mass agitations, that tested the settlement against the colonial state. In February 1921, at Nankana Sahib, the birthplace of Guru Nanak, the custodian Mahant Narain Das ordered his men to fire on an Akali jatha entering the shrine; about 130 Sikhs were killed, a massacre documented in the official enquiry that followed and seared into the movement's memory. At Guru ka Bagh in 1922, Akalis courted arrest in daily batches for cutting wood on shrine land the mahant claimed as his own, and the Reverend C. F. Andrews, who witnessed the beatings of non-resisting volunteers, wrote accounts that embarrassed the government into concession. At Jaito in 1923 and 1924, the movement protested the deposition of Maharaja Ripudaman Singh of Nabha and the interruption of an unbroken reading of the Guru Granth Sahib, and a jatha of 500 volunteers, the Shahidi Jatha, marched from Amritsar in February 1924 and was fired on at Jaito with deaths the official count placed at 19 and the movement's count considerably higher. The colonial government, having imprisoned thousands, conceded the principle. The Sikh Gurdwaras Act, passed by the Punjab Legislative Council in July 1925 and brought into force on 1 November 1925, vested the management of the listed historic gurdwaras in the Committee as an elected statutory body, with a central board elected by Sikh voters and local committees for individual shrines.

The Act's consequences were constitutional in the strict sense: they created a second electoral system inside Punjab that has operated, with interruptions under direct state administration during emergencies and insurgency, ever since. Elections to the Committee became the Akali movement's internal parliament, the arena where factions of the Dal proved their claim to speak for the Panth, and the Committee's presidency became a political office second only to the chief ministership in the movement's internal order. Gurcharan Singh Tohra, president of the Committee for a cumulative span of more than two decades between 1973 and 2004, wielded from the Committee's Amritsar headquarters an authority that chief ministers of his own party had to negotiate with. The under-cited records of this component are the Committee's own organisational archives at Amritsar, whose proceedings from 1925 to 1947 document the routine, unglamorous consolidation of the religious-political linkage: electoral rolls maintained, shrine budgets audited, school and hospital endowments administered, and a bureaucracy of the Panth trained in the management of a corporation. Historians of the militancy era rarely consult them, because they record administration rather than violence, but they are the primary evidence that the linkage was a working institution two decades before independence, not a slogan improvised in 1947.

Patwant Singh, in The Sikhs published in 1999, argued that this institutional inheritance explains the distinctive texture of Sikh politics: its disputes are fought as contests over legitimate custody, of shrines, of the party, of the programme, because the community's founding political experience was a custody dispute settled by statute. The reading also explains the Akali Dal's strange durability. The Dal has split repeatedly, into factions named for Master Tara Singh and Sant Fateh Singh in the 1960s, for Longowal, Barnala, Badal and Mann in the 1980s and 1990s, and into a breakaway Akali Dal (Sanyukt) and a revived Akali Dal (Waris Punjab De) alignment in the 2020s. In a party-narrative framing, each split reads as decay. In the architecture reading, splits are the mechanism by which custody of the first component is contested, and the Committee's elections supply the verdict: the faction that wins the Committee holds the institutional centre of the Panth whatever its assembly tally, which is why a Dal reduced to 3 assembly seats in 2022 could still sweep the Committee's internal offices and why the Aam Aadmi Party, for all its assembly dominance, has never contested the Committee's ground at all.

### How are members of the SGPC chosen?

Members of the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee are elected by an electorate of adult Sikhs who enrol on a confessional register maintained under the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925. The general house numbers about 170 elected members, supplemented by co-opted members and the head priests of the five takhts, and it elects the Committee's president and executive annually.

The confessional electorate described in that answer is the architectural point. No other Indian state contains a statutory religious corporation whose voters must profess a faith, whose revenues derive from shrine offerings, and whose elections function as a plebiscite on which political faction legitimately represents that faith. Harjot Oberoi, in The Construction of Religious Boundaries published in 1994, showed how the Singh Sabha era hardened the boundary of who counted as a Sikh for purposes such as these rolls, distinguishing the Khalsa identity from the looser, plural practice of the nineteenth century. The Committee's register is that boundary rendered administrative. Whoever administers the register administers the first component, and the Akali Dal's claim on Punjab politics begins there, in an electoral roll older than the state's universal franchise, which arrived only with the Constitution of 1950.

The first component's first great test was not electoral at all: it was Partition. The Akali Dal entered the 1940s as a provincial force of consequence, contesting the Punjab elections of 1937 and 1946, joining and leaving coalition ministries, and arguing, in Master Tara Singh's Azad Punjab scheme of 1942 and 1943, for a re-drawn province in which no single community would dominate, a proposal aimed squarely at the Muslim League's Pakistan demand. When the boundary award of August 1947 divided Punjab, the Sikh population of the western districts, perhaps two million people, abandoned homes, lands and the shrines of the faith's founding geography in a migration conducted under mass violence: Nankana Sahib, the Guru's birthplace, and more than a hundred other historic gurdwaras passed to Pakistan, outside the Committee's statutory reach forever, while the refugee columns that crossed to India carried the Panth's institutional memory with them. The architecture survived its own dismemberment because its carriers were portable: the Committee reconstituted itself in the east with its registers, its funds and its claim intact, the Dal rebuilt its organisation among the resettled peasantry of the new Punjab's canal colonies and eastern districts, and the demand for a Punjabi-speaking state, taken up within three years of the migration, gave the survivors' politics its forward programme. A party-narrative framing sees 1947 as a caesura after which Punjab politics began again. The record of the Committee's unbroken sittings, elections and budgets across the caesura, preserved in the Amritsar archives, shows an institution that treated Partition as a catastrophe to be administered rather than an ending to be mourned, and the distinction is the architecture thesis in miniature.

## Component Two: The Language Commitment and the Punjabi Suba, 1947 to 1966

The architecture's second component was added by Partition. The Punjab of 1947 was a bilingual province in which Punjabi, written by Sikhs in the Gurmukhi script and by many Hindus not at all, competed with Hindi as the language of schooling and administration, and in which the census itself had become a battlefield: in the enumerations of 1951 and 1961, large numbers of Punjabi-speaking Hindus, encouraged by organisations such as the Arya Samaj and the Hindi Raksha Samiti, returned Hindi as their mother tongue, so that the demographic case for a Punjabi-speaking state appeared weaker in print than it was in speech. Paul Brass, in Language, Religion and Politics in North India published in 1974, made this census politics the canonical case of his argument that language identities in north India were not primordial facts but resources that elites activated and deactivated as strategy required. The Akali Dal's leadership understood the same point from inside the struggle: unless Punjabi could be tied to the Sikh religious identity guarded by the first component, the demand for a Punjabi state could always be dissolved by a census. The language commitment was therefore never only about language. It was about which community's language would name the state.

The first institutional attempt to settle the question, the Sachar Formula of October 1949, named for Chief Minister Bhim Sen Sachar, divided Punjab into Punjabi and Hindi zones for purposes of school instruction, with parental choice at the margins. The formula satisfied neither side: Akali opinion treated it as a dilution of the Punjabi claim, and the Hindi movement treated the zones as a first surrender. It collapsed within the decade. The States Reorganisation Commission, appointed in December 1953 under Fazl Ali, received the Punjabi Suba demand in full form, and its report of 1955 rejected it, on the ground that the demand lacked the general support of the proposed state's population, a judgment delivered in the shadow of those contested census returns. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 accordingly merged Punjab with the Patiala and East Punjab States Union and left the linguistic claim unmet: [the 1956 deferred Punjab case](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/) stands in the constitutional record as the largest single exception to the linguistic principle the Act otherwise enacted, the one major language of the Union denied a state of its own. For the Akali Dal, the deferral converted a grievance into a programme, and the programme was prosecuted for a decade through the morchas the first component made possible.

The morcha of 1955, against a Punjab government ban on the Punjabi Suba slogan, filled the jails with about 12,000 volunteers on the movement's own count and ended with the ban withdrawn and an apology tendered in the Golden Temple. The morcha of 1960 and 1961, led by Master Tara Singh, the ageing Akali patriarch who had dominated the movement since the 1920s, ended with Tara Singh's fast of 48 days in the autumn of 1961, broken on the assurance of a commission of enquiry that the movement then denounced as a fraud when the Das Commission's proceedings were boycotted and its report rejected. Leadership passed to Sant Fateh Singh, who repositioned the demand in a form that proved decisive: the Suba he demanded from 1965 was a linguistic state, he insisted, not a Sikh state, a claim he pressed through fasts begun in August 1965, suspended during the war with Pakistan that September, and renewed with a threatened self-immolation postponed in 1966 as the government's intentions clarified. The repositioning mattered analytically. By separating the language claim from the religious claim in public argument, Fateh Singh made the demand grantable by a Union government that could never concede a confessional state, and the concession came from Indira Gandhi's government within months of her taking office in January 1966.

The Punjab Reorganisation Act of 1966, effective on 1 November 1966, divided the state three ways: the Punjabi-speaking areas became the new Punjab, the Hindi-speaking areas became Haryana, the hill areas passed to Himachal Pradesh, and Chandigarh, the capital built under Le Corbusier's plan after 1950, was made a Union Territory serving as the shared capital of both successor states. The Shah Commission, appointed in April 1966 to draw the boundary on the basis of the 1961 census village by village, awarded Chandigarh to Haryana in its majority report; the Union government overrode the award on the capital and deferred the question, a deferral that proved permanent. Punjab received its linguistic state and, in the same settlement, a docket of unfinished business that the third component would soon codify: the capital withheld, Punjabi-speaking areas left in Haryana and Himachal Pradesh, the waters of the Ravi and Beas to be shared with non-riparian Rajasthan under arrangements the state contested, and the headworks of the Bhakra system standing outside the state's sole control. The 1966 settlement is thus the hinge of the whole architecture. It completed the second component and manufactured the grievances the third component exists to prosecute, which is why [the constitutional politics of linguistic reorganisation](/2019/01/19/linguistic-reorganisation-constitutional-politics/) in India cannot be written as a story of a principle applied and closed: in Punjab, the principle was applied in a form that guaranteed its own continuation as politics.

The decades after 1966 showed the language commitment operating as the settled floor of Punjab politics rather than as a live controversy, which is the fate of completed architectural components. Every Punjab government, Congress, Akali or Aam Aadmi Party, has defended Punjabi's official primacy: the Punjab Official Language Act framework inherited from 1967, the compulsory teaching of Punjabi in the state's schools, the signage orders of the Bhagwant Mann government in 2022 and 2023 requiring Punjabi on commercial signboards, and the recurring state resolutions against any dilution of Punjabi in Union examinations or recruitment. The commitment also structured the state's diaspora politics, in which the defence of Punjabi in Canadian and British public life became an extension of the home state's founding settlement. Where the component bites hardest in ordinary governance is in the cadre question: the state's administrative and teaching services are recruited in Punjabi, and the language of the secretariat file remains a standing argument about which community the state belongs to. A component that has been fully absorbed into administration is invisible to the party-narrative framing, which counts only contests. The architecture reading counts absorptions as well, and on that count the language component is the most successful political project in the state's history: it was demanded in 1948, refused in 1956, conceded in 1966 and has not been seriously contested since.

## The Architecture in Office: Alternation and Consolidation, 1966 to 1980

Between the linguistic settlement and the federal programme, the architecture served its apprenticeship in ordinary government, and the fourteen years from 1966 to 1980 are the control case for the whole argument: a period with no insurgency, no accord and no landslide, in which Punjab's politics can be watched running on its structural rails. The state's first election after reorganisation, in February 1967, produced the first non-Congress government in Punjab's history, a United Front headed by the Akali Dal's Gurnam Singh, resting on a coalition that stretched from the Jana Sangh to the Communists. The front's collapse within the year, when the Akali legislator Lachhman Singh Gill defected with his followers in November 1967 and formed a minority government sustained by Congress votes from outside, taught the state's politicians the operating rule of the new Punjab: with 104 seats after reorganisation and a fractured party system, governments would be made and unmade by factional custody of the Dal, and the Congress, though reduced, retained the power of arbitration. A mid-term election in February 1969 returned the Akali Dal in strength, Gurnam Singh resumed office, and in March 1970 Parkash Singh Badal succeeded him, at forty-two the youngest chief minister the state had had, beginning an association between the Badal name and the office that would run, in total, for more than seventeen years across five decades.

The alternation had a deeper pattern that the party-narrative framing records without explaining. Congress governments in Punjab, those of Pratap Singh Kairon from 1956 to 1964 before reorganisation and of Giani Zail Singh from 1972 to 1977 after it, governed through the state's developmental machinery and treated the Akali programme as an opposition's noise to be managed. Kairon's Punjab, the Punjab of the Bhakra Dam's completion and the agricultural university at Ludhiana founded in 1962, was the Congress at its most institutionally confident in any Indian state, and Kairon himself, assassinated in February 1965 near Rai on the road from Delhi, was a Sikh chief minister who had built his dominance by defeating the Akali Dal at its own game of factional management. Akali governments, by contrast, governed as custodians of a programme larger than their tenure: even in office, in 1967, in 1969 and again in 1977, the Dal prosecuted the unfinished docket of 1966, pressing the centre on Chandigarh and territory from the treasury benches, an inversion of the usual logic of incumbency that only makes sense if the party understood itself as one component of an architecture rather than as the state itself. The Congress sweep of March 1972, 66 of 104 seats on the strength of the Bangladesh war, and the Zail Singh government's subsequent management of Punjab through the 1970s, including its cultivation of religious constituencies in the Majha region, belong to the same pattern from the other side: the Congress in Punjab could win the state, but it governed a polity whose opposition was incorporated, statutory and permanent, and whose programme it could neither adopt nor extinguish.

Two episodes of the period deserve the record because they prefigure the militancy decade's alignments. The first is the Akali Dal's split and reunion over the Suba's fruits: Master Tara Singh, who had led the movement since the 1920s, broke with Sant Fateh Singh in 1962 over the conduct of the morcha and the acceptance of the Das Commission, and for six years two Akali Dals contested the Committee and the assembly in the names of two men, until the factions reunited in 1968 under Fateh Singh's ascendancy after Tara Singh's death in November 1967. The split is the first clear instance of the architecture's custody mechanics in the electoral era: the prize in dispute was never a policy, since both factions held the same programme, but the custody of the institutions that embodied it, the Committee above all, whose elections in those years functioned as the primary in which the Dal's general election was effectively decided. The second episode is the Nirankari clash of April 1978, already noted, in which thirteen Sikhs died at Amritsar protesting a congregation of the Sant Nirankari Mission. In the alternation decades' politics the clash registered as a law-and-order event mishandled by the Janata-Akali coalition government of the day. In the architecture's longer chronology it was a boundary dispute of the kind Oberoi's work anatomised: a contest over who defines Sikh orthodoxy, fought on the first component's ground, two years before the state had a militancy to name. The programme, the custody contests and the boundary disputes of 1966 to 1980 were the architecture in its normal working state, and the normal working state is what the militancy decade interrupted and what the decades after 1995 restored.

## Component Three: The Federal Programme and the Anandpur Sahib Resolution

The third component gave the architecture its constitutional text. By 1972 the Akali Dal had won its linguistic state and lost its political dominance inside it: in the assembly election of March 1972, fought in the afterglow of Indira Gandhi's Bangladesh victory, the Dal fell to 24 of 117 seats and the Congress under Giani Zail Singh took the government. On 11 December 1972 the party's working committee appointed a twelve-member sub-committee, including Surjit Singh Barnala and Gurcharan Singh Tohra, to restate the party's programme for the new conditions. The document the sub-committee produced was adopted unanimously by the working committee at Anandpur Sahib, the town founded by Guru Tegh Bahadur and sacred as the birthplace of the Khalsa, on 16 and 17 October 1973. It was endorsed by the party's general house in 1977 and carried, in the form of twelve resolutions, by the eighteenth All India Akali Conference at Ludhiana on 28 and 29 October 1978. The Anandpur Sahib Resolution, in the two texts and their accompanying demands, is the founding document of the federal component, and it rewards being read in its own words rather than in the paraphrases of its enemies or its devotees.

The Resolution's text, in the standard English rendering preserved in the party's records and reproduced in the scholarly literature, proceeds from aims that are religious and internal: the propagation of the Sikh way of life, the maintenance of the Panth's separate corporate identity, the abolition of caste discrimination as contrary to the Gurus' teaching. Its political core is a federation clause. The Akali Dal demanded a genuine federal structure in which the Union's jurisdiction would be confined to a short list of subjects, with all residuary and concurrent powers resting in the states, and it attached to that clause a schedule of Punjab demands: the transfer of Chandigarh, the inclusion of Punjabi-speaking areas left in Haryana, Himachal Pradesh and Rajasthan, the control of the Bhakra and Beas headworks and a riparian share of river waters, and a larger state share in central revenues. Students and journalists reading the Resolution and the 1985 accord as scanned presentation files can open both texts side by side through a [free online PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which is the practical way to test the claim made in this section: that the document, read whole, is a federal programme with Sikh religious aims, and not the secessionist charter of the centralist paraphrase. The distinction matters because the two readings of the text generated two different politics for fifty years.

### Which subjects did the Anandpur Sahib Resolution leave with the central government?

The Resolution's federation clause confined the centre to four subjects: defence, foreign affairs, currency and communications. Every other field of legislation and taxation, including the residuary field, was to rest with the states, whose shares of central revenue the document demanded be raised in proportion to that transfer of responsibility.

The centralist reading, pressed by Indira Gandhi's governments after 1980 and echoed in much Delhi commentary of the insurgency years, held that the Resolution was secessionist in tendency: its demand for a political order in which the Sikh Panth's collective aspirations would find expression, read together with its federation clause, was said to sketch a sovereign Sikh entity inside a hollowed Union. The federalist reading, pressed by the Akali Dal at every period since 1973 and by scholars such as Pritam Singh in Federalism, Nationalism and Development published in 2008, holds that the document is a radical but recognisably federal text: it names the Indian Union as the frame, confines itself to a redistribution of the Seventh Schedule's subjects, and differs from the autonomy demands of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam in Tamil Nadu or the Sarkaria Commission's later recommendations in degree rather than in kind. A third dispute, over authorship, illustrates the charter discipline this article applies to contested points. Party tradition and the journalist Kuldip Nayar's account credit the drafting influence of Sirdar Kapur Singh, the former Indian Civil Service officer and Akali parliamentarian; Robin Jeffrey, in What's Happening to India? published in 1986, noted the chronological difficulty that Kapur Singh died in August 1972, months before the sub-committee was appointed, and read the Resolution as a bargaining instrument improvised by a party in electoral decline. The drafting attribution is genuinely disputed, and the dispute is recorded here as disputed. The text is not disputed. It exists, it was adopted on the dates given, and its clauses are the clauses summarised in this section.

The federal component did its work in the decade after adoption by converting the unfinished business of 1966 into a standing constitutional negotiation. During the Janata interlude of 1977 to 1980, with the Akali Dal inside the ruling coalition at the centre and Parkash Singh Badal chief minister in Punjab from June 1977, the Resolution's demands were pressed through intergovernmental channels: the demand for Chandigarh, the claim to a riparian settlement of the Ravi and Beas waters against the award Indira Gandhi had announced in 1976, which gave Haryana, a non-riparian successor state, an equal share of the assessed surplus, and the demand for an all-India gurdwara law bringing Sikh shrines outside Punjab under a central statute. The water question shows the component's grip with particular clarity, because it survived every change of regime. The agreement of 31 December 1981, signed by Indira Gandhi with the chief ministers of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan, revised the 1976 shares and committed the parties to the Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal, by which Haryana's share of the Ravi and Beas waters would cross Punjab's territory. The Akali Dal, out of office, treated the agreement as a surrender of the riparian principle and made the canal a central count in the agitation it launched the following summer. The canal's Punjab length was largely excavated by 1990 and never carried water, blocked first by insurgent violence against its engineers and then by law and politics, a history taken up in the militancy section below. The point for the architecture is that the dispute was never a party dispute. Congress governments in Punjab signed water agreements, Akali governments denounced and later defended versions of them, the Aam Aadmi Party government after 2022 litigated the state's claim before the Supreme Court, and at each step the parties argued from positions the federal component had fixed in 1973 and 1981: Punjab as riparian owner, the centre as an umpire suspected of favouring Haryana, and the unfinished 1966 settlement as the original fault.

One further episode fixed the component in the state's political memory before the insurgency began. The Akali Dal's campaign against the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, conducted from the precincts of the Golden Temple after the party's leadership refused to accept the suspension of constitutional government, sent more than 40,000 volunteers to arrest on the movement's count and gave the Dal a democratic credential it invoked for decades afterwards. J. S. Grewal, in The Sikhs of the Punjab published in 1990 in the New Cambridge History of India series, treated that campaign as evidence that the Akali movement's federalism was constitutionalist in instinct: the party that the centre would soon accuse of harbouring secession had been, within five years, running a sustained mass agitation in defence of the Constitution's federal and democratic provisions as proclaimed. The accusation and the credential coexist in the record, and any honest adjudication of the militancy decade has to hold both.

## The Militancy Years, 1980 to 1995: The Architecture Under Trauma

The militancy decade began as an argument inside the architecture, over who legitimately held its first component. The Congress victory in the Punjab election of June 1980 returned Darbara Singh as chief minister, and the Akali Dal, divided between the factions of Parkash Singh Badal, Gurcharan Singh Tohra and Jagdev Singh Talwandi, confronted a preacher who was capturing its religious ground from outside its discipline: Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale, head of the Damdami Taksal seminary, whose following among rural Sikh youth had grown since the clash of 13 April 1978 at Amritsar, where 13 Sikhs of the Akhand Kirtani Jatha were killed protesting a congregation of the Nirankari sect, whom orthodox Sikhs regarded as heretical. Two accounts of Bhindranwale's rise stand in the record and both are presented here at their strongest, as the stance of this series requires. The cultivation account, argued by journalists who covered the period and adopted in the scholarly literature on Indira Gandhi's Punjab strategy, holds that Congress figures, with President Zail Singh and the future chief minister Darbara Singh named in the strongest versions, encouraged Bhindranwale in the late 1970s as a counterweight to the Akali Dal in the Committee's elections, and that the instrument escaped its makers. The autonomy account, argued by Bhindranwale's defenders and by historians of the Taksal tradition, holds that he was an indigenous revivalist whose authority derived from the seminary and from the grievances of 1978, and that the cultivation story is a retrospective comfort for a Congress that in fact arrested him in September 1981 for the murder of the newspaper editor Lala Jagat Narain and released him within weeks for want of evidence. The documentary record supports elements of both: contacts between Congress intermediaries and Bhindranwale are attested, and so is the depth of his independent following. What is not disputed is the sequence. Arrested and released in 1981, he moved into the Guru Nanak Niwas guest house of the Golden Temple complex in July 1982, and in December 1983 into the Akal Takht building itself, converting the first component's holiest ground into a fortified political camp.

The Akali Dal's own vehicle in those years was the Dharam Yudh Morcha, the righteous-war agitation launched on 4 August 1982 under Sant Harchand Singh Longowal, which fused the Anandpur Sahib demands with the release of Sikh prisoners and the protest against the 1981 water agreement, and which sent tens of thousands of volunteers to arrest in waves through 1982 and 1983. The morcha's significance for the architecture reading is that it was the first component operating as designed: the Committee's precincts as sanctuary and headquarters, the Dal's jathas as disciplined mass instrument, the federal programme as the stated cause. Its tragedy was that it shared the stage with an armed movement it could neither command nor disown. Through 1983 and into 1984, killings of Hindu bus passengers, police officers, journalists and Sikh moderates mounted; the state government was dismissed and President's rule imposed in October 1983; and the Union government of Indira Gandhi, having exhausted negotiations conducted through intermediaries including the maharaja of Patiala's heir, Captain Amarinder Singh, then a Congress member of Parliament, ordered the army into the Golden Temple complex on the night of 5 and 6 June 1984. Operation Blue Star, examined in this series as [the 1984 Operation Blue Star and post-1984 anti-Sikh violence](/2019/05/09/operation-blue-star-1984-assassination/), killed Bhindranwale and his military adviser, the dismissed Major-General Shabeg Singh, destroyed the Akal Takht building under tank fire, and left casualty figures that remain disputed between the official count of 493 civilians and combatants inside the complex, together with 83 soldiers, and the substantially higher figures, running into thousands, maintained by Sikh organisations and human-rights enquiries. The dispute is recorded here as a dispute. The political consequences are not disputed: the assault on the faith's central shrine alienated the Sikh countryside from the Union as no event since 1947 had done, Sikh soldiers mutinied at several stations, and on 31 October 1984 Indira Gandhi was assassinated by two Sikh members of her own guard, Beant Singh and Satwant Singh.

The violence of November 1984 that followed the assassination belongs to the record of this section because it determined what the architecture had to survive. In Delhi, organised mobs, in case after case led or abetted by local Congress figures on the findings later recorded by the Nanavati Commission of 2005, killed Sikhs in their neighbourhoods over three days while the police stood aside or participated; the official Delhi toll recorded before that commission was 2,733 dead, and the all-India figure is generally placed above 3,000, with the widows' colonies of Tilak Vihar standing as the event's permanent memorial. Criminal accountability remained fragmentary for decades: the Congress leader Sajjan Kumar was convicted only in 2018, thirty-four years after the killings, by the Delhi High Court, which described the events as crimes against humanity engineered by political actors. For the architecture reading, November 1984 is the point at which the insurgency acquired its recruiting ground and its moral warrant in the eyes of its recruits: the young men who crossed into the militant organisations after 1984 were, on the testimony collected by Joyce Pettigrew in The Sikhs of the Punjab published in 1995, very often animated less by the Anandpur programme than by the unpunished killing of their community in the capital of the Union. Jugdep S. Chima, in The Sikh Separatist Insurgency in India published in 2010, formalised the point in a three-factor model in which the insurgency's mass phase required the conjunction of an available ethnic programme, a crisis of state legitimacy, and a weakened moderate leadership; 1984 supplied the second and third factors to the programme the first component had supplied since 1973.

Beneath the ideological sequence ran an economic one, and the scholarship of the insurgency is at its best on the connection. The Green Revolution had made Punjab India's richest agricultural state by the late 1970s, its wheat and paddy procured by the Union at administered prices into the central pool, but the revolution's gains were unevenly held: holdings fragmented with each generation, mechanisation shed labour, and the state's colleges produced, by the early 1980s, a large cohort of educated rural Sikh youth for whom neither the family farm nor the state's small industrial sector had work. Dipankar Gupta, writing on the insurgency's sociology in the essays collected across the 1980s and 1990s, insisted that the militant recruit was characteristically this figure, the educated son of a marginal Jat Sikh peasant household, and that the romance of Khalistan operated on him through the idiom of honour and blocked advancement rather than through theology. The Anandpur programme itself carried the economic counts in its schedules: the demand for the state's industries to be licensed to Punjab rather than concentrated elsewhere, the protest against the diversion of Punjab's power and water to neighbouring states, the demand that procurement prices be fixed by the state's costs. The architecture reading absorbs the economic evidence without strain, because the programme the insurgents claimed had been written, in 1973, by a party of the peasantry in a decade of agrarian prosperity, and its economic clauses were the peasantry's own. The militancy-era framing, which begins the story in 1978 or 1982, meets the economic substrate as an external cause. The architecture reading meets it where the participants put it, inside the programme.

The state's answer, attempted once in good faith, was the Punjab Accord of 24 July 1985, signed by Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi and Sant Harchand Singh Longowal and known by both names. The accord's text, one of the three primary documents on which this article rests, conceded the architecture's docket in terms: Chandigarh would be transferred to Punjab on 26 January 1986, with Hindi-speaking territory passing to Haryana in exchange as the Mathew Commission would determine; the Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal would be completed by 15 August 1986; a tribunal under a Supreme Court judge, the Eradi Tribunal, would adjudicate the Ravi and Beas water claims; compensation would be paid to the victims of the November 1984 violence and of the insurgency; and the Anandpur Sahib Resolution would be referred to the Sarkaria Commission on centre-state relations, in the clause that described the Resolution as asking for autonomy within the frame of the Union. The assembly election of September 1985, held on the accord's promise, gave the Akali Dal 73 of 117 seats, its finest result, and Surjit Singh Barnala became chief minister. The accord died by assassination and timetable. Longowal was shot dead by militants at Sherpur on 20 August 1985, less than a month after signing; the Chandigarh date of 26 January 1986 passed without transfer, the exchange territory commissions having produced findings the Union declined to execute; and the Barnala government, pressed between a resurgent militancy and a centre that treated Punjab's elected government as a security problem, was dismissed on 11 May 1987.

### Why was Chandigarh not transferred to Punjab in January 1986?

The transfer required a simultaneous exchange: Hindi-speaking territory from Punjab to Haryana, identified by the Mathew Commission and then by a successor commission whose award covered Abohar and Fazilka. Haryana's leadership rejected the adequacy of the exchange, the Union government declined to override the objection, and the 26 January 1986 date passed with Chandigarh left a Union Territory.

What followed was the insurgency's armed ascendancy and the state's long counter-insurgency. The militant organisations, the Khalistan Commando Force, the Babbar Khalsa, the Khalistan Liberation Force and others, escalated through the late 1980s: Operation Black Thunder in May 1988 cleared the Golden Temple complex with fewer casualties and more transparency than 1984, but by 1990 and 1991 the killing rate had reached its peak, with official compilations recording more than 2,500 civilian deaths in each of those years, alongside assassinations of police families, bank robberies, and the massacre of train passengers at Ludhiana district in June and December 1991. The counter-insurgency under Governor's rule, directed operationally by the Punjab Police chief K. P. S. Gill, combined intelligence-led operations, the fortification of the border with Pakistan, and methods whose human cost is itself a matter of record and dispute: the human-rights activist Jaswant Singh Khalra documented, from municipal records, more than 2,000 illegal cremations of unidentified bodies in Amritsar district alone, a documentation taken up by the Central Bureau of Investigation and the National Human Rights Commission, which eventually confirmed 2,097 such cremations across three cremation grounds and awarded compensation in a fraction of cases; Khalra himself disappeared in police custody in September 1995, and police officers were convicted of his murder a decade later. The architecture reading does not require minimising any of this. It requires noticing what the violence was fought over and what survived it. The insurgents claimed the Anandpur programme and the Khalsa's sovereignty; the state claimed the Union's integrity; and the moderate Akali leadership, Barnala dismissed, Longowal dead, Tohra and Badal in and out of prison, kept the first component's institutions staffed and functioning through the entire period, contesting Committee elections in 1980s conditions that resembled a siege, so that when the guns fell silent there was an institution ready to resume politics.

### When did Punjab's militancy end?

The insurgency's organised phase ended between 1993 and 1995: the last of the major militant commanders were killed or captured by 1993, election turnout recovered in the parliamentary election of 1996, and the terminal date most accounts adopt is 31 August 1995, when Chief Minister Beant Singh was assassinated at Chandigarh in the insurgency's final major operation.

The endgame ran through the election of February 1992, which the principal Akali factions boycotted and which recorded a turnout of about 24 percent, the lowest in the state's history, installing the Congress government of Beant Singh on a mandate everyone understood to be provisional. Beant Singh's government, with Gill's police, prosecuted the counter-insurgency to its conclusion and paid the price recorded in the answer given directly before this paragraph: the chief minister's assassination in 1995 closed the militancy era in the same act of violence that closed his government. The architecture's persistence through the trauma is the fact the militancy framing cannot digest. In February 1997, five years after the boycott election and eighteen months after the assassination, Punjab voted in an assembly election with a turnout above 68 percent, and the government it produced was formed by the two parties the trauma was supposed to have destroyed or discredited: the Akali Dal, on the Anandpur programme formally shelved, and the Bharatiya Janata Party, the party of the Union government that had sent the army into the Golden Temple. The system's capacity to reconstitute itself around its oldest institutions, rather than around new men or new programmes, is the strongest single piece of evidence that what organises Punjab politics is an architecture and not a sequence of moods.

## Component Four: The Coalition Order of 1996 to 2020

The fourth component assembled itself from the militancy era's debris. In December 1996, at a conference at Moga, the Akali Dal under Parkash Singh Badal adopted a repositioning that made the coalition possible: the party declared itself the party of Punjab and of all Punjabis, Punjabi and Punjabiat replacing the Panth as the lead register of its appeal, without surrendering its custody of the first component's institutions. The alliance with the Bharatiya Janata Party, negotiated through 1996 and sealed for the assembly election of February 1997, paired that repositioned Dal with the party of urban Hindu Punjab. The election of February 1997 returned the combination with 93 of 117 seats, the Akali Dal winning 75 and the Bharatiya Janata Party 18, and Badal began the first of the coalition's three governments. The architecture reading insists on what the combination was: not an ideological merger, the Dal's programme and the Bharatiya Janata Party's Hindutva remained distinct and at points opposed, but a constitutional device for governing a state whose religious demography punished single-community rule, a device that [the 1998 NDA's coalition-architecture context](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/) then scaled to the Union, where Akali ministers sat in Atal Bihari Vajpayee's cabinets and the Dal functioned as the alliance's Sikh guarantor.

The coalition's record across its three governments, from 1997 to 2002, from 2007 to 2012 and from 2012 to 2017, is the post-militancy settlement in administrative form: free electricity to the farm sector, announced in 1997 and defended by every successor government of every party as an untouchable inheritance; the restoration of routine electoral competition, with the Congress interregnum of Captain Amarinder Singh from 2002 to 2007 demonstrating that the order could lose office and return; and the management of the federal component by shelving, the Anandpur demands neither pressed nor renounced while the Dal's ministers sat in Delhi. The shelving is the point the party-narrative framing misses. A programme that is shelved by its own custodians for twenty-three years and survives the shelving, to be invoked again in 2020 as the warrant for breaking the coalition that required the shelving, is behaving as architecture behaves: load-bearing even when hidden. The coalition decades also produced the order's internal corrosion, documented in the scholarship of the period and in Anshu Saluja's essays on post-1995 Punjab politics: the Badal family's consolidation of the party and of the state government's contracts economy, the transport and mining interests attributed to the family's firms by opposition parties and journalists, the 2015 desecration incidents at Bargari and the police firing on protesters at Kotkapura and Behbal Kalan that October, in which two Sikhs were killed, and the erosion of the Dal's claim to guard the first component that those events inflicted. The coalition's third government ended in the election of February 2017 with the Dal reduced to 15 seats and its partner to 3, the Congress under Amarinder Singh taking 77. The order had lost office before, in 2002, and recovered. What distinguished 2017 was that a third force, the Aam Aadmi Party with 20 seats in its first full Punjab campaign, had arrived to contest the succession.

The coalition order also faced a challenger of a kind the architecture had not previously contained: the dera. Punjab's landscape of living-saint establishments, of which the Dera Sacha Sauda at Sirsa in Haryana, led by Gurmeet Ram Rahim Singh, was the largest, commanded followings numbered in the millions among the state's Dalit and marginal-caste Sikhs and Hindus, constituencies the architecture's institutions served thinly. In the election of 2007 the Dera's political wing instructed its followers to vote Congress, and the instruction was credited in the Malwa constituencies with the Congress holding ground it might otherwise have lost; two months later, in May 2007, the Dera head's appearance in dress resembling Guru Gobind Singh provoked a Sikh outrage that the Akal Takht answered with an edict of boycott, and Punjab lived for weeks on the edge of a Sikh-Dera confrontation. The episode matters to the adjudication because it shows the architecture's first component doing definitional work under electoral pressure: the Takht's edict, not any party's manifesto, was the instrument by which the boundary of the Panth was policed, and both the Congress and the Dal courted the Dera's vote in subsequent elections while publicly deferring to the edict. Gurmeet Ram Rahim Singh's conviction for rape in August 2017, and the violence his followers unleashed at Panchkula that month, diminished the Dera as a broker without dissolving the dera phenomenon, whose smaller establishments continue to arbitrate the votes of the state's most marginal electors. An architecture built on one community's institutions will always generate such para-institutions at its edges; the dera decades are the record of Punjab's generating them at scale.

The break, when it came in 2020, came on the architecture's own ground. The three farm laws enacted by the Union Parliament in September 2020, liberalising the sale, pricing and storage of farm produce, were read in Punjab across party lines as a federal transfer: agriculture is a state subject, and the Union's legislation by the trade and commerce entry was treated by the state's farm unions, and then by its parties, as the centre occupying the state's field and dismantling the mandi system on which the farm economy rested. The Akali Dal's Harsimrat Kaur Badal resigned from the Union cabinet on 17 September 2020, and on 26 September 2020 the party quit the National Democratic Alliance, ending the fourth component after twenty-three years. The farm protests that followed, centred on Delhi's borders from November 2020 to December 2021 and anchored demographically and organisationally in Punjab, were the largest mobilisation the state had produced since the morchas of the first component, and they were conducted substantially through the architecture's inherited forms: the jatha, the langar, the religious sanction of the akal takht jathedars, and the unions' insistence that the quarrel was with the centre's jurisdiction rather than with any community. The laws were repealed in November 2021. The Dal gained little electorally from its stand, and the Bharatiya Janata Party, contesting Punjab alone for the first time in a generation, gained little either. The component had broken, and the architecture's other three components decided what happened next.

## The Stress Test of 2020 to 2022: Farm Laws, Protest and Repeal

The farm protests of 2020 and 2021 are the largest single body of evidence for the architecture's post-2020 operation, because they show the first component's methods running at full capacity outside the control of any party. The three laws, the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, and the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, passed through Parliament in September 2020 by voice vote in the Rajya Sabha over opposition protests, dismantled in principle the regulated mandi system through which Punjab's wheat and paddy had moved for half a century, and with it the commission-agent and state-revenue structure the mandis supported. Punjab's farm unions, more than thirty of them acting through the Samyukt Kisan Morcha from November 2020, read the laws as the federal component had taught the state to read such measures since 1973: as the centre entering a state subject, agriculture standing on the State List of the Seventh Schedule, by the side door of inter-state trade. The reading was arguable as law, the Union relying on its own entries with opinions divided among constitutional lawyers, and it is recorded here as the protesters' stated ground rather than as a settled verdict. What is beyond argument is the mobilisation's form. The march on Delhi of 26 November 2020, the year-long encampments at Singhu, Tikri and Ghazipur on the capital's borders, the langars that fed tens of thousands daily, the stages at which kirtan and political speech alternated, and the discipline that kept a protest of that size overwhelmingly peaceful for a year, were the morcha method of 1922 and 1955 and 1982, scaled to a metropolitan siege, run by unions rather than by the Akali Dal, and sanctified by the same religious institutions whose custody the first component governs. The parties followed the mobilisation; they did not lead it. The Akali Dal had broken its alliance in September, the Congress government of Amarinder Singh passed state amendments in October 2020 purporting to nullify the laws' effect in Punjab, and the Aam Aadmi Party supported the protest from Delhi's government while contesting Punjab's succession. The architecture's programme had escaped its party entirely and was being executed by civil society through the architecture's own methods, which is the strongest form the persistence thesis can take.

The protest's endgame fixed its place in the chronology. After the violence of 26 January 2021, when a breakaway column entered the Red Fort and a protester died, the movement survived its worst day and settled back into attrition; after the deaths of more than 700 protesters across the year on the Morcha's own maintained count, a figure the Union government told Parliament it had no record of, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced the laws' repeal on 19 November 2021, the festival of Guru Nanak's birth anniversary, in an address that apologised to the country for his government's failure to persuade the farmers. Parliament repealed the laws on 29 November 2021, and the encampments lifted in December after the government conceded a committee on minimum support price and the withdrawal of prosecutions. Punjab's part in the sequence matters to the adjudication for one reason: the repeal was the first occasion since 1991 on which a Union government of commanding parliamentary strength reversed a major enacted policy under pressure from a mobilisation anchored in a single state's political architecture. The protesters' own framing of the victory was federal and religious in equal measure, the centre taught the limits of its jurisdiction by the Panth's methods, and the state's parties inherited a demonstrated fact for the election that followed three months later: that the incumbent orders, Congress in the state and the Akali Dal in its historic role, had both been bystanders at the largest political event in Punjab in a generation. The verdict of March 2022 was cast in that shadow.

## The Verdict of 2022 and the Test of the Architecture

The Punjab assembly election of March 2022 produced the largest single-party mandate in the state's history. The Aam Aadmi Party, a party founded in Delhi in 2012 from the anti-corruption movement, with no roots in any of the architecture's four components, won 92 of 117 seats, and Bhagwant Mann, a comedian turned parliamentarian from Sangrur, was sworn in as chief minister on 16 March 2022 at Khatkar Kalan, the ancestral village of Bhagat Singh. The Congress, which had replaced Captain Amarinder Singh with Charanjit Singh Channi in September 2021 in an attempt to answer the anti-incumbency the Aam Aadmi Party was harvesting, fell to 18 seats. The Akali Dal fell to 3. The brief for this article names the analytical task exactly: to show that this verdict tests the architecture rather than displacing it, and to answer the strongest counter, which is that a victory of this scope, followed by the fragmentation of the 2024 parliamentary verdict, looks like displacement wearing the mask of a test.

### How many seats did each party win in the 2022 Punjab assembly election?

Of the 117 seats, the Aam Aadmi Party won 92, the Congress 18, the Shiromani Akali Dal 3, the Bharatiya Janata Party 2, the Bahujan Samaj Party 1 and an independent candidate 1. The Aam Aadmi Party's tally was the largest single-party majority in the history of the Punjab assembly, exceeding the Akali Dal's 73 of 1985.

One further body of evidence belongs in the test, because it predates the landslide and qualifies what the landslide meant. Punjab had adopted the Aam Aadmi Party earlier and more warmly than any state outside Delhi: in the parliamentary election of 2014, the party's only four Lok Sabha victories in India were all Punjabi, at Sangrur, Faridkot, Fatehgarh Sahib and Patiala, and Bhagwant Mann's own parliamentary base at Sangrur dated from that year. The state's electorate, that is, had been auditioning an outside vehicle for a decade before it handed that vehicle the government, and it auditioned the vehicle on the architecture's own criteria: the 2014 victors campaigned on the drug epidemic that the coalition order had failed to master and on the state's unkept claims against the centre, and the audition deepened after the desecration incidents and police firings of October 2015 stained the Dal's custody of the faith it claimed to guard. The Aam Aadmi Party's Punjab rise is therefore not well described as an invasion from outside the architecture's world. It is better described as the architecture's electorate conducting a search, across three elections, for a carrier of its grievances untainted by the custody disputes of the first component, and finding one whose Delhi origins were, in Punjab's terms, almost an advantage: a party with no faction in the Committee, no position in the water dispute inherited from any ancestor, and no Badal or Congress surname to forgive.

The displacement case rests on that table and on three supporting facts. The Akali Dal's 3 seats were its worst result since the party's refoundation era, and its vote share, about 18 percent, was concentrated in its Malwa heartland rather than spread across the state. The party's president, Sukhbir Singh Badal, lost his own seat at Jalalabad, and in December 2024 the Akal Takht's jathedar pronounced a religious sentence, a tankhah, on Sukhbir Badal for the failures of the coalition governments, a public subordination of the party's leadership to the first component's religious authority that no earlier decade had produced in that form. The parliamentary election of June 2024 then fragmented the state's 13 seats among the Congress with 7, the Aam Aadmi Party with 3, the Akali Dal with 1, Harsimrat Kaur Badal holding Bathinda, and two independents: Amritpal Singh, the Waris Punjab De leader then detained under the National Security Act, winning Khadoor Sahib from custody, and Sarabjeet Singh Khalsa, son of Indira Gandhi's assassin Beant Singh, winning Faridkot. On the party-narrative framing, this is a system in dissolution: the old parties scattered, the electorate voting for a detained revivalist and an assassin's son in two constituencies, the architecture's carriers reduced to single seats.

The architecture reading answers by separating the fate of carriers from the fate of content, and the post-2022 record supports the separation at every point where the Mann government has had to govern. On the first component, the custody of Sikh institutions: the Mann government legislated in June 2023 to amend the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 so as to mandate free telecast of the Gurbani broadcast from the Golden Temple, a direct intervention in the Committee's most sensitive property right, resisted by the Committee's Akali-led executive in exactly the idiom of 1920, the state laying hands on the Panth's institutions; and the Committee's own elections and internal contests continued through the Aam Aadmi Party's ascendancy as the arena in which the Dal's factions, and the breakaway factions outside it, fought for custody of the Panth. On the second component: the Mann government's Punjabi-first orders on signboards and official correspondence extended the language settlement rather than revising it. On the third component: the Mann government prosecuted the federal docket against the centre with a fidelity the coalition-era Dal had suspended, refusing Haryana's claim on the Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal in the Supreme Court proceedings of October 2023, contesting the Union's rural development fund withholdings, and opposing the deployment of central forces at the Bhakra installations in 2025. Even the two independent victories of 2024 read, on this analysis, as the architecture's content escaping its carriers rather than the content dying: Amritpal Singh's Khadoor Sahib campaign was an explicit revival of the first component's claim that Sikh politics answers to the Panth's institutions before it answers to any party, and his electorate voted for the claim while its historic party took 8 percent in the same constituency. The complication named in this article's brief is therefore answered as follows. Electoral displacement has occurred; the Aam Aadmi Party displaced two parties from government and may hold office for a long period. Architectural displacement has not occurred, because the questions Punjab's politics must answer, the custody of the shrines, the language of the state, the claims against the centre, and the terms on which the two great communities share rule, are still the questions the architecture poses, and every actor of 2022 and 2024, governing or insurgent, has answered them in the architecture's vocabulary.

The post-2024 trajectory adds a transnational chamber to the same structure, and it must be handled with the same attribution discipline as the militancy record. The Sikh diaspora in Canada, Britain and the United States, descended in large part from the migrations of the 1980s and 1990s, carries the architecture's first component abroad in the form of gurdwara politics, and a fringe of that politics, organised around the Khalistan referendum campaign of the banned organisation Sikhs for Justice, kept the sovereignty claim of the insurgency years alive in diaspora referendums through the 2010s and 2020s. In June 2023 the Canadian citizen and referendum activist Hardeep Singh Nijjar was shot dead outside a gurdwara in Surrey, British Columbia; in September 2023 Prime Minister Justin Trudeau told the Canadian Parliament that Canada was pursuing credible allegations of a link between agents of the Indian government and the killing, a charge the Indian government rejected as absurd and politically motivated, and the two states expelled diplomats and suspended trade talks; in October 2024 the rupture deepened with further expulsions on both sides, and the United States separately prosecuted an alleged Indian-linked plot against a Sikh separatist lawyer in New York. The facts of responsibility in these cases remain contested between governments and before courts, and they are recorded here as contested. What is not contested is the structural point, which the post-2024 evidence makes unavoidable: the questions the architecture poses inside Punjab, who speaks for the Panth, what the Sikh political claim on India is, and how the Indian state answers it, are also posed in Surrey, Southall and Sacramento, and are answered there through institutions, the diaspora gurdwara, the referendum campaign, the parliamentary caucus, that replicate the first component's logic of custody and representation on foreign soil. An architecture that can be exported by migration and operated by citizens of other states has a durability that no theory of party fortunes can reach, and its existence abroad feeds back into the state's politics every time a jathedar's edict, a diaspora referendum or a diplomatic rupture sets the agenda in Amritsar and Chandigarh for a season.

## The First Component Contested from Within: The Committee after 2014

The strongest evidence that the first component remains load-bearing is that it continues to be fought over in law, and the sharpest of those fights came not from the centre but from within the Sikh institutional world itself. In July 2014, the Congress government of Haryana under Bhupinder Singh Hooda, weeks before an assembly election it would lose, enacted the Haryana Sikh Gurdwaras (Management) Act, creating a separate committee for the gurdwaras of Haryana and removing them from the jurisdiction of the Amritsar body, on the stated ground that Haryana's Sikhs had demanded their own management since the state's creation in 1966. The Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee treated the Act as a dismemberment of the settlement of 1925 and challenged it, and the Akali Dal, then in the Union government, pressed for central intervention that never came. In September 2022, the Supreme Court upheld the Haryana Act, holding the state legislature competent to enact it, and the Committee's jurisdiction contracted for the first time by statute since 1925. The episode is the militancy framing's opposite in every respect: no violence, no Delhi durbar politics, a state legislature and a constitutional court, and yet the event rearranged the architecture's first component more durably than any election of the period. It also demonstrated the component's centrality by the reaction it provoked: the Committee's leadership denounced the verdict in the language of 1920, as a state seizure of the Panth's property, and Haryana's Sikh leadership celebrated in the same language, as a liberation of their shrines from an unaccountable custody. Both sides agreed on the premise the architecture supplies, that custody of the gurdwaras is a sovereign question of Sikh politics; they disputed only the custody.

The Committee's own internal politics after 2022 tell the same story from inside. The general house elections due under the 1925 Act had last been held in 2011, and the long delay, through litigation and the insurgency's long administrative shadow, left the elected house of 2011 sitting for over a decade, its presidency passing to Harjinder Singh Dhami in 2021 and renewed annually thereafter. The Aam Aadmi Party government, commanding the state but holding no votes in the Committee's confessional electorate, governed alongside an institution it did not control and could not dissolve, and the friction surfaced wherever the two jurisdictions touched: the telecast of Gurbani, the appointment politics of the takhts, the jathedar of the Akal Takht's pronouncement of religious sentence on Sukhbir Singh Badal in December 2024, which the Dal's president accepted in a public act of penance at the Golden Temple. A party reduced to a single parliamentary seat in 2024 remained, through the Committee and the takhts, a party embedded in the state's religious constitution, answerable to it and sheltered by it. That is the architecture in its purest observable form: electoral annihilation on one register, institutional continuity on the other, in the same season.

## Adjudicating the Three Readings

Three readings of Punjab's politics have been set against a century of evidence in the sections above, and the adjudication can be made on stated criteria: which reading explains the most evidence with the fewest ad hoc exceptions, and which survives the two hardest cases, the militancy decade and the verdict of 2022. The militancy-era framing explains the salience of 1984 in Sikh political memory and the genuine caesura of the insurgency, and this article has used its evidence throughout. It fails on chronology. It cannot explain why the institutions of 1920 to 1925, and not the grievances of the Green Revolution countryside alone, structured the insurgency's own claims; it cannot explain the speed of the reconstitution of 1997; and it treats the fifty years before 1980 and the thirty years after 1995 as prologue and epilogue to a fifteen-year centre, which is a proportion the evidence does not support. The party-narrative framing explains the election record and nothing else: it registers the rotation of 1967, 1977, 1985, 1997, 2017 and 2022 as a sequence of verdicts, and it is silent on why the verdicts keep being argued on the same four questions, and why parties with no inheritance in those questions, the Aam Aadmi Party above all, must adopt the questions to govern. The architecture reading explains both the rotation and the recurrence. It predicts, correctly, that the Akali Dal's electoral collapses would not dissolve the Committee, that the federal docket of 1966 would outlive every signatory of every water agreement, and that a victorious outsider party would be found, within eighteen months of taking office, legislating about gurdwara broadcasts, litigating river waters and defending the Punjabi language settlement, which is to say governing inside the architecture it had never built.

The comparative evidence sharpens the verdict. Read against [the three-party-architecture comparator](/2020/01/12/bihar-mandal-politics-reanalysed/) of Bihar, where three parties rest on three social substrates and government is always a pairing from three, Punjab presents the rarer case of an architecture that is institutional before it is electoral: Bihar's substrates are caste coalitions carried by parties, while Punjab's are carried by a statutory religious corporation, a language statute and a programme text, which is why Punjab's architecture survived a decade in which its principal party was reduced, at the boycott election of 1992, to irrelevance, a test no Bihar party has faced. Read against [the long-arc party-evolution comparator](/2020/01/17/maharashtra-shiv-sena-evolution/) of Maharashtra, where a single regional party evolved through organisational, ideological and succession crises across five decades, Punjab shows the contrasting pattern of a party repeatedly broken and repeatedly reconstituted by the institutions beneath it: the Shiv Sena's history is the history of an organisation, while the Akali Dal's history is the history of an architecture that keeps producing organisations. And read within [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) that organises this series as a whole, Punjab is the state where the founding tensions of the Republic, between the centre and the states, between religious community and secular state, and between the claims of language and the claims of the Union, were never settled by the founding documents at all, but were instead delegated to a state-level architecture that has been renegotiating them, in morchas, accords, coalitions and elections, for a hundred years.

Two honest limits on the reading should be recorded, because a claim that explains everything explains nothing. The first is that the architecture reading is a reading of structure, not of outcomes: it says what Punjab's politics will be about, not who will win it, and it is fully compatible with the Akali Dal never again forming a government, provided the questions the Dal's institutions pose continue to organise the state's politics. The second is the demographic limit. The architecture's first component presupposes a Sikh majority in the state whose corporate institutions the component operates; Punjab's Sikh share of population, about 58 percent in the 2011 census, has been declining slowly for decades under differential fertility and out-migration, and a Punjab in which Sikhs were a minority would strain the first component's claim to be the state's organising institution in ways the framework does not disguise. Neither limit rescues the rival framings. Both mark the points at which the architecture reading itself would have to be revised, and a reading that names its own falsifying conditions is, on the standards this series applies, the stronger for it.

## A Century of Load-Bearing Walls

The namable claim of this article can be restated in its final form. Punjab's religious-linguistic Akali architecture, the coupling of shrine and party built in 1920, the language settlement won in 1966, the federal programme written in 1973 and the cross-community coalition practised from 1997, has operated for over a century as the load-bearing structure of the state's politics. The militancy of 1980 to 1995 was the gravest assault the structure has borne, and it bore it: the Committee sat through the siege years, the programme survived its own custodians' shelving of it, and the politics of 1997 resumed on the structure's lines within two years of the last assassination. The Aam Aadmi Party victory of 2022 removed the structure's historic party from office and reduced it to 3 seats, and it left the structure itself exactly where it stood, supplying the new government with its agenda of shrine, language and river, and supplying the state's voters, in 2024, with alternatives drawn from the structure's oldest claims. The strongest available test of the claim is the one the coming decades will administer: whether any Punjab government can complete a term without adjudicating the custody of the gurdwaras, the standing of Punjabi, or the state's claims against the centre. On the evidence of the century examined here, no government has yet done so, and the smart money of the historical record, from the settlement of 1925 to the verdict of 2024, is that none will.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the Shiromani Akali Dal?

The Shiromani Akali Dal is the political party of the Sikh Panth, founded at Amritsar on 14 December 1920 as the political and volunteer arm of the gurdwara reform movement, a month after the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee was constituted to administer Sikh shrines. Its name means the army of the immortal, and its structure has always joined the two registers its name joins: it contests elections to the Punjab assembly and Parliament, and it contests the Committee's internal elections as the arbiter of Sikh religious custody. The party governed Punjab in coalition or alone in 1967, 1969, 1977, 1985, 1997, 2007 and 2012, carried the Punjabi Suba and Anandpur Sahib programmes, and remains, despite its reduction to 3 assembly seats in 2022, the custodian party of the architecture described in this article.

### Q: What is the SGPC?

The Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee is the statutory corporation that manages the historic Sikh gurdwaras of Punjab, constituted at Amritsar on 15 November 1920 and given legal form by the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925. Its general house, of about 170 members elected by adult Sikhs enrolled on a confessional register, elects a president and executive annually, administers the Golden Temple and the other scheduled shrines, and runs schools, colleges and hospitals from gurdwara revenues. Its headquarters stand in the Golden Temple complex at Amritsar. Because its electorate is the Panth in registered form and its custody is the community's sacred property, the Committee functions as a second political system inside Punjab, older than universal franchise in India, and its elections are treated by every Sikh political faction as a plebiscite on legitimate religious leadership.

### Q: What was the Anandpur Sahib Resolution?

The Anandpur Sahib Resolution is the federal programme adopted unanimously by the working committee of the Shiromani Akali Dal at Anandpur Sahib on 16 and 17 October 1973, from the report of a twelve-member sub-committee appointed on 11 December 1972, and endorsed in twelve resolutions at the All India Akali Conference at Ludhiana on 28 and 29 October 1978. The document joins religious aims, the propagation of the Sikh way of life and the abolition of caste discrimination, to a federation clause confining the central government to defence, foreign affairs, currency and communications, and to a schedule of Punjab demands: Chandigarh, the Punjabi-speaking areas left outside the state in 1966, control of river headworks and a riparian share of waters. Akali readings treat it as a federal text within the Union; centralist readings of the 1980s treated it as secessionist in tendency, a dispute the document has outlived.

### Q: What was the 1985 Punjab Accord?

The Punjab Accord of 24 July 1985, signed by Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi and the Akali Dal president Sant Harchand Singh Longowal and also called the Rajiv-Longowal Accord, was the settlement intended to close the Punjab crisis. Its text promised the transfer of Chandigarh to Punjab on 26 January 1986 against an exchange of Hindi-speaking territory to Haryana, the completion of the Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal by 15 August 1986, a tribunal under a Supreme Court judge to adjudicate the Ravi and Beas waters, compensation to victims of the November 1984 violence and of the insurgency, and the reference of the Anandpur Sahib Resolution to the Sarkaria Commission. Longowal was assassinated by militants on 20 August 1985, the Chandigarh deadline passed unexecuted, and the accord's unimplemented clauses became a standing count in the state's federal indictment of the centre.

### Q: Who is Bhagwant Mann?

Bhagwant Mann is the chief minister of Punjab from 16 March 2022, when he was sworn in at Khatkar Kalan, the ancestral village of Bhagat Singh, after leading the Aam Aadmi Party to 92 of 117 seats in the assembly election of that month. Born in 1973 at Satoj in Sangrur district, he built a first career as one of Punjab's best-known satirical comedians before entering politics, and he represented Sangrur in the Lok Sabha from 2014 to 2022, one of the Aam Aadmi Party's four Punjabi parliamentarians of 2014. His government has prosecuted the state's federal docket against the centre on river waters and finance, legislated on the telecast of Gurbani from the Golden Temple, and governed through the Punjabi-first language orders that continue the settlement of 1966.

### Q: Why did Akali-BJP alliance break in 2020?

The alliance broke over the three farm laws enacted by the Union Parliament in September 2020, which liberalised the sale, storage and contract marketing of farm produce and were read in Punjab as a federal transfer, agriculture being a state subject, and as a threat to the regulated mandi system and the minimum support price regime on which the state's farm economy rests. The Akali Dal, whose electorate is the Sikh peasantry, first resigned its seat in the Union cabinet, Harsimrat Kaur Badal leaving the ministry on 17 September 2020, and then, on 26 September 2020, quit the National Democratic Alliance altogether, ending a partnership sealed in 1996 that had governed Punjab for fifteen years. The party calculated that custody of the farm protest then gathering mattered more than office in Delhi, a calculation the farm unions' year-long siege of the capital appeared to vindicate when the laws were repealed in November 2021.

### Q: What is the Punjabi Suba movement?

The Punjabi Suba movement was the Akali Dal's two-decade campaign, from the late 1940s to 1966, for a Punjabi-speaking state within the Indian Union. Led first by Master Tara Singh and from the early 1960s by Sant Fateh Singh, who recast the demand as linguistic rather than confessional, it proceeded by morchas, mass court-arrest campaigns run from the Golden Temple, in 1955, 1960 and 1961 and 1965, against a Union government that had conceded linguistic states elsewhere. The States Reorganisation Commission rejected the demand in 1955, in the shadow of census returns in which many Punjabi-speaking Hindus had recorded Hindi as their mother tongue. Indira Gandhi's government conceded it in 1966: the Punjab Reorganisation Act, effective 1 November 1966, created the Punjabi-speaking Punjab, Hindi-speaking Haryana and a shared capital at Chandigarh, completing the movement's aim and leaving its unfinished docket to the federal programme of 1973.

### Q: How and when was the Shiromani Akali Dal founded?

The Shiromani Akali Dal was founded at Amritsar on 14 December 1920, four weeks after the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, as the central political organisation of the Akali movement for the liberation of the gurdwaras from hereditary mahants. The movement's jathas, its bands of volunteers organised for disciplined non-violent action, needed a command structure for the confrontations that everyone expected, and the Dal supplied it: a party that was also an enrolled volunteer corps, under a president elected by the movement, with its authority flowing from the religious campaign it served. Through the morchas at Nankana Sahib, Guru ka Bagh and Jaito between 1921 and 1925 the Dal became the Panth's political general staff, and when the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 settled the campaign, the Dal emerged as a permanent party, the oldest regional party in India in continuous existence.

### Q: What was the Gurdwara Reform Movement?

The Gurdwara Reform Movement was the campaign of 1920 to 1925 by which the Sikh community, organised through the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee and the Shiromani Akali Dal, took control of the historic gurdwaras from the mahants, the hereditary custodians whom the British administration had recognised and whom reformers accused of treating shrine property as private estate and of corrupting shrine practice. Its method was the morcha, the disciplined mass agitation in which jathas of volunteers courted arrest or worse without retaliating: at Nankana Sahib in February 1921 the custodian's men massacred a reforming jatha, at Guru ka Bagh in 1922 volunteers endured daily beatings to cut wood on shrine land, and at Jaito in 1923 and 1924 the movement marched for the right to complete an interrupted scripture reading. Thousands were imprisoned. The campaign ended in the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925, which vested the shrines in the elected Committee, and it fixed the methods and the institutions of Sikh politics for a century.

### Q: What did the Sikh Gurdwaras Act of 1925 establish?

The Sikh Gurdwaras Act, passed by the Punjab Legislative Council in July 1925 and brought into force on 1 November 1925, established the legal constitution of Sikh religious self-government. It scheduled the historic gurdwaras of the province, provided a judicial tribunal to determine which shrines fell under the Act, and vested the management of the scheduled shrines in a central elected body, the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee, with local committees for individual gurdwaras. It defined the Sikh electorate for the Committee's elections by a profession of the Sikh faith, fixed the Committee as a body corporate able to hold property and sue, and subjected shrine finances to its audit. The Act converted the gurdwara reform movement's conquests into a permanent statutory order, and it remains, amended over the decades and read down for Haryana in 2022, the charter under which the first component of Punjab's political architecture operates.

### Q: Who was Master Tara Singh?

Master Tara Singh, born in 1885 at Haryal in Rawalpindi district and dead on 22 November 1967, was the dominant leader of the Akali movement for four decades. A schoolteacher by profession, the title Master stayed with him for life, he rose through the gurdwara reform movement to lead the Shiromani Akali Dal and to represent the Sikhs in the negotiations of the 1940s, where he opposed the Pakistan demand and proposed instead an Azad Punjab in which no community would dominate. After 1947 he made the Punjabi Suba the movement's cause, led its morchas of 1955 and 1960 and 1961, and undertook a fast of 48 days in the autumn of 1961 that ended in a disputed assurance of enquiry. His lieutenancy passed to Sant Fateh Singh, with whom he split in 1962, and the two factions of the Dal bore their names until the reunion that followed his death, a year after the Suba he had demanded for twenty years was finally granted.

### Q: Why is Chandigarh the shared capital of Punjab and Haryana?

Chandigarh is a shared capital because it was built to be one state's capital and inherited by two. The city was raised after 1947, on a plan associated with Le Corbusier, as the new capital of Indian Punjab in place of Lahore, lost to Pakistan. When the Punjab Reorganisation Act took effect on 1 November 1966, dividing the state between Punjabi-speaking Punjab and Hindi-speaking Haryana, the Shah Commission's award of the capital to Haryana was set aside in practice, and Chandigarh was made a Union Territory serving as joint capital of both states, as a temporary arrangement. The Anandpur Sahib Resolution demanded its outright transfer, the Punjab Accord of 1985 fixed the transfer for 26 January 1986 against an exchange of territory, and the exchange commissions' findings were never executed. The temporary arrangement has therefore outlasted every government that inherited it, and Chandigarh administers itself as a Union Territory while housing two state governments, two high court establishments' worth of claims, and the most durable symbol of the unfinished settlement of 1966.

### Q: What is the Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal dispute?

The Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal dispute concerns the unfinished canal intended to carry Haryana's share of the Ravi and Beas waters across Punjab. The waters were divided among the successor states by the award of 1976 and the agreement of 31 December 1981, which allotted Punjab 4.22 million acre-feet, Haryana 3.50 and Rajasthan 8.60 of the assessed flows, and the canal was the physical instrument of Haryana's share. Construction began in 1982 under the shadow of the Dharam Yudh Morcha, which counted the canal a theft of Punjab's riparian rights; the Punjab length was largely excavated by 1990, when work stopped amid insurgent attacks on its engineers. No water has ever flowed. Punjab's assembly answered with the Termination of Agreements Act of 2004, annulling the state's water agreements, which a presidential reference brought before the Supreme Court, whose advisory opinion of 2016 held the Act unconstitutional. The canal's dry bed remains the federal dispute in physical form, defended and denounced by every Punjab government since 1982.

### Q: Who was Sant Harchand Singh Longowal?

Sant Harchand Singh Longowal, born in 1932 at Gidderbaha and assassinated on 20 August 1985, was the president of the Shiromani Akali Dal who led the Dharam Yudh Morcha from August 1982 and signed the Punjab Accord with Rajiv Gandhi on 24 July 1985. A religious preacher turned politician, he represented the Akali tradition's moderate and negotiating line: the morcha he led pressed the Anandpur Sahib demands by mass arrest rather than arms, and he conducted the tortuous negotiations of 1982 to 1984 with the Union government through intermediaries even as Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale's parallel camp inside the Golden Temple complex drew the state's anger away from the negotiating table. After Operation Blue Star and the November 1984 violence, Longowal staked his authority on the accord's promise that the centre would honour the federal docket. Militants who regarded the accord as surrender shot him at Sherpur village in Sangrur district, less than a month after he signed it, and the accord's implementation died with him.

### Q: What was the Dharam Yudh Morcha?

The Dharam Yudh Morcha, the agitation for a righteous cause, was the mass movement the Shiromani Akali Dal launched from the Golden Temple at Amritsar on 4 August 1982 under Sant Harchand Singh Longowal. Its demands fused the Anandpur Sahib Resolution's federal programme with the grievances of the hour: the release of Akali volunteers arrested in earlier agitations, the rejection of the water agreement of December 1981, and the implementation of the 1973 programme including Chandigarh and state autonomy. Its method was the inherited one of the first component, wave upon wave of volunteers courting arrest, and its official count of arrests ran into the tens of thousands across 1982 and 1983. The morcha ran concurrently with Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale's armed ascendancy inside the same shrine complex, a duality the Akali leadership could neither resolve nor survive intact: the morcha's constitutional agitation and the militants' guns proceeded from the same ground to irreconcilable ends, until the army's entry into the complex in June 1984 ended both.

### Q: Who was Parkash Singh Badal?

Parkash Singh Badal, born on 8 December 1927 at Abul Khurana in the Malwa region and dead on 25 April 2023, was Punjab's longest-serving chief minister and the Akali Dal's patriarch for half a century. He held the office five times, in 1970 and 1971, from 1977 to 1980, from 1997 to 2002, and across the two consecutive terms of 2007 to 2017, a cumulative tenure above seventeen years that no other Punjab politician approaches. First elected to the Punjab assembly in 1957 and to office as a Union minister in 1977, he led the Dal through the militancy years in opposition and prison, authored its repositioning at the Moga conference of December 1996 from a Panthic party to a party of all Punjabis, and sealed the alliance with the Bharatiya Janata Party that governed the state for fifteen years. His family's consolidation of the party, and the corruption charges his opponents pressed against its business interests, defined the coalition order he built and the reaction that finally displaced it.

### Q: What happened at Nankana Sahib in 1921?

At Nankana Sahib, the birthplace of Guru Nanak in what is today Pakistani Punjab, the gurdwara reform movement met its bloodiest day on 20 February 1921. A jatha of Sikh reformers entered the Janam Asthan gurdwara to assert the community's claim against its custodian, Mahant Narain Das, whose conduct the reform movement had made a byword; the mahant's hired men, prepared for the entry, shot and hacked the unarmed jatha inside the shrine. About 130 Sikhs were killed on the count most often cited from the official enquiry that followed, and the bodies were burned in an attempt to destroy the evidence. The massacre converted a property dispute into a national cause: the British administration, shaken, handed the shrine's keys and management to a committee of Sikhs within the year, prosecuted the mahant, who was convicted and hanged, and conceded in practice the principle the Sikh Gurdwaras Act would codify in 1925. Nankana remains the reform movement's martyrdom shrine, commemorated annually across the border the movement's survivors were later forced to cross.

### Q: Why did Captain Amarinder Singh resign as Punjab Chief Minister in 2021?

Captain Amarinder Singh resigned on 18 September 2021 after a revolt inside the Punjab Congress destroyed his authority from within. The immediate cause was the party high command's elevation of his rival Navjot Singh Sidhu to the state Congress presidency in July 2021, against the chief minister's stated opposition, followed by a legislators' campaign that catalogued unkept promises from the 2017 manifesto and demanded his removal with an assembly election five months away. Summoned through a sequence of meetings in Delhi that he described, on resigning, as humiliation repeated three times, Amarinder Singh stepped down and was succeeded on 20 September 2021 by Charanjit Singh Channi, the state's first Dalit chief minister. Amarinder Singh then founded the Punjab Lok Congress, contested the 2022 election in alliance with the Bharatiya Janata Party without success, and merged his party into the Bharatiya Janata Party in September 2022, closing a Congress career that had begun in 1980 and carried him to the chief ministership twice.

### Q: How did the Aam Aadmi Party win the 2022 Punjab assembly election?

The Aam Aadmi Party won by being the only major contender the decade had not discredited. It took 92 of 117 seats with about 42 percent of the vote in March 2022, on a campaign that promised 300 units of free electricity, an anti-corruption government on the Delhi model's advertised lines, and Bhagwant Mann, the Sangrur parliamentarian since 2014, as chief minister. The Congress entered the election having replaced its own chief minister in September 2021 and fought its campaign amid open warfare between his successor and the state party president. The Akali Dal entered it carrying the coalition decades' record, the desecration cases of 2015 and the farm laws it had broken its alliance too late to escape, and fell to 3 seats. The farm protests of 2020 and 2021 had already delivered the verdict's premise, that the state's established orders had stood aside from its largest mobilisation in a generation, and the Aam Aadmi Party, present in Punjab since its four parliamentary victories of 2014, collected the conclusion.

### Q: What was Operation Black Thunder?

Operation Black Thunder names two clearances of the Golden Temple complex at Amritsar, of which the second, in May 1988, is the significant one. In Black Thunder II, from 12 to 18 May 1988, the National Security Guard and the Punjab Police besieged and cleared the complex, which militant groups had again occupied and fortified, using snipers, negotiated pressure and controlled entry rather than the armour of June 1984; about 200 militants surrendered, among them figures of the Khalistan Commando Force, and the dead numbered in the dozens rather than the hundreds or thousands disputed for 1984. Conducted before the press, the operation was presented by its commanders as the corrective to Operation Blue Star: the same objective taken with the shrine's fabric and the state's legitimacy kept in view. It marked the counter-insurgency's turn to intelligence-led police method under K. P. S. Gill, though the insurgency's worst killing years, 1990 and 1991, still lay ahead.
