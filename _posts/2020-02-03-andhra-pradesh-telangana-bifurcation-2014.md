The Andhra Pradesh Telangana bifurcation, effected on 2 June 2014 by the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, is usually explained in one of two ways. The first explanation is a movement story: a region that believed itself wronged for half a century organised, marched, struck and fasted until the Union conceded a state. The second is an arithmetic story: a Congress leadership in New Delhi, facing electoral ruin in 2013, calculated that conceding Telangana would rescue its prospects in one region even at the price of the other. Both stories record real events, and both miss the structure that gave those events their force. The bifurcation is better read as a constitutional completion. The unified Andhra Pradesh created on 1 November 1956 was assembled over the recorded misgivings of one of its constituent regions, under written safeguards that the unified state's politics then failed to honour; the Act of 2014 closed that unfinished transaction, and the two states that emerged have since developed along visibly divergent political trajectories that the joint architecture had suppressed for fifty-eight years. That is the claim defended here.

## The Unfinished Merger of 1956

The antecedent sequence is exact, and its exactness matters to the argument. The Telugu-speaking districts of the Madras Presidency obtained a separate Andhra State on 1 October 1953, after the Gandhian volunteer Potti Sreeramulu fasted for the demand and died on 15 December 1952, forcing Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru to announce the new state four days later. Andhra State, with its capital at Kurnool and Tanguturi Prakasam as chief minister, comprised the coastal districts and Rayalaseema, but it did not comprise Telangana, the Telugu-speaking region of the princely Hyderabad State that the Indian Army's Police Action of September 1948 had brought into the Union. Hyderabad State continued as a separate unit, governed from 1952 by an elected Congress ministry under Burgula Ramakrishna Rao, while the States Reorganisation Commission under Justice Fazl Ali, appointed on 29 December 1953, took evidence on whether the Telugu-speaking areas should be united at all.

The Commission's report, submitted on 30 September 1955, is the first document in the record that treats the merger as unfinished business. The Commission recorded the Telangana apprehension that in a united state the region's revenues, educational institutions and government employment would be absorbed by the more advanced coastal districts, and it proposed that Hyderabad State continue separately, with union reconsidered after the general elections expected around 1961 and then only if the Telangana legislature consented by a two-thirds majority. The recommendation was overridden by political decision. Nehru, who described the union at the time as a marriage that carried a possibility of divorce if the partnership failed, pressed for immediate integration, and the price of Telangana's consent was a written instrument: the Gentlemen's Agreement, signed in Delhi on 20 February 1956 by Burgula Ramakrishna Rao and K. V. Ranga Reddy for Telangana and by Bezawada Gopala Reddy and Neelam Sanjiva Reddy for Andhra. The States Reorganisation Act then took effect on 1 November 1956, and the deferred questions of that settlement, mapped in [the 1956 deferred-cases framework](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/), placed the Telugu merger at the head of the unfinished list.

The Gentlemen's Agreement deserves reading as a constitutional document in miniature, because the whole subsequent conflict consists of arguments about its clauses. It provided for a Telangana Regional Committee of the state legislature to oversee the region's development; it continued the Mulki rules, the residence-based employment protections inherited from Hyderabad State, as the governing law for public appointments in Telangana; it required that the sale of agricultural land in Telangana be subject to the Regional Committee's consent; it stipulated that Telangana's revenue surpluses be spent within Telangana; it reserved to Telangana students their existing educational facilities and a proportionate share of admissions across the state; and it arranged the ministry so that the chief ministership and the deputy chief ministership would be held by men from different regions, with two of the five senior portfolios, among them Home, Finance and Revenue, assigned to Telangana. Read alongside [the politics of linguistic reorganisation](/2019/01/19/linguistic-reorganisation-constitutional-politics/), the agreement shows what the linguistic principle left unresolved: language supplied the boundary of the new state, but it could not equalise the two regions that the boundary enclosed, and the equalisation had to be promised by contract instead.

The Telangana signatories' hesitation, and the protests that accompanied the merger in 1955 and 1956 in Hyderabad and Warangal, establish the point on which the architectural reading rests. The 1956 state was not a settlement that later soured; it was a conditional integration whose conditions were written down, dated and signed, and whose breach could therefore be documented clause by clause. Every subsequent phase of the Telangana question, the agitation of 1969, the dormant decades, the party founded in 2001 and the mobilisation of 2009 to 2014, consists of Telangana's political actors presenting the bill for that original document. K. Srinivasulu's Caste, Class and Social Articulation in Andhra Pradesh, published in 2002 and still the standard analytical treatment of the state's regional politics, locates the grievance in exactly this documentary ground: the regions entered the union with divergent social articulations, and the safeguards were the mechanism by which the weaker articulation was to be protected inside a single state until convergence arrived. Convergence never arrived, and the safeguards were the first casualty.

## The Safeguards and Their Erosion, 1956 to 1969

The erosion was administrative before it was political, and it proceeded through the three channels the Gentlemen's Agreement had singled out for protection: employment, money and water. In employment, the Mulki rules required fifteen years of residence in Telangana for appointment to most public posts in the region, yet Andhra officers and teachers were appointed to Telangana posts in large numbers on the argument of administrative necessity, and the Telangana Regional Committee, constituted in 1958, recorded its objections without possessing the power to reverse a single appointment. In the state's services the result was a standing anomaly that every Telangana schoolteacher and clerk could verify from personal knowledge: posts reserved by law for residents of the region were held, in thousands of documented cases, by men recruited from outside it.

In money, the grievance acquired a number. The committee under Justice Vashishth Bhargava, appointed by the Union government in 1969 to examine the disposition of Telangana's revenues, found that funds belonging to the region had been spent elsewhere in the state. The economist C. H. Hanumantha Rao, working from the committee's figures, computed the Telangana surplus diverted between 1956 and 1968, with accumulated interest, at a sum that entered the movement's literature as the measure of the breach.

### How much Telangana revenue did the Bhargava Committee find diverted?

The Bhargava Committee reported that 28.3 crore rupees of Telangana's revenue surplus had been diverted to expenditure outside the region between 1956 and 1968. Hanumantha Rao, extending the committee's own accounts with interest, put the cumulative Telangana surplus absorbed by the rest of the state at 117.4 crore rupees by 1969.

That figure entered the agitation's literature as documentary proof that the safeguards had failed, quoted wherever the movement stated its case in numbers.

Water completed the triad. The Krishna and Godavari rivers cross Telangana before they reach the coastal delta, and the unified state's irrigation investment flowed disproportionately to the delta's assured ayacut and to projects, among them the Nagarjuna Sagar dam sanctioned in 1955 and built across the following two decades, whose command areas and employment benefits were distributed in ways Telangana's representatives contested from the first plan period onward. Telangana's own tank irrigation, the chain of rain-fed reservoirs built under the Nizams, decayed as maintenance funds followed the canal systems. The grievance structure that K. Srinivasulu anatomised, and that Padmaja Shaw's writings on the movement later chronicled from within, was therefore never a sentiment in search of a cause. It was a ledger: jobs promised and not delivered, surpluses assigned and spent elsewhere, river water allocated by a legislature in which Telangana held a permanent minority of seats. A legislature so composed could not correct the ledger, because the majority that benefited from each line of it was returned by the other two regions. That structural fact, a region locked into minority status inside the very institution charged with protecting it, is the hinge between 1956 and everything that followed.

## Stage One: The 1969 Agitation and the Formula That Contained It

The first mobilisation arrived when the safeguards were due to lapse. The Mulki protections and the regional arrangements of 1956 had been framed for an initial period, and as 1969 approached, students at Osmania University in Hyderabad began agitating in January of that year for the safeguards to be enforced rather than retired. The immediate sparks were appointments: junior lecturer posts and government positions in Hyderabad filled in disregard of the residence rules. An all-party accord of 19 January 1969, promising the removal of non-local appointees from Telangana posts and the application of Telangana's surpluses within the region, failed to quiet the campuses. The agitation spread from Hyderabad to Warangal and the district towns, government employees threatened direct action in support of the students, and the demand hardened from enforcement of the safeguards into separation itself, the demand its adherents called Jai Telangana.

The state's answer combined concession and firing. Police firing on processions through the spring and summer of 1969 killed 369 people, most of them students, a toll commemorated at the Gun Park memorial near the Public Gardens in Hyderabad. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, whose Congress government in the state under Chief Minister Kasu Brahmananda Reddy was losing control of the streets, announced an Eight-Point Formula on 12 April 1969: committees to examine Telangana's grievances on surpluses, employment and plan implementation, a development board for the region, and a committee of jurists on the safeguards. The formula conceded the ledger's existence while refusing the separation demanded on its basis. Telangana's political organisation followed within weeks. Defectors from the Congress under Marri Chenna Reddy organised the Telangana Praja Samithi in 1969 to carry the statehood demand into elections, converting a student agitation into a party with a single item on its programme.

The Praja Samithi's electoral proof came in the parliamentary election of March 1971, when it won 10 of the 14 Lok Sabha seats in Telangana even as Indira Gandhi's Congress (R) swept the rest of India on the Garibi Hatao platform. The result demonstrated the demand's depth and its limit in the same count: Telangana could elect a separatist party, and the Union could still decline to listen, because the decision rested in New Delhi rather than in Hyderabad. In September 1971 the Praja Samithi merged into the Congress after negotiations that traded the party's dissolution for fresh protective commitments, and on 30 September 1971 P. V. Narasimha Rao, a Telangana Congressman from Karimnagar, became the state's first chief minister from the region. The merger is the first appearance of a pattern the whole history repeats: a Telangana mobilisation rises until it threatens the ruling party's arithmetic, and is then bought off with a formula that restates the safeguards in stronger language while leaving the unified state's structure untouched.

The sequel proved the pattern's cost. When the Supreme Court's affirmation of the Mulki rules in 1972 threatened the positions of Andhra employees in Hyderabad, the coastal region answered with its own separatist mobilisation, the Jai Andhra movement of 1972 and 1973, demanding a separate Andhra state if the residence rules were to be enforced in the capital. The unified state thus produced mirror-image secessionisms within four years, each region mobilising against the safeguards the other required. New Delhi's resolution was the Six-Point Formula of 21 September 1973, agreed between the Union government and leaders of both regions. It abolished the Mulki rules and the Telangana Regional Committee, substituted a system of zonal recruitment and regional development boards, accelerated the development of Hyderabad, and was entrenched by the Constitution (Thirty-second Amendment) Act of 1973, which inserted Article 371D into the Constitution and enabled the Presidential Order of 1975 that reorganised state employment into local cadres. The 1973 settlement is conventionally narrated as the Telangana question's resolution. In the architectural reading it is something else: the moment the 1956 contract was formally superseded by a weaker instrument. The Gentlemen's Agreement had protected Telangana as a region with a veto-bearing committee and a domicile law; the Six-Point Formula protected Telanganites as a category of job-seekers within zonal quotas, while dissolving the regional institution that could have policed the wider ledger of funds and water. What was traded away in 1973 was not a clause but the enforcement machinery itself.

## Stage Two: A Grievance Without a Vehicle, 1973 to 2001

Between the Six-Point Formula and the founding of the Telangana Rashtra Samithi in 2001, the grievance persisted and the mobilisation did not, and the contrast between the two facts is what the movement-framing of the bifurcation cannot explain. If sentiment alone produced statehood, Telangana would have been conceded in 1969 or in the decades of documented complaint that followed. What the intervening decades lacked was not grievance but an organisation willing to make statehood its sole programme and a national conjuncture in which that organisation's support was worth purchasing.

The documentary trail of the grievance through these decades is unbroken. The Presidential Order of 1975 divided state employment into local cadres, yet orders issued in its name were evaded wholesale in Hyderabad, where posts in the state secretariat and the corporations were filled as if the capital's location in Telangana imposed no local claim. The state government of N. T. Rama Rao, the Telugu Desam Party founder who had swept to power in 1983 on a platform of Telugu self-respect, issued Government Order 610 in December 1985, directing that non-local employees appointed to Telangana posts in violation of the Order be repatriated to their own regions by March 1986. The deadline passed with the transfers unexecuted. Successive governments renewed the promise; the one-man commission under J. M. Girglani, appointed in 2001 to examine the Order's implementation, documented continuing violations on a large scale. A grievance that survives the change of every ruling party, Congress and Telugu Desam alike, and the personal attention of chief ministers from both regions, has ceased to be a complaint about any government. It is a property of the structure, which is what the architectural reading claims and what K. Srinivasulu's 2002 analysis of the state's divergent regional articulations had already established from the data on employment, irrigation and educational access.

The state's party politics in these decades explains the missing vehicle. The Telugu Desam, founded in 1982, drew its cadre and its vote from all three regions and had no interest in a demand that would halve its field; its founder's son-in-law and successor, N. Chandrababu Naidu, who took the party and the chief ministership in 1995, built his reputation on Hyderabad's growth as a technology hub, an achievement that deepened rather than soothed the Telangana complaint, since the new economy's jobs and land appreciations appeared to flow to coastal capital and coastal migrants. The Congress, the other statewide party, treated Telangana as a factional question to be managed through ministry-making. Within this duopoly a single-issue statehood party had no room, and the demand survived instead in the interstices: in the cultural assertion of the Telangana dialect and its writers, in the songs of the balladeer Gaddar and the performance traditions that the later mobilisation would weaponise, in the Telangana Information Trust's documentation of the resource ledger, and in small forums of intellectuals and students who kept the 1956 file open. The comparative essays of A. R. Vasavi on the divergent political trajectories of the southern states capture the configuration exactly: two regions sharing a language and a state, diverging in economy and in political temper, with the divergence itself becoming the grievance's proof. By the turn of the century every element of 1969 was present except the vehicle and the moment. Both arrived within three years of each other, in 2001 and in 2009.

## Stage Three: A Party Built for One Purpose, 2001 to 2009

The vehicle arrived on 27 April 2001, when K. Chandrashekar Rao resigned from the Telugu Desam Party, in which he had served as deputy speaker of the state assembly, and founded the Telangana Rashtra Samithi at a meeting in the Hyderabad residence known as Jala Drushyam. Rao's argument was the architectural one in party form. Every safeguard since 1956 had failed, he held, not because its text was weak but because no party dependent on votes in all three regions would ever enforce a text against two of them; only a party that existed for Telangana alone, and could trade its parliamentary support for statehood alone, could close the transaction. The Samithi's early growth was rapid in the local bodies of the northern districts, and its test came in the elections of 2004, fought in alliance with a Congress that had written Telangana into its state platform and needed the Samithi's seats to dislodge Chandrababu Naidu.

### How many seats did the Telangana Rashtra Samithi win in 2004?

In the elections of April and May 2004 the Telangana Rashtra Samithi, contesting in alliance with the Congress, won 26 seats in the Andhra Pradesh Legislative Assembly and five seats in the Lok Sabha, and Rao entered the Union council of ministers.

The Samithi also entered the state government, and the United Progressive Alliance's Common Minimum Programme carried a promise to consider the Telangana demand, the first time a national coalition's programme had done so.

The alliance years demonstrated the party's dilemma and its discipline. The Congress government in Hyderabad under Y. S. Rajasekhara Reddy moved on every development theme except statehood, and the Samithi's ministers resigned from the Union government in August 2006 and from the state government in protest at the delay, converting office back into pressure. The party's 2004 performance belongs inside [the 2004 UPA verdict's three-state sectoral concentration](/2019/06/22/2004-upa-shock-verdict-explained/), in which a national result assembled from regional verdicts gave a small statehood party a leverage its vote share alone would never have commanded. Rao triggered a by-election in his own Karimnagar Lok Sabha seat in December 2006 and won it by a large margin, reading the result as a referendum. When the party's legislators resigned en masse in 2008 to force by-elections on the issue, the verdict cut the other way: the Samithi retained only seven of sixteen assembly seats and two of four parliamentary seats, and Rao offered a resignation his party refused. In the general election of 2009, fought inside the Telugu Desam-led Grand Alliance, the Samithi fell to ten assembly seats and two in the Lok Sabha. By the autumn of 2009 the vehicle had been built, tested and apparently exhausted, and the statehood demand was weaker in seats than at any point since 2001. The architectural reading draws attention to what the arithmetic conceals: the party's defeats did not dissolve the ledger, and the Congress's own Telangana legislators, elected on a state manifesto that had promised sympathy to the demand, were restive under a high command that kept deferring it. The conjuncture required a trigger. Rao supplied it from his own body.

On 29 November 2009 Rao began a fast unto death, first in Karimnagar district and then, after his arrest and removal through Khammam, at the Nizam's Institute of Medical Sciences in Hyderabad. Student organisations at Osmania and Kakatiya universities turned the fast into a general mobilisation; suicides by young men who left notes dedicating their deaths to Telangana gave the movement its martyrology; and on the eleventh day, 9 December 2009, the Union home minister, P. Chidambaram, announced that the process of forming the state of Telangana would be initiated and an appropriate resolution moved in the state assembly. Rao broke his fast. Fourteen days later, on 23 December 2009, after legislators from the coastal districts and Rayalaseema had resigned in scores and Hyderabad's coastal investors had carried their alarm to New Delhi, Chidambaram issued a second statement: the situation had altered, and no step would be taken until consensus emerged from consultation with all parties. The Telangana Joint Action Committee, organised under the Osmania political scientist M. Kodandaram on 24 December 2009, held the movement together through the reversal, and the reversal itself taught the movement its decisive lesson, repeated in every account from Padmaja Shaw's movement chronicles to the Joint Action Committee's own literature: a promise extracted under pressure would be withdrawn when the pressure moved, so the pressure had to become permanent.

## The Hinge: A Death in the Nallamala Hills, 2009

The conjuncture of 2009 has a biographical core that neither rival reading handles well. The unified state's last effective defender was Y. S. Rajasekhara Reddy, the Congress chief minister who had won the undivided assembly in 2004 with 185 of its 294 seats and retained it in 2009 with 156. Rajasekhara Reddy's authority rested on a welfare architecture of free electricity to farmers, fee reimbursement for students, health insurance under Rajiv Arogyasri and a vast irrigation programme, Jalayagnam, that promised to still the water grievance by construction rather than by division. On Telangana he held the classical Congress position with uncommon personal force: the demand was a sentiment to be managed, the party's 2004 promise was a consultation to be prolonged, and the committee under Pranab Mukherjee that the party appointed in 2005 to build a consensus on the question was allowed to deliberate for the remainder of his life without reporting. As long as a chief minister of his weight governed the state and policed the party's Telangana wing, the demand's enforcing organisation faced a statewide counterweight that could concede nothing and survive.

On 2 September 2009 Rajasekhara Reddy died in a helicopter crash in the Nallamala hills, travelling to Chittoor district to launch a programme. The succession exposed the statewide party's fragility at the moment of the fast. K. Rosaiah, the finance minister who succeeded him, held the office without a faction of his own until November 2010; his successor, N. Kiran Kumar Reddy, was a Speaker elevated over stronger claimants, and both men governed a party already splitting along the line the bifurcation would take. The dead chief minister's son, Y. S. Jagan Mohan Reddy, was denied the succession, undertook a consolation tour of the districts against the high command's instructions, left the Congress in November 2010 and founded the YSR Congress Party in 2011, carrying the family's coastal and Rayalaseema following out of the ruling party. The anti-bifurcation position thereby lost what it had never before lacked: a single organisation capable of speaking for the two regions that opposed separation, with a leader capable of enforcing the position in Hyderabad and New Delhi alike. When Chidambaram made his statement of 9 December 2009, the resignations that reversed it within a fortnight came from legislators of parties whose leaderships could not coordinate a common price for relenting; when the Congress high command finally decided in 2013, the chief minister who led the residuary revolt, Kiran Kumar Reddy, did so as the head of a state unit whose strongest organisation had already seceded from the party. The architectural reading absorbs the contingency without strain. The contract of 1956 had awaited an enforcing organisation and a permissive conjuncture for half a century; the organisation was built between 2001 and 2009, and the conjuncture was opened, in part, by a helicopter's failure over the Nallamala hills. Contingency decides when such files close. It does not decide what they contain.

## Stage Four: From the Srikrishna Committee to the Appointed Day, 2010 to 2014

New Delhi's instrument for converting the crisis into a process was a committee. On 3 February 2010 the Union government appointed a five-member committee under Justice B. N. Srikrishna, a retired judge of the Supreme Court, to consult all sections of opinion in the state and report by the end of the year. The committee travelled the three regions, received thousands of memoranda, and submitted its report to the home ministry on 30 December 2010. The report, a document of some 461 pages in its main volume with further volumes of data, remains the most complete official anatomy of the 1956 settlement's failure, and its chapters on the accumulation of Telangana's grievances between 1956 and 2009, on the working of the safeguards and on the constitutional alternatives before the Union constitute the under-cited primary source on which this article's reading principally rests.

The committee's recommendations must be stated precisely, because both camps later misquoted them. The report laid out six options. It found the status quo, a Hyderabad union territory and the redrawing of the state into a Rayala-Telangana and a coastal unit all unworkable or unacceptable in the conditions it surveyed. Its preferred way forward, stated in terms, was to keep the state united while creating a statutory and empowered Telangana Regional Council with assured transfers of funds, functions and functionaries, expressly in keeping with the spirit of the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1956. Bifurcation, with Hyderabad as Telangana's capital and a new capital to be built for the residuary state, was its second option, recommended only if separation proved unavoidable and could be reached amicably among the three regions. A supplementary note on law and order, held back from the published volumes and later extracted by litigation in the Andhra Pradesh High Court, deepened the movement's suspicion that the exercise had been designed to defer. Telangana's parties rejected the preferred option outright: a regional council was, in the movement's reading, the Telangana Regional Committee of 1958 returning in statutory dress, an enforcement promise from the same institution whose non-enforcement was the complaint. The committee had, without intending it, restated the architectural case. Fifty-four years after the Gentlemen's Agreement, the official mind could still propose no remedy inside the unified state except the machinery the unified state had already failed to operate.

The movement's answer across 2011 was to make the state ungovernable on the question. The Million March of 10 March 2011 filled Hyderabad's Tank Bund; the Sakala Janula Samme, a general strike of government employees, teachers, transport workers and lawyers across Telangana, ran for forty-two days from 13 September 2011; the Telangana March on the Necklace Road followed on 30 September 2012. The Congress's own position moved under this pressure and under the pressure of by-elections its candidates kept losing in the region. On 30 July 2013 the Congress Working Committee resolved in favour of a separate Telangana, and on 3 October 2013 the Union cabinet approved the bifurcation, appointing a Group of Ministers under A. K. Antony to draft the bill. The Congress in 2013 still governed in the habits formed under [the 1991 minority-government-precedent](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), consulting, deferring and assembling consent in sequence; what distinguishes the Telangana file is that the sequence, this time, was run to its end. The bill was sent to the state legislature, as Article 3 of the Constitution requires for the legislature's views though not for its consent, and on 30 January 2014 the Andhra Pradesh assembly returned it rejected, on a resolution moved by the Congress chief minister, N. Kiran Kumar Reddy, against his own party's decision. Parliament proceeded regardless. The bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 13 February 2014 amid a pepper-spray attack by a coastal member, passed that house on 18 February with the Bharatiya Janata Party's support, and passed the Rajya Sabha on 20 February, where Prime Minister Manmohan Singh announced assurances to the residuary state: special category status for five years, the Polavaram project as a national project, and fiscal support for a new capital. President Pranab Mukherjee gave his assent on 1 March 2014. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, Act 6 of 2014, named 2 June 2014 as the appointed day on which Telangana would come into existence as the twenty-ninth state of the Union, and Kiran Kumar Reddy's resignation on 19 February left the undivided state under President's Rule for its final hundred days.

## The Assembly That Said No: Article 3 and the Limits of Consent

The winter of 2013 and 2014 staged the federation's founding question in its purest form: whether a state of the Union exists by the consent of its legislature or by the authority of Parliament. Article 3 of the Constitution empowers Parliament to form new states and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing ones, requiring only that the President refer the bill to the affected state legislature for its views within a period the President fixes, and that a bill affecting a state's representation receive the recommendation the Article prescribes. The views are solicited; they do not bind. The Andhra Pradesh legislature, given the bill in December 2013, used its six weeks to assemble the fullest record of dissent the procedure has produced: thousands of suggested amendments, a chamber repeatedly adjourned by its own members, and on 30 January 2014 a resolution, moved by the chief minister against the bill of his own party's government at the Centre, returning the bill rejected. Both houses of the state legislature recorded their opposition. Parliament's answer, delivered across February 2014, was the Constitution's answer. The Lok Sabha passed the bill on 18 February in a sitting whose live telecast was suspended amid disorder, a blackout that the residuary state's parties cite as the procedure's disgrace and the bill's supporters as its necessity; the Rajya Sabha passed it on 20 February with the Prime Minister's assurances attached. The legal challenges that followed, in the Supreme Court and the high court, obtained no stay and no invalidation, and the appointed day arrived on schedule.

The episode matters to the architectural reading for the asymmetry it exposed and then ratified. The consent that Article 3 does not require had been, in substance, the consent whose absence defined 1956: Telangana entered the unified state through a bargain struck by its leaders and overridden in its safeguard clauses by the very institution, a Parliament and an assembly dominated by the other regions, that the bargain was meant to restrain. In 2014 the institutional positions reversed. The region that had been merged without a mechanism of refusal obtained its state over the formal refusal of the legislature it was leaving, because the Constitution lodges the map in Parliament and the ledger's enforcement had at last reached Parliament in organised form. Defenders of the unified state in 2014 argued federal comity, the impropriety of dividing a state against its assembly's vote; the argument was honestly available and constitutionally beside the point, as the state's own creation had demonstrated from the other side. The completion thesis takes the symmetry as its confirmation. A settlement imposed by plenary power in 1956, on conditions the imposed-upon region recorded at the time, was unmade by the same plenary power in 2014, on the record the region had spent fifty-eight years compiling. The law never changed its answer about who decides. What changed was the evidence, and the organisation presenting it.

## The Movement's Repertoire: Making the Pressure Permanent

The Joint Action Committee's achievement between December 2009 and 2013 was organisational before it was theatrical. Under M. Kodandaram, a professor of political science at Osmania University, the Committee federated the movement's otherwise competing carriers: the employees' unions of the state government and the electricity and transport corporations, the teachers' and lawyers' associations of the Telangana districts, the students' joint action committees of Osmania, Kakatiya and the other universities, the cultural organisations that carried the demand into song, theatre and festival, and, at a calculating distance, the political parties themselves. The structure learned the lesson of 1971 in reverse. Where the Praja Samithi had been a party that a prime minister could dissolve by negotiation with its leader, the Committee was a federation whose constituents could not all be bought with the same formula, and whose programme, a separate state, admitted no intermediate instalment. Its methods were calibrated to demonstrate that the unified state's administration in Telangana ran on Telangana's consent. The non-cooperation movement of February and March 2011 withdrew that consent in the secretariat and the districts; the Million March of 10 March 2011 advertised the withdrawal to the country; the rail blockades and road blockades of the following months rehearsed it; and the Sakala Janula Samme of September and October 2011, forty-two days in which government offices, schools, buses and courts across the ten districts stopped work, proved that the federation could idle the state without capturing it. The movement's cultural front worked in the same direction, carrying the Bathukamma and Bonalu festivals, the Telangana dialect's literature and the songs of Gaddar and the balladeers of the armed-struggle era into a civic assertion that statehood was the recovery of a personality and not merely a boundary.

The electoral by-laws of the pressure were as disciplined as its street forms. When Telangana legislators of the Samithi resigned their seats to force by-elections on the statehood question, the party swept the twelve assembly by-elections of July 2010, winning eleven, and repeated the pattern in the by-elections of 2012, converting each vacancy into a plebiscite the Congress could neither win nor ignore. The Congress's Telangana ministers and legislators, caught between their high command's deferrals and their constituents' federation, began to resign in their turn, and the party's state unit spent the years from 2010 to 2013 in a rolling crisis of credibility in the region. This is the mechanism the architectural reading identifies and the arithmetic reading mistakes for its own handiwork. The Congress's 2013 decision was taken in New Delhi, and its timing owed everything to the party's electoral position; but the decision's direction had been fixed by a federation that had spent four years demonstrating that the status quo in Telangana was no longer a low-cost option for any government at the Centre. The movement did not draft the Act. It determined that there would be an Act, and it left the drafting, as this article has shown, to the file of 1956.

## The Srikrishna Evidence: The Ledger Audited

Because the committee's preferred remedy has dominated discussion of its report, the report's evidentiary chapters have been read less than they deserve, and they are the part of the document that a reader of the 1956 file most needs. The committee did what no government since 1956 had done: it took the ledger's three channels, employment, funds and water, and audited them against the undivided state's own statistics across five decades. On employment, it documented the composition of the state services and the public sector in the capital region, and the persistent shortfalls in Telangana's share of gazetted and non-gazetted posts relative to its population. On funds, it traced plan expenditure and revenue flows by region, rehearsing the line of inquiry that ran from the Bhargava Committee of 1969 through the Girglani Commission, and found the pattern of Telangana surpluses financing development elsewhere reproduced in modern forms. On water, it set out the created irrigation potential in the Krishna and Godavari basins by region, the figures on which Telangana's case had always rested: the state's major projects had delivered assured irrigation overwhelmingly to the coastal delta, while Telangana's share of the waters of the rivers that rise and run across its plateau remained, in the committee's own presentation, far below its basin share and its ayacut entitlement as the movement computed it.

The committee was equally careful on the points where the movement's case was weakest, and the care cuts in the architectural reading's favour rather than against it. It found that on several human development indicators, literacy, infant mortality, access to higher education, Telangana excluding Hyderabad compared unfavourably with coastal Andhra, while Hyderabad itself stood apart as an economy of all-India significance whose revenues and employment had been built by investment from every region and from outside the state altogether. It recorded that the capital's growth engine, the information technology economy constructed after 1995, drew its workforce from the whole country and its capital from the whole world, so that the question of to whom Hyderabad belonged could not be answered from the ledger alone. And it documented the anxieties of the coastal and Rayalaseema populations invested in the capital, the students in its colleges, the employees in its offices, the purchasers of its land, whose settlements a bifurcation would disturb and whom any just completion had to protect. This is precisely the material from which the Act of 2014 was drafted: the ten-year common capital, the Governor's special responsibility for the capital area, the decade of common admissions under Section 95 and the service protections of the transition all answer anxieties the committee catalogued, while the decision it declined to recommend answers the ledger it verified. The report's fate, its evidence absorbed into the statute and its remedy rejected by the statute's authors, is the cleanest demonstration the file affords that by 2013 the question before the Union was no longer what had happened inside the unified state. On that, the committee and the movement agreed in substance. The question was what machinery could end it, and on that the committee proposed, one final time, the machinery of 1956.

## Hyderabad: Capital, Prize and Condominium

Every thread of the long arc runs through one city. Hyderabad, founded in 1591 and the capital of the Nizam's dominions until 1948, was the administrative heart of Telangana and the prize whose possession organised the politics of both regions for the entire period. The States Reorganisation Commission had understood the city's weight: its misgivings about the merger were in large part misgivings about a capital in Telangana governing Andhra, and its proposal to defer union until Telangana's legislature consented was a proposal to let the capital question cool. The Gentlemen's Agreement took the opposite course, seating the unified state's government in Hyderabad at once and compensating Telangana with the safeguards. From 1956 the anomaly stood at the centre of the state: the coastal districts' legislators, officers and businessmen governed from a city in the region that had not wanted the union, and the city's growth under that dispensation became Exhibit A in both regions' cases. For Andhra's political class, Hyderabad's expansion proved the union's success; for Telangana's, the same expansion, financed substantially by the state's revenues and staffed substantially by coastal officers, employers and landlords, proved the union's extractive character. The city's land told the story in its sharpest form, as the villages around the old city were absorbed into an urban land market in which coastal capital and coastal political connections were widely believed, in Telangana's telling, to have taken the commanding positions.

The government of N. Chandrababu Naidu after 1995 raised the stakes by an order of magnitude. Naidu's project of Cyberabad, the technology district built around the HITEC City development on Hyderabad's western edge, converted the capital into the undivided state's growth engine and its claim on the world's attention, and it deepened every element of the Telangana complaint at once: the new economy's construction, services and land appreciation enriched the city's coastal investor class, its technology employment drew on national and coastal educational pipelines that Telangana's colleges did not feed, and the political credit for the miracle accrued to a chief minister from Rayalaseema governing a state whose name was Andhra Pradesh. The Srikrishna Committee's data gave the complaint its figures: by the end of the unified state's life, the Hyderabad metropolitan region accounted for a share of the state's revenues, its services output and its organised employment entirely out of proportion to its population, a primate city whose surplus the residuary state would lose and whose retention Telangana's movement regarded as the irreducible core of statehood. No Telangana settlement that surrendered Hyderabad was ever negotiable, and no Andhra settlement that surrendered it was ever painless; the state's politics from 2009 to 2014 is unintelligible without that symmetry.

The Act's answer was the condominium. Hyderabad would serve both states for ten years, under a Governor charged with the capital area's security, while the residuary state built a capital of its own and Telangana consolidated sole possession in June 2024. The decade worked as the Act intended in the narrow sense: the two secretariats separated, the residuary government shifted its working seat to the interim complex at Velagapudi in the Amaravati region from 2016, the separate high court opened at Amaravati on 1 January 2019, and on 2 June 2024 the common capital period expired by efflux of the statute's own term, leaving Hyderabad the capital of Telangana alone. The wider sense is the one the architectural reading presses. The condominium was the last clause of the 1956 transaction to be performed, the physical unwinding of a capital that a conditional merger had shared and a completed reorganisation finally assigned, and its quiet expiry, without agitation, without a formula and without a committee, is the measure of what changed in 2014. The city that had organised fifty-eight years of conflict between the regions ceased, on a date printed in a statute ten years earlier, to be a subject of conflict at all.

## The Architecture of the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act

The Act is a long document, and its length is the argument in statutory form. It runs to 108 sections arranged in twelve parts, with thirteen schedules, and nearly every provision answers a clause of the older unfinished transaction. Section 3 constitutes Telangana from ten districts of the undivided state: Adilabad, Karimnagar, Medak, Nizamabad, Warangal, Rangareddi, Nalgonda, Mahbubnagar, Khammam and Hyderabad. Section 4 leaves the remaining twenty-three districts to the residuary Andhra Pradesh. Section 5 makes Hyderabad the common capital of both states for a period not exceeding ten years, after which it belongs to Telangana alone; Section 8 gives the Governor, common to both states at the outset, a special responsibility for the security of life, liberty and property within the common capital area. The High Court of Judicature at Hyderabad was to serve both states until a separate high court for Andhra Pradesh could be constituted. The Act thereby created a decade in which the two states would be disentangled provision by provision, a statutory admission that a bifurcation is not an event but a process with a timetable, and it set the timetable's dates, 2019 for the separate high court and 2024 for the capital, as the milestones by which the completion would be measured.

### How many assembly seats did each successor state receive?

The Act allotted 175 seats in the Legislative Assembly to Andhra Pradesh and 119 to Telangana, 25 seats in the Lok Sabha to Andhra Pradesh and 17 to Telangana, and 11 seats in the Rajya Sabha to Andhra Pradesh against seven to Telangana.

Each state also received a Legislative Council, of fifty members for Andhra Pradesh and forty for Telangana, under amendments to the First and Fourth Schedules of the Constitution.

The financial architecture followed the population. The assets and liabilities of the undivided state, its public debt, its guarantees and its share of central taxes, were apportioned on the population ratio of 58.32 to 41.68 fixed by the 2011 census between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, with the assets of state-level institutions divided by committees and, where an institution sat in Hyderabad, by rules that deferred a large share of the quarrel to the schedules. The Act's later parts assign the services: All India Services officers were allotted between the two cadres by the Union government on the advice of an advisory committee under the retired civil servant Pratyush Sinha, and state-service employees were divided under guidelines framed by the committee chaired by C. R. Kamalanathan, with options invited from the employees themselves and the disputes that followed running through the high court for years. Part IX of the Act, on water, constitutes the Godavari and Krishna River Management Boards and an Apex Council under the Union water minister to supervise them. Section 90 declares the Polavaram irrigation project a national project, and an ordinance of 29 May 2014, later folded into the Act by amendment, transferred seven mandals of Khammam district, including parts of Bhadrachalam, to Andhra Pradesh so that the project's submergence zone would lie within one state's administration. Section 95 guarantees students of both states equal access to the undivided state's educational institutions for ten years. The document repays being read as an inventory rather than a summary: its schedules list the corporations, boards and training institutions whose futures the text assigns one line at a time, and a reader working through those schedules in an [office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) can watch the bifurcation proceed institution by institution, which is how, in practice, it proceeded.

The article's findable artifact, Table 1, the bifurcation architecture table, sets the long arc and the post-2014 trajectories in one citable frame: its first rows give the four stages by which the unfinished reorganisation of 1956 was completed, and its last rows give the differentiated state-political trajectories that the completed architecture enabled.

Two features of the Act distinguish it from the earlier reorganisation statutes with which Pillar 8's other cases invite comparison. The first is its candour about time. The Reorganisation Acts of 1956, 1960, 1966 and 2000 largely divided their states on the appointed day and left the consequences to administration; the Act of 2014 scheduled its own incompleteness, naming the years across which the capital, the court, the services and the student quotas would be separated, and thereby conceded in law what the architectural reading asserts in analysis, that the two regions' entanglement was the substance of the old state and could not be undone by a signature. The second is its treatment of the residuary state as a construction project. The Act and the Prime Minister's assurances of 20 February 2014 promised Andhra Pradesh a capital city, a national irrigation project, fiscal bridging and special category status, an inventory of compensations that made the residuary state's politics, for the decade that followed, a politics of claims against the Union's promissory notes. The bifurcation's completion would be measured in Hyderabad; the residuary state's grievance would be measured in Amaravati.

## The Division That Refused to Finish

The decade the Act had scheduled for disentanglement produced disputes in nearly every provision it had deferred, and the disputes' common form is instructive: each arose where the Act had substituted a committee or a future notification for a decision. The Ninth Schedule lists ninety-one state-level corporations and companies whose assets were to be divided, and the Tenth Schedule lists eighty-nine institutions, among them training academies and research bodies, that were to serve both states until apportioned. Years after the appointed day, a substantial share of both schedules remained undivided, with the division of Schedule IX institutions referred to a committee under the retired civil servant Sheela Bhide and the two governments disputing valuations, headquarters and the treatment of institutions physically located in Hyderabad. Employee allocation generated its own litigation, as officers and staff contested the cadre and district assignments made under the Kamalanathan guidelines, and the state services' final lists were still being corrected in court long after the All India Services cadres had been split. The Governor's special responsibility for Hyderabad under Section 8 produced friction between the two governments and the Raj Bhavan until the separate high court for Andhra Pradesh began functioning at Amaravati on 1 January 2019, five years into the ten-year condominium, leaving the common capital arrangement itself to run to its statutory end on 1 June 2024.

Water, the third channel of the original ledger, reproduced the whole argument in miniature. The Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal under Justice Brijesh Kumar, constituted in 2004 for the undivided state's quarrels with Karnataka and Maharashtra, had reported in 2010 with a further report in 2013, and the bifurcation converted its allocations into a two-state dispute overnight: Telangana, which had not been a party in its own name, demanded a fresh adjudication of the Krishna shares between the successor states, while Andhra Pradesh insisted the tribunal's award bound both. The Act's River Management Boards existed from 2 June 2014 but without notified jurisdiction, and the vacuum showed in June and July 2021, when Telangana's power utility, acting on a state government order to generate hydroelectric power at full capacity at Srisailam and Nagarjuna Sagar, released water that Andhra Pradesh protested was its drinking and irrigation share. The Union government answered by gazette notifications of 15 July 2021, effective 14 October 2021, that finally clothed the Krishna and Godavari boards with jurisdiction over the listed projects in both basins, thirty-five in the Krishna basin and seventy-one in the Godavari. The fiscal distribution told a parallel story: the residuary state's demand for the special category status promised on the floor of the Rajya Sabha in February 2014 was met in September 2016 by the finance minister, Arun Jaitley, with a package of special assistance measures announced as equivalent in value, a substitution the state's parties rejected in principle and its governments pursued in diluted forms thereafter. The pattern across assets, employees, water and money is the pattern of the whole article: the Act of 2014 had genuinely completed the reorganisation of territory and legislatures, and had deliberately left the reorganisation of the ledger to a decade of negotiation, so that the bifurcation's politics continued inside the two states long after the map had been settled.

Education carried the condominium into the lives of families who never read the Act. Section 95 continued the undivided state's admission regime in its professional colleges for ten years, so that a student from Vijayawada and a student from Warangal competed for the medical and engineering seats of both states under the quotas and local-candidate definitions inherited from the Presidential Order era. The arrangement was the 1956 safeguards' last surviving relative, a domicile protection scheduled to expire, and its expiry in 2024 returned the definition of a local candidate to each state's separate rules, with the predictable litigation following in the admission season of that year. The property settlement in the capital followed the same statutory clock. Through the common-capital decade the residuary state retained its allotted office buildings and guest houses in Hyderabad, an archipelago of Andhra Pradesh administration inside Telangana's capital; when the term expired on 2 June 2024 the Telangana government took possession of the buildings the Act had assigned for the decade, and the residuary state's physical presence in the city it had governed since 1956 ended with a change of locks rather than a negotiation. Observers of both states remarked on the absence of drama. That absence is the finding: provisions that would have been detonators in 1969, 2009 or 2013 executed themselves in 2024 as routine administration, because the architecture that contained them had been consented to in the only forum whose consent the Constitution requires, and because the grievance that would once have organised around them had been answered in the document itself.

## Telangana's Trajectory: The Statehood Party in Office, 2014 to 2024

Telangana's first election was held alongside the undivided state's final one, in April and May 2014, and it converted the movement's organisation into a government. The Telangana Rashtra Samithi won 63 of the new assembly's 119 seats and 11 of the state's 17 Lok Sabha seats, and on 2 June 2014 K. Chandrashekar Rao took office as the first chief minister of the state his party had been founded to create. The government he built across the following nine and a half years displayed the first differentiated trajectory in its pure form: a politics organised around the vindication of statehood itself. Its programmes were framed as restitution for the ledger. Mission Bhagiratha promised piped drinking water to every household in the state, answering the old charge that Telangana's water had served other regions; the restoration of minor irrigation tanks under Mission Kakatiya answered the decayed tank economy of the unified decades; Rythu Bandhu, launched in May 2018, paid investment support directly to landowners, an income transfer on a scale no Indian state had attempted at the time; and the Kaleshwaram Lift Irrigation Project on the Godavari, inaugurated on 21 June 2019, was presented as the region's reclamation of its river, a multi-stage lift scheme of a size the unified state, in the government's telling, would never have sanctioned for Telangana.

The trajectory's second feature was the conversion of a movement party into an electoral machine with the statehood dividend at its centre. Rao dissolved the assembly early and won the election of December 2018 with 88 of 119 seats, crushing a Congress-led alliance; the party's organisation absorbed defectors from the opposition until the assembly's arithmetic resembled the Praja Samithi's old nightmare inverted, a Telangana legislature in which Telangana's grievance had no remaining institutional expression because the grievance's party owned the institution. In October 2022 the party renamed itself the Bharat Rashtra Samithi, announcing national ambitions that repositioned its leader from a regional founder to a would-be national alternative. The repositioning coincided with the trajectory's turn. The state's borrowing for its welfare and irrigation programmes had grown into an opposition theme on debt; the Kaleshwaram project, the vindication's monument, suffered the sinking of piers at its Medigadda barrage in October 2023, weeks before the election, and the Congress under A. Revanth Reddy campaigned on six guarantees of welfare delivery joined to an indictment of the founding family's enrichment. The verdict of December 2023 gave the Congress 64 seats, the Bharat Rashtra Samithi 39 and the Bharatiya Janata Party eight, and Revanth Reddy took office on 7 December 2023. In the Lok Sabha election of 2024 the Congress and the Bharatiya Janata Party took eight seats each of the state's seventeen, the All India Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen retaining Hyderabad. The sequence matters more than any single result: within a decade of statehood, Telangana had acquired the ordinary furniture of a competitive state system, alternation between two parties, a national party's beachhead, elections fought on debt, guarantees and a damaged barrage, which is to say it had acquired a politics whose content was Telangana's own. Nothing in that politics required the consent, the arithmetic or the grievance of a neighbouring region. That autonomy, not any single verdict, is the differentiated trajectory the bifurcation enabled.

## Andhra Pradesh's Trajectory: The Politics of the Promissory Note, 2014 to 2024

The residuary state's trajectory ran on the opposite fuel. Where Telangana's politics celebrated a completed transaction, Andhra Pradesh's organised itself around the compensations the transaction had promised and the capital it had lost. The Telugu Desam Party of N. Chandrababu Naidu won 102 of the residuary assembly's 175 seats in 2014, and Naidu, sworn in on 8 June 2014, made the construction of a new capital the state's defining project. The Andhra Pradesh Capital Region Development Authority Act of December 2014 created the machinery; a land-pooling scheme assembled some 33,000 acres from the farmers of twenty-nine villages between Vijayawada and Guntur; and on 22 October 2015 the Prime Minister, Narendra Modi, laid the foundation stone of Amaravati, a greenfield capital whose master plan was drawn with Singapore's assistance and whose very name, borrowed from the ancient Satavahana-era city nearby, asserted that the residuary state would answer its truncation with a foundation myth of its own. Naidu's government simultaneously pursued the Polavaram project as the state's second claim on the Union, and pressed the special category demand at every forum, until the Union's September 2016 substitution of special assistance for special status converted the demand into a standing indictment his successors inherited.

The verdict of 2019, which gave Y. S. Jagan Mohan Reddy's YSR Congress 151 of 175 seats and reduced the Telugu Desam to 23, turned on welfare promises and anti-incumbency rather than on the capital, but the new government promptly made the capital its own battlefield. Holding that a single greenfield city concentrated the state's investment unjustly, Jagan Mohan Reddy's administration enacted the Andhra Pradesh Decentralisation and Inclusive Development of All Regions Act in July 2020, distributing the state's functions among three capitals: an executive capital at Visakhapatnam, the legislative capital at Amaravati and a judicial capital at Kurnool. The farmers who had pooled their land for one capital, and who had been promised developed plots in it, answered with a protest movement sustained across years, including long marches that carried the Amaravati cause the length of the state in 2021 and 2022. The state legislature repealed the three-capital Act in November 2021, and on 3 March 2022 a division bench of the Andhra Pradesh High Court directed that Amaravati be developed as the capital in accordance with the original statute and its timelines, a judgment the state government carried in appeal to the Supreme Court. The capital question had by then become the residuary trajectory's organising fact: every party's position on Amaravati defined its position on the bifurcation's fairness, and the Union's promissory notes, on the capital, on Polavaram, on special status, functioned as the state's opposition could use them, as evidence that 2014 remained an open account.

### What majority did the TDP-led alliance win in Andhra Pradesh in 2024?

The alliance of the Telugu Desam Party, the Jana Sena and the Bharatiya Janata Party won 164 of the 175 assembly seats in the election of May 2024: 135 for the Telugu Desam, 21 for the Jana Sena and eight for the Bharatiya Janata Party, with the YSR Congress reduced to eleven.

N. Chandrababu Naidu took office as chief minister for the fourth time on 12 June 2024, restoring the alliance arithmetic that had governed the residuary state in its first five years.

The verdict of 2024 and its aftermath closed the decade's symmetry. The returning government recommitted the state to Amaravati as its sole capital, the Union budget of July 2024 arranged financing of 15,000 crore rupees for the capital's development through multilateral agencies, and the Polavaram project resumed its place at the head of the state's claims on New Delhi. Read beside Telangana's trajectory, the residuary state's first decade shows the second half of the article's claim. The bifurcation had not merely permitted two governments to differ; it had assigned each state a distinct political question. Telangana's question was what a statehood movement does with power after the state exists, and its elections answered in the vocabulary of welfare delivery, debt and alternation. Andhra Pradesh's question was what a truncated state is owed, and its elections answered in the vocabulary of capitals, projects and promissory notes. Inside the unified state, neither question could be posed in this form: Telangana's restitution claims would have been one region's demands on a legislature it could not control, and Andhra's capital claim would have had no object, since the state's capital stood in Hyderabad and its loss was the price the old architecture had hidden. The differentiated trajectories are therefore not decoration on the completion thesis. They are its evidence, the visible operation of two political systems that the joint architecture had constrained into a single, permanently unbalanced one.


The Jagan Mohan Reddy government's own architecture, the Navaratnalu or nine jewels of welfare promises on which it had been elected, paid pensions, housing, farm support and school assistance directly to households on a scale that reordered the state's budget around transfers, and its defenders argued that a residuary state owed its people visible compensation in welfare for the assets it had lost in Hyderabad. Its critics, in the Telugu Desam and in the Union's finance establishment, answered with the debt ledger: the state's borrowings, on and off the budget, had grown across the five years to levels that constrained every other claim, and the capital's abandonment had in their telling converted the bifurcation's compensation into current consumption. The election of 2024 was fought substantially on that exchange, between a welfare record and a capital-and-debt indictment, and the verdict's scale suggests the indictment prevailed in the form the completion thesis would predict: the residuary state's electors judged their government by what the bifurcation had promised them and what of it remained undelivered.

## The Two States in the Union After 2024

The decade of disentanglement closed with a reversal that no participant in 2014 predicted: the residuary state's governing party became indispensable in New Delhi. The Telugu Desam's sixteen Lok Sabha seats in 2024 made it the second-largest constituent of the National Democratic Alliance government formed that June, and Chandrababu Naidu's support, with the Jana Sena's two seats beside it, stood between the Bharatiya Janata Party and a minority. The promissory notes of 2014 were presented for payment within weeks. The Union budget of July 2024 committed the Centre to arranging 15,000 crore rupees for Amaravati's development through multilateral agencies, recorded the Polavaram project as a national obligation whose completion the Centre would underwrite, and rehearsed the Reorganisation Act's promises as the coalition's programme. The architectural reading notes the mechanism without embarrassment: the Act had made the residuary state a creditor of the Union, and the arithmetic of 2024 made the debt collectable. The same statute that Telangana reads as its completion certificate, Andhra Pradesh reads as a schedule of receivables, and both readings were being honoured in the same budget speech.

Telangana's commerce with the Union ran on different paper. The Congress government that took office in December 2023 inherited a debt stock its campaign had indicted and a welfare structure its six guarantees extended, and its contests with New Delhi concerned clearances and shares rather than the Act's receivables: Krishna water, where the state's demand for an equal division of the river's waters between the two successor states went to the Brijesh Kumar tribunal under fresh terms of reference that the Union cabinet approved in October 2023, and the state's claim that the tribunal's original award, framed for an undivided Andhra Pradesh in dispute with Karnataka and Maharashtra, could not bind a state that had not been a party to it. The tribunal question is the ledger's last open channel, and its posture is instructive. In 1956 the distribution of the Krishna's waters among regions was an internal matter of one state's plan documents, settled by the majority the coastal districts commanded. After 2014 the same water is the subject of an inter-state adjudication in which Telangana appears as a sovereign party to the dispute, with standing the unified state's constitution of politics had denied it. Whatever the tribunal finally awards, the forum itself registers the completion: the region that petitioned committees in 1969 and 2010 litigates as a state.

The two trajectories' convergence in the Union's forums closes the article's evidence. Telangana's politics after 2024 continued its internal alternation, its Congress government implementing the guarantees on which it was elected and its displaced founding party reorganising in opposition, a state system reproducing itself by its own logic. Andhra Pradesh's politics continued to be arbitrated by its claims on the Centre, its capital rising on multilateral credit its coalition weight had secured, its opposition measuring the government against the same promissory inventory. A federation's map is sometimes described as the residue of its history; the Telugu case suggests the sharper formulation, that the map is the history, still at work. The unfinished transaction of 1956 governed the politics of two regions for fifty-eight years because it was written down and never performed. Its performance, completed between 2014 and 2024, governs two states, and the governing is visible precisely in how differently it governs each.

## The Precedent: Statehood Demands After 2014

A completed reorganisation rearranges the expectations of every pending one, and the demands that pressed against the Indian Union after 2014 all conducted their arguments in the Telangana case's vocabulary. The Gorkhaland demand in the Darjeeling hills, older than Telangana's in its organised form, had been settled provisionally by the Gorkhaland Territorial Administration agreement of 2011, and the news of Telangana's concession in July 2013 immediately revived the statehood agitation there; the hills' parties read the Congress Working Committee's resolution as proof that sustained mobilisation, timed to a national party's need, could still move Article 3. The Vidarbha demand in Maharashtra, resting on grievances that include the same irrigation and fund-diversion ledger as Telangana's, acquired new documentation and new advocates after 2014, though it confronted a structural obstacle Telangana had not faced: the Bharatiya Janata Party, the region's dominant party in the state, held statehood as a manifesto promise it was unwilling to redeem against the opposition of its own Maharashtra unit, and the demand's advocates cited Telangana to show what a ruling party's promise is worth when redemption is deferred. The Bodoland demand in Assam travelled the opposite road to a settlement short of statehood: the Bodo accord of 27 January 2020 created an enhanced Bodoland Territorial Region with wider powers, and its negotiators on both sides treated Telangana as the cautionary outer limit, the case that showed how a territorial demand, once armed with a single-purpose organisation and a national conjuncture, does not stop at councils.

The comparison set widened by the 2014 Act also includes the reorganisations the Act's own authors had in their files. The divisions of 2000, which created Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand and Uttarakhand by consensus among the national parties and without a fast, a martyrology or a suspended telecast, showed that Article 3 could still operate as routine federal housekeeping where no region's ledger of grievance ran against the division itself; the bifurcation of Jammu and Kashmir in August 2019, dividing a state into two Union territories by the same parliamentary article, showed the instrument turned to a purpose its 1956 framers would not have recognised, its reading alongside [the J&K Reorganisation as later constitutional-architectural antecedent](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/) marking how far the architecture of reorganisation had travelled from the linguistic settlement. Against that range, the Telangana case's precedent value is precise. It is the case in which a state was divided against the declared wish of its own legislature, over the dissent of the majority region's representatives, on the strength of a grievance documented for fifty-eight years and a mobilisation that made deferral costlier than concession. Demands that can assemble those two elements, a ledger and a lever, cite it as a promise; demands that cannot, cite it as a reproach. Both citations confirm the case's centrality to [the constitutional design of Indian federalism](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/), in which the Union's power to redraw the map is plenary and its exercise is governed, in practice, by the politics of the unfinished file.

The comparative frame of Pillar 8 sharpens the point. Tamil Nadu's durable two-party alternation, examined as [the bipolar-stability comparator](/2019/12/26/tamil-nadu-dmk-aiadmk-system/), shows a regional system that achieved its settlement early and has reproduced it for half a century; the Telugu states show the opposite case, a regional system whose settlement was imposed incomplete in 1956 and which therefore kept generating politics, agitation in 1969, formulas in 1973, a party in 2001, a statute in 2014 and two divergent state systems thereafter, until the file was closed. The lesson for the comparative study of Indian states is that settlements are not events but conditions: a settlement whose terms are enforced reproduces itself quietly, and a settlement whose terms are evaded reproduces the conflict that produced it, at intervals set by the grievance's organisational opportunities. On the four dimensions of [the four-dimensional comparative framework](/2019/12/21/comparative-indian-state-political-settlements/), the Telugu case scores as the federation's clearest demonstration that the durability of a state settlement is a function of its enforcement machinery, not of the goodwill recorded at its signing.


The oldest ancestor of the Hyderabad condominium stands inside the federation's own case law of reorganisation. The Punjab Reorganisation Act of 1966 divided the bilingual Punjab of the 1956 settlement into a Punjabi-speaking Punjab and a Hindi-speaking Haryana, transferred the hill areas to Himachal Pradesh, and made Chandigarh a Union territory serving as the common capital of both successor states, an arrangement the Srikrishna Committee's second option expressly invoked as the model for a Hyderabad union territory. The comparison fixes the 2014 Act's distinctiveness by contrast. Chandigarh's condominium was made permanent and remains, six decades later, a standing item in Punjab and Haryana's quarrels, complete with an unexecuted transfer promise; Hyderabad's was given a term of ten years and a named heir, and it expired on schedule in 2024, its buildings changing hands by inventory. The drafters of 2014 had the Punjab file open before them, as the committee's report shows, and they chose the sunset over the perpetuity. On the architectural reading, that choice is the completion thesis in miniature: the earlier reorganisation had left its capital question open in perpetuity and reaped a permanent dispute, while the later one closed its capital question in the text, with a date, and the dispute died with the term. Settlements endure, the two cases together suggest, in proportion to the completeness with which their instruments perform themselves.

## The Andhra Pradesh Telangana Bifurcation and Its Three Readings

The three readings can be adjudicated on the record assembled. The movement reading, stated at its strongest by the Telangana Joint Action Committee's literature and by chroniclers of the mobilisation such as Padmaja Shaw, holds that statehood was won by the region's people against the unified resistance of the coastal establishment and its own state's machinery: students who courted expulsion and firing in 1969 and again after 2009, employees who struck for forty-two days in 2011, a political class in Telangana that resigned, fasted and marched until the Union moved. The reading is true in everything it touches, and insufficient in what it omits. It cannot explain why the mobilisation of 1969, which cost 369 lives and won ten parliamentary seats, obtained a formula while the mobilisation of 2009 to 2014 obtained a state; it cannot explain why the demand slept, as an organised force, through the decades in which its grievance was being freshly documented by government commissions; and it cannot explain the form of the concession, a statute whose schedules and timetables address the ledger of 1956 item by item, unless the document the movement was enforcing is placed at the centre of the account.

The arithmetic reading, stated at its strongest by the Congress's own strategists in retrospect and by the residuary state's parties ever since, holds that the bifurcation was a ruling party's electoral transaction: a Congress facing defeat in Andhra Pradesh after Y. S. Rajasekhara Reddy's death in 2009, and the rise of his son's breakaway party, salvaged Telangana for itself by conceding statehood, while the Bharatiya Janata Party supported the bill to enter both successor states. The reading captures the timing of the final decision, and the Congress Working Committee's resolution of 30 July 2013 is unintelligible without the party's position in the state. It fails on its own terms at the count. The Congress won no seat in the residuary assembly in 2014 and did not lead a government in Telangana until 2023, a decade after the concession; the transaction's supposed beneficiary in Telangana, the Samithi, had bargained for statehood and not for the Congress, and the party that harvested the statehood dividend was the one that had organised the demand. A calculation so consistently unrewarded is better described as the occasion of the bifurcation than as its cause, and the arithmetic reading, like the movement reading, is best understood as an account of the trigger mistaken for an account of the charge.

The constitutional-architectural reading defended in this article subsumes what is true in both. The 1956 integration was a conditional transaction whose conditions were written, signed and progressively breached in employment, in funds and in water, while the institution charged with policing the conditions was abolished in 1973 and its replacements were evaded. A political system carrying an unenforced contract of that age does not resolve it by goodwill; it resolves it when an organisation arises that can make the breach continuously expensive, which the Telangana Rashtra Samithi after 2001 and the Joint Action Committee after 2009 jointly achieved, and when a national conjuncture makes concession cheaper than continued deferral, which the Congress's position in 2013 supplied. Movement and arithmetic are the mechanism's moving parts. The architecture is what they moved. This reading also explains the two facts its rivals handle worst: the fifty-eight-year delay, which is the time required for the contract's breach to produce its enforcing organisation, and the post-2014 divergence, which is the contract's parties, released from a joint legislature neither could command for its own purposes, building the distinct political systems their substrates had always implied. Read through [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), the bifurcation stands as the Indian federation's most consequential renegotiation of the claims of region, language and development in the period the framework covers.

The strongest objection to this reading deserves its full force. The differentiated trajectories of the two states after 2014, the objection runs, may record nothing more than the ordinary variation of Indian state politics, which would have produced different governments in Hyderabad and Amaravati under any constitutional arrangement; to credit the bifurcation with the divergence is to over-attribute to architecture what belongs to the routine plurality of a large federation. The objection is answered by the constraint the unified state imposed and the Act removed. Before 2014, no Telangana restitution programme could be enacted, because the legislature that would enact it contained a permanent majority returned by the regions the programme taxed; no Andhra capital question could exist, because the capital stood in the other region and its status was precisely what could not be debated. The trajectories differ from ordinary state variation in their organising questions, restitution in one state and compensation in the other, and both questions are unintelligible except as consequences of the bifurcation's terms. Ordinary variation supplies the weather of the two states' politics after 2014; the architecture supplied the climate, and it is the climate that the completion thesis claims. The namable claim therefore stands as the brief for this article framed it and as the record supports it: the 2014 bifurcation completed an unfinished 1956 reorganisation, and the post-2014 architecture differentiates two state-political trajectories that the integration had constrained.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When was Telangana formed?

Telangana was formed on 2 June 2014, the appointed day named in the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, Act 6 of 2014, which had received the assent of President Pranab Mukherjee on 1 March 2014. On that date the ten Telangana districts of the undivided Andhra Pradesh, Adilabad, Karimnagar, Medak, Nizamabad, Warangal, Rangareddi, Nalgonda, Mahbubnagar, Khammam and Hyderabad, ceased to form part of Andhra Pradesh and constituted Telangana as the twenty-ninth state of the Indian Union. K. Chandrashekar Rao, whose Telangana Rashtra Samithi had won 63 of the new assembly's 119 seats in the elections held that spring, was sworn in as the state's first chief minister on the same day. The date closed a sequence that had run from the Congress Working Committee's resolution of 30 July 2013 through the Union cabinet's approval of 3 October 2013, the state legislature's rejection of the bill on 30 January 2014, and its passage by the Lok Sabha on 18 February and the Rajya Sabha on 20 February 2014. Hyderabad served from the appointed day as the common capital of Telangana and the residuary Andhra Pradesh, a shared status that expired on 2 June 2024, leaving the city the sole capital of Telangana.

### Q: What is the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act?

The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act is the Act of Parliament, Act 6 of 2014, by which the state of Andhra Pradesh was bifurcated into Telangana and the residuary Andhra Pradesh with effect from 2 June 2014. It runs to 108 sections in twelve parts with thirteen schedules. Its early sections constitute the two states and allot their parliamentary and assembly representation; Section 5 makes Hyderabad the common capital of both states for a period not exceeding ten years; Section 8 gives the common Governor a special responsibility for security within the common capital; and its middle parts divide the undivided state's assets, liabilities, public debt and services between the successors, principally on the population ratio of 58.32 to 41.68. Part IX constitutes the Godavari and Krishna River Management Boards for the two states' shared rivers, Section 90 declares the Polavaram irrigation project a national project, and Section 95 continues common admissions to educational institutions for ten years. The Ninth and Tenth Schedules list the state corporations and institutions whose division the Act schedules. The statute is the legal instrument that completed the reorganisation begun, and left unfinished, by the States Reorganisation Act of 1956.

### Q: Who is K. Chandrashekar Rao?

K. Chandrashekar Rao, widely known by his initials KCR, is the founder of the Telangana Rashtra Samithi and the first chief minister of Telangana. Born in 1954 in Chintamadaka in Medak district, he rose through the Telugu Desam Party, serving as a minister and as deputy speaker of the state assembly, before resigning in April 2001 to found the Samithi as a party devoted to the single aim of a separate Telangana state. The party he built won 26 assembly seats and five Lok Sabha seats in alliance with the Congress in 2004, and Rao served briefly as a Union minister before withdrawing over the statehood delay. His fast unto death of November and December 2009 forced the Union government's announcement of 9 December 2009 that the process of forming Telangana would begin, and his party's agitation carried the demand to the Act of 2014. Rao was chief minister of Telangana from 2 June 2014 until December 2023, winning a second term in 2018 with 88 of 119 seats; in October 2022 he renamed his party the Bharat Rashtra Samithi. His government lost the election of December 2023 to the Congress under A. Revanth Reddy.

### Q: Why was Andhra Pradesh divided?

Andhra Pradesh was divided because the settlement under which it had been created in 1956 failed on its own written terms. The Gentlemen's Agreement of 20 February 1956, signed as the price of merging the Telangana region of Hyderabad State with Andhra State, promised Telangana safeguards in employment, revenue expenditure, education and ministerial representation, policed by a Telangana Regional Committee. The safeguards were progressively breached; the committee was abolished by the Six-Point Formula of 1973; and Telangana's grievances over jobs, diverted revenue surpluses and irrigation water were documented by official inquiries from the Bhargava Committee of 1969 to the Girglani Commission of 2001 without remedy, because Telangana held a permanent minority in the legislature charged with correcting them. The agitation of 1969, which cost 369 lives, won formulas rather than enforcement. A single-purpose party, the Telangana Rashtra Samithi founded in 2001, and the mass mobilisation of 2009 to 2014 then made continued deferral costlier to the Union government than concession, and Parliament divided the state by the Reorganisation Act of 2014, completing the unfinished reorganisation of 1956.

### Q: What is the Gentlemen's Agreement?

The Gentlemen's Agreement is the written settlement signed in Delhi on 20 February 1956 by the leaders of Andhra State and the Telangana region of Hyderabad State, as the condition on which Telangana's representatives accepted the merger of the two units into a single Andhra Pradesh under the States Reorganisation Act. Burgula Ramakrishna Rao and K. V. Ranga Reddy signed for Telangana; Bezawada Gopala Reddy and Neelam Sanjiva Reddy signed for Andhra. The agreement promised Telangana a Regional Committee of the state legislature to oversee its development; the continuation of the Mulki rules, the residence-based protections governing public employment in the region; the expenditure of Telangana's revenue surpluses within Telangana; protection for Telangana students' educational facilities and a proportionate share of admissions; control by the Regional Committee over the sale of agricultural land in Telangana; and a ministry in which the chief minister and deputy chief minister came from different regions, with two of the five senior portfolios held by Telangana ministers. Its progressive breach, documented in employment, funds and water across the following decades, formed the evidentiary basis of the Telangana movement and of the eventual bifurcation of 2014.

### Q: Who governs Andhra Pradesh after the bifurcation?

Andhra Pradesh is governed by the coalition of the Telugu Desam Party, the Jana Sena Party and the Bharatiya Janata Party that won the assembly election of May 2024. The alliance took 164 of the assembly's 175 seats: 135 for the Telugu Desam, 21 for the Jana Sena and eight for the Bharatiya Janata Party, reducing the incumbent YSR Congress Party, which had governed the state from 2019 under Y. S. Jagan Mohan Reddy with 151 seats, to eleven. N. Chandrababu Naidu, the Telugu Desam leader, was sworn in as chief minister on 12 June 2024, his fourth tenure in the office across the undivided and residuary states, having previously governed the undivided Andhra Pradesh from 1995 to 2004 and the residuary state from 2014 to 2019. The Jana Sena leader Pawan Kalyan serves as deputy chief minister. The government's programme centres on completing Amaravati as the state's single capital, the project Naidu had founded in 2015 and the intervening administration had sought to replace with a three-capital arrangement, and on the Polavaram national project and the fiscal assurances recorded in the Reorganisation Act of 2014.

### Q: What was the Srikrishna Committee?

The Srikrishna Committee was the five-member committee appointed by the Union government on 3 February 2010, under the chairmanship of Justice B. N. Srikrishna, a retired judge of the Supreme Court, to examine the demand for a separate Telangana state and the counter-demand for a united Andhra Pradesh, and to recommend a course of action. Appointed in the crisis that followed the Union's announcement of 9 December 2009 in favour of statehood and its suspension a fortnight later, the committee toured the state's three regions, received memoranda from parties, associations and citizens in large numbers, and submitted its report to the Union home ministry on 30 December 2010. The report's main volume ran to some 461 pages, with further volumes of statistical annexures auditing the regions' claims on employment, revenue and irrigation. It set out six options for the state's future, found the status quo and several redrawing schemes unworkable, and recommended as its preferred way forward a united state with a statutory and empowered Telangana Regional Council, with bifurcation as its second option if separation proved unavoidable. Telangana's parties rejected the preferred option, and the report's evidence, rather than its remedy, became the bifurcation's documentary foundation.

### Q: What was the 1969 Telangana agitation?

The 1969 Telangana agitation was the first mass movement for a separate Telangana state, mounted in the thirteenth year of the unified Andhra Pradesh. It began in January 1969 among students at Osmania University in Hyderabad, protesting the filling of posts reserved for Telangana residents under the Mulki rules by appointees from the coastal districts, and the threatened lapse of the safeguards promised in the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1956. Government employees threatened direct action in support, an all-party accord of 19 January 1969 failed to restore calm, and the demand escalated from enforcement of the safeguards to separation itself under the slogan Jai Telangana. Police firing on processions through 1969 killed 369 people, most of them students, whose memorial stands at Gun Park in Hyderabad. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi answered with an Eight-Point Formula of development boards and grievance committees in April 1969, refusing separation. The movement organised politically as the Telangana Praja Samithi under Marri Chenna Reddy, which won ten of Telangana's fourteen Lok Sabha seats in 1971 before merging into the Congress that September in exchange for fresh safeguards, leaving the agitation's demands formally unmet.

### Q: What were the Mulki rules?

The Mulki rules were the residence-based employment protections that governed public appointments in Hyderabad State and, after 1956, in the Telangana region of Andhra Pradesh. Originating in a firman of the last Nizam, Mir Osman Ali Khan, in 1919, they defined a Mulki, a local person, principally by fifteen years of residence in the state, and reserved public posts for those who qualified. The Gentlemen's Agreement of February 1956 expressly continued the rules in Telangana as a safeguard of the merger, and the Andhra Pradesh Public Employment (Requirement as to Residence) Act of 1957 gave them statutory form for the region's posts. Their widespread evasion, with Telangana posts held by appointees who did not meet the residence test, stood at the head of the grievances that produced the 1969 agitation, and the Supreme Court's affirmation of the rules in the early 1970s provoked the counter-mobilisation of the Jai Andhra movement. The Six-Point Formula of September 1973 abolished the Mulki rules and replaced them with zonal recruitment under Article 371D of the Constitution and the Presidential Order of 1975, a substitution Telangana's movements later treated as the safeguards' formal dismantling.

### Q: Why did K. Chandrashekar Rao found the Telangana Rashtra Samithi in 2001?

K. Chandrashekar Rao founded the Telangana Rashtra Samithi on 27 April 2001 because he had concluded that no statewide party would ever deliver Telangana statehood. Rao had served in the Telugu Desam Party as a minister and as deputy speaker of the state assembly, and he resigned from it after being denied a place in Chandrababu Naidu's council of ministers, but the argument he carried into the founding meeting at the Hyderabad residence Jala Drushyam was structural rather than personal: every safeguard since 1956, the Gentlemen's Agreement, the Six-Point Formula and Government Order 610 among them, had failed for one reason, that the parties charged with enforcing them depended on votes in the coastal districts and Rayalaseema as much as in Telangana, and would not enforce a Telangana text against their own wider electorates. A party that existed for Telangana alone, he reasoned, could trade its support in Hyderabad and New Delhi for statehood alone, refusing every substitute of funds, formulas and councils. The Samithi organised on that single programme, grew first through the local body elections of the northern districts, and within three years had won the parliamentary leverage to test the theory in the alliance politics of 2004.

### Q: Why did the Union government announce a Telangana process in December 2009 and then pause it?

The Union government announced the Telangana process on 9 December 2009 because K. Chandrashekar Rao's fast unto death, begun on 29 November 2009, had carried the statehood movement into a crisis the government could not contain: students at Osmania and Kakatiya universities had turned the fast into a general mobilisation, deaths by suicide in the cause were being reported across the region, and the Congress's own Telangana legislators were pressing New Delhi to concede. Home Minister P. Chidambaram's statement that evening declared that the process of forming Telangana would be initiated and a resolution moved in the state assembly, and Rao broke his fast. The pause followed within a fortnight because the announcement detonated the other two regions: legislators from coastal Andhra and Rayalaseema resigned in scores, including members of the Congress, investors in Hyderabad carried their alarm to the capital, and the state government appeared to be disintegrating. On 23 December 2009 Chidambaram issued a second statement, that the situation had altered and that no step would follow until a consensus emerged from consultation with all parties. The government then appointed the Srikrishna Committee in February 2010, converting the crisis into a process.

### Q: For how long was Hyderabad the joint capital of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana?

Hyderabad was the joint capital of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana for ten years, from the appointed day of the bifurcation, 2 June 2014, until 1 June 2024. Section 5 of the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014 designated Hyderabad as the common capital of both successor states for a period not exceeding ten years, after which it would form part of Telangana alone. Section 8 of the Act gave the Governor, initially common to both states, a special responsibility for the security of life, liberty and property within the common capital area during the term. The arrangement wound down in stages inside the decade: the two states separated their secretariats and cadres, the residuary government of Andhra Pradesh shifted its working seat to the interim government complex at Velagapudi in the Amaravati capital region from 2016, and a separate High Court for Andhra Pradesh began functioning at Amaravati on 1 January 2019, ending the period in which the High Court of Judicature at Hyderabad served both states. At the term's expiry the Telangana government took possession of the office buildings in Hyderabad that the Act had allotted to the residuary state for the decade, and Hyderabad became Telangana's capital exclusively.

### Q: What is the Polavaram dispute between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana?

The Polavaram dispute concerns the multipurpose irrigation and hydroelectric project on the Godavari river at Polavaram in Andhra Pradesh, declared a national project by Section 90 of the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act 2014, with its cost to be borne by the Union government. The project, centred on a dam with a large reservoir, required the submergence of villages lying in Telangana's Khammam district, and in May 2014, days before the appointed day, the Union government promulgated an ordinance transferring seven mandals of Khammam district, including parts of the Bhadrachalam division, to Andhra Pradesh so that the project's submergence zone and its displaced settlements would lie within a single state's administration; the ordinance was afterwards folded into the Act by amendment. Telangana's government and parties protested the transfer as a taking of the new state's territory without its legislature's consent, and contested the project's design, submergence and flood calculations in the years that followed. Andhra Pradesh treated the transfer and the national project status as part of the bifurcation's settlement, the compensation architecture for the loss of Hyderabad, and pressed the Union to complete the project and its rehabilitation colonies.

### Q: What is the Krishna river water dispute between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana?

The Krishna river water dispute is the quarrel between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana over the division of the Krishna's waters, inherited from the undivided state's allocations and reopened by the bifurcation. The Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal under Justice Brijesh Kumar, constituted in 2004 for the claims of Maharashtra, Karnataka and undivided Andhra Pradesh, delivered its award in 2010 and a further report in 2013, assigning the undivided state a share under project-wise allocations. Telangana, which had not appeared before the tribunal as a state, demanded that the award be reopened and the waters divided afresh between the successor states, claiming an equal share on the basis of its basin area and its historical under-irrigation; Andhra Pradesh held that the award bound both states and that its projects and Rayalaseema's supplies rested on it. The Reorganisation Act constituted a Krishna River Management Board for the shared projects, and the Union government notified the board's jurisdiction in 2021 after disputes over releases from Srisailam and Nagarjuna Sagar. In October 2023 the Union cabinet approved fresh terms of reference for the Brijesh Kumar tribunal to adjudicate the two states' competing claims.
### Q: Why does Andhra Pradesh demand special category status?

Andhra Pradesh demands special category status because the promise of it formed part of the terms on which the bifurcation was carried through Parliament. On 20 February 2014, as the Reorganisation Bill passed the Rajya Sabha, Prime Minister Manmohan Singh assured the House that special category status, carrying central assistance and tax concessions, would be extended to the residuary state for five years, alongside the Polavaram national project and support for a new capital. The assurance does not appear in the Act's text, and its implementation faltered: the Fourteenth Finance Commission's tax devolution to all states was held by the Union government to have overtaken the category, and in September 2016 the finance minister, Arun Jaitley, announced special assistance measures in place of the status, calculated as equivalent in value. Andhra Pradesh's parties rejected the substitution in principle. The Telugu Desam left the National Democratic Alliance in 2018 partly on the question; the YSR Congress campaigned on the demand in 2019 and pressed it in office; and the returning coalition government of 2024 raised it again with a Union government its seats sustained, treating the promise as an unpaid item of the 2014 settlement.
### Q: What happened to the institutions listed in the Ninth and Tenth Schedules of the Reorganisation Act?

The institutions listed in the Ninth and Tenth Schedules of the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act became the slowest-moving part of the bifurcation's division. The Ninth Schedule lists ninety-one state-level corporations and companies, among them transport, power, finance and industrial development corporations, whose assets, liabilities and staff were to be apportioned between the two states, principally on the population ratio. The Tenth Schedule lists eighty-nine institutions, training academies and research bodies concentrated in and around Hyderabad, which were to serve both states until their division could be settled. A decade after the appointed day, a substantial share of both schedules remained undivided. The division of the Schedule IX corporations was referred to a committee under the retired civil servant Sheela Bhide, and the two governments continued to dispute valuations, the treatment of headquarters assets located in Hyderabad, and the apportionment of the corporations' debts and pension liabilities, while several Schedule X institutions operated in an extended common regime and the education institutions among them were overtaken by the expiry of the Act's ten-year common admission provisions. The schedules' unfinished state is the standing evidence that the Act scheduled the hard divisions rather than completing them.
### Q: What was the Sakala Janula Samme?

The Sakala Janula Samme, the strike of all sections of the people, was the forty-two-day general strike conducted across Telangana from 13 September to 24 October 2011, the largest single action of the statehood movement's final phase. Organised by the Telangana Joint Action Committee under M. Kodandaram and the region's employees' associations, it withdrew the labour on which the undivided state ran in Telangana: government offices and state corporations closed, teachers left the schools, transport employees stopped the buses, lawyers boycotted the courts, and workers at the Singareni coal mines, which fed the state's power stations, joined the stoppage. The strike's purpose was demonstrative as much as economic. The Joint Action Committee sought to prove, to New Delhi and to the state's other regions, that administration in Telangana subsisted on the consent of the governed, and that a Union government which had announced statehood in December 2009 and then suspended it would face a region it could not administer. The strike ended without a concession, after the festival season and the government's refusal to negotiate under duress, but it fixed the movement's strategy for the remaining road to the Congress Working Committee's resolution of July 2013.
### Q: What is the Kaleshwaram Lift Irrigation Project?

The Kaleshwaram Lift Irrigation Project is the multi-stage lift irrigation scheme built by the Telangana government on the Godavari river, the flagship work of K. Chandrashekar Rao's administration and the centrepiece of its claim to have reversed the water grievance on which the state was founded. Inaugurated on 21 June 2019, the project lifts Godavari water from the Medigadda barrage near the Pranahita confluence and raises it through a chain of barrages, pump houses and reservoirs, among them Annaram and Sundilla, to irrigate the drought-prone uplands of northern and central Telangana that gravity canals from the state's older projects had never reached. Its scale made it a subject of national attention and of opposition attack on its cost, its borrowings and its design. In October 2023, weeks before the state election, piers of the Medigadda barrage sank and cracked, and the damage became an election issue, with the Congress charging haste and defective construction. The project remains the clearest physical expression of the bifurcation's first differentiated trajectory: a work of a size and orientation that Telangana's governments maintain the unified state, which directed its irrigation investment to the coastal delta, would never have sanctioned for the region.
### Q: Why did Andhra Pradesh propose three capitals?

Andhra Pradesh proposed three capitals under the government of Y. S. Jagan Mohan Reddy as a deliberate repudiation of the single-capital project it inherited. The Telugu Desam government of 2014 to 2019 had concentrated the residuary state's investment in Amaravati, a greenfield city assembled from 33,000 acres of pooled farmland between Vijayawada and Guntur, and Jagan Mohan Reddy's YSR Congress, elected in 2019, argued that the concentration repeated the error of Hyderabad: one city's growth at the other regions' expense, with the land's appreciation accruing to those who had known where the capital would rise. The Andhra Pradesh Decentralisation and Inclusive Development of All Regions Act of July 2020 answered with a distribution of the state's functions: an executive capital at Visakhapatnam, the legislative capital retained at Amaravati, and a judicial capital at Kurnool in Rayalaseema. The farmers who had pooled their land resisted the change in a protest movement sustained for years, and the state legislature repealed the three-capital Act in November 2021. On 3 March 2022 the Andhra Pradesh High Court directed that Amaravati be developed as the capital under the original statute, and the verdict of 2024 restored the single-capital programme under the returning Telugu Desam government.
### Q: How were All India Services officers allotted between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana?

The All India Services officers of the undivided Andhra Pradesh cadre, the Indian Administrative Service, Indian Police Service and Indian Forest Service, were allotted between the two successor states by the Union government under Part VIII of the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, on the advice of an advisory committee chaired by the retired civil servant Pratyush Sinha. The committee applied the Act's principles to a joint cadre of several hundred officers: the two states' entitlements were computed from their sanctioned strengths and population, officers were invited to express their preferences, and allotments were made balancing preference against the requirements of each cadre, with direct recruits of the bifurcation years assigned by the same formula. The allotment lists were published in stages across 2014 and 2015, and a number of officers carried their grievances to the Central Administrative Tribunal and the high court, contesting the committee's application of its guidelines on seniority, domicile and spouse cases. State-service employees, a far larger body, were divided separately under guidelines framed by the committee chaired by C. R. Kamalanathan, through options and provisional lists whose corrections ran in court for years after the All India Services division closed.
