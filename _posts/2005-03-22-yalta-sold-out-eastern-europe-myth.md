## The Verdict

The myth that Yalta sold out Eastern Europe holds that Franklin Roosevelt and Winston Churchill, through weak diplomacy, naive trust of Stalin, failing health, and even treachery inside the American delegation, handed Eastern Europe to Soviet domination at the Crimea Conference of February 4 to 11, 1945. The narrative became a staple of postwar American conservative politics, a charge repeated in campaigns and memoirs from 1946 through the 1950s: the Cold War was born at Yalta, and it was born because American diplomats failed.

The record, examined against the military map of February 1945, supports a different conclusion. By the time the three leaders met at Livadia Palace, the Red Army occupied Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia, substantial parts of Hungary, and much of Czechoslovakia. Soviet forces on the Oder stood roughly forty miles from Berlin while the Western Allies had not yet crossed the Rhine. No Western military option existed that could have dislodged Soviet power from Eastern Europe without a war against the Soviet Union itself, an undertaking no Allied leader contemplated and no Allied public would have sustained. The Yalta arrangements ratified the map the war had drawn. They did not concede territory the Soviet Union did not already hold.

That does not mean the Cold War outcome for Eastern Europe was benign. Millions of Poles, Czechs, Hungarians, Romanians, Bulgarians, and others lived under governments imposed by Soviet power in violation of the very Yalta provisions that promised free elections and representative government. The suffering was real, and this article does not diminish it. But the suffering flowed from Soviet occupation and Soviet non-compliance, not from diplomatic failure at a conference table. The myth confuses the crime with the crime scene. Yalta was where the terms were written down. The war was where Eastern Europe was decided, months earlier, by the armies that held the ground.

The stakes of getting Yalta right extend beyond historical accuracy. The sellout myth teaches a false lesson: that firmness and willpower can overcome any constraint, that diplomacy fails only when diplomats fail. The true lesson is harder and more useful. Statecraft operates within limits set by power, geography, and the commitments of coalitions. Recognizing those limits is not weakness; it is the precondition of effective action. The leaders at Yalta understood their limits, negotiated within them, and secured what could be secured: the alliance's continuation, the United Nations, the Far Eastern commitment, and a set of principles against which Soviet behavior could be judged. The myth demands that they should have done the impossible. History should judge them for what was possible, and for the courage to do it while the war was still being fought.
## Direct Answers

### What is the Yalta sold-out myth?

The Yalta sold-out myth claims that Roosevelt and Churchill betrayed Eastern Europe to Stalin at the February 1945 conference through weak negotiation, naive trust, declining health, or deliberate treachery in the American delegation. It became standard postwar political rhetoric, especially in American conservative attacks on the Roosevelt and Truman administrations through the 1950s.

### What is the kernel of truth in the myth?

The kernel of truth is substantial. Eastern Europe did fall under Soviet domination after 1945. The Yalta Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections that the Soviet Union systematically violated through 1948. Millions endured decades of imposed rule. The myth is false not because its subject was trivial but because it misidentifies the cause.

### Who held Eastern Europe when Yalta opened?

The Red Army did. By February 4, 1945, Soviet forces occupied Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia, substantial parts of Hungary, and much of Czechoslovakia. The Vistula-Oder offensive had carried Soviet troops to the Oder, within roughly forty miles of Berlin. The Western Allies had not yet crossed the Rhine.

### Could different diplomacy have changed the outcome?

The military-diplomatic evidence available through this article's 2005 publication horizon supports a negative answer. Displacing Soviet forces would have required sustained combat against the Soviet Union itself, beyond the grand strategy, public support, and military reach of the Western coalition. Historians dispute details of the negotiations, but no serious assessment identifies a negotiable position the West surrendered.

### Why does the myth persist?

The myth persists because it served and serves political purposes. From 1946 onward, the sellout narrative gave American conservatives a weapon against Democratic foreign policy, a simple story of betrayal in place of the complex reality of a coalition constrained by military facts. Myths with political utility outlive the evidence against them.

## Yalta Sellout Myth: The Myth-Bust Method

The myth-bust method applied in this series follows five stages. First, state the myth plainly, without straw-man distortion. Second, identify the kernels of truth that give the myth its power. Third, present the evidence that revises the understanding. Fourth, explain the revised understanding in full. Fifth, account for why the myth persists despite the evidence. The myth under examination is politically charged, and the method demands equal rigor toward both the myth and its refutation. The myth is not dismissed as fabrication; its true parts are honored before its false parts are exposed.

The Yalta sold-out myth is the second-tier case in the series' myth-bust tier: less about a single decision than about a political narrative retroactively imposed on a diplomatic event. Its importance lies in how it shaped American politics for a decade and distorted the public understanding of how the Cold War began. The method is especially necessary here because the kernels of truth are so substantial that they can look like proof. Eastern Europe did suffer. The declarations did go unhonored. Stating the evidence carefully is the only way to separate what happened at Yalta from what the myth claims happened there.

The method faces its hardest test in myths that are politically alive. A dead myth, like the claim that a single cavalry charge decided a battle, can be examined dispassionately. The Yalta myth is not dead. It shaped elections, ruined careers, and organized American foreign policy debate for a generation. Applying the method here requires distinguishing the historical question, what did the conference decide and why, from the political question, who benefited from describing it as a betrayal. The five stages keep these questions separate. The myth is stated in its strongest form so the refutation cannot be accused of evasion. The kernels of truth are honored so the refutation cannot be accused of indifference. The evidence is presented from the records, not from later polemics. The revised understanding is stated plainly. And the persistence is explained by the myth's function, not dismissed as mere ignorance.
## The Myth, Stated Plainly

The myth, in its fullest form, runs as follows. At the Yalta Conference in February 1945, President Franklin D. Roosevelt, a dying man whose judgment had deteriorated along with his health, and Prime Minister Winston Churchill, desperate to preserve the wartime alliance, negotiated with Joseph Stalin from a position of avoidable weakness. Through naive trust in Soviet promises, through failure to press American and British interests, and, in the most extreme version, through deliberate sabotage by Soviet agents inside the American delegation, the Western leaders conceded Eastern Europe to Soviet domination. Poland was the central exhibit: the London-based Polish government-in-exile, the legitimate representative of the Polish nation, was abandoned in favor of the Soviet-backed Lublin Committee. The Declaration on Liberated Europe, with its language about free elections and governments responsive to the will of the people, was a worthless paper concession extracted by gullible diplomats from a cynical dictator who never intended to honor it. The Cold War, in this telling, began not in Moscow's decisions but at the conference table in Crimea, and it began because of diplomatic failure.

The myth comes in several intensities. The moderate version blames FDR's failing health and excessive trust. The harder version blames deliberate treachery, naming Alger Hiss, the State Department adviser present at Yalta who was later identified as a Soviet intelligence source. The hardest version treats Yalta as the original sin of American foreign policy, the moment the United States sold its principles for alliance convenience. All versions share a common premise: that better individual diplomacy could have produced a substantially different Eastern European outcome.

The three intensities of the myth deserve separate attention because they make different evidentiary demands. The moderate version, declining health and naive trust, is an argument about Roosevelt's capacity and judgment. It can be tested against the medical record and the conference minutes. The harder version, deliberate treachery, is an argument about conspiracy. It can be tested against the documentary record of who decided what. The hardest version, Yalta as the original sin of American foreign policy, is an argument about historical causation. It can be tested against the military map. Each version fails its own test, but the tests are different, and the myth-bust must administer each one. What unites the versions is the premise that the individuals at the table determined the outcome. That premise is the myth's load-bearing wall, and the military evidence removes it.
## The Myth in American Political Life

The sellout narrative was born almost immediately after the war. Churchill's Iron Curtain speech at Fulton, Missouri, in March 1946 framed the Soviet domination of Eastern Europe as a completed fact and invited the question of who had allowed it. The Berlin Blockade of 1948 and 1949 hardened the confrontation and gave the narrative a continuing relevance. By the late 1940s, Republican critics of the Truman administration were treating Yalta as shorthand for Democratic failure in foreign policy.

The narrative reached its full political form in the early 1950s. It became a standard Republican position through the decade: the Roosevelt administration had been soft on communism, penetrated by Soviet agents, and responsible for the loss of Eastern Europe and then China. The accusations against Alger Hiss, a Yalta participant convicted of perjury in 1950 in connection with espionage allegations, gave the treachery version of the myth its most potent evidence. The broader claims extended to the State Department as an institution, with Yalta presented as the showcase of what went wrong. The political utility of the narrative was obvious. It converted the painful complexity of the Cold War's origins into a simple story of betrayal, and it fixed blame on a dead president who could not answer.

What the political narrative never addressed was the military map. The critics spoke as though Eastern Europe in February 1945 had been an unoccupied prize, a set of territories whose disposition was open to negotiation. The records of the war show that it was not.

The 1952 presidential campaign made the myth a governing doctrine. The Republican platform charged the Democratic administrations with the loss of Eastern Europe, and John Foster Dulles, the incoming Secretary of State, promised a policy of liberation that would roll back Soviet power. The liberation rhetoric was politically potent and strategically hollow: no administration, Republican or Democratic, developed a plan to dislodge Soviet forces from Eastern Europe, because no such plan was feasible without general war. The myth thus produced a characteristic pattern of American Cold War politics: maximalist rhetoric about the past, quiet acceptance of the present. The Democrats, for their part, could never fully rebut the charge without admitting the structural truth, that Eastern Europe had been lost to Soviet armies rather than to Democratic diplomats, an admission that would have required explaining military realities to an electorate primed for moral simplicity.
## The Kernel of Truth: Soviet Domination Happened

The first kernel of truth is the one the myth handles most accurately. Eastern Europe did fall under Soviet domination, and the domination was neither brief nor mild. By the late 1940s, Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia had governments imposed or controlled by Soviet power. Yugoslavia under Tito took an independent path after the 1948 split, but the rest remained within the Soviet sphere through the Cold War's full duration.

The human consequences were severe and are not in dispute. Soviet-imposed governments conducted purges, show trials, nationalizations, collectivizations, and suppressions of dissent that destroyed lives by the hundreds of thousands and blighted societies for two generations. The Polish elections of January 1947 were rigged. The Czechoslovak coup of February 1948 ended the country's democratic experiment. The Hungarian and Romanian and Bulgarian transitions followed the same pattern of Communist consolidation under Soviet direction. For the populations affected, the difference between Soviet promises and Soviet practice was the difference between hope and prison.

The myth-bust does not deny this. The revised understanding incorporates it fully. The question is not whether Eastern Europe suffered under Soviet domination. The question is whether the Yalta Conference caused that suffering, whether it could have prevented it, and whether the arrangements made there were the mechanism of betrayal or something else entirely.

The timeline of consolidation varied by country but the direction never did. Poland's rigged referendum of June 1946 and falsified elections of January 1947 established the pattern. Romania's King Michael was forced to abdicate in December 1947. Bulgaria's opposition leader Nikola Petkov was executed in September 1947 after a show trial. Hungary's non-Communist parties were dismantled through 1947. Czechoslovakia held out longest, until the February 1948 coup. Each step was accompanied by the language of democracy and the practice of coercion. The Yalta Declaration's promises were not merely unfulfilled; they were systematically inverted, with the forms of popular consultation used to legitimize the destruction of political pluralism. This is the kernel of truth at its hardest and most painful, and no honest account of Yalta can minimize it.
## The Declaration on Liberated Europe: The Promise on Paper

The Declaration on Liberated Europe was the Yalta document most directly concerned with Eastern Europe's political future. Signed by all three leaders, it committed the Allies to assist liberated peoples in solving their political and economic problems by democratic means, to foster conditions in which liberated peoples could exercise their right to choose the form of government under which they would live, and to support the holding of free elections.

The declaration's language was sweeping, and its subsequent violation was systematic. Soviet authorities in Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia did not permit free elections, did not foster democratic conditions, and did not assist liberated peoples in choosing their governments. The declaration's promises were, in practice, dead letters in the Soviet sphere.

The myth treats the declaration as evidence of Western naivety: the Western leaders extracted worthless promises because they trusted Stalin's signature. The revised understanding treats the declaration differently. The Western delegations knew, or had strong reason to know, that Soviet compliance would depend on Soviet interests rather than paper commitments. The declaration's purpose was partly to establish a standard against which Soviet behavior could later be judged, and partly to give the Western leaders something to present to their publics and parliaments. Its value was always contingent on enforcement, and enforcement was always contingent on military power. The declaration was not honored because the power to enforce it did not exist in the territories where it mattered. The failure was not in the drafting. It was in the map.

The declaration's text repays close reading. It spoke of the right of liberated peoples to choose their governments and of the Allies' joint responsibility to assist them. What it did not contain was any enforcement mechanism, any timetable, any definition of free elections, or any provision for Allied supervision in Soviet-occupied territory. These omissions were not oversights. They reflected the reality that the three powers could agree on principles precisely because the principles would be implemented separately, in zones defined by military occupation. The declaration was a statement of common aspiration papering over incompatible intentions. The Western leaders understood this; the Soviet leaders understood this; the declaration's value lay in establishing the standard by which subsequent Soviet behavior would be judged in the West.
## Poland: The Unfulfilled Promise

Poland was the most contentious Eastern European question at Yalta and remains the myth's central exhibit. The Polish arrangements had three components. First, the eastern border was set at the Curzon Line, the boundary the Soviet Union demanded and Britain had proposed in 1919. Second, Poland would receive substantial territorial compensation in the north and west, at Germany's expense, on a provisional basis pending the final peace settlement. Third, the Soviet-backed Lublin Committee government would be reorganized on a broader democratic basis, with the inclusion of democratic leaders from Poland itself and from the London-based Polish government-in-exile, and would hold free elections.

The myth reads the Polish outcome as the sellout's purest form: the legitimate London government abandoned, the Lublin Communists installed, the elections rigged, the nation delivered to Soviet rule. The record supports parts of this indictment. The reorganized Polish government was dominated by the Communists. Stanislaw Mikolajczyk, the London Pole brought into the coalition, found himself in an untenable position and fled in 1947. The January 1947 elections were conducted under intimidation and falsified. Soviet non-compliance was complete.

What the myth omits is the context in which the Polish arrangements were made. Poland was already occupied by the Red Army. The Lublin Committee was already functioning as the Soviet-backed authority. The London government-in-exile had no presence on Polish territory, no military force inside the country, and no means of asserting authority except through Allied enforcement that did not exist. The Western leaders at Yalta were not choosing between a free Poland and a Soviet Poland. They were choosing between a Soviet Poland with paper guarantees and a Soviet Poland without them. The paper guarantees were worth what the myth says they were worth. But the alternative the myth imagines, a Poland kept free by diplomatic firmness, did not exist on the ground.

Stanislaw Mikolajczyk's fate personalizes the Polish tragedy. The former prime minister of the London government, he accepted the Yalta formula in good faith, returned to Poland as deputy prime minister in the Provisional Government of National Unity, and attempted to build a genuine peasant-party opposition. He was harassed, his supporters were arrested and murdered, the referendum and elections were falsified against him, and in October 1947 he fled the country with Western assistance. His trajectory from London premier to hunted fugitive traces the arc of the Yalta promise from paper to ashes. The myth cites his fate as proof of Western betrayal. The evidence shows it as proof of Soviet bad faith operating in a country the West had no power to protect.
## Alger Hiss: The Traitor in the Room

The hardest version of the myth centers on Alger Hiss. Hiss was a State Department official who served as a technical adviser to the American delegation at Yalta. In 1948, Whittaker Chambers accused Hiss of having been a Soviet intelligence source in the 1930s. Hiss denied the charges, was convicted of perjury in 1950, and became the most famous American espionage case of the early Cold War. For proponents of the sellout narrative, Hiss's presence at Yalta was proof that Soviet agents had shaped the American negotiating position from the inside.

The record on Hiss requires careful handling. Hiss was indeed at Yalta, and he was indeed a Soviet intelligence source, a conclusion supported by the Venona decrypts and the post-Cold War archival evidence assembled by historians working before this article's 2005 publication date. The myth's leap is from Hiss's presence to Hiss's influence on the Eastern European outcomes. The Yalta records show that the major Eastern European decisions were made by Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin themselves, in the plenary sessions and the foreign ministers' meetings, on the basis of positions prepared by the full American and British delegations and constrained by the military realities described above. No documentary evidence connects any specific Yalta concession to Hiss's advice or intervention. Hiss's documented role concerned the United Nations organization questions, where he worked on the trusteeship and Security Council provisions.

The treachery version of the myth thus reverses the burden of evidence. It treats Hiss's presence as sufficient proof of sabotage and demands that skeptics prove the negative. The documentary record shows a negotiation driven by the principals and the map. The Hiss story, whatever its significance for the history of American counterintelligence, is not the explanation for Eastern Europe's fate.

The Hiss case's full dimensions extend beyond Yalta and must be stated accurately. Whittaker Chambers, a former Soviet courier turned Time editor, accused Hiss before the House Un-American Activities Committee in August 1948. Hiss denied the charges under oath. The statute of limitations barred espionage prosecution, so the government tried him for perjury; the first trial deadlocked, the second convicted him in January 1950. The Venona decrypts, released in the 1990s, corroborated the espionage allegations to the satisfaction of most historians working before this article's publication date. None of this establishes that Hiss influenced the Yalta decisions on Eastern Europe. The myth's logic requires an additional step, from Hiss's guilt as a Soviet source to Hiss's authorship of American concessions, and that step has no documentary support. The conference's Eastern European outcomes are fully explicable without him.
## The Human Costs: Honoring What the Myth Gets Right

Before proceeding to the evidence that revises the understanding, this article honors the myth's most important true component in full. The Cold War arrangements for Eastern Europe were harsh for the affected populations, and the harshness lasted for decades. Soviet-imposed governments imprisoned, tortured, and executed political opponents. They expropriated property, collectivized agriculture, suppressed churches, censored the press, and closed the borders. The Hungarian revolution of 1956 was crushed by Soviet tanks. The Prague Spring of 1968 met the same fate. For millions of people, the postwar settlement meant lives lived under surveillance, careers destroyed by political unreliability, families divided by the Iron Curtain.

The complication the brief requires is honored here as a substantive section, not a footnote. A myth-bust that treats Eastern European suffering as a rhetorical inconvenience would be a moral failure and a methodological one. The revised understanding must hold two things simultaneously: that the diplomatic record does not support the sellout charge, and that the human consequences of the Cold War settlement were catastrophic for the populations of Eastern Europe. Both are true. The myth is wrong about the mechanism, not about the pain.

The uprisings of 1956 and 1968 stand as the human costs' most visible monuments. In October 1956, Hungarians rose against Soviet domination, formed a reform government under Imre Nagy, and appealed to the West for support that never came; Soviet tanks crushed the revolution in November, killing thousands and driving two hundred thousand into exile. In August 1968, the Warsaw Pact invasion ended Czechoslovakia's Prague Spring. These were not abstractions of geopolitics but streets full of young people facing armor, and their fates were sealed by the same military realities that had shaped Yalta: the Soviet Union held the ground, and no Western power would fight a war to change that. The continuity from 1945 to 1968 is the continuity of force, not of diplomatic failure.
## The Military Reality: The Map of February 4, 1945

The central evidence for the revised understanding is the military map. By the time the conference opened on February 4, 1945, the Red Army held Eastern Europe. This was not a matter of diplomatic assertion or historical interpretation. It was a matter of where the armies stood, recorded in the operational records of both sides.

The Soviet advance across Eastern Europe in 1944 had been one of the great military operations of the war. Operation Bagration, launched on June 22, 1944, destroyed German Army Group Center, the Wehrmacht's strongest formation in the east, and carried Soviet forces through Belarus to the approaches of Warsaw. The companion account of that operation, [Operation Bagration](/2006/02/26/operation-bagration-june-1944-army-group-center/), examines the campaign in full; the details belong to that article rather than this one. What matters here is the result: by August 1944, the Red Army had broken the German front in the center and advanced to the Vistula.

The Lublin-Brest offensive of July and August 1944 carried Soviet forces to the Vistula and established bridgeheads on its western bank. The Warsaw Uprising of August to October 1944, the Polish Home Army's attempt to liberate the capital before the Soviets arrived, was crushed by the Germans while Soviet forces held their positions on the Vistula's eastern bank. The uprising's defeat, with some two hundred thousand Polish dead, removed the most potent non-Communist Polish force from the postwar equation and left the field to the Lublin Committee. The military details of the uprising belong to its own article; the consequence for Yalta was that no independent Polish authority existed inside Poland.

The Stavka's planning for the winter offensive reveals how completely the military initiative lay with Moscow. The Soviet high command sequenced the Vistula-Oder operation to exploit the German collapse in Poland, timed it to relieve pressure on the Western Allies during the Ardennes crisis, and positioned its fronts for the final drive on Berlin. These were the decisions of a headquarters confident in its material superiority and its operational art. The Western leaders at Yalta were negotiating with the representatives of this headquarters' political master. The disparity was not merely in the number of divisions on the ground in Eastern Europe; it was in the momentum of victory, the confidence of success, and the political will to convert military conquest into political control. Diplomacy does not reverse momentum of that magnitude.
## Romania, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia: The Southern Advance

Romania fell to the Soviet advance in August 1944, when King Michael's coup removed the Antonescu regime and switched Romania to the Allied side as Soviet forces crossed the border. Bulgaria, which had avoided war with the Soviet Union, was occupied by Soviet forces in September 1944 after a declaration of war that preceded the occupation by a matter of days. Yugoslavia was liberated substantially by Tito's Partisans with Soviet cooperation in October 1944, the Belgrade operation marking the Red Army's passage through the country.

These were not diplomatic arrangements awaiting negotiation. They were completed military facts. Romanian, Bulgarian, and Yugoslav territory was under Soviet or Soviet-allied control. The governments that emerged in Bucharest and Sofia did so under Soviet supervision. By February 1945, the question of who would determine Eastern Europe's political future in these countries had been answered by the armies, not left open for the diplomats.

The Romanian turn of August 1944 deserves closer attention because it shows how military collapse translated into political revolution. As Soviet forces broke through in the Jassy-Kishinev operation, King Michael arrested Marshal Antonescu, announced Romania's defection from the Axis, and ordered Romanian forces to turn against the Germans. The Soviet Union treated Romania as a defeated enemy despite the coup, imposing an armistice with harsh terms and a supervisory Allied Control Commission dominated by Moscow. In Bulgaria, the Fatherland Front government that emerged after the Soviet occupation was progressively purged of non-Communists. In Yugoslavia, Tito's Partisans, who had liberated most of the country themselves, negotiated with Stalin as a junior partner, a relationship that would fracture in 1948 but that in 1944 and 1945 placed Yugoslavia within the Soviet orbit. In each case, the political outcome followed the military facts without the need for any conference's permission.
## Hungary and Czechoslovakia: The Advance Continues

Hungary was invaded in September 1944, and the siege of Budapest, one of the war's most destructive urban battles, continued through February 1945, ending only days after the Yalta Conference closed. Soviet forces held substantial Hungarian territory when the conference opened. Czechoslovakia was partially occupied through December 1944, with the Soviet advance continuing into the new year.

The pattern was uniform across the region. Everywhere the Red Army had advanced, Soviet political authority followed. The advance was still in motion when Yalta convened: the war in the east was not over, and Soviet forces were still fighting their way west. The Western leaders at Yalta were negotiating with an ally whose armies were simultaneously winning the war in Eastern Europe and establishing the political facts that would follow the victory.

The siege of Budapest, which ended on February 13, 1945, two days after Yalta closed, illustrates the war's continuing violence during the diplomacy. The German and Hungarian defenders held the city for nearly two months against Soviet assault, and the fighting destroyed much of Budapest and killed tens of thousands of soldiers and civilians. While the three leaders discussed Eastern Europe's future at Livadia Palace, Soviet artillery was reducing the Hungarian capital. The juxtaposition captures the conference's essential character: diplomats deliberating over a region where the war was still being fought and where the fighting itself was determining the political future. The Budapest garrison's resistance, like the Warsaw Uprising's, was heroic and futile, and its futility was a military fact that no declaration could alter.
## The Vistula-Oder Offensive: Forty Miles from Berlin

The Vistula-Oder offensive, launched on January 12, 1945, was the operation that fixed the map beyond revision. In three weeks, Soviet forces advanced from the Vistula to the Oder, a distance of some three hundred miles, shattering the German defenses in Poland and reaching positions roughly forty miles from Berlin by early February 1945. The speed of the advance stunned even the Soviet high command and forced a pause to consolidate, but the strategic result was irreversible. Germany's eastern front had collapsed. The Red Army stood on the Oder. Berlin was the next objective.

The significance for Yalta is direct. The Soviet delegation arrived at the conference with its armies forty miles from the German capital, holding all of Poland and most of Eastern Europe. The Western delegations arrived with their armies still east of the Rhine. The disparity in military position was not a detail of the negotiations. It was the negotiations' determining condition.

The offensive's operational details underscore the scale of Soviet power that Yalta confronted. Launched on January 12, 1945, from bridgeheads on the Vistula, the operation committed multiple fronts, thousands of tanks, and overwhelming artillery against German defenses that had been stripped to meet the Ardennes offensive in the west. The breakthrough was immediate and the exploitation rapid; by the end of January, Soviet spearheads had reached the Oder. The advance outran its logistics, forcing the pause that gave Berlin a few more months, but the strategic decision had been made on the battlefield. Germany could no longer defend its eastern frontier. The Red Army would dictate the terms of the war's end in the east, and the Yalta conferees, meeting as the offensive's final positions were being consolidated, were ratifying a victory already won.
## The Western Military Option That Did Not Exist

The myth implies, without always stating, that the Western Allies had a military option they failed to use: that firmer diplomacy at Yalta could have been backed by the credible threat of Western force in Eastern Europe. The military record shows that no such option existed.

The Western Allies' grand strategy was Germany-first, and the main effort was directed at Germany itself, not at Eastern Europe. The Allied armies in the west were engaged in the campaign to cross the Rhine and advance into Germany's industrial heartland. Diverting the main effort to a push into Eastern Europe, across hundreds of miles of territory, would have meant abandoning the agreed strategy, extending supply lines beyond their capacity, and fighting through or around German forces that still held the intervening ground. The geographic distance from the Allied advance to Eastern European territory substantially exceeded practical military reach.

The practical question is sharper. Displacing Soviet forces from Eastern Europe would have required combat operations against the Soviet Union. The Red Army in February 1945 numbered in the millions, battle-hardened, victorious, and positioned across the region. No Allied plan contemplated war with the Soviet Union. No Allied public, weary after five years of war, would have supported turning the guns on the ally whose armies had broken the Wehrmacht in the east. The military option to enforce different Eastern European arrangements did not realistically exist for the Yalta-era Allied coalition. It was not a card the Western leaders held and failed to play. It was a card that did not exist in the deck.

Consider what the myth's implied alternative would have required in practice. Confronting the Soviet Union over Eastern Europe in early 1945 would have meant halting the agreed campaign against Germany, redeploying forces eastward across devastated territory, establishing supply lines hundreds of miles long, and engaging the Red Army, the largest and most experienced land force in the world, in sustained combat. The Western Allies would have fought this war with public opinion demanding demobilization, with economies strained by years of total war, and with the Pacific war still requiring every available resource. The Soviet Union, by contrast, would have fought on interior lines, with its supply bases close and its political leadership fully mobilized. No responsible military staff in London or Washington recommended such a course, because no responsible staff could. The option's absence was not a failure of imagination; it was a recognition of reality.
## The Rhine Line: Where the Western Armies Stood

The contrast in positions deserves precise statement. When Yalta opened on February 4, 1945, the Western Allies had not yet crossed the Rhine in force. The first crossing at Remagen would not occur until March 7, and the substantial crossings of March and April were still weeks away. The Allied armies stood on Germany's western frontier, preparing the final campaign into the Reich. Eastern Europe lay hundreds of miles to the east, beyond the German armies still fighting in the west and beyond the Soviet armies already in occupation.

The myth's implied counterfactual, that the Western Allies could have raced the Soviets to Eastern Europe or confronted them there, ignores the operational reality. The Western advance was proceeding on its own schedule toward its own objectives. The race to Berlin, such as it was, was already decided by geography: the Soviets were forty miles away, the Western Allies several hundred. The Yalta negotiators could not bargain with forces they did not have in places they could not reach.

The Allied dispositions in early February 1945 confirm the analysis. Montgomery's 21st Army Group in the north, Bradley's 12th Army Group in the center, and Devers's 6th Army Group in the south were preparing the Rhineland campaign, the systematic clearing of the west bank before the crossings. The Ardennes offensive had been contained only weeks earlier, at heavy cost, and the Allied command was focused on the methodical destruction of German forces west of the Rhine. These were the operations that would end the war in the west, and they consumed the full attention and resources of the Allied command. The suggestion that these armies might simultaneously have projected power into Eastern Europe ignores the operational tempo, the logistical constraints, and the strategic purpose of the campaign they were actually fighting.
## The Germany-First Grand Strategy

The absence of a Western military option in Eastern Europe was not an accident or an oversight. It was the consequence of the grand strategy the Allies had agreed at Casablanca, Tehran, and subsequent conferences: Germany first, the main effort against the German army in the west, the Soviet Union carrying the main burden in the east. The strategy had won the war. It had also determined, as a structural matter, who would liberate Eastern Europe.

This is the committee-architecture point at the heart of the house thesis. The Yalta arrangements were not the product of individual diplomatic genius or failure. They were the product of a coalition whose architecture, its agreed strategy, its division of effort, its political commitments, had already allocated Eastern Europe to the Soviet sphere of military operations. The diplomats at Yalta were working within constraints set by years of combined planning. To blame the individuals at the table for the structure of the coalition is to mistake the level at which the outcome was determined.

The strategy's origins confirm its structural character. At Casablanca in January 1943, Roosevelt and Churchill formalized the Germany-first principle over the objections of those who favored concentrating on Japan. At Tehran in November 1943, the three leaders coordinated the cross-Channel invasion with the Soviet summer offensive of 1944, explicitly linking Western and Eastern operations. The Combined Chiefs of Staff spent the war allocating shipping, landing craft, and air power according to this strategy. By 1945, the strategy had produced exactly the military map it was designed to produce: Germany crushed between converging offensives, with the Soviet Union bearing the main burden in the east and reaping the territorial consequences. To complain at Yalta that the strategy had given Eastern Europe to the Soviets is to complain about the war's agreed design after it has succeeded.
## The Atomic Bargain: Soviet Entry Against Japan

One of the Yalta Conference's most consequential secret agreements concerned the Far East, and it illuminates the constraints on the Western position. In a secret protocol, the Soviet Union agreed to enter the war against Japan two to three months after Germany's defeat, in exchange for territorial concessions including southern Sakhalin, the Kurile Islands, and rights in Manchuria and Port Arthur. Roosevelt sought the agreement because American planners estimated that the invasion of Japan would be enormously costly and Soviet participation would shorten the war and save American lives.

The Far Eastern agreement matters for the sellout myth in two ways. First, it shows the Western leaders bargaining from acknowledged dependence: they needed Soviet help against Japan and paid for it with concessions. The myth treats this dependence as further evidence of weakness. Second, and more importantly, it shows why the Western leaders could not afford a confrontation with Stalin over Eastern Europe. The coalition had to hold through the defeat of Germany and into the war against Japan. Breaking with the Soviet Union over Poland in February 1945 would have jeopardized the Soviet commitment that American planners considered essential. The Eastern European question and the Far Eastern question were linked in the diplomacy, and the linkage constrained the West on both.

The linkage between the Far Eastern bargain and the Eastern European question operated at multiple levels. Militarily, the Joint Chiefs wanted Soviet forces to pin down the Kwantung Army in Manchuria and to provide bases for the bombing campaign. Politically, Roosevelt wanted the Soviet Union committed to the Pacific war before the atomic bomb's uncertain prospects were known; the Trinity test lay months in the future, and no planner could count on a weapon that had not yet been proven. Diplomatically, the bargain required maintaining working relations with Stalin through the war's end. Each of these considerations reinforced the others, and each argued against confrontation over Poland. The myth treats the Far Eastern concessions as further evidence of weakness. The strategic logic treats them as the price of a coalition that had to function on two fronts simultaneously.
## What the Arrangements Actually Were

The Yalta arrangements for Eastern Europe, examined as documents rather than as symbols, were substantially a ratification of military reality. The conference did not transfer territory from Western to Soviet control. It recorded, in diplomatic language, the disposition that the armies had already imposed.

The Polish settlement is the clearest case. The eastern border at the Curzon Line confirmed the Soviet Union in possession of territory it had held since 1939 and reoccupied in 1944. The western and northern compensation at Germany's expense was provisional, pending the final peace settlement, and was confirmed in its essentials at Potsdam. The government question, the reorganization of the Lublin authority with the inclusion of London Poles and the promise of free elections, was the one element where diplomacy attempted to modify reality rather than record it. It was also the element that failed, because the power to enforce it did not exist.

The myth reads the Polish arrangements as concessions. The documents read as descriptions. The distinction matters because it locates the failure where it belongs: not in the drafting of the agreements but in the absence of the power to enforce the one provision that ran against the grain of military fact.

The protocol's structure reveals the negotiators' understanding of their own limits. The document records agreements where agreement was possible, the United Nations voting formula, the Far Eastern protocol, the occupation zones, and papers over disagreements where the underlying power could not be reconciled, the Polish government question above all. This is the characteristic texture of summit diplomacy: precise where the parties' interests align, vague where they do not. The vagueness on Poland was not a trick played on the West; it was the honest reflection of an irreconcilable difference. Stalin would not accept an independent Poland, the West would not publicly abandon the principle of Polish independence, and the resulting language satisfied neither side's private intentions. The myth reads the vagueness as Western failure. The diplomat reads it as the accurate registration of a power imbalance.
## The Curzon Line: The Eastern Border

The Curzon Line, the boundary established for Poland's east, requires historical context the myth usually omits. The line was not invented at Yalta. It was proposed by the British Foreign Secretary, Lord Curzon, in 1919 as the eastern frontier of the reconstituted Polish state. The Soviet Union had held the territory east of the line since the 1939 partition of Poland under the Nazi-Soviet pact and had reoccupied it during the 1944 advance.

The Yalta decision confirmed the Soviet Union in territory it already held and had held for most of the preceding six years. The Western leaders were not giving Stalin something he lacked. They were acknowledging something he possessed. The alternative, insisting on Poland's 1939 eastern border, would have required the Soviet Union to surrender territory its armies occupied, a demand no negotiation could have enforced. The Curzon Line decision was a recognition of fact, and the fact predated Yalta by half a decade.

The line's history before Yalta is instructive. After the Polish-Soviet War of 1919 to 1921, the Treaty of Riga established Poland's eastern border well east of the Curzon Line, incorporating large Ukrainian and Belarusian populations into the Polish state. The Soviet Union never accepted the Riga border as legitimate, and the 1939 partition restored approximately the Curzon alignment. When the Big Three confirmed the line at Yalta, they were thus endorsing a border with a contested twentieth-century history, one that each party read through its own national narrative. For the Poles, the Curzon Line meant the loss of the eastern borderlands, the Kresy, and the displacement of millions. For the Soviets, it meant the recovery of territory seized in 1921. The human cost of the border's confirmation, the population transfers that followed, was enormous. But the border's location was determined by the armies of 1939 and 1944, not by the diplomats of 1945.
## The Oder-Neisse Line: The Western Border

Poland's western border was the more genuinely negotiable question at Yalta, and its treatment illustrates the limits of the sellout charge. The conference agreed that Poland would receive substantial accessions of territory in the north and west, but left the exact line open. The Soviet position favored the western Neisse as the boundary; the Western leaders preferred to defer the final determination. The Potsdam Conference subsequently confirmed the western Neisse line, placing the former German territories east of the Oder and western Neisse under Polish administration pending the final peace settlement.

The myth sometimes presents the western border as another concession extracted by Stalin. The record shows a provisional arrangement, deferred for final settlement, that gave Poland extensive compensation for its eastern losses. The displacement of the German population from these territories was one of the war's great demographic upheavals, and its human costs were enormous. But as a diplomatic matter, the western border question was handled through the committee process of the two conferences, with the Western leaders reserving their position at Yalta and accepting the Soviet preference at Potsdam under the pressure of accomplished fact. The pattern is consistent: diplomacy recorded what power had decided.

The population transfers that implemented the western border were among the war's great forced migrations. Some twelve million Germans fled or were expelled from the territories east of the Oder-Neisse line, with mortality estimates in the hundreds of thousands. The Potsdam Conference authorized the transfers in principle, calling for them to be conducted in an orderly and humane manner, a phrase that the reality brutally contradicted. The expulsions were driven by Polish and Czechoslovak authorities with Soviet acquiescence, and the Western Allies, having accepted the territorial settlement, accepted its demographic consequences. This dimension of the Yalta-Potsdam settlement is often absent from the sellout debate, which focuses on the diplomacy rather than the human upheaval. The omission is telling: the myth is interested in who is to blame, not in what was suffered.
## The Polish Government Question

The reorganization of the Polish government was the Yalta provision that came closest to a genuine diplomatic contest, and its failure is the myth's strongest evidence. The agreement called for the Lublin-based Provisional Government to be reorganized on a broader democratic basis, with the inclusion of democratic leaders from Poland and from abroad, and for the reorganized government to hold free and unfettered elections.

The Western delegations pressed for specific guarantees: the inclusion of named London Poles, Allied observers for the elections, and a tripartite commission to supervise the reorganization. Stalin resisted the specifics and accepted the generalities. The reorganized Provisional Government of National Unity, formed in June 1945, was dominated by the Communists. Mikolajczyk, the principal London figure, was isolated and eventually driven into exile. The January 1947 elections were conducted under fraud and intimidation.

The question the myth poses is whether harder negotiation could have produced a different Polish government. The assessment of historians working within this article's publication horizon varies on the details but converges on the structural point: the Soviet Union controlled Poland's territory, its security apparatus, and its administration. No form of words agreed at Yalta could have created an independent Polish government under those conditions without Soviet consent, and Soviet consent was not available for any government that threatened Communist control. The Western leaders extracted the maximum that paper could provide. Paper was not enough, because the underlying power was not theirs.

The Moscow commission's work illustrates the diplomacy's futility in detail. Harriman for the United States, Clark Kerr for Britain, and Molotov for the Soviet Union met through the spring of 1945 to implement the Yalta formula, consulting Polish democratic leaders as instructed. Molotov vetoed candidate after candidate, insisting that only figures acceptable to the Lublin government could be considered. The commission's eventual agreement in June 1945 produced the Provisional Government of National Unity, but the consultations had demonstrated the formula's emptiness: the Soviet Union would accept only a government it controlled, and the Western commissioners had no leverage to insist on more. The commission's records are the documentary refutation of the claim that better drafting at Yalta would have mattered. The implementation was attempted in good faith and failed against Soviet obstruction that no wording could have prevented.
## FDR's Performance at Yalta

The myth's moderate version centers on Roosevelt's personal performance: that his failing health impaired his judgment, that he was outmaneuvered by Stalin, that a healthier or harder president would have done better. The assessment of FDR's Yalta performance has been substantially contested, and the companion [reassessment of Roosevelt's wartime leadership](/2005/06/18/fdr-reassessed-internment-yalta-health/) examines the health question and the diplomatic record in full; tthe contest deserves a fair hearing here as well.

The participants' accounts, recorded in the conference records and memoirs, generally describe Roosevelt as functional and engaged. He chaired the plenary sessions, managed the agenda, pursued the United Nations organization questions that were his principal interest, and intervened on the Polish and Far Eastern questions. His positions were within the range of reasonable Allied alternatives given the constraints. The defenders of his performance note that no specific Yalta outcome can be traced to a lapse in his attention or a failure of his will.

The critics note that Roosevelt's manner was conciliatory, that he avoided direct confrontation with Stalin, and that he placed great weight on preserving the personal relationship and the alliance. These observations are accurate as descriptions. Whether they constitute failures depends on the counterfactual: what would confrontation have achieved? The structural analysis suggests that confrontation over Eastern Europe would have produced a breach with the Soviet Union without changing the map, jeopardizing the Far Eastern agreement and the United Nations project for no gain in Poland. Roosevelt's conciliatory manner may have reflected his health, his temperament, or his judgment. The outcomes it produced were the outcomes the constraints permitted.

The participants' accounts converge on a president diminished in body but functioning in mind. Bohlen, who served as Roosevelt's interpreter and was present at the key sessions, described the president as tired but in command of the discussions. Stettinius, the new Secretary of State, recorded Roosevelt's active management of the agenda. Leahy, the chief of staff, noted the president's fatigue but not any impairment of judgment. Churchill's own account, written before the memoirs' retrospective turn, described Roosevelt as handling the conference with his accustomed skill. These are not the descriptions of a man incapacitated. They are the descriptions of a dying man doing his job, and the distinction matters for the historical record even if the myth finds it inconvenient.
## The Health Question: Bruenn's Diagnosis

The health question requires precise facts. Admiral Ross McIntire, Roosevelt's personal physician, had concealed the seriousness of the president's condition. In March 1944, the cardiologist Howard Bruenn examined Roosevelt and diagnosed congestive heart failure, hypertension, and cardiac enlargement. The diagnosis was kept from the public and from most of the administration. By February 1945, Roosevelt's physical decline was visible to those around him: weight loss, exhaustion, episodes of acute illness.

The myth treats the diagnosis as the explanation for Yalta: a dying man negotiated away Eastern Europe. The medical record supports the premise of serious illness but not the inference of impaired negotiation. Bruenn's own assessments, and the accounts of participants including Churchill, Eden, Stettinius, and Bohlen, describe a president whose stamina was reduced but whose decision-making capacity remained functional. The specific decisions attributed to his decline, the Polish arrangements, the Far Eastern protocol, the United Nations voting formula, were consistent with positions the administration had developed over months and with the constraints described throughout this article.

The health question also suffers from a logical difficulty the myth never resolves. If Roosevelt's illness explains the Yalta outcomes, then a healthy Roosevelt should have been able to achieve different outcomes. But the constraints were military and structural, not personal. No president, healthy or ill, could have displaced the Red Army from Eastern Europe by force of personality at the conference table. The health of the negotiator was not the variable that determined the outcome.

The concealment of the diagnosis compounds the historical difficulty. McIntire, the White House physician, maintained publicly that the president's health was good, a fiction that deceived the press, the public, and much of the administration. Bruenn's findings, hypertension, cardiac enlargement, congestive failure, were known to a small circle. The president was placed on a regimen of rest, diet, and digitalis, and his schedule was managed to conserve his strength. The secrecy meant that the American public and later political debaters judged Yalta without knowing the medical facts, and the revelation of those facts after Roosevelt's death in April 1945 gave the myth a retrospective medical foundation it had lacked at the time. The myth's health argument is thus partly an artifact of the cover-up: the truth, concealed during the war, became ammunition after it.
## Churchill at Yalta: Participant, Not Protester

The myth sometimes presents Churchill as the clear-eyed realist who saw through Stalin while Roosevelt was deceived, or as the junior partner dragged along by American weakness. The conference record does not support either version. Churchill participated in all the major decisions at Yalta. His positions on the Eastern European arrangements were substantially similar to Roosevelt's. He accepted the Curzon Line, the provisional western border, the Lublin-based reorganization, and the Declaration on Liberated Europe. The British-American differences at Yalta were limited; on the central Eastern European questions, the two Western leaders were aligned.

Churchill's subsequent critical view of the Yalta outcomes, stated most fully in the final volume of his war memoirs published in 1953, was substantially retrospective. Writing with the Cold War fully developed and with the political incentive to distance himself from the settlement, Churchill presented the Yalta arrangements as concessions he had doubted at the time. The contemporary record shows a participant, not a protester. The retrospective criticism is a reminder that memoirs are political documents, and that the sellout narrative was constructed after the fact by men who had been present at the creation.

Eden's role reinforces the point. The Foreign Secretary conducted the detailed negotiations on Poland in the foreign ministers' sessions, pressing Molotov on the commission's composition and the election guarantees. The British minutes show Eden pursuing the same objectives as Stettinius, with the same limited results. The Foreign Office's briefing papers before the conference had already concluded that the Soviet Union would dominate Poland and that Britain's aim must be to secure the best available paper guarantees. This was the considered position of the British government, not a failure of will at the table. Churchill's later memoirs transformed this deliberate policy of limited aims into a narrative of foreboding and resistance, but the contemporary documents tell the story of a government that knew the limits of its power and negotiated within them.
## The Soviet Violation Pattern, 1945 to 1948

The Soviet Union's systematic violation of the Yalta provisions is the historical fact that gives the myth its enduring emotional force, and it must be stated without euphemism. The Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections. What followed was a program of political consolidation that used the forms of democracy to destroy its substance.

In Poland, the Communist-dominated government suppressed the opposition, manipulated the 1946 referendum, and rigged the January 1947 elections. In Romania and Bulgaria, Soviet-backed governments eliminated non-Communist parties through intimidation, arrests, and fraudulent elections. In Hungary, the Communist Party used the technique of salami tactics, slicing away the opposition party by party until only the Communists remained. In Czechoslovakia, the last democracy in the Soviet sphere, the Communist coup of February 1948 ended the experiment and completed the pattern.

The Truman administration recognized the violation pattern progressively through 1945, 1946, and 1947, and American policy adjusted accordingly: the firming of the position on Poland, the Byrnes-Stalin exchanges, the eventual articulation of containment. The recognition was slow, partly because the administration hoped that continued engagement might moderate Soviet behavior, and partly because the public and congressional mood demanded it. But the recognition came, and it came from the documentary record of Soviet actions, not from a sudden discovery that Yalta had been a sellout. The violations were Soviet decisions, taken in Moscow and in the occupied capitals, in breach of agreements the Soviet Union had signed. The responsibility for the breach lies with the party that breached.

The country cases repay individual attention. In Poland, the January 1947 elections were conducted with the opposition's candidates arrested, its rallies broken up, and its votes falsified; the official results gave the Communist bloc eighty percent. In Hungary, the Smallholders' Party, which had won a genuine majority in 1945, was dismantled through arrests and intimidation, its leaders forced into exile or prison. In Romania, the Groza government, installed under Soviet pressure in March 1945, manipulated the 1946 elections and forced the king's abdication. In Bulgaria, the 1946 referendum abolished the monarchy and the subsequent elections consolidated Communist control. In Czechoslovakia, the Communists used their control of the police and their Soviet backing to stage the February 1948 coup. Each case followed the local conditions, but the direction was set in Moscow and the methods were consistent: the elimination of alternatives under the cover of democratic forms.
## The Medical Dimension: Armies, Casualties, and the Conference Table

The Yalta Conference is remembered as a diplomatic event, but its context was medical and logistical as much as political. The armies whose positions determined the negotiations were sustained by medical systems of unprecedented scale, and the conference itself was an exercise in moving fragile human bodies across a war-shattered continent. Roosevelt traveled to Yalta despite congestive heart failure, hypertension, and the exhaustion of a man with months to live. The journey itself, by sea and air and finally over the Crimean roads, was a medical risk undertaken for diplomatic purposes. The fact of his presence, ill as he was, testifies to the importance the participants attached to the meeting and to the constraints under which they operated.

The broader medical context belongs to the armies. The Red Army's advance across Eastern Europe was sustained by a medical system that returned millions of wounded soldiers to duty, and the casualties of the 1944 and 1945 offensives numbered in the hundreds of thousands. The Vistula-Oder offensive alone cost the Soviet forces heavily even in victory. The Western Allies' medical services performed the same function on a smaller scale. The map that constrained Yalta was drawn by soldiers who survived their wounds and returned to the advance, and by the medical systems that made their survival possible. The study of wartime battlefield medicine, including the institutional comparison of Allied medical systems, is developed in the companion resource on [battlefield medicine](https://reportmedic.org/tools/wwii-battlefield-medicine.html). The diplomats at Livadia Palace negotiated over territory held by men whose lives had been saved, or lost, in the offensives of the preceding year. The medical dimension is not a footnote to the diplomacy. It is part of the machinery that produced the map.

Roosevelt's journey to Yalta was itself a medical ordeal that the myth rarely acknowledges. The president traveled by sea to Malta, then by air to the Crimea, then by automobile over mountain roads to Livadia Palace, a journey of thousands of miles undertaken by a man with congestive heart failure. The effort exhausted him; those present noted his pallor and fatigue on arrival. That he made the journey at all, rather than sending a deputy, testifies to the importance he attached to the personal meeting and to the alliance it sustained. The myth's image of a passive, failing president must be set against this fact: the dying man crossed the world to do the job, and the job's constraints were not of his making.

## House Thesis: Committee Diplomacy Within Constraints

The Yalta myth-bust supports the house thesis at the committee-architecture level of coalition diplomacy. Yalta was a committee-architecture coordinated summit: three national delegations, months of staff preparation, foreign ministers' meetings feeding the plenary sessions, position papers, drafting committees, and a secretariat producing the formal record. The arrangements it produced were reasonable within the constraints the coalition's military position imposed.

The myth's individual-leader framing, the claim that better personal diplomacy by Roosevelt or Churchill could have produced substantially different outcomes, is not supported by the military-diplomatic evidence. The individuals operated within a structure: the Germany-first strategy, the division of operational effort, the dependence on Soviet participation against Japan, the public exhaustion of the Allied populations, and the map itself. The structure determined the range of possible outcomes. The individuals selected within that range. To demand that the individuals transcend the structure is to demand the impossible and then to condemn them for failing to achieve it.

The moderate intensity of the house thesis for this article reflects the genuine tragedy of the outcome. Committee diplomacy produced reasonable arrangements, and reasonable arrangements produced, through Soviet violation, a harsh Cold War settlement for Eastern Europe. The thesis does not celebrate the outcome. It explains it.

The committee character of the conference extended to its smallest details. The American delegation arrived with briefing books prepared by the State Department, the Joint Chiefs, and the White House staff. The British brought Foreign Office memoranda and Chiefs of Staff appreciations. The Soviet delegation operated under Stalin's direct control but with the full apparatus of the Foreign Ministry behind it. The plenary sessions were the visible tip of an immense bureaucratic effort, and the agreements reflected staff work as much as personal chemistry. This is why the myth's focus on the three men misleads: the conference was an institutional event, and its outcomes were institutional products. The individuals mattered at the margins, in tone, in emphasis, in the handling of specific issues. The structure determined the substance.
## Why the Myth Persists

The myth persists for reasons the evidence cannot dislodge, because the reasons are political rather than historical. From 1946 onward, the sellout narrative gave American conservatives a weapon against Democratic foreign policy that was simple, emotional, and unfalsifiable in political debate. It converted the structural tragedy of the Cold War's origins into a morality tale with villains and victims. It allowed critics to demand, retroactively, a firmness whose costs they never had to pay.

The myth also persists because its kernels of truth are so painful. Eastern Europe did suffer. The promises were broken. For the populations affected and their descendants, the distinction between Soviet violation and Western diplomatic failure can feel like a lawyer's distinction. The suffering demands an explanation, and the myth supplies one that is easier to grasp than the structural analysis of military constraint. Myths with emotional resonance and political utility outlive the evidence against them. That is why the myth-bust must be restated, and why this article exists.

The myth also persists because the scholarly correction has never reached the popular audience. The historians' consensus, that Yalta ratified military reality, is well established in the academic literature but has not displaced the political narrative in public memory. Textbooks, popular histories, and political speeches continue to present the sellout version, partly from inertia and partly because the simple story serves rhetorical needs that the complex story cannot. The gap between scholarship and memory is a familiar phenomenon in historical understanding, but it is unusually wide and unusually consequential in the Yalta case, because the myth continues to shape how Americans think about diplomacy, weakness, and betrayal.
## The Complication: Human Costs and Diplomatic Reality

The complication required by the brief is restated here as the article's moral center. The myth-bust must not dismiss the Cold War human costs for Eastern European populations, and it does not. The Soviet-imposed governments produced substantial suffering for millions of Poles, Czechs, Hungarians, Romanians, Bulgarians, and others. The suffering lasted for decades. It included imprisonment, torture, execution, expropriation, and the systematic destruction of civil society. No structural analysis of military constraint diminishes these facts.

The balance this article strikes is deliberate. The diplomatic reality, that Yalta ratified rather than created Soviet domination, is a historical finding. The human consequences, that Soviet domination was a catastrophe for its victims, is a moral finding. The two findings do not conflict. The myth's error is to fuse them: to argue from the catastrophe to the sellout, from the suffering to the diplomatic failure. The suffering is real. The sellout is not. Honoring both truths is the obligation the complication imposes, and this article has honored it.

The obligation to honor both truths extends to the article's own rhetoric. It would be easy, in refuting the myth, to adopt a tone of cold realpolitik that treats Eastern Europe's suffering as the inevitable price of great-power politics. This article rejects that tone. The suffering was not inevitable in any moral sense; it was the product of specific Soviet decisions that could have been different, and the victims deserved better than they received. The historical finding, that Western diplomacy could not have prevented the outcome, does not excuse the outcome or diminish its horror. It locates responsibility where the evidence puts it: with the power that occupied, promised, and betrayed.
## The Percentages Agreement: The Moscow Precedent

The Yalta arrangements did not emerge from a vacuum; they followed a precedent set four months earlier in Moscow. In October 1944, Churchill flew to the Soviet capital and proposed to Stalin a division of influence in the Balkans, the famous percentages agreement: Romania ninety percent Soviet, Greece ninety percent British, Yugoslavia and Hungary fifty-fifty, Bulgaria seventy-five percent Soviet. Stalin accepted with minimal discussion, marking the paper with his characteristic blue pencil check.

The percentages agreement is often cited as evidence that Churchill himself accepted spheres of influence, undermining the later Western pose of surprise at Soviet behavior. The myth's defenders rarely mention it, because it complicates the story of Western innocence betrayed. But its significance for the Yalta analysis is structural rather than moral. The agreement shows that the Western leaders understood, months before Yalta, that Eastern Europe's disposition would follow military occupation, and that they were already bargaining over the margins rather than the substance. Yalta's Declaration on Liberated Europe, with its universalist language, sat uneasily alongside the Moscow percentages, and the tension between the two documents captures the Western position's essential duality: public commitment to self-determination, private acceptance of Soviet predominance where Soviet armies stood.

## The Warsaw Uprising's Shadow Over Yalta

No event hung over the Polish discussions at Yalta like the Warsaw Uprising of August to October 1944. The Polish Home Army, loyal to the London government, had risen against the German occupiers as Soviet forces approached the Vistula, hoping to liberate the capital and present the Soviets with a Polish authority they could not ignore. The Germans crushed the uprising over sixty-three days of fighting, killing some two hundred thousand Poles and destroying the city, while Soviet forces held their positions on the river's eastern bank and declined to intervene.

The uprising's defeat had two consequences for Yalta. First, it eliminated the Home Army as a political and military factor inside Poland, removing the one non-Communist force that might have given the London government a presence on the ground. Second, it poisoned Polish-Soviet relations beyond repair, ensuring that the Polish question would be handled in an atmosphere of bitterness and mistrust. The Western leaders at Yalta knew the uprising's history; Churchill in particular had followed it with anguish. But knowledge could not reverse the military facts. The uprising's failure meant that when the diplomats discussed Poland's government, they were discussing a country whose independent military force had been destroyed and whose capital lay in ruins. The shadow of Warsaw fell across every Polish conversation at Livadia Palace.

## Truman's Inheritance: From Yalta to Potsdam

Roosevelt died on April 12, 1945, less than two months after Yalta, and Harry Truman inherited the agreements, the alliance, and the developing Soviet violation pattern. Truman's first months brought the war in Europe to an end, the discovery of the full extent of Soviet obstruction in Poland and elsewhere, and the Potsdam Conference of July and August 1945, where the Big Three met again with Truman and Clement Attlee replacing Roosevelt and Churchill.

Potsdam confirmed the essential Yalta framework while registering the changed atmosphere. The Oder-Neisse line was confirmed, the occupation zones were finalized, and the reparations question was settled on terms that reflected the growing Western-Soviet estrangement. The companion account of the Potsdam Conference examines these proceedings in detail. What matters for the Yalta myth is that the new leaders, operating without Roosevelt's supposed naivety and without Churchill's supposed weakness, produced outcomes substantially identical to Yalta's. Truman was no one's image of a trusting innocent, yet he too accepted the Curzon Line, the Soviet sphere, and the paper guarantees. The continuity from Yalta to Potsdam across a complete change of Western leadership is the strongest evidence that the outcomes were structurally determined rather than personally negotiated.

## The View from Moscow: Stalin's Objectives at Yalta

The myth's telling often treats Stalin as a passive beneficiary of Western failure, a dictator who simply pocketed what naive diplomats handed him. The conference records show a more active and purposeful Soviet strategy. Stalin arrived at Yalta with clear objectives: international recognition of the Soviet Union's western security perimeter, confirmation of the territorial gains of 1939 to 1944, a Polish government that would never again serve as a corridor for invasion, and the Far Eastern concessions that would restore Russia's position in Asia lost in 1905. He pursued these objectives with a negotiator's patience and a conqueror's confidence, conceding language where language cost nothing and holding firm where power was at stake.

Stalin's flexibility on the Declaration on Liberated Europe is the clearest illustration. He accepted the declaration's democratic language because he understood that its implementation would occur under Soviet occupation, where Soviet authorities would define democracy in practice. The concession was tactical and costless. On the Polish government question, by contrast, he was immovable, because the Polish government would exercise real power on the Soviet Union's most sensitive frontier. The pattern reveals a leader who distinguished perfectly between paper and power, and who negotiated accordingly. The myth's image of Western leaders fooled by Soviet promises inverts the actual dynamic: Stalin made promises he knew he would not keep, the Western leaders accepted promises they knew they could not enforce, and both sides understood the arrangement. The cynicism was mutual and transparent. What was not transparent, to the Western publics, was the power imbalance that made the paper promises hollow.

## The Yalta Protocol's Eastern European Provisions: A Close Reading

The protocol's Polish section repays the close reading the myth never gives it. The three leaders agreed that Poland's eastern frontier should follow the Curzon Line, with digressions in certain regions of five to eight kilometers in Poland's favor, a detail that shows the negotiators attending to the map at a fine grain. They agreed that Poland should receive substantial accessions of territory in the north and west, that the final delimitation of the western frontier should await the peace settlement, and that the existing Provisional Government, then functioning in Poland, should be reorganized on a broader democratic basis with the inclusion of democratic leaders from Poland itself and from Poles abroad. A commission of Molotov, Harriman, and Clark Kerr was to consult in Moscow and make recommendations.

Each clause tells the same story. The border provisions record Soviet possession and defer the genuinely open question. The government provisions create a procedure, the Moscow commission, for achieving a result, a broadly based government, that the procedure had no power to produce. The election promise sets a standard without a mechanism. The close reading confirms the structural analysis: the protocol is a document written by men who knew what power they held and what power they lacked. The myth reads the document as a record of Western surrender. The text reads as a record of Western realism, the realism of leaders who wrote down the best terms available and hoped, against the evidence of Soviet behavior they already possessed, that the paper might constrain the power.

## The Counterfactual That Matters

Myth-busts benefit from stating the serious counterfactual explicitly, and the Yalta case has one worth examining: what if the Western leaders had refused to sign the Eastern European provisions, denounced the Soviet position, and broken the conference over Poland? The scenario is the myth's implicit demand, so it should be tested rather than assumed.

The immediate consequence would have been the rupture of the wartime coalition while Germany remained undefeated. The Soviet Union would have continued its advance regardless, since its armies needed no one's permission. The Far Eastern agreement would have collapsed, depriving the Pacific war of Soviet participation. The United Nations project, Roosevelt's central postwar objective, would have been stillborn. Poland's fate would have been unchanged: the Red Army held the country, the Lublin government functioned, and no Western denunciation could have altered either fact. The Western leaders would have purchased moral clarity at the price of every other objective, and the purchase would not have helped a single Pole.

This is the counterfactual that matters because it reveals the myth's hidden premise: that moral denunciation is a form of power. It is not. Power is divisions, logistics, and the willingness to use them. The Western leaders at Yalta lacked the first two in Eastern Europe and could not contemplate the third. Refusing to sign would have been a gesture, and gestures do not liberate occupied countries. The serious alternative to Yalta was not a better Yalta. It was a different war, fought against a different enemy, at a cost no democracy would have paid. The myth never names this alternative because naming it exposes the myth's emptiness.

## Verdict Restated

The Yalta sold-out myth claims that Roosevelt and Churchill betrayed Eastern Europe to Stalin through weak or treacherous diplomacy. The military record shows that Eastern Europe was already held by the Red Army when the conference opened, that no Western military option existed to displace Soviet power, and that the Yalta arrangements ratified the map rather than redrawing it. The suffering of Eastern Europe under Soviet domination was real and prolonged, but it flowed from Soviet occupation and Soviet violation of the Yalta provisions, not from the diplomacy at Livadia Palace. The myth persists because it served political purposes, converting structural tragedy into a story of betrayal. The revised understanding is less comforting and more accurate: armies decide what diplomats can only record.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does the Yalta sellout myth claim?

The myth claims that Roosevelt and Churchill betrayed Eastern Europe to Stalin at the February 1945 Yalta Conference. In its moderate form, it blames FDR's failing health and naive trust. In its harder form, it alleges deliberate treachery by Soviet agents inside the American delegation, particularly Alger Hiss. All versions share the premise that better individual diplomacy could have kept Eastern Europe free, and all versions treat the Cold War division of Europe as the product of diplomatic failure rather than military fact.

 The myth's political career extended well beyond the 1950s. It resurfaced whenever American foreign policy faced a setback, from the debates over Vietnam to the arguments about the end of the Cold War, each time offering the same simple moral: weakness and naivety lose countries, while firmness saves them. The persistence of this framing across decades shows how little the myth depends on the historical record and how much it depends on the needs of political argument.

### When and where was the Yalta Conference held?

The conference met at Livadia Palace near Yalta in the Crimea from February 4 to 11, 1945. Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin attended with their foreign ministers and full delegations. The timing matters for the myth-bust: the conference convened while the war in Europe was still being fought, with Soviet armies on the Oder and Western armies east of the Rhine. The military positions of that week, not the diplomatic language of the protocol, determined what the conference could accomplish.

 The palace itself symbolized the conference's constraints. The American and British delegations had traveled thousands of miles to meet Stalin on ground his armies controlled, in a Crimea still scarred by the war's fighting. The setting was not neutral, and neither was the military situation. Every discussion of Eastern Europe took place in the shadow of the armies outside the windows, a fact the participants understood even when later political narratives pretended otherwise.

### Who actually controlled Eastern Europe in February 1945?

The Soviet Union did, through the Red Army's occupation. By the conference's opening, Soviet forces held Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia, substantial parts of Hungary, and much of Czechoslovakia. The Vistula-Oder offensive of January 1945 had carried Soviet troops to the Oder, roughly forty miles from Berlin. These were completed military facts recorded in the operational records of both sides, not diplomatic positions open to negotiation at the conference table. No Western army stood anywhere near these territories, and no Allied plan contemplated contesting them by force.

 The occupation was also an administrative fact, not merely a military one. Soviet military administrations, security services, and political cadres followed the advancing fronts and established control over local government, police, and communications. By February 1945, the Soviet-backed authorities in Warsaw, Bucharest, and Sofia were functioning governments with armies of occupation behind them. Diplomacy could not conjure alternative authorities into existence where none stood on the ground.

### What was promised in the Liberated Europe declaration?

The Declaration on Liberated Europe was the Yalta document addressing the political future of the liberated countries. All three leaders signed it. It committed the Allies to help liberated peoples solve their problems by democratic means and to support free elections. The Soviet Union systematically violated these commitments through 1948, imposing Communist-controlled governments across Eastern Europe. The declaration's failure was a failure of enforcement, and enforcement required military power the West did not possess in the relevant territories. The gap between the signed promises and the facts on the ground was the central diplomatic reality of 1945, and closing it would have demanded a confrontation nobody at Yalta was prepared to begin.

 The declaration's drafting history reinforces this reading. The American delegation proposed the declaration partly to create a public standard, knowing that its enforcement would depend on conditions beyond the conference's control. The British shared this understanding. The document was a statement of principles for the postwar order, not a contract with an enforcement mechanism. Treating it as a contract that the West negligently failed to enforce misreads what the signatories believed they were signing.

### What did Yalta decide about Poland's borders?

Yalta set Poland's eastern border at the Curzon Line, the boundary proposed by Britain in 1919 and held by the Soviet Union since 1939, and provided for substantial Polish territorial compensation in the north and west at Germany's expense on a provisional basis. The western border's exact line was left for the final settlement and confirmed at [Potsdam](/2005/12/22/potsdam-conference-july-august-1945/) on the Oder and western Neisse. The eastern decision ratified Soviet possession; the western decision was provisional and deferred.

 The provisional character of the western border decision was deliberate. Roosevelt and Churchill resisted fixing the final line at Yalta precisely because they understood that the territorial settlement would require a full peace conference. The deferral was not evasion but prudence: the Big Three were still fighting a war, and the final disposition of Germany's eastern territories belonged to the peace settlement. Potsdam's confirmation of the line reflected the same military facts that had shaped the provisional agreement.

### Did Yalta abandon Poland's London-based government-in-exile?

The London-based Polish government-in-exile was not recognized as Poland's authority in the Yalta arrangements. The agreement called for reorganizing the Soviet-backed Lublin government on a broader democratic basis with the inclusion of democratic leaders from Poland and from abroad, including London Poles, followed by free elections. The reorganization produced a Communist-dominated government, the elections were rigged, and the London Poles were marginalized. The abandonment was real in its consequences, but the London government had no presence on Polish territory and no means of asserting authority there.

 The London government's isolation had deepened throughout 1944. The Warsaw Uprising's destruction eliminated the Home Army as a political force, the Soviet advance brought the Lublin Committee into the capital, and the Western Allies, bound by the coalition's military arrangements, could offer the London Poles sympathy but not territory. By February 1945, the London government existed as a legal fiction sustained by British hospitality, with no lever on events inside Poland. Yalta recorded this reality; it did not create it.

### Did FDR's health affect his Yalta performance?

Roosevelt was seriously ill at Yalta. Howard Bruenn had diagnosed congestive heart failure in March 1944, and the president's physical decline was visible by February 1945. But the participants' accounts describe his decision-making as functional, and his positions were consistent with long-developed administration policy and with the structural constraints. The myth treats his illness as the explanation for the outcomes, but the constraints were military rather than personal, and no healthy negotiator could have displaced the Red Army by force of personality.

 The counterfactual test is decisive here. Suppose a president in perfect health, confrontational in manner and uncompromising in demands, had sat in Roosevelt's chair. The Soviet armies would still have held Poland, the Western armies would still have stood east of the Rhine, and Stalin would still have controlled the territory under discussion. Confrontation might have produced angrier minutes and a colder atmosphere. It could not have produced a different Poland, because Poland's fate was not being decided by the men in the room.

### Was Alger Hiss a Soviet spy at Yalta?

Alger Hiss was a State Department adviser present at Yalta, and the evidence assembled before this article's publication date, including the Venona decrypts, supports the conclusion that he had been a Soviet intelligence source. But no documentary evidence connects any Yalta concession on Eastern Europe to Hiss's advice or intervention. The major decisions were made by the three principals on the basis of staff positions and the military map. Hiss's documented work at the conference concerned the United Nations organization questions.

 Hiss's actual documented contributions at Yalta concerned the trusteeship provisions and the Security Council voting formula, subjects far removed from the Polish border or the Declaration on Liberated Europe. The conference records, the delegation's internal memoranda, and the accounts of his colleagues place him in the technical discussions of international organization, not in the great-power bargaining over Eastern Europe. The myth's version requires Hiss to have shaped decisions in rooms where the records do not place him and on subjects outside his assignment.

### Could the Western Allies have used military force in Eastern Europe?

No realistic military option existed. The Western armies had not yet crossed the Rhine when Yalta opened, the Germany-first grand strategy committed the main effort to the campaign against Germany itself, and displacing Soviet forces would have required war against the Soviet Union. No Allied plan contemplated such a war, no Allied public would have supported it, and the geographic and logistical obstacles were prohibitive. The myth implies a card the Western leaders held and failed to play; the card did not exist.

 The logistical dimension reinforces the point. The Western supply system in early 1945 was stretched to support the advance into Germany itself, with the famous Red Ball Express and its successors straining to move fuel and ammunition forward. A redeployment toward Eastern Europe would have required not merely a change of orders but a different war: new supply lines across hundreds of miles, new air cover, and the abandonment of the agreed campaign. The general staffs never drew up such a plan because the political leadership never ordered one, and the political leadership never ordered one because the war's agreed strategy pointed west.

### What was the Far Eastern agreement at Yalta?

In a secret protocol, the Soviet Union agreed to enter the war against Japan two to three months after Germany's defeat, in exchange for southern Sakhalin, the Kurile Islands, and rights in Manchuria and Port Arthur. Roosevelt sought the agreement because American planners wanted Soviet participation to shorten the Pacific war and save American lives. The agreement constrained the Western position on Eastern Europe, since confronting Stalin over Poland would have jeopardized the Soviet commitment the planners considered essential. This linkage is central to understanding why Roosevelt and Churchill pressed Poland only within limits: the Pacific war's costs made the Soviet entry a strategic priority that could not be traded away.

 The Joint Chiefs' estimates of the cost of invading Japan without Soviet help ran into the hundreds of thousands of American casualties, figures that weighed on every Far Eastern discussion. Roosevelt's pursuit of the Soviet commitment was not a personal whim but a response to military advice. The concession of Sakhalin, the Kuriles, and the Manchurian rights was the price of shortening the Pacific war. Whether the price was too high is a legitimate historical question; the atomic bomb's subsequent role belongs to a different article. What matters here is that the bargain tied the Western leaders to the coalition's continuation.

### How did the Soviet Union violate the Yalta agreements?

The violations were systematic across 1945 to 1948. In Poland, the Communist-dominated government suppressed the opposition and rigged the January 1947 elections. In Romania, Bulgaria, and Hungary, Soviet-backed authorities eliminated non-Communist parties through intimidation and fraudulent elections. In Czechoslovakia, the Communist coup of February 1948 ended the country's democracy. Each violation breached the Declaration on Liberated Europe's commitments to free elections and representative government. The responsibility for the breaches lies with the Soviet Union, and the article's revision of the sellout myth in no way excuses them. The breaches were deliberate policy decisions, executed through a consistent pattern of subversion, and their human cost across millions of lives stands as a documented fact of the postwar order.

 The pattern's documentation is extensive. American diplomatic reporting from Warsaw, Bucharest, Sofia, Budapest, and Prague recorded the arrests, the intimidated voters, the falsified counts, and the suppressed newspapers in real time. The State Department's files for 1945 through 1948 constitute a running indictment of Soviet non-compliance, compiled by observers on the ground rather than by later historians. The Truman administration's progressive disillusionment was a response to this accumulating evidence, not to a sudden ideological conversion.

### What was Churchill's role at Yalta?

Churchill participated fully in all major Yalta decisions, and his positions on Eastern Europe were substantially similar to Roosevelt's. He accepted the Curzon Line, the provisional western border, the Lublin-based reorganization, and the Declaration on Liberated Europe. His later critical view of the Yalta outcomes, expressed in his 1953 memoirs, was substantially retrospective, written with the Cold War developed and with political incentive to distance himself. The contemporary record shows a participant in the arrangements, not a protester against them.

 The British records confirm the alignment. Eden's minutes, the Foreign Office briefs, and Churchill's own conference notes show the prime minister pressing for Polish concessions within the same framework Roosevelt accepted. Churchill's particular concern was the London Poles, for whom Britain bore a special responsibility as their host, but his proposed solutions, the commission, the broader government, the elections, were the solutions the conference adopted. The later memoirs' tone of foreboding does not appear in the contemporary documents.

### How did the Yalta myth become a political weapon?

From 1946 onward, American conservatives used the sellout narrative to attack Democratic foreign policy. Churchill's Iron Curtain speech in March 1946 and the Berlin Blockade of 1948 and 1949 gave the narrative continuing relevance. Through the 1950s it became a standard Republican position: the Roosevelt administration had been soft on communism and responsible for the loss of Eastern Europe. The narrative converted the complex structural origins of the Cold War into a simple story of betrayal, which made it politically potent regardless of its historical accuracy.

 The myth's utility extended beyond elections. It shaped the personnel politics of the early Cold War, contributing to the loyalty investigations and the climate of suspicion that damaged careers and distorted policy debate. The charge that Yalta proved the State Department's unreliability was used to purge expertise and intimidate dissent. The political weapon thus did double damage: it misdescribed the past and it impoverished the government's capacity to understand the present.

### What do the Yalta Conference records show?

The published conference records show three leaders negotiating within acknowledged constraints. The plenary minutes record Stalin's insistence on the Curzon Line and the Lublin basis for the Polish government, Roosevelt's pursuit of the United Nations and Far Eastern agreements, and Churchill's efforts on Poland and the Balkans. The records show hard bargaining on details but no Western surrender of positions that military power could have sustained. The companion article on [the Yalta Conference itself](/2006/01/29/yalta-conference-february-1945-postwar-division/) examines the proceedings in full; this article addresses only the myth built upon them.

 The records also show what the conference did not do. It did not partition Europe into spheres in the manner of the later Cold War map; the Declaration on Liberated Europe explicitly rejected such an outcome. It did not authorize the Soviet Union to impose Communist governments; it required the opposite. The gap between the documents and the subsequent reality is the measure of Soviet violation, not of Western intent. Reading the protocol with attention to what it actually says is the first step in dismantling the myth.

### Did Yalta cause the Cold War?

Yalta did not cause the Cold War in the sense the myth claims. The Cold War's origins lie in the Soviet Union's determination to control Eastern Europe, a determination expressed through military occupation and then through the systematic violation of the Yalta provisions. The conference recorded the military outcome of the war's final year; the confrontation developed as the West recognized, through 1945 to 1947, that the Soviet Union would not honor its commitments. The myth locates the origin in Western diplomatic failure; the evidence locates it in Soviet policy.

 Alternative explanations for the Cold War's origins that center on Western provocation or on impersonal structural forces have their own scholarly literatures, but they do not rehabilitate the sellout myth. Whatever role Western actions played in the developing confrontation, the specific charge that Yalta's diplomacy surrendered Eastern Europe fails against the military record. The Cold War began where the myth says it began, in the ruins of the wartime alliance, but it began for reasons the myth misidentifies.

### Why do historians reject the sellout framing?

Historians working within this article's publication horizon reject the sellout framing because the military-diplomatic evidence does not support it. The assessments vary on details, FDR's performance, the wisdom of specific concessions, the handling of the Polish question, but converge on the structural point: the Yalta arrangements ratified military reality, and no plausible alternative diplomacy could have produced a free Eastern Europe given the Red Army's occupation. The consensus treats the myth as a political narrative rather than a historical explanation.

 The convergence is worth emphasizing because the myth presents the scholarly debate as unsettled in its favor. It is not. Disagreements persist about Roosevelt's judgment, about Churchill's foresight, about whether the Declaration on Liberated Europe was wisely drafted, and about the handling of specific issues like the Far Eastern protocol. But on the central question, whether Yalta's diplomacy caused Eastern Europe's fate, the historians working from the documentary record agree that it did not. The myth survives in politics, not in scholarship.

### What misunderstanding about Yalta matters most?

The most important misunderstanding is the belief that Eastern Europe's fate was decided at the conference table. It was decided on the battlefields of 1944: in the destruction of Army Group Center, in the advance to the Vistula and the Oder, in the occupations of Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia. By February 1945, the diplomats were writing down what the armies had accomplished. Understanding Yalta requires understanding the war that preceded it, and the myth's fundamental error is to begin the story in February 1945 as though the preceding year had not happened.

 This reordering of the story is the myth's most consequential distortion. By beginning in February 1945, the myth presents Eastern Europe as a diplomatic prize whose disposition was open. By beginning in June 1944, with the destruction of Army Group Center, the story becomes one of military conquest whose political consequences diplomacy could only modestly shape. The choice of starting point determines the conclusion, and the myth's starting point is chosen to indict the diplomats rather than to explain the outcome.

### How does Yalta illustrate the series' institutional thesis?

The Yalta case supports the thesis at the committee-architecture level. The conference was a coordinated summit of three national delegations operating within a coalition structure, the Germany-first strategy, the division of operational effort, the Far Eastern linkage, that determined the range of possible outcomes. The myth's claim that better individual diplomacy could have changed Eastern Europe's fate is unsupported because the individuals operated within constraints set by the coalition's architecture and the military map. Institutions and armies, not personalities, decided.

 The committee-architecture analysis also explains why the myth's counterfactuals fail. Imagined alternatives, a tougher Roosevelt, a more insistent Churchill, a delegation purged of suspected agents, all operate at the level of personality while leaving the architecture untouched. But the architecture, the strategy, the force dispositions, the coalition's needs, was what constrained the outcomes. Changing the personalities without changing the architecture changes the atmosphere of the negotiations, not their results.

### Which sources extend the Yalta reassessment further?

Readers should begin with the primary records: the published Yalta Conference proceedings, the State Department's Foreign Relations volumes for the conferences, and the wartime correspondence of the three leaders. For the military context, the operational histories of the 1944 Soviet offensives and the Vistula-Oder operation establish the map. For the political aftermath, the State Department's postwar reporting and the congressional record document the violation pattern. The labeled post-publication works noted in the historians section extend the inquiry beyond this article's horizon.

 The documentary foundation remains the essential starting point. The Foreign Relations of the United States volumes for the Yalta Conference reproduce the American record in full, including the briefing papers that show what the delegation expected and the minutes that show what it achieved. The British Documents on British Policy Overseas provide the parallel record. Together they allow readers to test every claim in this article, including its claims about the myth, against the sources the participants left behind.
