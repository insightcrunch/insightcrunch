Slum rehabilitation law in India begins from an uncomfortable fact that every other part of the subject must be read against: no Indian statute confers an enforceable right to housing. A family that has lived for thirty years on land it does not own holds, in strict law, a bundle of procedural protections rather than a title or an entitlement. A competent authority may have to take permission before an eviction suit is filed against it. A municipality may have to serve a notice, hold a hearing and record reasons before a structure is brought down. A redevelopment scheme may promise the family a free tenement, but only if the family can prove residence before a date fixed by an executive decision and only if enough of its neighbours consent to the scheme. The statutes, the schemes and the judgments that make up this field are therefore best understood as a law of process: they regulate how a settlement may be declared, improved, cleared or rebuilt, and what must happen before a person may be removed from it, while stopping short of saying that the person has a right to be housed at all.

That framing explains the shape of everything that follows. Because the entitlement is missing, the practical questions in this field are never "what am I owed" but "which statute covers the land I stand on, what stage has the process reached, and what does the authority still have to do before it may act". The answers differ by State, by land ownership and by the category into which the settlement falls, and the differences are not technicalities. A settlement declared under a State slum statute enters a regime of surveys, eligibility lists and redevelopment consent. A settlement standing on a footpath or beside a railway line stands, after the Supreme Court's demolition judgment of November 2024, largely outside the most detailed procedural code the Court has written, because that code expressly excludes the ground on which such settlements stand. A tenant inside a declared slum area in Delhi holds a protection created in 1956 that a resident of an undeclared settlement in the same city does not. This article works through the whole structure: the central Act of 1956 and its eviction control, the State statutes where most of the law sits, the eligibility cut-off that decides who is rehabilitated and who is merely removed, the housing missions that deliver houses without creating rights, and the two lines of Supreme Court authority, one constitutional and one procedural, that have done the work legislation declined to do.

## Four questions that decide every case in this field

The whole subject can be organised, for any given settlement, by four questions, and the order in which they are asked matters because each answer forecloses part of the next. The first question is the category of the land: is the settlement on private land, on municipal or State land, on railway land, on the margin of a road or drain or water body, or on land reserved in a development plan for a public purpose? The land answer selects the removal power, and after November 2024 it also selects whether the detailed demolition procedure applies at all. The second question is the status of the settlement: has it been declared under the State slum statute, surveyed for a scheme, or counted in a census or a policy list, or does it stand unrecognised in every official record? The status answer selects the protective regime, because declaration and survey are the gateways to eviction control and eligibility alike. The third question is the household's date: can this family prove residence before the operative cut-off, and with which documents? The date answer selects the remedy, rehabilitation as a scheme entitlement on one side of it and bare process on the other. The fourth question is the stage of the process: has a notice been served, in the manner the applicable law requires, has a hearing been held, has an order been made, and has the time to challenge it run? The stage answer selects what can still be done, by the resident to resist and by the authority to proceed.

The four questions are not a summary of the law; they are its operating system, and they explain the feature of the field that outsiders find hardest to credit, which is that two settlements indistinguishable to the eye can stand in entirely different legal worlds. A declared settlement of pre-cut-off households on municipal land, at the stage before any notice, sits at the maximum of protection the system offers: eviction control, scheme eligibility, constitutional doctrine and, for structures outside the excluded land categories, the 2024 procedure all run in its favour. An undeclared settlement of post-cut-off households on a railway margin, facing a removal party, sits at the minimum: no scheme, no list, no code, and a constitutional argument to be carried to a court against time. Between the poles, every real case is a mixture, and the work of the lawyer, the community organisation and the honest official alike is to locate the settlement on each of the four axes before saying anything about what the law allows. The sections that follow take the axes in the order the instruments created them: the statutes that draw the categories, the schemes that work the dates, and the judgments that govern the process.

## Why the law of slums is State law

The constitutional allocation of the subject explains the fragmentation. Land, including rights in or over land and land tenures, sits in the State List, and local government sits there with it, so the power to legislate on the improvement, clearance and redevelopment of settlements rests with the States. The Union Parliament has no general slum statute for the country, and the one central enactment in the field, the Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1956, extends to the Union territories rather than to the States, which is why its living operation is concentrated in the National Capital Territory of Delhi. The consequence is a map of parallel statutes, similar in their architecture of declaration, improvement and clearance, different in their dates, their authorities and their local detail. A lawyer advising on a settlement in Mumbai works from the Maharashtra Act of 1971; in Chennai from the Tamil Nadu Act of 1971; in Bengaluru from the Karnataka Act of 1973; in Lucknow from the Uttar Pradesh Act of 1962; in Kolkata from the West Bengal Act of 1972; and in Delhi from the central Act of 1956 read with the Delhi Urban Shelter Improvement Board Act, 2010.

The municipal layer sits beneath the State statutes and matters because the Constitution assigns the function without assigning the power. The Seventy-fourth Amendment placed "slum improvement and upgradation" at item ten of the Twelfth Schedule, among the eighteen matters a State legislature may devolve to municipalities, a design the series article on the [74th Amendment and urban local bodies](/2018/11/01/74th-amendment-urban-local-bodies/) examines in full. The Schedule entry names the function; it does not fund it, staff it or compel a State to hand it over, and in most States the work is done not by the elected municipality but by a specialised board or authority created by the State slum statute itself. The result is an institutional pattern that repeats across the country: a slum development board, a slum clearance board or a rehabilitation authority holds the statutory powers, the municipality holds the municipal services and the demolition machinery under its own Act, and the resident deals with both while being governed, in the strict sense, by neither's promise of a house.

### What is the difference between a notified slum and an unnotified settlement?

A notified or declared slum is a settlement that the competent authority has formally brought under the slum statute by a declaration, usually after a survey, and that declaration is what switches on the statute's protections, its eligibility machinery and its redevelopment process. An unnotified settlement, however old, stands outside that regime until a declaration is made.

The distinction carries the whole practical weight of the subject. Declaration is the gateway to the eligibility list, and the eligibility list is the gateway to a rehabilitation tenement. A settlement that has never been surveyed or declared generates no list, and its residents hold only the general protections of municipal law and the Constitution. The census machinery recognises the same divide from the statistical side: the Census of India counts settlements in three classes, notified slums recognised under a slum statute, recognised slums acknowledged by a State or local body without formal notification, and identified slums picked out by the census itself against a definition based on congestion and the condition of the housing. The 2011 Census, counting on that basis, recorded a slum population of about sixty-five million people. The figure matters less than the structure behind it: a large share of the counted settlements, and a larger share of the uncounted occupation of pavements, drains and railway margins, sits in the second and third classes, where recognition is administrative grace rather than statutory status. The count also understates by design: the census canvasses the slums of the towns within its frame for the purpose, and the pavement population, the smallest settlements and the settlements of towns outside the frame enter the national figure weakly or not at all, so that the official number and the visible city part company at exactly the margin this article is concerned with. Policy made on the counted figure therefore plans for a population smaller and more legible than the one the law will actually meet, and the gap between the two is filled, in practice, by the cut-off and eligibility machinery deciding case by case who among the uncounted will be treated as belonging to the counted.

### How does a settlement come to be declared a slum?

Under the central Act of 1956, the competent authority declares an area a slum area where the buildings are unfit for human habitation, or detrimental to safety, health or morals, by reason of dilapidation, overcrowding, faulty arrangement or design of buildings, narrow streets, or want of ventilation, light or sanitation. State statutes use materially similar tests.

The declaration is an administrative act with legal consequences in both directions: it subjects the area to improvement and clearance powers, and it subjects evictions within the area to the statute's controls. The conditions in the statutory test are worth reading slowly, because they describe a building stock rather than a population: it is the dilapidation of the structures, the arrangement of the streets and the absence of light, air and sanitation that the authority must find, and a settlement of sound self-built housing on contested land may fall outside the test just as a rented tenement district of crumbling authorised buildings may fall within it. The slum of the statutes is a legal category built out of physical conditions, and it has never mapped exactly onto the settlements that public discussion calls by the same name. Everything in the field flows from whether the declaratory act has been performed for the ground in question.

## The Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1956

The central Act, Act 96 of 1956, is the oldest instrument in the field and still the operative slum statute for Delhi. Its scheme is the template from which the State Acts were drawn, and its structure is worth setting out because the same moves recur everywhere: declare, improve where improvement is possible, clear where it is not, acquire where the work requires the land, and control eviction throughout.

The Act opens with the declaration power in Section 3, described above, vested in a competent authority appointed by the Administrator. Chapter III then arms that authority with the improvement powers. Section 4 addresses buildings unfit for human habitation and empowers the authority to require the owner to execute works of improvement within a stated time, with power to carry out the works on default and recover the cost. Section 6A, inserted later, lets the authority enforce restrictions on the erection of buildings in slum areas, so that a declared area does not keep growing in the very condition that justified the declaration. Section 7 empowers the authority to order the demolition of a building that is unfit for human habitation and incapable of repair at reasonable cost, after hearing the owner. The clearance machinery follows in Sections 9 and 10: where the authority is satisfied that the most satisfactory method of dealing with the conditions in a slum area is the demolition of all the buildings in the area, it may make a slum clearance order, which is then confirmed through the process the Act prescribes, and Section 12 empowers the Central Government to acquire land where acquisition is needed to let the competent authority execute works in the area, on payment of compensation. The pattern is characteristic of the statute's generation: the powers are powers over buildings and land, framed by condition and fitness, and the human content enters through the eviction control rather than through any promise of rehousing.

That eviction control is Section 19, the provision for which the Act is remembered and the strongest statutory protection in the entire field. Its text repays attention. Notwithstanding anything in any other law, no person shall, except with the previous permission in writing of the competent authority, institute any suit or proceeding for obtaining a decree or order for the eviction of a tenant from any building or land in a slum area, or execute a decree or order for eviction obtained earlier. The bar therefore operates at both ends of the litigation: a landlord cannot begin, and a landlord holding a decree cannot execute, without the authority's prior written permission. The authority, on application, must give the parties an opportunity of being heard, make such summary inquiry as it thinks fit, and grant or refuse permission by a written order. In deciding, Section 19(4) directs it to take into account whether alternative accommodation within the means of the tenant would be available if the tenant were evicted, whether the eviction is in the interest of improvement and clearance of the slum areas, and any other prescribed factors. A refusal must record brief reasons and a copy must go to the applicant, and Section 20 gives a person aggrieved by a refusal an appeal to the Administrator, whose decision is final.

Three features of Section 19 deserve emphasis because later law is weaker on exactly these points. The first is that the protection is a permission regime, not a notice regime: the default is that the eviction does not proceed at all, and the burden of moving the authority sits on the person seeking eviction. The second is that the statutory factors force the deciding authority to confront the tenant's means and the availability of alternative accommodation as part of the legal test, not as a matter of grace. The third is the limit, which is equally instructive: the section protects a tenant, and its language of tenancy fits the mid-century city of rented rooms in declared areas. Much of the informal city that grew later is not tenancy at all but occupation of public land without any landlord, and for that occupation Section 19 has nothing to say. The strongest protection in the field is thus bounded by both geography and legal category: it works inside declared areas in the territories where the Act runs, for persons the law recognises as tenants.

The improvement chapters repay one further observation, because they contain the seed of the whole later law of in situ upgrading. The 1956 Act does not treat clearance as the default. Its sequence is improvement first: the authority identifies the buildings that can be made fit, compels the works at the owner's cost, restricts fresh construction that would deepen the condition, and reserves demolition and clearance for the buildings and areas where improvement will not answer. That sequence, improvement before clearance, is the statutory ancestor of every later policy that prefers upgrading and redevelopment in place to relocation, from the board improvement programmes to the in situ vertical of the housing missions. It is equally the ancestor of the field's characteristic disappointment, because improvement powers depend on the authority's initiative and budget, and in the decades when the Act was the capital's only slum law the improvement chapters were used sparingly against the growth of the settlements they governed. A power to improve that is not exercised does not hold a settlement in legal suspense; it merely leaves the settlement to grow until clearance or redevelopment becomes the only remaining instrument, which is the biography of more than one Delhi settlement that began as a cluster the Act could have serviced in its first decade.

### What does Section 19 stop a landlord from doing?

Section 19 stops a landlord from suing to evict a tenant from a building or land in a declared slum area, and from executing an eviction decree already obtained, unless the competent authority has first given written permission. The bar operates before the suit and after the decree, at both ends of the litigation.

The working of the permission deserves a closer look, because it is the one place in the older law where the merits of an eviction are examined before the eviction exists. The inquiry is summary, but it is not a rubber stamp: the authority must hear the parties, and the factors in Section 19(4) convert what would elsewhere be a landlord's unilateral legal right into a discretionary public decision in which the tenant's means and the availability of alternative accommodation within those means are express considerations. A landlord whose tenant can be rehoused within the tenant's means, and whose proposal serves the improvement and clearance of the area, will ordinarily obtain permission; a landlord seeking to clear a tenement for a more profitable use, against tenants with nowhere within their means to go, faces a statutory obstacle that no drafting of the plaint can remove. The appeal to the Administrator under Section 20 completes the design: the refusal of permission is appealable by the landlord, and the finality of the Administrator's decision keeps the question administrative rather than reopening it as ordinary civil litigation. The whole apparatus belongs to a period when the declared slum area was typically a district of rented rooms and chawls, and its protection is calibrated to that tenancy. Where the settlement is a colony of owner-built huts on public land, there is no landlord, no tenancy and no Section 19, which is the precise point at which the 1956 model stops protecting the informal city that grew after it.

The Act's later history in the States is equally instructive. Offered to the country as a central statute, it was not adopted as the general law of the States, which preferred to legislate for themselves, several of them within a few years of the central Act and most of them in its image. The Law Commission of India returned to the position in its 138th Report, a study devoted to legislative protection for slum and pavement dwellers, whose synopsis of the central and State enactments records the same structure from the official side: protections that are real but local, category-bound and procedural, stopping at the boundary of the declared area and the recognised tenancy. That structure, and not any single statute, is the legislative inheritance on which the constitutional case law of the nineteen eighties was built.

Delhi gives the Act its modern setting. The Delhi Urban Shelter Improvement Board, created under the Delhi Urban Shelter Improvement Board Act, 2010, is the agency charged with the improvement and rehabilitation of the capital's jhuggi settlements, and the operative policy instrument is the Delhi Slum and JJ Rehabilitation and Relocation Policy, 2015. The policy draws the capital's version of the eligibility line: settlements that existed before 1 January 2006 are within the rehabilitation frame, and settlements are not to be removed without rehabilitation, with the resident's eligibility turning on documentary proof of residence before the policy date. The Delhi High Court had already supplied the constitutional reasoning behind that posture in Sudama Singh v. Government of NCT of Delhi in 2010, holding that the right to housing forms part of the right to life for the city's jhuggi dwellers and that eviction without a prior survey, consultation and a rehabilitation plan would not survive Article 21. In Ajay Maken v. Union of India in 2019 the same court returned to the subject after the demolition of a settlement at Shakur Basti, restated the right to housing of long-standing settlement residents, and required that forced evictions follow a protocol of survey, consultation and rehabilitation planning rather than treating the settlement as a mere encroachment to be cleared. The two decisions matter beyond Delhi because they show the division of labour that defines the field: a statute or policy fixes the date and the machinery, and a court supplies the proposition that removal without process and without a rehabilitation plan is unconstitutional.

Delhi also displays, in an unusually pure form, the dependence of protection on the list. The capital's settlements are administered through the Board's surveys and the policy's date, and a settlement that figures in the Board's records with residents documented before 2006 stands inside the rehabilitation frame, while a settlement of identical age that the survey missed, or whose residents' documents begin later, stands outside it and is treated in the municipal register as an encroachment on public land. The litigation the Ajay Maken case concluded arose from exactly that sorting: a settlement beside a railway line, demolished in the winter of 2015 in an operation its residents challenged, where the contest in court was over the survey, the notice and the rehabilitation obligation rather than over any title. The case's practical legacy in the capital is procedural in the Delhi manner: the forced eviction of a long-standing settlement is to be preceded by a survey of its residents, by consultation through the Board, and by a rehabilitation plan settled before the demolition rather than improvised after it. For the rest of the country the Delhi line of cases functions as persuasive statement of what Article 21 requires of any city that proposes to clear a settled population, and it is cited in the High Courts in those terms.

## Municipal demolition law: the machinery the judgments regulate

Between the slum statutes and the Constitution stands a third body of law that does most of the actual demolishing in Indian cities: the municipal Acts. Their architecture is standard across States, and it must be understood because both the Olga Tellis litigation and the 2024 directions are, in form, interventions into this machinery rather than replacements of it. A municipal corporation is charged with keeping public streets and places free of obstruction, and its Act empowers the commissioner to remove encroachments on streets, drains and public land, in the summary class of cases without the notice that ordinary adjudication would require, which is the power Section 314 of the Bombay statute conferred and Olga Tellis upheld. The same Acts separately govern buildings: construction without a sanctioned plan, or in deviation from one, exposes the structure to a notice requiring the owner to show cause, to the sealing of the premises in some regimes, and to an order of demolition against which an appeal lies to the appellate forum the State has designated, with compounding of minor deviations on payment of a fee available where the statute or the building rules allow it. A ruinous or dangerous building travels down a third track, of notice to make safe or vacate, aimed at collapse rather than illegality. The three tracks have different subjects: the encroachment track addresses occupation of public space, the unauthorised construction track addresses the building's want of permission, and the dangerous building track addresses its condition, and a single settlement can find itself proceeded against on any of them.

### Who orders a demolition under municipal law, and where does the appeal lie?

The order is made by the municipal commissioner or the officer holding the delegated power, after the notice and hearing the municipal Act prescribes for that class of case. The appeal lies to the appellate forum the State has designated, and beyond it to the High Court in its writ jurisdiction, where demolition disputes are in practice fought.

In the largest cities the designated forum is a specialised appellate tribunal, and its docket is the surest index of how much demolition a city's municipal machinery attempts in a year. The street is where the municipal scheme and the livelihood question meet, and Parliament has once legislated for exactly that meeting in the adjacent field. When the national legislature decided to protect the livelihoods practised on public space by vendors, it did not enact a right to vend; it enacted procedure, a survey of vendors, certificates of vending, town vending committees, and a bar on eviction or relocation until the survey was complete, in the statute examined in the series article on the [Street Vendors Act 2014](/2019/01/15/street-vendors-act-2014-explained/), which is the due process answer Parliament gave for the pavement as a workplace. No equivalent statute exists for the pavement as a home. The contrast is structural and worth stating plainly: the vendor's occupation of public space is governed by a national procedural code with a survey and a certificate at its centre, while the dweller's occupation of the same space is governed by the municipal summary power, the rehabilitation schemes of the State, and the constitutional case law. The 2024 directions entered this structure from the constitutional side, and their design choice, to build a procedure on top of the municipal tracks for some categories of structure and to leave the encroachment track untouched, is the choice the rest of this article examines.

## The State statutes and the Maharashtra model

The State Acts of the decades after 1956 follow the central template through its first three moves, declaration, improvement and clearance. Their clustering in time is itself evidence: Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu legislated in 1971, Karnataka in 1973 and West Bengal in 1972, in the years when the census counts of the urban slum population had become a political fact no State government could leave to a borrowed central Act, and when the politics of the metropolitan city, in Bombay above all, had made the settlement vote a constituency to be organised rather than a nuisance to be cleared. The statutes of that wave share more than dates. They share the discovery that a slum statute is also an electoral instrument, since the declaration of a settlement, the issue of a photo pass and the promise of a tenement are acts of recognition that residents remember, and the cut-off politics examined below is unintelligible without that discovery. The Maharashtra Slum Areas (Improvement, Clearance and Redevelopment) Act, 1971 is the fullest example of the form. Its very name records an addition: redevelopment, which the 1956 Act does not organise as a scheme, became the centre of the Maharashtra statute through Chapter I-A, inserted in 1995, which created the Slum Rehabilitation Authority and the Slum Rehabilitation Scheme. The older chapters of the Act still matter and are still used: declaration of slum areas by the competent authority, improvement works, clearance orders, the owner's right to redevelop after a clearance order under Section 12, the competent authority's redevelopment power under Section 13 where the owner does not act, and acquisition of land under Section 14. Chapter I-A then overlays a different and more ambitious machine.

Section 3A constitutes the Slum Rehabilitation Authority for a Slum Rehabilitation Area, Section 3B provides for a general Slum Rehabilitation Scheme to be prepared and published, Section 3C empowers the declaration of an area as a Slum Rehabilitation Area, and Section 3D applies the earlier chapters to such areas with modifications, under which the owner's preferential right to redevelop survives in modified form and the Authority may take up redevelopment where the landholders or occupants do not come forward within a reasonable time. The Supreme Court has confirmed that structure, holding that the landowner holds a preferential right to propose redevelopment of a rehabilitation area and that acquisition and Authority-led redevelopment are answers to the owner standing back, not substitutes that erase the preference. The courts have kept the scheme's categories supple where the statute's text allowed it. In a decision of 2025 concerning a settlement on land of the State housing authority in Mumbai, the Supreme Court held that a slum recorded in the official census of slums is a slum for the purposes of the Act without a separate declaration, so that redevelopment of a censused settlement under the development control regulations requires no additional notification to become lawful; the appellants, who had occupied transit accommodation during the widening of an expressway and claimed as tenants of the housing authority, were held to be transit occupants rather than eligible dwellers, and their resistance to a scheme the majority had accepted was not entertained. The two halves of that decision are the scheme in miniature: generous on the status of the settlement, strict on the status of the claimant, with the eligibility list doing the decisive work in both.

The Authority's own guidelines for the implementation of rehabilitation schemes in Greater Mumbai, published in 1997, supply the operating detail that the statute leaves open, including the consent threshold discussed below.

The economics of the Maharashtra model are what distinguish it from every clearance statute that came before, and they must be understood because the legal questions in the field are usually the economics wearing legal dress. A developer, or the residents' co-operative society acting with a developer, redevelops the site in place. Eligible residents receive permanent tenements in the rehabilitation component without payment. The cost of that component is carried by a sale component: additional floor space, sanctioned as incentive, which the developer sells on the open market, together with transferable development rights that can be used or sold for use on other plots. Land in a city like Mumbai is the subsidy: the same parcel, rebuilt at higher density, is expected to pay for the rehousing of the people who stood on it and a profit besides. The model's strengths and its pathologies both follow from that design. It requires no budgetary outlay, which is why governments favour it. It works only where land values are high enough to carry the cross-subsidy, which is why it is a model of the expensive city and not of the small town. And it makes the developer a central actor in a welfare process, which is why the questions that reach the courts are so often about consent, eligibility and the preferential rights of owners rather than about the condition of the housing.

The procedure of a Maharashtra rehabilitation scheme, stated as the sequence a resident actually experiences, shows how many legal events stand between a settlement and a building. A proposal is initiated, by the landowner exercising the preferential right, by the residents' proposed co-operative society with a developer, or by the Authority itself. The settlement is surveyed, structure by structure and household by household, and the survey hardens into the eligibility list, certified after scrutiny of the documents each household produces. The consent of the eligible dwellers is gathered and recorded, the society is formed, and the scheme is submitted to the Authority, which issues its letter of intent after examining title, eligibility and the development control regulations that govern the rehabilitation and sale components, principally Regulation 33(10) of the Development Control Regulations for Greater Mumbai and its successors. Approvals follow, the residents move to transit accommodation, the site is cleared, and construction proceeds in phases, with allotment of the completed tenements by the scheme's own process and the transfer of the rehabilitation component to the society. Each stage is a point at which the scheme can stop: an eligibility appeal, a consent challenge, a title dispute, a failure of the developer's finance, a change in the development control regime. The statute supplies the stages; it supplies no clock that the whole sequence must beat, and the interval between the letter of intent and the allotment letter is where the scheme's promises are discounted by experience.

What happens after allotment completes the Maharashtra picture, because a rehabilitation tenement is not an ordinary flat with an unusual history. The scheme restricts the transfer of the tenement for a period after allotment, a lock-in designed to prevent the immediate resale of the rehabilitation component into the market the scheme was built to bypass, and it collects a contribution at allotment towards a maintenance corpus for the building, because a co-operative society of households with small incomes inherits a lift-served building whose running costs were never part of the settlement's economy. Both devices acknowledge the same fact: the scheme delivers an asset whose carrying costs and market temptations the recipient household may be unable to manage, and the law's answer is to fetter the asset rather than to support the household. The post-allotment record, of societies struggling with maintenance, of informal transfers defeating the lock-in on paper while the record stays unrevised, and of buildings ageing faster than their sinking funds, is the least litigated and most consequential part of the rehabilitation story, because it decides whether the scheme's product is a permanent exit from the settlement condition or a slower return to it in vertical form.

The Authority model did not remain a Mumbai peculiarity. Slum Rehabilitation Authorities on the same pattern were constituted for other urban areas of Maharashtra, Pune among them, and the 1971 Act itself extends across the State, with the rehabilitation scheme applied in the cities where the land economics can carry it. The limiting case is Dharavi, administered through a dedicated redevelopment project structure with a developer selected by tender, where the eligibility arrangements drawn at the 2000 line, and the treatment of upper floors, of tenants in rented rooms and of the settlement's dense economy of workshops, have been the subject of sustained public dispute. Dharavi matters to the legal analysis because it concentrates every general question of the scheme in one site: a cut-off deciding between neighbours, a consent process running alongside a tender, a population whose homes and workplaces are the same rooms, and a land value so high that the cross-subsidy argument and the displacement argument are both at their strongest. What is decided there, administratively and in litigation, will be cited in every smaller scheme for a generation.

### What is transit accommodation and why does it decide whether a scheme is real?

Transit accommodation is the temporary shelter provided to residents between the demolition of their settlement and the completion of the rehabilitation building on the same site. It decides whether a scheme is real because consent is given, and eligibility is spent, against the promise of return, and the transit years are where that promise is tested.

The shelter itself may stand on or near the site or in transit camps elsewhere in the city, and its quality, distance and duration are not incidental to the legal analysis. A resident asked to consent to a scheme is being asked to accept years in transit on the strength of a promise, and the litigation record of the Maharashtra scheme is full of projects in which the transit phase outlasted every estimate, with rent for the interim period, where the scheme provided it, becoming a dispute of its own when the developer's finances failed. The Supreme Court's treatment of transit camp occupants in Mumbai illustrates the legal fragility at this joint: persons accommodated in a transit camp during a public work were held to be transit occupants paying transit charges, not tenants of the housing authority, and not eligible slum dwellers for the redevelopment that followed, so that the interim arrangement generated no rights of its own. A scheme's paperwork can therefore be in perfect order while the human bridge between the old settlement and the new building carries almost no legal security at all.

The other States built boards rather than the Maharashtra market model. The Tamil Nadu Slum Clearance Board, constituted in 1970 and working under the Tamil Nadu Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1971, is the classic clearance and improvement board: it builds tenements, improves settlements in place with basic services, and administers resettlement colonies on the edges of Chennai. Karnataka works through the Karnataka Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1973 and its slum development board, with a statutory pattern of declaration, service improvement and protection against eviction for declared settlements that has been litigated over the status of undeclared ones. Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal retain their Acts of 1962 and 1972 respectively, closer in spirit to the 1956 template, and the Andhra Pradesh Slum Improvement (Acquisition of Land) Act, 1956 took the narrower route of easing land acquisition for improvement works. The variation is not random. Where land values are extreme and the political organisation of settlement residents is strong, the law grows a redevelopment scheme with consent and eligibility machinery. Where they are not, the law remains a clearance and improvement statute administered by a board, and the resident's protection stays closer to the 1956 model of process before removal.

The board model has its own characteristic legal life, different from the scheme litigation of Maharashtra. A clearance board works by acquiring or being vested with land, building tenements, and allotting them on licence or hire purchase terms, and its disputes are the disputes of a landlord State: arrears, unauthorised transfer, subletting, and the eviction of allottees who breach the terms. An improvement board works in place, laying water supply, drainage, paving and street lighting in a declared settlement and recovering a betterment charge, and its disputes concern the charge and the standard of the works. Both models leave the resident's tenure derivative: the allottee holds from the board, the improved settlement's residents hold their original precarious title plus services, and neither model converts occupation into ownership as a matter of course. The Tamil Nadu board's long-running in situ improvement work in Chennai's settlements, and its resettlement colonies at the city's edge for settlements cleared from waterways and road margins, display the two modes side by side, improvement where the settlement stands and relocation where it may not. Karnataka's statute adds a feature worth singling out: protection of the declared settlement against arbitrary eviction is built into its scheme, so that the declaration itself, and not only a redevelopment proposal, carries legal security, and the litigation under it has turned on the difference between declared and undeclared settlements on the same government land. The comparison across States yields the field's working rule: the resident's legal position is a function of the instrument the State chose decades ago, and identical facts produce a scheme beneficiary in Mumbai, a board allottee in Chennai, a protected occupant in Bengaluru and an encroachment on the municipal register in a city that never enacted beyond the clearance model.

## Eligibility, the cut-off date and consent

Every protective regime in this field contains a line, and on one side of the line a person is a beneficiary while on the other the same person, in a materially identical house, is an encroachment to be removed. The line is the cut-off date. State schemes define eligibility by residence before a fixed date, proved by documents: the electoral roll of the relevant year, a ration card, an electricity bill, or the photo pass issued in a government survey of the settlement. The survey produces the eligibility list, Annexure II in Maharashtra practice, and the list is the operative legal document of the entire scheme, because it converts a population into a set of named entitlements and a residue of the ineligible. Challenges to exclusion from the list, on proof, on identity, on the treatment of tenants within a structure, of upper floors, of separated families and of purchasers of structures after the survey, are the characteristic litigation of the rehabilitation statutes, and they are decided list by list and name by name.

The date itself is not statutory in character even where the scheme is. It is fixed by government resolution and moved by executive decision, and the Maharashtra history shows the mechanism plainly. The original protected date for the rehabilitation scheme was 1 January 1995, tied to the electoral rolls and photo passes of that period. By a government resolution of 23 July 2014 the regularisation cut-off was extended from 1 January 1995 to 1 January 2000, bringing structures of the intervening years within the frame on the strength of the municipal photo identity records of the period, and later State policy addressed the band of structures between 2000 and 2011 on terms that included payment of a charge rather than free rehabilitation. Delhi's policy date of 1 January 2006 performs the same function in the capital. Three consequences follow and the article states them without varnish. A date fixed administratively can be moved administratively, so eligibility is a function of the political calendar in a way that a statutory criterion would not be. Each extension validates the political strategy of occupying first and regularising later, which governments know and say, and then extend the date again. And the person whose home falls a month outside the date has no argument from need, duration or vulnerability available within the scheme: the scheme does not ask those questions. The cut-off is, as the brief for any honest treatment of this subject must concede, the single most consequential rule in slum rehabilitation, and it is the rule with the least legal reasoning inside it.

Around the date the schemes build a ring of secondary exclusions that express a one-benefit principle. A household that owns a house or a residential plot within the city is ordinarily ineligible, since the scheme exists for those the market and the State have not already housed. A household that has received a government or board allotment before is ineligible again. A structure used wholly for commercial purposes travels a different track from the dwelling, and mixed use is apportioned or contested according to the scheme's rules. A purchaser of a structure takes it subject to the survey record and the transfer conditions, not as a clean title. Each exclusion is defensible in the language of targeting, and each produces its characteristic hard case at the margin: the family that sold a village plot years ago and is recorded as an owner, the allottee of a board tenement in another city who returned, the widow whose ration card was never separated from the joint family's. The list officer does not weigh these equities, because the instrument gives no scale for them, and the writ court, which could, sees only the cases that reach it. The exclusions are therefore best read as the scheme's theory of desert, administered as a checklist: one household, one benefit, proved on paper, within the date. The theory is coherent. Its collision with the actual distribution of documents and property among the poor is the eligibility system's daily business.

The survey that produces the list is itself a legal process, and it deserves to be read as one rather than as a clerical preliminary. Survey teams record the structure, its dimensions, its use, the household in occupation and the documents produced, and the record is then published for objections before certification. The categories the survey must resolve are the hard cases of the informal city: the structure in which two households live, one on each floor; the room rented to a tenant household whose name appears on no document of the structure's owner; the family that has divided since the last electoral roll; the purchaser who bought the structure, in the informal market in which such structures are in fact bought and sold, after the cut-off but before the survey; and the household whose breadwinner's name alone appears in the roll while the family has grown around it. Maharashtra practice has built answers of a kind to some of these: transfers of structures can be recognised on payment of a transfer charge and proof of residence for the period the government resolution prescribes, and scheme practice, pressed over the years by community organisations that organised the settlements themselves, moved towards recording both spouses in the allotment rather than the male head alone. None of the answers is in the statute. All of them live in guidelines, circulars and resolutions, which is the recurring condition of this field: the entitlements that decide a family's future are specified one level below legislation, where they can be changed without a debate and are correspondingly hard to challenge as law.

The documents themselves have a history that explains their hierarchy. The electoral roll stands first because it is the one record the State prepares household by household, locality by locality, for a purpose unconnected with housing, and its entry for the cut-off year is correspondingly hard to fabricate after the event. The photo pass systems came next, in Mumbai through the municipal surveys that photographed structures and issued identity cards to their occupants around the operative dates, converting the settlement itself into a record. The ration card, issued for food distribution and held by households that no other system reached, fills the gaps the first two leave, and utility bills and receipts accumulate behind them as corroboration. Each document has a characteristic failure. The roll misses the young adult who was a child at the cut-off and the migrant whose enrolment stayed in the village. The photo pass misses the household that was absent on the survey day and the floor the survey team did not climb to. The ration card records a family as the distribution system knew it, which may not be the family that lived in the room. The eligibility officer's art, such as it is, lies in reading the set together, and the appellate and writ record of the rehabilitation schemes is substantially a record of that reading being done well or badly on particular names. Later surveys have added photographing of occupants and biometric capture to the older paper methods, which hardens the record against substitution while leaving untouched the prior question of which date the record must reach back to.

### What happens when two households claim the same structure in the survey?

Competing claims to one structure are resolved by the certifying authority on the documents each household produces, principally the electoral roll entry, the ration card and the survey record itself. The household that cannot document occupation before the cut-off date loses; its remedy is an objection before certification and thereafter the High Court's writ jurisdiction.

The survey records occupation as it finds it. The deeper difficulty is that the survey's categories are built for a city of single-household huts, while the mature settlement is a city of subdivided rooms and rented floors. A structure recorded to one eligible household may shelter three, and the two whose names are not recorded are not defeated by a rival claim so much as by a form. Tenancy inside settlements, invisible to a survey designed around the hutment owner, is the point at which the eligibility system most often fails to see the population it governs, and it is the point on which the community organisations that work in the settlements have concentrated their documentation work, building household records over years precisely so that the survey, when it comes, finds a paper trail instead of an assertion.

Consent is the second gate. The Maharashtra guidelines require that seventy percent or more of the eligible hutment dwellers in a settlement show willingness to join the rehabilitation scheme and come together in a co-operative housing society before the scheme proceeds, and the courts have enforced the threshold in substance, asking whether the requisite proportion of eligible dwellers has in fact consented rather than treating the figure as a formality. The requirement is a genuine participatory safeguard in design: it prevents a developer and an authority from redeveloping a settlement over the heads of the people who live in it, and it gives the majority's preference legal effect against a holdout minority. Its operation has been questioned on grounds that can be stated generally and fairly: the consent is gathered by the developer who profits from the scheme, the information on which residents consent is supplied largely by the same source, and consent once recorded is difficult to withdraw even where the project then stalls for a decade. The statute's own adjustments acknowledge part of the tension: where the landowner himself files the scheme, the Authority's circulars have relaxed the consent requirement, on the reasoning that the owner's preferential right should not be defeated by a threshold designed for third-party proposals. The Supreme Court has also refused to let the consent debate become a universal solvent, holding in the Maharashtra cases that courts will look at whether the majority of eligible dwellers has decided for redevelopment and will not entertain delay by persons found ineligible. Consent in this field is therefore best described as a real safeguard with a structural weakness: the person who must obtain it is the person with the strongest interest in obtaining it.

The mechanics of consent generate their own disputes, which the Authority and the courts work through in a recognisable pattern. A settlement may produce two proposed societies, each with a developer, each claiming the requisite proportion of the eligible dwellers, and the contest is then decided on the consent record itself: which dwellers are eligible, which signatures are genuine, and which proposal the majority of the eligible actually supports. Dwellers move between proposals, withdraw, and allege that consent was taken on a different understanding of the scheme than the one filed, and the Authority's scrutiny of the consent list at the letter of intent stage is the forum in which those contests are first fought. The co-operative society form matters to the substance and is not a shell: the society is the body that will hold the rehabilitation component, contract with the developer and receive the conveyance, so that a dispute about who speaks for the society is a dispute about who will own the rebuilt settlement. A further asymmetry runs through the whole design and should be named. The eligible dweller's consent is solicited at the beginning, when the scheme is a promise; the events that determine whether the promise was worth making, the developer's performance, the transit years, the quality of the building, occur afterwards, when the consent is spent and the dweller's leverage with it. The scheme answers with the Authority's supervision and the courts' willingness to intervene where a project is abandoned, and the record of stalled projects shows both the supervision working and its limits. Consent, in short, is a gate and not a guarantee, and the honest description of the Maharashtra model has to hold both halves of that sentence together.

The strain between the two gates produced a legislative episode that shows the system's own assessment of itself. In 2017 the Maharashtra cabinet approved an amendment bill whose object was to bring the ineligible within the redevelopment project itself: protected occupiers, those in structures standing before the operative date, would continue to receive free rehabilitation, while residents found ineligible would be accommodated on the same site under the central housing mission, so that a scheme would no longer produce a cleared residue of households with nowhere to go. The proposal was contentious within the government itself, its law department's reservations being reported alongside its approval, and its logic is the point for present purposes: the State that operates the most developed rehabilitation scheme in the country accepted, at the level of cabinet decision, that a scheme which rehabilitates the listed and removes the unlisted is incomplete as an instrument, and reached for the mission system to close the gap the statute leaves. Whether the gap is closed by legislation or left to resolutions and project-level improvisation is the standing question of the Maharashtra model.

The position of the ineligible completes the picture. A resident who cannot prove residence before the date, or whose structure is recorded as commercial where the scheme rehousing is residential, or who occupies a floor or an extension the survey did not count, is outside the scheme, and outside the scheme there is no rehabilitation: the land is cleared of the structure when the scheme is executed, and the resident's protection shrinks to the general law of demolition process discussed below. The authorities' answer is administrative and not dishonest: a scheme needs a boundary, every boundary has a far side, and a date that moves whenever it is tested is not a boundary at all. The residents' answer is experiential: two households in one lane, divided by an electoral roll entry, receive a permanent flat and a demolition notice respectively, and no feature of their lives corresponds to the difference. Both answers are true, and the law's task, largely unperformed, would be to build a principled way of arguing about the boundary instead of treating each movement of it as a political event.

## Housing boards: the allotment-era machinery

One institution predates the missions and explains their habits: the State housing board. The Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Authority, created under the Maharashtra Housing and Area Development Act, 1976, is the type specimen, an authority that combined the older housing board's functions with the repair and reconstruction of the city's cessed buildings and came, over decades, to hold land, tenements, transit camps and redevelopment mandates across Mumbai. Its counterparts in other States, the housing and development boards of Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and the rest, were the delivery arm of the allotment era: the State acquired or was vested with land, the board built tenements to a standard design, and allotment proceeded by application, draw and waiting list, on rent or hire purchase, to categories the rules defined. The boards matter to this subject for three reasons. They were the landlord of the transit camps whose occupants the Supreme Court later held to be transit occupants without eligibility, so that the interim housing of one generation's public works became the contested settlement of the next. They hold, in the old cities, a large stock of the land on which settlements stand, which is why the housing authority appears as landowner in so much of the rehabilitation litigation, exercising or withholding the consent and the no-objection on which schemes depend. And their allotment model supplied the administrative template the missions inherited: categories, lists, draws and licences, a machinery for distributing a scarce built product among claimants, which is a different thing from a machinery for recognising rights in the place where a claimant already lives. The rehabilitation scheme and the mission both descend from the board in their paperwork, and the descent explains their common blind spot: each is organised to decide who receives, and none is organised to ask what is owed to those who do not.

## The settlement and the plan

A fourth actor stands behind the authority, the board and the municipality: the development plan. Every statutory city plan reserves land for roads, railways, drains, schools and open spaces, and a large share of the settlements in any old city stand, in whole or part, on land the plan reserves for something else. Reservation is the quiet legal fact behind a great deal of demolition that is publicly described in the language of encroachment alone. A settlement on a road reservation is an obstruction of a planned public work; a settlement on a water body reservation is an obstruction of the city's drainage; and the planning authority's programme for executing the plan supplies both the occasion and the justification for clearance, often decades after the plan was drawn and the settlement formed in ignorance of it. The planning statutes give the reservation a long life and the land's owner, where the land is private, a purchase notice remedy when the authority delays acquisition beyond the statutory period; the settlement resident, who is not the owner, has no corresponding remedy, and the reservation hangs over the settlement as a permanent contingency. Redevelopment law and planning law meet at this point and do not always agree: a rehabilitation scheme may rebuild a settlement in place that the plan has reserved for a road, and the conflict is then resolved, as such conflicts usually are, by the State government amending the plan or the scheme prevailing in practice. The resident reading a clearance notice learns more from the plan extract than from the notice, because the plan states the public purpose that the authority will plead, and the strength of the settlement's position, in negotiation and in court, turns substantially on whether the reserved purpose is imminent and funded or a line on a map older than the settlement itself. The Haldwani settlement stood on railway land wanted for expansion of the tracks; the Ahmedabad encroachers in Nawab Khan stood on a main road the city needed to keep clear; the Mumbai settlements that courts have protected longest are those whose land no funded public purpose presently claims. The pattern is not a rule of law. It is the gravitational field in which the rules operate.

## Housing missions: delivery without entitlement

Alongside the statutes stands a second delivery system that is not law at all in the relevant sense: the central urban housing missions. The Jawaharlal Nehru National Urban Renewal Mission, launched in 2005, carried a sub-mission for basic services to the urban poor through which settlement upgrading and housing were funded in mission cities. The Rajiv Awas Yojana, launched in 2011, aimed at a slum-free India through in situ redevelopment and the conferment of property rights, and was absorbed into the successor mission before its property-rights design matured. The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban), the Housing for All mission launched on 25 June 2015, works through four verticals, of which the first is in-situ slum redevelopment: the private partner redevelops the settlement on its own land value, central assistance of one lakh rupees per house flows to the project, and the other three verticals, a credit-linked subsidy, affordable housing in partnership and beneficiary-led construction, serve households outside the redevelopment frame. The mission was extended to complete sanctioned houses and then recast: Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban) 2.0 took effect from 1 September 2024 with its own four verticals of beneficiary-led construction, affordable housing in partnership, affordable rental housing and an interest subsidy, the redevelopment vertical of the first mission not carried forward in the same form.

The legal character of all of this must be stated exactly, because the public description of missions habitually overstates it. A mission is an executive programme. Its guidelines are administrative instructions; its sanctions are budgetary decisions; its beneficiary lists are prepared by the implementing State or city under the programme's norms. No provision of the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana creates a right in an identified person to be given a house, enforceable against the State if the programme passes the person by, and the same is true of its predecessors. This is the sense in which the series article on [welfare laws in India](/2017/07/01/welfare-laws-india-complete-guide/), linked at this point because the scheme and entitlement distinction is its organising subject, treats housing programmes: as schemes that confer benefits on those they select, under norms they set, for as long as they run. The contrast with a statute like the land acquisition law is sharp, and it is drawn in the assessment below. For the resident, the practical consequence is that mission housing is claimed through the survey and the list, not through the court: the remedy for exclusion is a representation to the implementing agency and, at the constitutional margin, a writ petition arguing arbitrariness, not a suit for the house.

The missions also reveal, in their design history, the direction in which policy travels when it cannot legislate entitlements. The 2005 mission's sub-mission for basic services to the urban poor funded both housing and infrastructure in identified settlements, and its projects were in many cities built on peripheral land the State could assemble, so that the pattern of the older clearance colonies repeated itself inside a modern programme: sound houses at distances that taxed the livelihoods of the allotted. The Rajiv Awas Yojana attempted to correct the pattern at the level of design, preferring redevelopment in place and speaking the language of property rights for residents, and its absorption into the 2015 mission before that design was generally enacted is itself a datum about the field: programme cycles are shorter than the processes they fund. The 2015 mission's redevelopment vertical, with central assistance of one lakh rupees a house against the land value the project itself releases, aligned the Centre's money with the Maharashtra mechanism rather than with the board model, and the mission's extension, to the end of 2024, for the completion of houses sanctioned within its original period shows the same programme-cycle pressure in reverse, completions outrunning the mission's formal life. The 2024 recast then dropped the redevelopment vertical in favour of, among its four components, affordable rental housing, a shift worth reading carefully: rental housing answers the case of the household that the ownership schemes cannot see, the tenant and the recent migrant, and its arrival in the mission is an implicit concession that the rehabilitation frame built around the documented owner-occupier leaves the renting poor outside. None of this is law in the sense this article has used the word. All of it decides, in a given decade, which of the law's categories will have houses attached to them.

## The constitutional line: livelihood, shelter and the limits of both

Because the statutes would not do it, the courts built the substantive protection, and they built it inside Article 21. The foundation is Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation, decided in 1985 by a Constitution Bench of five judges speaking through Chandrachud CJ. The case arose from the decision of the Maharashtra government and the Bombay Municipal Corporation to remove pavement dwellers and settlement residents from the city's pavements under the Bombay Municipal Corporation Act, 1888, including Section 314, which empowers the Commissioner to remove encroachments on public streets without notice. The petitioners, pavement dwellers and a journalist, contended that eviction from the pavement was eviction from their livelihood, since they lived where they worked, and that a law permitting removal without notice violated Articles 19 and 21.

The Court's holding is precise and is routinely overstated in both directions, so it is set out here in its actual components. The right to life under Article 21 includes the right to livelihood, because a person deprived of the means of living near the work that sustains life is deprived of life in the sense the Article protects; eviction from a dwelling that is also the platform of a livelihood therefore engages Article 21, and the procedure that deprives a person of it must be just, fair and reasonable, not arbitrary or oppressive. At the same time, no person has a right to encroach upon a pavement, footpath or public street, which is public property dedicated to passage; a pavement dweller is in law a trespasser on the space, and Section 314, an enabling provision to be exercised reasonably, was not unconstitutional for permitting removal without notice in the class of cases it addresses. The petitions therefore largely failed on the power, and the pavement dwellers were not held entitled to remain. What the State had given in the case itself filled part of the gap: the government's assurances that pavement and settlement dwellers enumerated in the 1976 census would be given alternate pitches were directed to be honoured, settlements of long standing were not to be removed capriciously, and the removal was in the event deferred to after the monsoon. The accurate summary is uncomfortable for every camp: Olga Tellis constitutionalised the question, recognised livelihood as the content of the right at stake, insisted on fair procedure, and then upheld the removal power and denied the right to remain. The decision is the charter of Indian slum law precisely because it contains both halves, and any account that keeps only one half is advocacy dressed as doctrine.

The later cases extended the vocabulary without reversing the structure. In Shantistar Builders v. Narayan Khimalal Totame in 1990 the Court treated shelter as among the basic needs comprehended in the right to life. In Chameli Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh in 1996 a three-judge bench held that the right to shelter is a fundamental right under Article 19(1)(e) read with Article 21, and described its content generously: not a mere roof, but adequate living space, a safe and decent structure, clean surroundings, light, air, water, electricity, sanitation and civic amenities. In Ahmedabad Municipal Corporation v. Nawab Khan Gulab Khan, reported at (1997) 11 SCC 123, the Court faced encroachers on a main road in Ahmedabad and held that while the encroachment had to go and the pavement was not theirs, the Corporation's scheme of alternative accommodation for the eligible occupants had to be honoured before ejectment, so that removal followed the scheme rather than preceding it. The thread through the three is consistent with Olga Tellis and often misread as going further: the right to shelter operates as a discipline on the manner and sequencing of State action, and as the foundation of scheme-based duties the State has assumed, not as a free-standing claim to be allotted a house, and not as a veto on the removal of encroachments from public land.

The courts' own sources for the shelter right deserve a note, because they show how the doctrine was assembled. The Nawab Khan bench reasoned from the text outward: the right to reside and settle in Article 19(1)(e), the right to life in Article 21, and then the international commitments India had accepted, quoting Article 25(1) of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, with its standard of living adequate for health and well-being including housing, and Article 11(1) of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, with its recognition of the right to an adequate standard of living including housing and to the continuous improvement of living conditions. The international instruments do no independent legal work in the cases; they supply the content of a domestic right whose domestic text says only life. That method, reading the content of Article 21 from the international register and the directive principles while enforcing it only as process and scheme discipline, is the characteristic judicial technology of Indian socio-economic rights, and slum law is its most fully worked example. It produces a right that is rhetorically generous and remedially specific, and a reader who understands the technology will not be surprised by either quality, or by the uses to which each can be put by the parties on opposite sides of a demolition. The Delhi decisions in Sudama Singh and Ajay Maken are the same thread worked at the level of a city's settlements: survey first, consult, plan rehabilitation, then and only then remove.

Two companion lines of authority complete the constitutional picture, because the settlement resident meets the State as often through the municipality's duties and the street's regulation as through eviction itself. In Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand in 1980, residents of a Ratlam locality, driven by open drains and public nuisances, obtained directions against the municipal council, and the Supreme Court held that a municipality charged by statute with sanitation duties cannot plead financial inability to escape them: the duty to abate the conditions that made the locality a health hazard was enforceable, and the council's budget was its problem to solve, not a defence. Ratlam is the affirmative twin of Olga Tellis, decided five years before it: where Olga disciplines the State's power to remove, Ratlam enforces the State's duty to service, and together they describe the two directions in which the courts have been willing to move the municipal State, compulsion on its obligations, procedure on its powers. In Sodan Singh v. New Delhi Municipal Committee in 1989, the Court took the street itself as the subject, holding that the right to carry on trade includes hawking on pavements, subject to reasonable regulation, but that no trader holds a fundamental right to occupy a particular spot, so that the municipality may regulate the pavement as a workplace through schemes and zones. Sodan Singh matters here because it fixed the constitutional grammar of the pavement for both its uses: occupation for livelihood is within the Constitution's concern, occupation of a chosen place is not a right, and regulation is the space between the two propositions. The dwelling cases and the vending cases are one jurisprudence read from two sides, and the resident who also works from the pavement outside the dwelling stands at their intersection.

The High Courts did the daily work of this doctrine in the decades after Olga Tellis, and their pattern is consistent enough to state. Faced with a removal power in a municipal or development authority statute, and a settlement of any age, the courts read the power down with the requirements of natural justice where the statute was silent, asked for the survey and the eligibility determination before the demolition rather than after it, and treated a long-settled population differently from a fresh encroachment on the reasoning that the State's own acquiescence over years had created the equities Olga Tellis recognised. The eviction that survives this scrutiny is the one the authority can document: notice given, hearing held, eligibility decided, alternative arrangement addressed where a scheme or policy requires it. The eviction that fails is characteristically the one executed first and documented later. That is a modest doctrine, and deliberately so, but it changed the administrative practice of demolition in the cities whose courts enforced it, because it made the file, and not the squad, the operative instrument.

Dignity is the value the courts name when they explain why shelter sits inside Article 21 at all, and the reasoning runs parallel to the privacy jurisprudence: a home is where a person sleeps, keeps documents and possessions, raises children and is safe from the street, so that its summary destruction injures the person and not only the property, which is the insight the Constitution Bench carried into the dignity analysis in the [Puttaswamy privacy and Aadhaar judgment](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/) in the context of informational and decisional autonomy. The doctrinal consequence for this field is that demolition and eviction are never merely property events in Indian constitutional law. They are Article 21 events, and the State that carries them out must be able to show a procedure worthy of what is being taken away.

## The procedural line: the demolition directions of 2024

The second judicial line is about machinery rather than rights, and it culminates in the judgment of 13 November 2024 in In Re: Directions in the Matter of Demolition of Structures, Writ Petition (Civil) No. 295 of 2022, delivered by a bench of Gavai and Viswanathan JJ. The case grew out of demolitions carried out against the properties of persons accused of offences, in several States, in the pattern that public discussion named bulldozer justice: a crime or a disturbance, an official statement, and a demolition squad at the home of the accused within days, on the asserted ground that the structure was unauthorised. The Court's central holding is constitutional and short. The executive cannot demolish the property of a person merely because the person is accused or convicted of an offence. Determining guilt and imposing punishment are judicial functions; demolition as a punitive measure, ordered by the executive on its own assessment of guilt, violates the rule of law, which is part of the basic structure, and the separation of powers, and it visits on the family of the accused a collective punishment the Constitution does not permit. Even a convicted person retains rights, and the State's power over an unauthorised structure is the power conferred by the municipal statute, to be exercised through that statute's process and for that statute's reasons, not as an annex to the criminal process. The Court added an evidentiary discipline that practitioners will use for years: where a structure is selected for demolition suddenly after its occupant is accused, while similar structures stand untouched, a presumption arises that the real motive was penal, and the authorities must satisfy the court otherwise.

Exercising its power under Article 142 to do complete justice, the bench then laid down a pan-India procedure to govern demolitions of unauthorised structures, and it is the detail of that procedure that makes the judgment the most operationally significant demolition decision in the field. No demolition is to be carried out without a prior show cause notice, returnable within the time provided by the applicable local municipal law or within fifteen days from the date of service, whichever is later. The notice must be served on the owner or occupier by registered post and also affixed on the outer portion of the structure, and the fifteen days run from receipt. The notice must state the nature of the unauthorised construction, the details of the specific violation and the grounds on which demolition is proposed. A copy is to go to the District Magistrate, and the Collector or District Magistrate is to designate a nodal officer for the process and make the machinery accountable at the district level. The designated authority must give the noticee a personal hearing, and the minutes of the hearing must be recorded. The authority must then pass a reasoned final order dealing with the contentions raised, recording whether the unauthorised construction is compoundable and, where only a part is non-compoundable, confining the demolition to that part, and recording why demolition is the only option where that conclusion is reached. The final order is to be uploaded on a designated digital portal, so that the paper trail of a demolition is public and checkable. Even after the order, demolition is not to be executed for fifteen days from its receipt, so that the affected person can challenge it before the appellate forum where one exists, and where no appellate remedy exists the fifteen days still run; a person who does not wish to contest must still be given time to vacate, and the owner may remove the unauthorised construction voluntarily within the window. Before demolition, an inspection is to be carried out and a report prepared; the entire demolition is to be videographed; and a report naming the officials who participated is to be sent to the Municipal Commissioner and uploaded on the portal. The sanctions are personal and were plainly meant to be read by every municipal commissioner in the country: a demolition carried out in violation of the directions will amount to contempt of court, and the officials responsible will be personally liable, including liability to restore the demolished property at their own cost, apart from contempt.

Read closely, the directions are a small code of administrative law for one act, and each element answers a documented abuse. The registered post requirement, with affixture as the supplement rather than the substitute, answers the notice pasted on a wall the night before the squad arrives, which the Prayagraj case later showed in operation. The requirement that the notice state the specific violation and the grounds answers the cyclostyled notice that recites illegality in general and leaves the owner unable to answer or to cure. The personal hearing, with minutes, answers the file that records a hearing nobody attended. The reasoned final order, which must deal with the contentions and with compoundability, answers the order that is a signature on a printed form; and the compoundability inquiry is substantive, not decorative, because a large share of unauthorised construction in Indian cities is construction that deviates from a sanction in ways the building rules themselves treat as regularisable on payment, so that demolition of the whole for a compoundable part is a disproportion the order must confront on its face. The confinement of demolition to the non-compoundable portion carries the same proportionality into execution. The digital portal answers the invisibility of the demolition file, the feature that made selective action hard to prove: a notice and an order that must be uploaded are a record that can be compared across a city's cases, which is exactly the comparison the presumption of penal motive requires. The fifteen-day pause after the order answers the demolition executed inside the appeal period, the single commonest procedural fact in the reported cases. The intimation to the District Magistrate and the designation of a nodal officer answer the diffusion of responsibility among the municipality, the police and the district administration, by naming one office answerable for the process in each district. The inspection report and the videography answer the dispute about what stood on the land and what was done to it, which in demolition litigation is ordinarily unresolvable by the time the court hears it. And the personal liability, extending to restoration at the officer's own cost, answers the calculation that a wrongful demolition costs the demolisher nothing, because the structure is gone, the family is scattered and compensation, if it comes, is paid by the State years later. A code of this granularity is open to the objection that a court has legislated, and the bench's own justification was of the kind the Court has given since it first filled a legislative vacuum with guidelines: the procedure operationalises Articles 14 and 21 for an act the executive performs daily, and it holds the field until a legislature occupies it. No legislature has occupied it, and the directions' afterlife in the municipal circulars and portals of the States is the measure of their acceptance as the working law of demolition.

The record on which the Court acted should be stated as carefully as its conclusions, because the judgment is sometimes described as a general charter against demolition, which it is not. The batch of petitions, in which Jamiat Ulema-i-Hind was among the petitioners, challenged a series of demolitions carried out in 2022 and after, in several States, against the homes and shops of persons accused of offences, characteristically in the aftermath of communal disturbances or crimes that had drawn official statements. The Union, through the Solicitor General, resisted the framing: no property, it was submitted, is demolished because its owner is accused; demolitions follow violations of municipal law, and the coincidence of an accusation and an unauthorised structure in the same premises is not a conspiracy. The Court's answer to that submission is the intellectual centre of the judgment. It did not hold that every demolition of an accused person's property is punitive; it held that the pattern can make it so, that selective and sudden action against one structure among many similar ones raises a presumption of penal motive, and that the discipline of notice, hearing, reasons and record is what separates the municipal power from the punitive use of it. The guidelines are therefore addressed to the ordinary case as much as to the abusive one: they are the procedure by which a lawful demolition proves itself lawful.

### Why did the 2024 judgment leave footpaths and railway land outside its code?

The exclusion was a choice about where summary power remains necessary. The Court accepted that encroachments on roads, footpaths, railway land and water bodies obstruct public use, and that a notice regime would defeat the summary removal those spaces require, so it confined its procedure to structures outside those categories.

The reasoning connects to the oldest stratum of the subject. Section 314 of the Bombay statute, the summary power Olga Tellis upheld in 1985, is the ancestor of every municipal encroachment provision in the country, and the 2024 bench, building its code for unauthorised construction, declined to disturb the settlement of 1985 for the occupation of public ways. The two judgments thus divide the field between them on a line drawn by land category: construction, however unauthorised, attracts the new procedure; encroachment on the public way remains with the old one. A family whose unauthorised house stands on its own plot receives notice, hearing and a reasoned order. A family whose identical house stands on the far side of the plot boundary, on the margin of the road, receives the summary power and the older case law. The line is administrable and was defended as necessary. It is also the line along which the informal city is sorted, since the margin of the road, the bank of the drain and the edge of the tracks are where the city's poorest housing has always stood.

The exclusions must be stated with the same prominence as the directions, because they are the article's central fact. The Court declared that its directions will not apply where there is an unauthorised structure in a public place such as a road, street or footpath, abutting a railway line, or on a river body or water body, and will not apply where a court of law has passed an order of demolition. Read against the map of Indian informal housing, the exclusion is not a footnote. The pavement settlement, the settlement along the railway margin, the settlement on the drain and the riverbank are not marginal cases of informal housing; they are among its commonest forms, and the residents of precisely those settlements are the persons Olga Tellis was about. For a structure on private land, or on public land outside the excluded categories, the 2024 judgment supplies the most detailed procedural code Indian demolition law has had: notice, hearing, reasons, appeal time, videography, personal liability. For a structure on a footpath or beside the tracks, the code by its own terms does not run, and the resident is remitted to the municipal statute, which in many States authorises summary removal, and to Article 21 as worked out in the older case law. The judgment's limit is deliberate, stated in terms, and was defended in the hearing by the Union's law officer on the ground that action against encroachments on public land could not be held up by a notice regime. A reader who knows only the directions knows the smaller half of the judgment.

Enforcement of the directions has already been tested, and the test shows both the code's teeth and its boundary. In Zulfiquar Haider v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided on 1 April 2025 by a bench of Oka and Bhuyan JJ, the Prayagraj Development Authority had demolished residential structures in 2021 after affixing notices without the repeated attempts at personal service the statute required, executing the demolition within a day of the last communication and inside the appeal period. The Court held the action illegal and inhuman, restated that the right to shelter is an integral part of Article 21, directed the Authority to follow the 2024 directions scrupulously, and ordered it to pay ten lakh rupees in each appeal to the persons whose homes had been destroyed. The case is instructive on the mechanism: the directions bite through the ordinary appellate and writ process, at the instance of a person with the resources and representation to reach the Supreme Court, in respect of structures that fall within the code's field. They supply no machinery for the excluded categories, and no authority has been charged with auditing demolitions on footpaths and railway land against any equivalent code.

The excluded categories are not, for all that, outside constitutional discipline, and the railway land litigation of the same period shows the difference between the code and the discipline. In Haldwani in Uttarakhand, a large settlement stood on railway land, and in December 2022 the Uttarakhand High Court directed the removal of the encroachments within a week, with force if necessary. In January 2023 the Supreme Court stayed that direction, in a matter affecting a population counted in the tens of thousands, observing that the right to shelter of the residents had to be balanced against the Railways' need for the land and that a workable arrangement for rehabilitation had to be found rather than a summary clearance executed. The episode is the exclusions' necessary counterweight. A settlement beside the tracks receives no notice code from the 2024 judgment, but it does not stand in a legal vacuum: the constitutional courts remain available, case by case, to insist that removal of a settled population be preceded by a rehabilitation arrangement, and the larger and older the settlement, the heavier that insistence has in practice been. The difference between the two regimes is the difference between a rule and a remedy. Inside the code, the resident holds the procedure in advance, and the authority must build the file before it acts. Outside it, the resident holds a constitutional argument, which must be carried to a court, at the resident's initiative, usually against time. Both are law. They are not the same quantity of protection, and the distance between them is the measure of what the 2024 exclusions withheld.

## One settlement under four legal skies

The four questions can be made concrete without inventing a single fact about any real place. Take a settlement of some hundreds of households, thirty years old, its lanes paved by its residents over time, its households working in the surrounding city as drivers, vendors, domestic workers and mechanics. Run the same settlement through four configurations of land and status, and watch the law change around an unchanging human fact.

In the first configuration the settlement stands on municipal land, was declared under the State slum statute a decade ago, and its households hold photo passes and roll entries from before the cut-off. This settlement is inside every protection the system has. A redevelopment proposal will survey it, list its households, gather its consent and rebuild it in place, with transit accommodation between; a demolition outside a scheme will meet the eligibility list, the scheme's own machinery and, for any structure the municipal power proceeds against directly, the constitutional doctrine and the 2024 procedure where the land category allows it. The settlement's disputes will be the good disputes of the field, about names on lists and the genuineness of consent.

In the second configuration the identical settlement stands two hundred metres away, on the margin of a railway line, undeclared. The rehabilitation scheme does not reach it, because the land is the Railways' and no State scheme runs there. The 2024 procedure does not reach it, by the judgment's express exclusion. What reaches it is the estate officer's notice under the public premises statute when the Railways move to recover the land, and behind that notice the constitutional case law, which the settlement must invoke for itself, in the High Court or the Supreme Court, to convert a summary recovery into a considered one. Its realistic best outcome is the Haldwani outcome: a stay, a direction that rehabilitation be worked out, and years of negotiation conducted in the shadow of a removal that remains lawful in principle throughout.

In the third configuration the settlement stands on private land whose owner, watching the surrounding plots redevelop, files a rehabilitation proposal of his own. The households discover that their consent threshold has been relaxed for the owner's scheme, that their preferential position in the design belongs to the man whose title their occupation burdens, and that the Authority's role is to hold the ring between his preference and their eligibility. Their protection is the list and the consent that remains, and the litigation, if it comes, will be about whether the owner's proposal is genuine and whether the dwellers' society was heard before it prevailed.

In the fourth configuration the settlement is identical in all respects but one: most of its households arrived after the cut-off date, drawn by the work the older settlement's economy created. No dishonesty attaches to them and no document can save them. The scheme, if one comes, will rehabilitate their neighbours and clear their homes, and the law they meet is the law of the notice and the removal, dignified by the doctrine's insistence that they be treated as persons and entitled to nothing else in particular. In the fifth configuration the settlement stands on land whose ownership is itself in litigation, claimed by the State under an old acquisition and by a private family under an older title, with the suit older than many of the settlement's households. Here the law does not refuse the settlement anything; it simply cannot begin. No authority will run a scheme on land whose owner the courts have not identified, no developer will price a project whose free sale component rests on a contested title, and no demolition will issue from a department that cannot say whose land it is clearing. The settlement lives in a suspended category the typology of this article has no box for, protected by the dispute itself, growing older and denser while the title crawls, and its residents learn the strangest lesson the field teaches: that being beneath a lawsuit can be, for a generation, the most secure tenure in the city, and the least useful, since a settlement under litigation can neither be improved by scheme nor removed by notice, and waits for a decree about somebody else's rights to learn its own.

The five configurations are not five settlements. They are one settlement, five times, and the distance between the first and the last is the distance this article set out to measure: the distance between a legal system that has decided, category by category and date by date, exactly how much process and how much housing each kind of poverty is owed.

## Public premises: the estate officer regime on central land

One more removal regime must be placed on the map, because a large share of settlement land in Indian cities belongs neither to the State nor to a private owner but to the Union: railway land, port land, defence land and the land of central public enterprises. Occupation of such land is governed by the Public Premises (Eviction of Unauthorised Occupants) Act, 1971, a central statute that creates its own complete procedure and displaces the ordinary civil process for the premises it covers. The Act works through an estate officer, appointed for the premises, who issues a show cause notice to the unauthorised occupant under Section 4, hears the occupant, and may then order eviction under Section 5, with an appeal lying to the district judge under Section 9 and the civil courts' jurisdiction barred for matters the Act commits to the estate officer. The model is older than the 2024 directions and resembles them in structure, notice, hearing, reasoned order, appeal, because both are answers to the same administrative question of how the State recovers its own land without a suit. The resemblance ends at the margins that matter here. The estate officer procedure is a landlord's remedy in administrative form: its subject is the recovery of premises, its timelines are its own, and it contains no equivalent of the 2024 code's compoundability inquiry, its portal, its videography or its personal liability, and no rehabilitation component at all. A settlement on railway land therefore faces, in strict law, a notice-and-hearing regime from 1971 administered by the Railways' own officer, the exclusion of that land from the 2024 code, and the Article 21 case law above both, which is the combination the Haldwani litigation presented and which the Supreme Court answered with a stay and a direction to find a workable rehabilitation arrangement. The port and defence settlements of the port cities stand in the same combination. For the researcher, the public premises regime is the reminder that Indian demolition law is not one code with exceptions but several codes with different centuries and different logics, allocated by the accident of which government owns the ground.

## What process is owed and when it is not

The table below is the article in one view. It states, for each situation in which a structure faces demolition, the governing process, whether the 2024 directions apply, the notice the law requires, and the remedy open to the occupier. The rows in which the directions do not apply are the rows that hold most of the informal city.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unauthorised structure on private land | Municipal statute process as supplemented by the 2024 directions | Yes | Show cause notice by registered post and affixture, returnable in the municipal period or fifteen days from receipt, whichever is later | Reply and personal hearing, appeal or challenge within fifteen days of the final order |
| Structure in an area declared under a slum statute | Declaration, eligibility survey and scheme process under the State Act, with eviction controls of the kind in Section 19 of the 1956 Act where it applies | In part: the scheme process governs rehabilitation, the directions govern demolition outside it | Scheme notices, survey and eligibility lists under the State Act; prior written permission of the competent authority before eviction proceedings in a declared area under the 1956 Act | Objection to the eligibility list, appeal to the Administrator under the 1956 Act, writ on Article 21 |

## How demolition disputes actually move through the courts

The doctrine reads differently from the docket, and a pillar treatment of this subject owes the reader the docket's version. A demolition dispute typically begins at speed: a notice with a short fuse, or a rumour of the squad, and a writ petition filed in the High Court within days, seeking a stay of the threatened action until the petition can be heard. The first order is the practical judgment in most cases. A court that grants status quo preserves the settlement while the law is argued; a court that declines it watches the argument become academic, because a demolished settlement is rarely rebuilt by a decree. The petitions that succeed at the first hearing share a documentary profile: the settlement is old, the residents are listed or surveyed or can show the paper trail of recognition, the notice is defective or absent, and a scheme or policy covers the ground. The petitions that fail share the opposite profile. Between the poles, the litigation performs a sorting function that the doctrine's language conceals: it is, in effect, an emergency audit of the four questions, land, status, date and stage, conducted at interim stage on affidavits. The eligibility dispute follows the slower track, through the scheme's own objection and appeal channels and then to the writ court, and can run for years alongside the construction it concerns, which is why the Maharashtra record contains buildings allotted under lists that litigation later disturbed and lists corrected after the buildings rose.

The formal system does contain one bridge across that gap, and its performance belongs in the account. The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 entitles defined categories of persons, among them women and children, members of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, industrial workmen and persons below a prescribed income ceiling, to free legal services, and the district legal services authorities maintain clinics and front offices through which an eviction notice can be taken for advice without fee. In demolition matters the bridge is used unevenly: a notice that arrives days before execution leaves little time for a legal aid application to become a drafted petition, and the authorities' clinics are better known to the settlements that organisations have connected to them than to the settlements that need the connection most. The point is structural rather than critical. Every procedural protection in this field presupposes a person able to invoke it within its window, and the legal aid system is the only part of the design addressed to that presupposition. Its reach, more than any doctrine, decides how much of the paper law the unorganised settlement will ever see.

The economics of this litigation shape its incidence. A settlement that can reach the High Court is a settlement with an organisation, a community body, a non-governmental organisation that documents settlements, or lawyers working in the public interest tradition that Olga Tellis itself exemplified, since that case was carried by a journalist petitioner and the dwellers' own organisation of their record. A settlement without that infrastructure experiences the law as event rather than process: the notice, if any, and the demolition. The constitutional doctrine is uniform across the country; its distribution is not, and the unevenness tracks the map of civil society organisation in the settlements rather than any feature of the statutes. This is the practical setting in which the 2024 judgment's design choices will work themselves out. Inside the code's field, the procedure is self-executing in the sense that the file must exist before the act, and a resident with no lawyer at all is protected by the authority's own obligation to create the record. Outside it, protection still depends on reaching a court in time. A reformer choosing where to spend effort will read that sentence twice: the code's deepest virtue is not any single direction but the relocation of protection from the resident's initiative to the authority's routine, and its deepest limit is that the relocation stops at the boundary of the excluded land.

A last feature of the practice deserves record, because it descends directly from the founding case. The removal in Olga Tellis was in the event deferred until after the monsoon, and the deferral hardened into an administrative convention in several cities under which settlement removals are not scheduled in the rains, when a demolished household has no dry ground to go to. The convention has no statutory text. It survives because no authority wishes to defend, in court or in print, the demolition of homes at the height of the monsoon against the authority of the case that first named the season as a reason. The episode is a small parable of how this field actually develops: a concession recorded in one judgment becomes a practice, the practice becomes an expectation, and the expectation disciplines officials who have never read the judgment, which is the same path by which the 2024 directions, whatever their formal exclusions, are likely to change the conduct of authorities in categories the directions do not bind, since an officer who must build a file for one class of demolition builds the habit of the file for all of them.

## The two cases, stated fairly

The authorities' case deserves its strongest form. Unauthorised occupation of roads, drains, footpaths and water bodies is not a victimless informality: it obstructs the public use for which the space exists, it floods and burns and collapses in ways that kill the occupants first, and it hardens, if left, into a claim that the public must buy back its own streets. Municipal statutes provide for summary removal in these categories precisely because a notice-and-hearing cycle measured in months makes it impossible to keep a carriageway clear or a drain flowing, and the 2024 judgment accepted that logic for the excluded categories rather than extending its code across them. On rehabilitation, the official case adds that the State does in fact run the largest housing programmes in its history, that a cut-off date is the only administrable boundary between a scheme and an open-ended commitment, and that each extension of the date rewards fresh occupation and penalises the citizen who obeyed the law and rented or bought within it.

The residents' case, at its strongest, answers on the same facts. The excluded categories are not where people choose to live; they are where people without alternatives live, because the formal city's housing market offers them nothing at the wage the city pays them for the work the city needs done. Removal without alternative accommodation destroys the livelihood that Olga Tellis held to be part of life itself, interrupts schooling, scatters the documents and networks through which work is found, and relocates the settlement rather than housing it, since the settlement re-forms at the next margin. A procedural code that protects the owner of an unauthorised floor on private land and excludes the pavement family inverts, on this view, the rationale of protection itself: the greater the vulnerability, the thinner the process. And on the cut-off, the residents' case is that a boundary which governments move at election time is not being administered as a boundary at all; it is being administered as a promise, renewed to each cohort in turn, and the household outside it is punished for believing the previous promise was final.

The comparison with the other great displacement statute of the period sharpens what is missing. Under the land acquisition law examined in the series article on the [LARR Act 2013](/2013/11/01/larr-act-2013-explained/), a family displaced by acquisition holds a statutory entitlement to rehabilitation and resettlement: the entitlements are enumerated in the Act, they attach to the family by force of the statute, and they can be demanded as a matter of right in the acquisition process. No equivalent schedule of entitlements exists for the family removed from a settlement. Its rehabilitation depends on fitting a scheme's date and list, and where it does not fit, the law of acquisition's elaborate solicitude for the displaced has no counterpart. The difference is not a difference in the human event, which is the loss of a home in both cases. It is a difference in the legal trigger: the State that takes land for a project must rehabilitate, while the State that clears land it already owns need only follow process, and outside the 2024 code's field, very little process at all.

## What the resident, the official and the researcher should each take away

For a resident or a community organisation, the operative knowledge is situational: which side of each line the settlement stands on. Is the area declared under the State statute, and is the household on the eligibility list, and if not, on what document does the objection rest? Is the land private, municipal, railway or water body, because that answer decides whether the 2024 procedure must precede any demolition? Has a notice actually been served in the manner the law requires, registered post and affixture, and what does it say about the violation and the time to reply? The record a household keeps is the substance of every one of those questions: proof of residence across the years, the ration card, the electoral roll entries, the survey acknowledgement and photo pass, and every notice received, kept together and kept safely, because eligibility and any challenge both run on paper. That is the practical point at which documentation tools earn their place in this subject: a single encrypted file in which a family or a community worker keeps the residence proofs, the survey papers and the notices is the record on which rehabilitation is claimed, and [the VaultBook account of keeping sensitive records protected](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describes the discipline of holding such a record so that it survives the loss of the home it describes. The general position set out in this article is legal information about how these regimes work; it is not advice on an individual case, and any household facing a notice should take advice on its own facts, because the windows for reply and appeal in this field are short and unforgiving.

For the official and the authority, the 2024 judgment converted good practice into personal risk. The file must show service by registered post and affixture, a hearing with minutes, a reasoned order that engages with compoundability, the fifteen-day pause after the order, an inspection report, videography, and a report naming the participating officials to the Municipal Commissioner. Zulfiquar Haider shows that the bill for skipping those steps is presented to the authority and, under the 2024 directions, can be presented to the officer. The official's protection is the record, built at the time, in the order the directions prescribe.

For the researcher and the journalist, the discipline this subject demands is mostly negative: do not describe the 2024 directions as applying to all demolitions, do not describe Olga Tellis as having barred the eviction of pavement dwellers, and do not describe any housing mission as conferring an entitlement. Each of those errors is in wide circulation, and each flatters the legal position of the vulnerable in a way the law does not support. The accurate account is more interesting than the flattering one: a detailed national procedure with a deliberate hole in the middle of it, a constitutional right to shelter that disciplines process without allotting houses, and a rehabilitation system whose decisive rule is a date in a government resolution.

## Assessment

The series to which this article belongs has repeatedly found that the exceptions to a protective instrument determine its reach, and this subject is the purest instance. The 1956 Act's eviction control, the strongest in the field, is confined by territory and tenancy. The rehabilitation schemes' promise of a free home is confined by a date. The right to shelter is confined, by the very cases that announced it, to a discipline on State action rather than a claim to a house. The 2024 directions are confined by their exclusions. In each instrument the boundary was drawn deliberately, by a legislature choosing a category, a government choosing a date, or a court choosing the ground on which its code would not run. The law of slum rehabilitation in India is therefore best read as a set of concentric protections, strongest at the centre, where a declared settlement, a pre-cut-off household and a structure on private land coincide, and thinning to the municipal constable's summary power at the margin where the poorest housing stands. Whether that gradient is the unavoidable shape of administering scarcity or an inversion of the order in which protection ought to run is the live question of the field, and it is a question about the boundary lines, not about the sincerity of the instruments inside them.

The evidence available for judging the system as a whole is of a particular kind, and its limits should be acknowledged rather than papered over. There is no national count of demolitions, no official series that records how many structures were removed in a year, under which power, with what notice, and with what outcome for the households, and the absence is itself a finding: the State that surveys settlements household by household for its schemes keeps no corresponding account of its removals. What exists instead is the litigation record, which overrepresents the settlements that reached a court; the scheme record, which counts sanctions and completions for the listed and is silent on the unlisted; and the reporting of the press and of housing rights organisations, which documents individual demolitions in detail without a denominator. A judgment about the field must therefore be built the way this article has built it, from the instruments and the decided cases, with the aggregate outcome stated as a structure rather than a statistic. The structure is not in doubt even where the numbers are: protection in this field is allocated by category and date, the categories and dates are set by the authorities the protection restrains, and the population with the weakest documents and the most contested land holds the thinnest version of every instrument the system contains. A reader who has followed the argument from the 1956 Act to the 2024 directions has watched that allocation being made, deliberately and in the open, by legislatures, governments and courts in turn, and the open quality of the choices is what makes reform arguable at all: boundaries drawn in public law can be redrawn in public law, and the history of the cut-off date shows governments redrawing them whenever the politics of the settlement vote requires it.

What would repair look like, within the grain of the instruments that exist? The field's own materials suggest the shape of the argument, and it is an argument about where rules are placed rather than about new rights. A cut-off date fixed by statute, or by a statutory formula tied to the survey of each settlement, would move the decisive boundary of rehabilitation from the resolution book to the Act, where it could be debated once and applied generally instead of being renegotiated by cohort. An entitlement schedule for the displaced, of the kind the land acquisition statute carries, would give the household outside every scheme a floor of enumerated consequences, transit support, notice and a rehabilitation offer scaled to the loss, instead of a residue of discretion. And the extension of the 2024 procedure, by legislation rather than by judgment, to the categories the judgment excluded, with the summary power preserved for the genuine obstruction and the notice regime applied to the settled dwelling, would close the distance between the rule and the remedy that the Haldwani litigation exposes. None of these moves requires the invention of a statutory right to housing, which no legislature has shown an appetite to enact. Each takes a boundary that is presently drawn by category, date or discretion and redraws it as a rule. The history recorded in this article gives ground for expecting the boundary work to continue to be done where it has always been done: in the next government resolution moving a date, in the next scheme guideline adjusting a category, and in the next constitutional bench asked to say how far procedure must travel before a home may be taken down. The statutes will be read, as they have been read throughout, by the light of their exceptions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which laws govern slum rehabilitation in India?

Slum rehabilitation is governed mainly by State law, because land and local government are State subjects. Most States have a slum areas statute providing for declaration of slum areas, improvement, clearance and, in Maharashtra's case, redevelopment through a Slum Rehabilitation Authority. The Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1956 is a central Act, but it extends to the Union territories and operates principally in Delhi. Constitutional protection comes from Article 21 case law on livelihood and shelter, municipal statutes govern demolition of unauthorised structures, and central housing missions fund delivery without creating statutory entitlements.

### Q: What makes an area a declared slum?

Under the Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1956, the competent authority may declare an area a slum area where the buildings there are unfit for human habitation, or detrimental to safety, health or morals, because of dilapidation, overcrowding, faulty arrangement or design of buildings, narrowness or faulty arrangement of streets, or lack of ventilation, light or sanitation facilities. State statutes use similar tests. The declaration is an administrative act, usually following a survey, and it is the gateway to the statute's improvement and clearance powers, its eviction controls and its eligibility machinery.

### Q: Who is eligible for rehabilitation and what is a cut off date?

Eligibility depends on proof of residence in the settlement before a fixed date, called the cut-off date. The household must show documents such as an entry in the electoral roll of the relevant year, a ration card or a photo pass issued in a government survey, and its name then enters the eligibility list prepared for the scheme. A household that cannot prove residence before the date is ineligible, however long it has lived in the settlement since. The date is fixed by executive decision rather than by the statute, and governments have extended it from time to time, as Maharashtra did in moving its date from 1995 to 2000.

### Q: What consent is required for a redevelopment scheme?

Under the Maharashtra Slum Rehabilitation Authority's guidelines, seventy percent or more of the eligible hutment dwellers in a settlement must show willingness to join the scheme and form a co-operative housing society of the eligible dwellers before redevelopment proceeds. Courts have treated the threshold as substantive, asking whether the requisite proportion of eligible dwellers has genuinely consented. Consent is the residents' collective safeguard against redevelopment imposed from above, although its gathering by the developer who profits from the scheme has been questioned. Where the landowner files the scheme himself, the Authority's circulars have relaxed the requirement.

### Q: What did the Olga Tellis judgment decide?

In Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation in 1985, a Constitution Bench held that the right to life in Article 21 includes the right to livelihood, since eviction from a dwelling near a person's work deprives that person of the means of living, and that any procedure depriving a person of livelihood must be just, fair and reasonable. The Court, however, upheld Section 314 of the Bombay Municipal Corporation Act, which permits removal of encroachments from pavements without notice, held that no one has a right to encroach on public property, and declined to hold that pavement dwellers could remain. It directed that the State's assurances of alternate pitches to dwellers enumerated in the 1976 census be honoured.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court direct about demolitions in 2024?

In In Re: Directions in the Matter of Demolition of Structures, decided on 13 November 2024, the Supreme Court held that the executive cannot demolish a person's property merely because the person is accused or convicted of an offence, since deciding guilt and punishing are judicial functions. Acting under Article 142, it required a show cause notice served by registered post and affixture, allowing at least fifteen days or the municipal period, whichever is later; a personal hearing with minutes; a reasoned final order dealing with compoundability; upload of the order on a digital portal; a fifteen-day pause after the order; and inspection, videography and personal liability of officials for violations.

### Q: Do the demolition safeguards cover pavement and railway land?

No. The Supreme Court stated expressly that the 2024 directions do not apply to an unauthorised structure in a public place such as a road, street or footpath, abutting a railway line, or on a river or water body, and do not apply where a court has ordered the demolition. Settlements in those locations, which include a large part of India's informal housing, remain governed by the municipal and railway statutes, which often permit summary removal, and by the Article 21 case law on livelihood and shelter. This exclusion is the most consequential limit on the 2024 judgment and the point most accounts of it omit.

### Q: Does Indian law recognise a right to housing?

No statute in India confers an enforceable right to housing that a person can claim against the State. The right to shelter exists as constitutional doctrine: the Supreme Court has read it into Article 21, holding in cases such as Chameli Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh that shelter, with adequate space, light, air, water and sanitation, forms part of the right to life. That doctrine disciplines how the State evicts and demolishes, and it obliges the State to honour housing schemes it has adopted. It does not entitle an individual to be allotted a house on demand, and housing missions remain executive programmes rather than legal entitlements.

### Q: What is the Slum Rehabilitation Authority and what does it do?

The Slum Rehabilitation Authority is the statutory body created by Chapter I-A of the Maharashtra Slum Areas (Improvement, Clearance and Redevelopment) Act, 1971, inserted in 1995. It prepares and publishes the general Slum Rehabilitation Scheme, declares areas to be Slum Rehabilitation Areas, approves redevelopment proposals for those areas, supervises the eligibility surveys and lists, and can take up redevelopment itself where landowners or occupants do not come forward. Its model is in situ redevelopment: eligible residents are rehoused free on the same site, financed by a market sale component, which distinguishes it from the clearance boards of other States.

### Q: How does the free sale component finance free rehabilitation housing?

A redevelopment scheme builds two components on the same land. The rehabilitation component contains the tenements given free to eligible residents. The sale component contains additional floor space, sanctioned as an incentive, which the developer sells at market prices, together with transferable development rights usable on other plots. The sale proceeds are expected to cover the cost of the free tenements, the infrastructure and the developer's return. The mechanism works only where land values are high enough to carry the cross-subsidy, which is why the model belongs to expensive cities and why its projects are exposed to property market downturns.

### Q: What happens to residents who are found ineligible under a rehabilitation scheme?

A resident found ineligible, because residence before the cut-off date cannot be proved or the structure falls outside the survey's categories, receives no tenement under the scheme. When the scheme is executed and the site is cleared, the structure is demolished and the resident must leave, with protection limited to the general law governing demolitions and any alternative arrangement the government of the day chooses to offer. The resident may object to the eligibility list and pursue the appeal or writ remedies the State system provides, since errors in surveys and lists are common. Ineligibility is the point at which rehabilitation law ends and removal law begins.

### Q: Is the consent of the landowner needed to redevelop a slum on private land?

The landowner holds a preferential right in the Maharashtra scheme. Chapter I-A of the 1971 Act preserves the owner's right to redevelop a Slum Rehabilitation Area, and the Supreme Court has confirmed that the Authority's power to acquire the land or take up redevelopment arises where the owner does not come forward with a scheme within a reasonable time. A residents' society and developer can therefore propose redevelopment of privately owned slum land, but the proposal operates subject to the owner's preference. The owner's own right after a clearance order, under the older chapters, follows the same pattern of preference followed by authority action on default.

### Q: What documents prove residence before the cut-off date?

The standard proofs are those generated by the State itself: the electoral roll of the year of the cut-off date showing the resident's name at the settlement address, the ration card, and the photo pass or survey acknowledgement issued when the government surveyed the settlement and photographed its structures. Utility records such as electricity bills are used in support. The survey list, Annexure II in Maharashtra practice, records the structure, the occupant and the documents relied on, and disputes are fought over entries in that list. Keeping the full set of documents, including every notice received, is what allows a household to prove eligibility or challenge an exclusion.

### Q: Does the Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana give a slum dweller an enforceable right to a house?

No. The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (Urban) is an executive mission, launched in 2015, whose guidelines are administrative instructions and whose sanctions are budgetary decisions. Its in-situ slum redevelopment vertical funded redevelopment projects with central assistance, but it created no statutory right in an identified household to be given a house, and a person passed over by the programme has no suit for the house itself. The remedy for exclusion is a representation to the implementing agency and, at the constitutional margin, a challenge to arbitrary action. The successor mission, in force from September 2024, likewise operates through programme verticals rather than entitlements.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in the Prayagraj demolition case?

In Zulfiquar Haider v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided on 1 April 2025, the Supreme Court dealt with houses demolished by the Prayagraj Development Authority after notices were merely affixed, without the repeated attempts at personal service the statute required, and the demolition was carried out within a day of the last communication and inside the appeal period. The Court held the demolitions illegal and inhuman, restated that the right to shelter is an integral part of Article 21, directed the Authority to follow the 2024 demolition directions scrupulously, and ordered it to pay ten lakh rupees in each appeal to the persons whose homes had been demolished.

### Q: How does slum law in Delhi work, and what does DUSIB do?

Delhi is governed by the central Slum Areas (Improvement and Clearance) Act, 1956, which applies to the Union territories, together with the Delhi Urban Shelter Improvement Board Act, 2010, which created DUSIB as the agency for improvement and rehabilitation of the capital's jhuggi settlements. The Delhi Slum and JJ Rehabilitation and Relocation Policy, 2015 fixes the capital's eligibility line at settlements existing before 1 January 2006 and proceeds on the basis that covered settlements are not removed without rehabilitation. The Delhi High Court, in Sudama Singh in 2010 and Ajay Maken in 2019, required survey, consultation and rehabilitation planning before forced eviction of long-standing settlements.

### Q: What process must follow when a demolition notice is served?

Where the 2024 Supreme Court directions apply, the notice must be served by registered post and affixed on the structure, and it must state the nature of the unauthorised construction, the specific violation and the grounds for demolition. The recipient has the period in the municipal law or fifteen days from receipt, whichever is later, to answer. The authority must then hold a personal hearing, record minutes, and pass a reasoned order dealing with the contentions and with whether the construction is compoundable. Demolition cannot follow for fifteen days after the order reaches the recipient, so that a challenge can be filed, and the recipient may remove the offending construction voluntarily within that period.

### Q: Who fixes the cut-off date for slum rehabilitation, and why does it keep changing?

The cut-off date is fixed by the government of the State through executive decisions such as government resolutions, not by the text of the slum statute. Maharashtra fixed 1 January 1995 for its rehabilitation scheme and extended regularisation to 1 January 2000 by a government resolution in 2014; Delhi's policy uses 1 January 2006. The date changes because it is politically and administratively responsive: each extension is defended as recognising settlements that have existed for decades, and each is criticised as rewarding fresh occupation. Because the instrument is executive, the entitlement of a household can turn on a resolution it had no part in making.

### Q: What can a person recover if a demolition was unlawful?

Under the 2024 directions, a demolition carried out in violation of the prescribed procedure amounts to contempt of court, and the officials responsible are personally liable, including liability to restore the demolished property at their own cost. Separately, the constitutional courts can award compensation for an illegal demolition as a public law remedy for the violation of Article 21, as the Supreme Court did in the Prayagraj case in 2025, where the development authority was ordered to pay ten lakh rupees in each appeal. The affected person may also pursue the civil remedies the judgment leaves open for establishing rights over the land and claiming compensation.

### Q: What is the difference between in situ redevelopment and relocation?

In situ redevelopment rebuilds the settlement on the same land: residents move into transit accommodation for the construction period and return to permanent tenements on the site, preserving the location on which their livelihoods, schools and networks depend. Relocation moves the settlement to a different site, usually on cheaper land at the city's edge, where the housing may be better built but the distance from work and services is greater. The Maharashtra rehabilitation model is in situ by design. Clearance boards historically relied on relocation colonies, and the livelihood reasoning of Olga Tellis explains why the difference matters legally as well as socially.
