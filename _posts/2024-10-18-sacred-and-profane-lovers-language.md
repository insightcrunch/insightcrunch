The first words spoken about desire in Romeo and Juliet are a threat to cut off maidenheads, and the most famous words spoken about desire in the play are a prayer at a shrine. Between Sampson's boast in the opening scene and Romeo's pilgrim sonnet at the Capulet feast, the tragedy sets its two great registers of love side by side and refuses, from that point onward, to let either of them speak alone. Romeo calls Juliet a saint, a shrine, a bright angel; Mercutio conjures him out of hiding with a catalogue of a woman's body; the Nurse remembers a toddler's fall and turns it into a joke about the wedding night. The question the language of the play keeps posing is whether these registers are enemies, the holy at war with the bawdy for possession of the lovers, or partners in a single account of what love is. The verdict argued here is that they complete each other. The sacred register without the profane curdles into the empty Petrarchan sighing of the Rosaline scenes, worship addressed to an absence; the profane register without the sacred curdles into the violence of the servants' opening quarrel, appetite with a sword in its hand. What the lovers build, in the brief span the feud allows, is a language in which worship keeps the body honest about its own dignity and bawdy keeps worship honest about its own object, and the play's final judgment falls not on the mixture but on the city that gave the mixture no lawful room to live in.

That verdict needs the evidence assembled in order. The collision begins inside a single sonnet at the feast, where a seduction is conducted entirely in the vocabulary of pilgrimage and where Juliet, far from being overwhelmed by the borrowed holiness, edits it as it goes. It widens in the orchard wall's shadow, where Mercutio performs a rival rite of conjuration that parodies the pilgrim language point for point while Romeo, yards away, begins the balcony scene by overhearing a woman think. It runs through the Nurse's earthy memory, through the Friar's warnings spoken inside the wedding itself, through Juliet's wedding-night soliloquy, where a hymn of invocation carries the frankest desire in the play, and it ends in a tomb that Romeo calls a feasting presence and that Juliet enters with a dagger she calls happy. At every station the pattern is the same. Neither register wins. Each corrects the other's characteristic lie, and the correction is where the play's vision of love is located. Quotations in what follows are from the Arden Third Series text of the play edited by René Weis, cited by act, scene, and line.

## Do the Sacred and the Profane Clash or Complete Each Other?

The case for clash is easy to state and has the play's loudest voices behind it. Mercutio treats Romeo's devotion as a sickness to be purged by laughter, and his conjuration in Act 2 Scene 1 is built as an anti-liturgy, a summoning of the body's parts against the soul's vocabulary. The Friar treats the lovers' intensity as a fire that will consume itself and warns them, in the middle of their own wedding, that violent delights have violent ends. The city treats the whole affair as a public nuisance to be managed by edict and exile. On this reading the sacred language is a beautiful self-deception, the gilding on an appetite that Mercutio names more honestly, and the profane register is the play's ground truth breaking through the gilt whenever the lovers stop performing for each other. The ending then reads as the exposure of the fraud: the shrine turns out to be a tomb, the saint turns out to be a corpse, and the pilgrimage ends at a grave that no prayer reopens.

The case for completion is quieter and rests on sequence rather than volume. The profane register speaks first in the play and speaks, significantly, in the mouths of men who are bragging about women they do not know and threatening women they will never touch. Appetite in its pure form, unmixed with any devotion, enters the tragedy as a pretext for a street fight. The sacred register also appears first in a corrupted form, in Romeo's Petrarchan exercises over Rosaline, a woman who never appears onstage and who has forsworn the whole enterprise; worship in its pure form, unmixed with any answering body, enters as a young man talking to himself in the dark about an absence. Each register alone is shown failing before the lovers ever meet. What changes at the feast is not that Romeo finds a new vocabulary, since the pilgrim conceit is as conventional as anything he said about Rosaline, but that the vocabulary meets an answering intelligence. Juliet takes the conceit up, corrects its terms, sets its conditions, and grants its petition only when the rite has been renegotiated to her satisfaction. From that moment the play's love language is a duet between registers as much as between people, and the duet is the thing neither the servants' bawdy nor the Rosaline worship could produce on its own.

The completion thesis also fits the play's distribution of survival. Mercutio's register does not outlive him as a living voice, yet its truths persist inside the lovers' married speech, in Juliet's refusal to pretend that her longing is anything other than what it is. The sacred register does not outlive the lovers as a living voice either, yet its forms persist in the closing scene, where the fathers vow golden statues and the Prince pronounces a judgment in the cadences of a reckoning. What dies in the play is never a register. What dies is the possibility of the two registers being spoken together by people allowed to grow old inside them. The sections that follow test that claim against the passages where the collision is densest, beginning with the fourteen lines in which the whole system is contained in miniature.

## Before the Feast: Appetite Without Devotion and Devotion Without a Body

The play is scrupulous about showing each register failing alone before it permits the two to meet, and it spends its entire first act on the demonstration. The profane failure comes first, in the mouths of Sampson and Gregory, whose opening exchange converts desire into ordnance. Gregory observes that the quarrel is between their masters and the masters' men; Sampson answers that he will push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall, and when Gregory asks whether he means to cut off heads, Sampson clarifies: "Ay, the heads of the maids, or their maidenheads, take it in what sense thou wilt" (1.1.24-27). The joke is the register's whole philosophy stated as a pun. Women are a category of enemy property, desire is a form of assault, and the taking of a maidenhead is grammatically indistinguishable from the taking of a head. Nothing in the speech is erotic in any sense the lovers would recognize. It is appetite as feud logistics, and its immediate consequence is the brawl: within a hundred lines the speakers are fighting in earnest, and the Prince is threatening their lives for disturbing the streets. The play's judgment on the pure profane is delivered by the plot before any critic can formulate it. Desire that speaks only this language produces violence and then requires the state's violence to quiet it, and the women it boasts about never appear, because the register does not need women. It needs an audience of other men.

Romeo's entrance, minutes later, presents the mirror failure in a finer fabric. His first speeches are a liturgy of oxymoron, "O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1.175), addressed to a Rosaline who has, in Benvolio's report and Romeo's own, forsworn the entire subject: she has Dian's wit, she will not be hit with Cupid's arrow, she has sworn to live chaste (1.1.205-210). The sacred register is all here, the vows, the siege, the saint's refusal, the worshipper's living death, and it is entirely unidirectional. Romeo describes the effect on himself with an accuracy his friends cannot match: he lives dead, he tells an audience of one, shut up in darkness, without judgment, having no joy in the entire enterprise. The worship has curdled into a technology for feeling exalted about misery, and its object might as well be a statue for all the answering she does. Diana, the presiding deity of this first devotion, is the goddess of the refusal itself, and Romeo's cult of her votary is a shrine at which the worshipper is also the only priest, the only congregation, and the only sacrifice. The play takes care that the audience meet this version of the sacred register first and register its sterility, so that the feast sonnet's identical vocabulary, answered at last, can be heard as a different event rather than a repetition.

Between the two failures stands Benvolio, whose advice to Romeo is the profane register in its respectable, therapeutic form: forget Rosaline by examining other beauties, take some new infection in the eye and the rank poison of the old will die (1.2.46-50). The counsel is Mercutio's philosophy in a lower key, desire as a fluid to be exchanged for a fresher fluid, and its presence in the mouth of the play's peacemaker matters. The profane register is not the property of wits and servants. It is Verona's common sense, the advice every reasonable person gives a lovesick young man, and its reasonableness is exactly what the play is about to test. Benvolio means well, his prescription is practical, and when Romeo follows it to the feast he finds, not a replacement fluid, but a person who answers him in two registers at once and audits both. The reasonable cure works, in short, by accident and by excess: it leads Romeo to a love that obeys neither the appetite doctrine, since it refuses substitution outright, nor the worship doctrine, since its saint talks back. The first act's careful triptych, appetite alone, worship alone, and common sense mediating between them, is the control experiment against which the feast's experiment is run, and criticism that begins the register argument at the sonnet has skipped the controls.

One further detail of the control experiment repays attention, which is the distribution of the word love itself. In the servants' mouths the word scarcely appears; appetite speaks in verbs of pushing, thrusting, and cutting, and needs no noun for the feeling it claims to serve. In Romeo's Rosaline speeches the noun is everywhere and the verbs are all sighs, vows, and forswearings, grammar without an object in the world. The feast sonnet is the first passage in the play where the noun acquires verbs that do something in the room: hands touch, lips stand ready, sins are purged and demanded back, a fine is paid. The mixture, that is, is not detectable as a theme but as a change in the language's physics, from declaration to transaction. What the lovers build across the following acts is an extension of that transaction across every obstacle Verona can place in its path, and what Verona builds in reply is a series of increasingly desperate attempts to force the language back into its separate, governable halves: the daughter returned to the book of Paris, the son banished beyond the walls, the wife threatened with the street. The feud's final defeat is linguistic before it is moral. It cannot make the lovers speak in single registers again.

## The First Kiss: How a Pilgrim Sonnet Turns Worship into a Kiss

Romeo's opening move at the feast is an apology for a touch that has barely happened, and the apology is dressed as theology. "If I profane with my unworthiest hand / This holy shrine, the gentle fine is this: / My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand / To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss" (1.5.92-95). Every term in the quatrain does double work. The hand is unworthy, the beloved is a shrine, the kiss is a fine to be paid, and the lips are pilgrims, travellers who have journeyed to a holy place in hope of a blessing. The conceit converts an advance into an act of devotion and converts Juliet, in the same gesture, from a girl at a dance into the presiding saint of a cult. It is a seduction line, and a practised one, and the scene knows it: the word profane stands at the head of the speech like a warning label the speaker has pasted on himself, conceding in advance that the holy place and the trespass are the same event described from two sides.

What keeps the moment from being a simple exposure of technique is Juliet's answer, which accepts the rite and rewrites its rubrics in real time. "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this; / For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5.96-99). The Capulet daughter declines the role of distant icon. Saints, she points out, have hands; touch is built into the devotion Romeo claims to be practising; the palmer's kiss is a kiss of palms, sanctioned by the very cult he invokes. Where Romeo's quatrain had used holiness as a ladder toward a kiss of the lips, Juliet's quatrain uses holiness as a level floor on which two people may stand as equals in the rite. The exchange continues through the granting of the prayer, the purging of sin, and the comic demand for the sin back, until Juliet delivers the scene's most famous audit of the whole performance: "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.112). The line is the collision in a single sentence. She has received the worship, enjoyed it, granted it, and she still marks it as bookish, learned, a form performed by someone who has read the manual. The sacred register survives her audit, since she kisses him again, but it survives on her terms, which is the pattern the entire play will repeat.

### Why does Romeo open the courtship by calling his own hand profane?

The self-accusation is the conceit's engine. A pilgrim who confessed no unworthiness would have no fine to pay and no petition to press, so Romeo builds the trespass into the devotion: the kiss he requests comes as the remedy for his confessed fault. The shrine is holy because he is not, and the sonnet runs on that difference.

The first-kiss scene has its own dedicated close reading elsewhere in this series, in [the study of the first kiss and its pilgrims' and palmers' hands](/2023/07/14/first-kiss-pilgrim-imagery-analysis/), and the concern here is not the scene's internal mechanics but its function in the play's wider economy of registers. Read as the collision's first instance, the sonnet establishes three facts that everything later depends on. First, the sacred language is instrumental from the start: it is spoken to obtain something, and both speakers know it. Second, the instrument is not thereby exposed as false, since Juliet's corrections assume the rite is worth correcting rather than discarding. Third, the profane is present inside the sacred form from the beginning, not as an invader but as the form's acknowledged content: the sin that is purged and then demanded back is the kiss itself, and the joke about sin only works because both parties understand exactly what has been exchanged. A reading of the play that treats the pilgrim language as a pure elevation later dragged down by bawdy has to explain away the evidence of its own founding scene, in which elevation and appetite are grammatically inseparable from the first quatrain.

The sonnet also establishes the asymmetry that the rest of the play works to correct. Romeo brings the sacred register to the feast as a solo instrument, perfected in the Rosaline period when nobody answered it. Juliet's answering quatrains make it dialogic, and dialogue is the condition under which the register stays honest, because an answering saint can refuse, qualify, and set terms. Rosaline's silence had let Romeo's worship drift into self-regard; the whole pathology of Act 1, the locked chamber, the artificial night, the sighs counted like coin, is what sacred language becomes when it has no body answering it. The feast sonnet is therefore best read not as the sacred register's triumph but as its rescue, performed by the person it was addressed to. That rescue has a price the scene also names: once the saint answers, she acquires a stake in the rite, and the play will hold her to it in scenes where the register's consolations are withdrawn one by one.

## Mercutio at the Wall: The Bawdy Conjuration of Act 2 Scene 1

The next voice raised in the play after the lovers' parting at the feast belongs to Mercutio, and it is raised in the dark outside the Capulet orchard, calling for a Romeo who is no longer listening. Believing his friend still sick with Rosaline, Mercutio resolves to conjure him as a magician raises a spirit, and the conjuration is a catalogue. "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes, / By her high forehead and her scarlet lip, / By her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh, / And the demesnes that there adjacent lie, / That in thy likeness thou appear to us" (2.1.27-31). The form is liturgical, a solemn adjuration by sacred objects in ascending order, and the content is an inventory of a body, descending from eyes to thighs to the demesnes adjacent. It is the pilgrim sonnet read backward. Where Romeo had ascended from a hand to a shrine, Mercutio descends from a face to the flesh, and he does it in the grammar of a rite, because the joke depends on the audience hearing the sacred form carrying the profane freight without breaking stride.

The conjuration has a second layer that the sonnet's elegance tends to conceal. Mercutio imagines Romeo's desire as a spirit to be raised and laid, and his image for the laying is explicitly sexual: the spirit must be raised in his mistress's circle and left standing there until she has laid it and conjured it down (2.1.22-25). Love, in this metaphysics, is a haunting that the body exorcises. Benvolio, listening, supplies the scene's quiet gloss when he observes that Mercutio speaks of a Romeo who no longer exists: the man being conjured by Rosaline's body has already transferred his devotions, and the rite fails not because it is profane but because its information is out of date. The bawdy register, for all its claims to ground truth, is working from a superseded map of its friend's heart. Within minutes the audience will hear Romeo, hidden and overhearing, mark the distance himself: "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2.1). The play is careful to give the profane register its full brilliance and its full limitation in the same movement. Mercutio's anatomy of desire leaves out the one datum that matters, which is that desire has changed its object and its nature while he was talking.

### What kind of rite is Mercutio performing outside the orchard wall?

The conjuration is an anti-liturgy, a summoning rite aimed at the body, and its blasphemy is structural. A liturgy adjures by names to raise the spirit toward worship; Mercutio adjures by a woman's parts to drag his friend toward appetite. It sets the rite against the pilgrim devotion it parodies, so the audience hears ceremonies competing for the man.

The conjuration scene also clarifies what the profane register is for, in the play's economy, by showing what it cannot do. It cannot locate Romeo. The loudest, wittiest, most sexually knowing voice in Verona calls into the dark and receives no answer, because the man it seeks is inside the wall, in the territory of the answered shrine. Yet the scene is not a simple defeat for bawdy, since Mercutio's diagnosis of the Rosaline sickness was correct in every particular and his purge by laughter had been the only medicine Act 1 offered that Romeo could not argue with. The full account of [Mercutio's bawdy, his sex, wit, and wordplay](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/) belongs to its own article in this series; within the collision, the conjuration's role is to state the profane register's strongest claim at maximum force, that worship is appetite in costume, at the exact moment the play is preparing to disprove the claim's completeness in the orchard. The registers pass each other in the dark, one outside the wall shouting a body upward, one inside it about to hear a mind speak. The wall itself is the play's neatest emblem of their relation: they are separated by a boundary that the action keeps crossing in both directions.

Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, delivered an act earlier, belongs to the same system and deserves its place in the evidence. Mab is a fairy midwife of dreams, and among her works is the education of maids: she is "the hag, when maids lie on their backs, / That presses them and learns them first to bear, / Making them women of good carriage" (1.4.92-94). The speech dresses a sexual initiation in the language of a folk rite, complete with a ministering spirit and a curriculum, which is precisely the pilgrim sonnet's trick performed from the other side. Where Romeo borrows the sacred to dignify appetite, Mercutio borrows the supernatural to administer it. The two friends are doing the same thing to each other all through the early acts, raiding the same treasury of rite and wonder for opposite treasuries of meaning, and the play's refusal to declare either raid illegitimate is the foundation of its verdict. Bawdy in this play is never merely the absence of the sacred. It is a rival sacred, with its own spirits, its own sacraments of initiation, and its own doctrine, that love is a weight the body learns to bear.

The banter scene of Act 2 Scene 4 then performs the experiment the conjuration could not: it puts the two friends in one room, in daylight, with Romeo answering. Mercutio's delight is immediate and his verdict famous: "Now art thou sociable, now art thou Romeo; now art thou what thou art, by art as well as by nature" (2.4.84-86). The exchange that earns the verdict is a fencing match of puns, goose and wild-goose, pump and pink, conducted entirely in the profane register's native medium, and Mercutio reads Romeo's recovered fluency as a resurrection: the Petrarchan groaner is dead, the sociable man is back. The audience, alone in the theatre, knows the reading is exactly wrong in the interesting way. Romeo is not cured of love; he is cured of the single register. He can fence in bawdy at noon because at midnight he fenced in liturgy, and the doubleness Mercutio hears as a return to nature is in fact the mixture announcing itself in the profane key. The scene's comedy depends on the misreading, and its melancholy does too, since Mercutio will die within the day still believing he has his friend back, when what he has is a friend who has acquired a second language and a secret that the second language is sworn to protect. When the Nurse arrives with her fan and her errand, and Mercutio salutes her as a bawd and sings at her, the farce is covering the scene's real event, which is the profane register being used, for the first time, as the lovers' camouflage: the marriage is arranged, in public, in bawdy's own street, under cover of bawdy's own noise. The register that claimed to expose all worship as appetite ends up providing the screen behind which the worship proceeds to its sacrament. The play wastes nothing, not even its counter-liturgy.

## The Nurse's Bawdy: Earthiness as the Marriage's Ground Crew

If Mercutio is the profane register's theologian, the Nurse is its parish priest, and her parish is the Capulet nursery. Her great speech in Act 1 Scene 3, delivered while Lady Capulet attempts to raise the subject of marriage in the language of a book to be read, keeps dragging the topic back to the body that marriage will use. Remembering the weaning, she retells her dead husband's joke over the fallen toddler: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou hast more wit, / Wilt thou not, Jule?" and adds, of the child who answered "Ay," that she will bear watching (1.3.42-54). The joke's mechanism is the whole register in miniature. A fall, an injury, a bump on the brow, all the accidents of a child's body, are read forward into the posture of the marriage bed, and the reading is done with love, by the person who knows the body in question best, in the room where the marriage is being arranged. Lady Capulet's book metaphor proposes a Juliet who is read; the Nurse's anecdote insists on a Juliet who falls, bruises, hungers, and will lie down. Both women are describing the same wedding. The collision is not between the household and an outside corruption but inside the household's own conversation, between the mother's euphemism and the nurse's memory.

The mother's euphemism deserves a closer look, because it constitutes a third language in the room, neither sacred nor profane, and the play's verdict quietly condemns it below both. Lady Capulet proposes Paris to her daughter as a volume: Juliet is to find written in the margent of his eyes the delight that is writ there, to read the bound book of his face, since this precious book of love, this unbound lover, lacks only a cover to beautify him (1.3.81-90). The conceit is elegant, commercial, and chilling. A husband is a text to be inspected for its binding; the wife is the cover the volume lacks; marriage is the act of shelving the two together, and the daughter's consent is solicited as a reader's opinion of the product. Nothing in the speech is bawdy, since no body is admitted to exist, and nothing in it is sacred, since no devotion is imagined either. It is the register of the showroom, and the Nurse's interjections keep puncturing it with the body's data: the age, the weaning, the fall. The scene's laughter is the sound of the showroom register failing to govern a conversation in a room where a woman who has actually raised the bride is present. Later criticism sometimes treats the Nurse's speech as comic delay before the tragedy's real business. The register argument reverses the valuation. Her speech is the real business, the play's first demonstration that a language which admits the body will always outlast, in honesty and in usefulness, a language which admits only the binding.

The book conceit also has a long fuse. Romeo and Juliet will take the mother's metaphor and baptize it at the feast, where the beloved is indeed treated as a text, but a sacred one, a shrine's inscription to be kissed rather than a volume to be shelved; Juliet's verdict on the kiss, that Romeo kisses by the book (1.5.112), is the daughter returning the mother's metaphor with interest, having converted it from an instrument of the market into an instrument of judgment. The covers and margents of Act 1 Scene 3 are the last time the household's official language sounds plausible. After the feast, the book belongs to the lovers.

The Nurse's bawdy differs from Mercutio's in the one way that matters most to the play's verdict: hers serves the love it jokes about. It is the Nurse who carries the messages, who goes to shrift as a cover story, who procures the rope ladder, who stands between Juliet's worship and its consummation as the indispensable profane machinery of a sacred event. Her teasing in Act 2 Scene 5, when she withholds Romeo's answer while Juliet burns for it, is cruelty in the service of intimacy, and her observation that the wanton blood comes up in Juliet's cheeks at any news (2.5.71-72) is the profane register doing its proper work, reading the body truthfully at the moment the body's owner is trying to speak of nothing but arrangements. The play's most sacramental act, the secret marriage, is logistically impossible without this woman and her register. A church wedding performed by a friar depends, hour by hour, on a bawdy old servant's errands, lies, and ladders. Any account of the sacred in the play that omits the ground crew has mistaken the rite for the whole religion.

Her register also survives the play's worst hour of news-bearing, and the survival is diagnostic. In Act 3 Scene 2 the Nurse arrives with the report of the street fight tangled past recognition, keening that he is dead, he is dead, he is dead, without settling whose death she means, until Juliet is driven to believe Romeo slain and then to understand that Romeo is the slayer and banished (3.2.37-72). The scene is painful comedy built from the profane register's one structural weakness: it reports bodies faster than it sorts them, and grief in its mouth becomes a noise before it becomes information. Yet the same scene shows why the lovers cannot dispense with the register even in disgrace. The Nurse's body is the play's only reliable communications network, and her lament, for all its confusion, carries the facts she has: a corpse she saw, a wound she can describe, a name she finally produces. Juliet's correction of the report, her great speech of oxymoron over the beautiful tyrant and the angelical fiend (3.2.73-85), is the sacred register doing the sorting the bawdy could not, arranging the facts into a judgment. The two speeches together are the collision at the level of the news itself: appetite's messenger garbles, devotion's audit restores, and the marriage holds because the wife can perform the second operation on the first's raw material.

The register's failure, when it comes, is equally instructive. In Act 3 Scene 5, after the banishment, the Nurse advises Juliet to marry Paris, and her counsel is the profane register collapsing into mere prudence: the second match is better, the first husband is as good as dead, and Romeo himself is demoted to a dishclout beside the County. Juliet's response is to reclassify the speaker: "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend!" (3.5.235). The damn language is sacred vocabulary, hell's vocabulary, turned on the woman whose bawdy had been the marriage's scaffolding. The moment matters for the collision because it shows the profane register's limit from inside the lovers' camp. Bawdy that grounds worship is the play's ally; bawdy that counsels surrender to the market is its enemy, and Juliet, who has absorbed both registers, can tell the difference in a single speech. She leaves the scene speaking of the Friar in the language of last resort and of herself in the language of the grave, and the registers she carries forward from that room are the ones she has audited, not the ones she was given.

## The Balcony Scene: Oaths, Idolatry, and a Love That Swears by Itself

The balcony scene is where the two registers stop taking turns and start sharing sentences. Romeo enters it fresh from the sonnet's world, ready to swear by the moon, and Juliet stops him with the play's most consequential piece of liturgical criticism: "O swear not by the moon, th' inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111). Pressed for an object worthy of the oath, she first forbids swearing altogether and then makes the offer that has startled readers for centuries: "Or, if thou wilt, swear by thy gracious self, / Which is the god of my idolatry, / And I'll believe thee" (2.2.113-115). The line should be allowed its full weight. Juliet names the danger that critics of the sacred register always raise, idolatry, the worship of a creature in the place of the creator, and she names it not as an accusation but as a description of her own devotional state, offered as the ground of the oath. The profane and the sacred are not merely mixed here. The speaker has audited the mixture, located its theological fault line, and chosen to stand on it with her eyes open.

The scene's other great movement runs the same audit in the opposite direction. Juliet is ashamed of what the darkness has let her say, and she says so in the vocabulary of the rite she is constructing: she would fain deny what she has spoken, she wishes she could unsay it, and she grounds her frankness in the accident of having been overheard before she knew he was there (2.2.105-108). Her desire, in other words, reaches speech by a breach of decorum, and the scene refuses to launder that fact. What the orchard produces is not worship purified of appetite but appetite that has found, in worship's forms, the only language strong enough to bind it. Her great affirmation runs on gift rather than seizure: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133-135). The bounty speech is the sacred register's economics, grace that increases by being spent, and it is spoken by a girl on a balcony negotiating a marriage she intends to consummate within the day. The play does not ask an audience to choose between the sea image and the intention. It asks the audience to notice that the intention is what makes the image more than Petrarchan weather.

### Why does Juliet, and not Romeo, set the terms on which oaths may be sworn?

Juliet has never had the luxury of unexamined devotion, since every word she speaks is spoken inside a household that owns her marriage. She therefore accepts the oath's force, rejects its carelessness, and rewrites its terms until the form can bear the weight of a promise. Romeo arrives ready to swear by whatever shines; she makes him swear by himself.

The balcony scene is also where the name question does its work inside the collision, and the fuller treatment of [the balcony's great question of what a name carries](/2023/08/04/whats-in-a-name-romeo-and-juliet/) belongs to its own article in this series. For the register argument, the essential point is that "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" (2.2.34) is the sacred register turned against the social order: Juliet proposes to treat the feud's names the way her century's reformers treated the saints' names, as human inventions that may be set aside when they obstruct the true object of devotion. It is a reformer's argument applied to a father's house, and its daring is easy to miss at this distance. She is not asking Romeo to stop being himself. She is asking him to accept reclassification, to be baptized again under a name of her choosing, and the sacramental word is hers, not the Friar's, which is one more sign that the play locates the authority of its mixed rite in the woman who audits it.

The scene's handling of the word contract deserves its own sentence in the evidence, because it is the legal register conscripted into the same audit. Juliet tells Romeo she has no joy of this contract tonight, calls it too rash, too unadvised, too sudden, and compares it to lightning that ceases to be before it can be said to lighten (2.2.116-120). A contract is the market's instrument and the church's, the form in which Verona arranges marriages it approves of, and Juliet applies the form to the orchard's improvisation and finds the improvisation wanting in everything except force. Her remedy is not to abandon the contract but to complete it: if Romeo's bent of love be honourable, she instructs him, his purpose marriage, and a messenger will come tomorrow to learn where and at what time he will perform the rite (2.2.142-144). The sacred register is thereby sent to fetch its own institution. Desire that began the night as an overheard soliloquy ends it as an appointment with a friar, scheduled by the beloved, on terms she dictated, in language that has passed through law, liturgy, and the plainest speaking in the play without surrendering to any of them. No other scene in the tragedy compresses the whole argument so tightly: the registers do not merely coexist in the orchard, they process each other, and the product of the processing is a wedding.

What the orchard scene finally proves, against both rival readings, is that the lovers' language can survive cross-examination. Romeo's Rosaline worship never faced a respondent; Mercutio's bawdy never faces one either, since its addressees are a hidden friend and a nurse he is performing for. Juliet cross-examines everything, the oath, the name, the haste, the contract she has no joy of that night because it is "too rash, too unadvised, too sudden" (2.2.118), and the language that emerges from her examination is stronger than what entered it. The play's vision of love is not located in Romeo's first vocabulary or in Mercutio's counter-vocabulary but in the audited mixture the balcony produces, a devotion that has heard the bawdy objection, the prudential objection, and the theological objection, and has answered all three without surrendering either its body or its soul.

## The Idolatry Risk: When Worship of a Woman Borrows God's Language

The strongest objection to the lovers' sacred register was available to every member of the play's first audience, and it was not Mercutio's objection. It was the second commandment's. Within living memory of the first performances, England had dismantled the devotional world the lovers' language describes. The shrines that pilgrims touched had been stripped and their saints' images burned or buried; the pilgrimage roads that palmers walked had been closed by proclamation; the cult of the saints had been reclassified, by the state that licensed the playhouse, as idolatry. When Romeo kneels in imagination at Juliet's shrine and calls his lips pilgrims, an Elizabethan audience heard a young man performing, for erotic purposes, a set of rites its own church had abolished as false worship within the lifetime of the oldest playgoers present. The frisson of the sonnet is not an antiquarian reconstruction. It is the sound of forbidden liturgy being spoken in a public theatre by a boy actor to another boy actor, in a city where the real version of that liturgy had been criminalized, and the play knows precisely what temperature that gives the lines.

The risk runs deeper than period colouring, because the text itself keeps supplying the idolatry charge with evidence. Juliet names Romeo the god of her idolatry in the orchard, using the period's most damning theological noun as a term of endearment. Romeo, at the tomb, looks at a woman he believes dead and finds that her beauty makes the vault a feasting presence full of light (5.3.84-85), which is to say he worships at the shrine after the saint has, to his knowledge, left it, and the worship continues undisturbed by the fact. The fathers close the play by vowing statues in pure gold, images of the lovers to be set up and gazed at by the city, which is the idolatry mechanism rebuilt at civic expense by the very men whose prohibitions drove the lovers underground. A hostile reader, and the play's first culture produced hostile readers in quantity, could assemble from these passages a complete case that the tragedy is a cautionary tale about creature-worship: two children make gods of each other, the gods fail as gods must, and the city is left polishing the idols.

### What made pilgrim language dangerous in a Protestant playhouse?

Pilgrimage was not quaint but condemned, abolished by injunction, its shrines dismantled and its pardons denounced. Speaking a pilgrim conceit in a London theatre borrowed abolished rites for a love scene, in a venue the city's preachers denounced as a school of vice. The holiness carried the banned original's charge, so the sonnet's wit lands as daring, not decoration.

The completion verdict survives this objection, but only just, and only because the play builds the answer into the distribution of the charge. No character in the tragedy ever accuses the lovers of idolatry. The Friar, the one figure with professional standing to bring the charge, instead performs the marriage, and his warnings are about measure and haste, about violent delights and moderate love, never about false gods. The accusation exists in the play's atmosphere, in the abolished shrines and the preachers outside the theatre, but inside the fiction it is never spoken, and the fiction's silence is an argument: the play declines to let its own world endorse the charge its first audience brought in with them. What the lovers worship, moreover, is audited worship, corrected by the beloved at every step, and the corrections are the opposite of idolatry's mechanism. An idol does not answer back, set terms, forbid oaths, or audit the technique of the worshipper's kiss. Juliet does all four. The play's boldest theological suggestion is that devotion answered is devotion rescued from idolatry, and that the danger lies not in the lovers' shrine but in the unanswering ones, Rosaline's silence, the golden statues, where worship proceeds unchecked by any living respondent.

The system of sacred imagery across the whole play, its saints, shrines, confessions, and sacraments taken as a single structure, is catalogued in the companion study of [religion and pilgrimage imagery in the play](/2024/03/15/religion-pilgrimage-imagery-analysis/), and the idolatry question is pursued there at the scale of the system. The collision's narrower point is that the risk is functional. Sacred language that carries no risk would be mere compliment, and the play needs the worship to be strong enough to bind two people against their houses, their names, and eventually their fear of death. Only a language with real theological voltage could do that work, and real voltage is dangerous by definition. The bawdy register's office in the mixture is to keep that voltage earthed, to remind the worshipper hourly that the god of the idolatry has a body, a nurse, a temper, and a wedding night to get to. Idolatry is what the sacred register becomes when the profane correction is refused, which is why the play's idolaters are not the lovers but the fathers with their statues, men who arrive at worship only when the beloved can no longer answer back.

## The Friar's Garden: Grace and Rude Will in One Plant

Before the Friar warns the lovers at their wedding, the play gives him a solo scene in which the whole collision is stated as botany. Alone at dawn in his garden, gathering herbs, Laurence reflects that the earth is both mother and tomb, and that the plant in his basket holds poison and medicine in the same rind: "For naught so vile that on the earth doth live / But to the earth some special good doth give; / Nor aught so good but strained from that fair use / Revolts from true birth, stumbling on abuse" (2.3.13-16). The doctrine arrives at its formula a few lines later, in the period's own theological vocabulary: "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, / And vice sometime by action dignified" (2.3.17-18), and within the infant rind of this small flower, he concludes, poison hath residence and medicine power (2.3.19). This is the mixture stated as natural philosophy by the play's professional theologian, an hour of stage time before he is asked to marry anybody. Nothing in the creation, the speech argues, is single in its tendency. Every good carries its abuse inside it, every vile thing its use, and the difference between medicine and poison is not the substance but the application.

The speech matters to the register argument because it supplies the collision with a metaphysics. The lovers' mixed language is not, on the Friar's showing, an aberration to be explained by youth or by Verona. It is the local instance of a universal structure, in which sacred and profane are properties that substances acquire in use rather than essences they carry in themselves. A shrine visited by a pilgrim is devotion; the same shrine worshipped as an end is idolatry. A body desired within an answered, audited, sacramental bond is the marriage the play blesses; the same body bragged about in a street is the brawl of the opening scene. The garden speech even frames the point in the vocabulary of the will, contrasting grace with rude will as the two tenants of the human breast (2.3.23-28), so that when Romeo enters begging for the marriage rite, the audience has already been handed the interpretive key: the question will never be whether the lovers' language is sacred or profane, but whether grace or rude will is governing its application. The Friar's tragedy, and it is one, is that he states the doctrine flawlessly and then fails to apply it to his own plot, whose sleeping potion is the garden's poison-medicine ambiguity made literal, a substance whose residence in Juliet's body is medicine in the plan and poison in the event. The registers' theologian is undone by his own thesis, which is the play's way of insisting that the mixture can be spoken but not managed, lived but not engineered.

## Married Speech: The Friar's Cell and the Wedding-Night Soliloquy

The wedding itself, the play's one completed sacrament, is staged as a collision compressed into a single short scene. Friar Laurence brings the lovers together with a warning that belongs to the profane register's philosophy of appetite: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6.9-11). The image is a bawdy physics, desire as an explosive that destroys itself in the act of satisfaction, and it is spoken by the celebrant, inside the rite, as part of the rite. His counsel that follows, to love moderately so that long love may do so (2.6.14), is the church attempting to install a governor on the machine it is blessing. Then the same voice performs the union in the language of incorporation: the pair shall not stay alone "Till Holy Church incorporate two in one" (2.6.35-37). Within thirty lines the sacred register has housed the profane warning, pronounced it, and proceeded anyway. The scene is the play's thesis in small: the mixture is not a failure of discipline but the form the event actually takes when a real church marries real bodies, and the Friar's willingness to hold both languages in one speech is what makes him the lovers' only possible priest.

What the marriage authorizes, Juliet's wedding-night soliloquy displays at full stretch, and it is the play's supreme exhibit for the completion verdict. The speech opens as a hymn. "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1-2) invokes the sun's horses with the confidence of a liturgy, and the invocation modulates into an address to night that is half prayer and half summons: "Come, gentle night, come, loving, black-browed night, / Give me my Romeo" (3.2.20-21). Then, inside the hymn, without any change of gear, the bride does her accounts: "I have bought the mansion of a love, / But not possessed it, and, though I am sold, / Not yet enjoyed" (3.2.26-28). The language of purchase, possession, and enjoyment is the marriage market's own vocabulary, the vocabulary of Act 1 Scene 3 and the Paris negotiation, and Juliet speaks it in the first person about her own longing, in the middle of an invocation to the cosmos. No bawdy on Mercutio's scale could be franker than this soliloquy, and no prayer in the play is more formally beautiful. The registers do not alternate in the speech. They are the same voice at the same pitch, which is what completion means as a technical fact about the verse rather than a claim about the theme.

### What does the Friar's presence do to the lovers' language that the lovers cannot do for themselves?

The lovers can mix the registers but cannot authorize the mixture: their worship is private, their sacrament unlicensed, their witnesses unwilling. The Friar brings the institutional sacred, a cell, a rite, a theology of incorporation, and a church that will say words over them. He converts style into marriage, which is why his failures wound so deeply.

The morning after the wedding night, the mixture faces its hardest working conditions and holds. The dawn parting in Act 3 Scene 5 conducts the lovers' separation as an argument about birds, whether the singer outside the window was the nightingale or the lark, and the argument is a lovers' game with a death sentence underneath it, since to misread the bird is to stay and die. Even here, at the pitch of danger, the speech refuses to choose a register. Juliet's farewell looks down the acts ahead: "Methinks I see thee, now thou art so low, / As one dead in the bottom of a tomb" (3.5.55-56), and her cry that she has an ill-divining soul (3.5.54) turns the scene's bird lore into prophecy. The profane facts, a husband climbing down from a bed he must leave or be killed in, and the sacred machinery, omen, vision, foreboding, occupy the same dozen lines without friction. By this point in the play the audience has been trained by the mixture itself: nobody hearing the tomb vision thinks the less of the marriage bed for it, and nobody hearing the nightingale thinks the less of the vision. The training is the play's quiet pedagogical achievement, and it is why the ending, when it strips the mixture away and leaves each register to perform alone over the bodies, lands with the force of a deprivation the audience has been taught to feel.

The confession scene of Act 4 Scene 1 supplies the mixture's last working demonstration, and its darkest. Juliet, sent to the Friar's cell for shrift, finds Paris there, and must fence with him in the church's own room using the church's own weapons. Her answers are masterpieces of the double register: "That may be, sir, when I may be a wife" (4.1.18) concedes the form of his claim while withholding its substance, and when Paris presses her to confess that she loves him, she replies that if she does confess it, the confession will be worth more spoken behind his back than to his face (4.1.24-28). Every sentence is true and every sentence misleads, spoken in the house of truth-telling, to the man the sacred order of her family has chosen for her. The profane element here is not bawdy but its first cousin, equivocation, the body's self-defence conducted in the liturgy's own grammar. The scene completes the pattern the sonnet began. At the feast, sacred language was the instrument of desire; in the cell, sacred language is the instrument of refusal, and in both scenes the woman wielding it is the one who keeps it honest by knowing exactly what it is doing.

## Porter, Lewis, and the Editor's Crux: What the Critics Dispute

The argument of this article has a scholarly pedigree on both sides, and the dispute is worth stating at full strength before it is settled. C. S. Lewis, anatomizing the courtly tradition in The Allegory of Love, gave the lovers' sacred language its classic description as a religion of love: a devotional system, complete with a god, a code of service, and a liturgy of humility and praise, which the medieval poets had built by borrowing the forms of feudal loyalty and Christian worship and applying them to the beloved. Lewis's point was that the borrowing was systematic and sincere within its conventions, a parody of religion only in the technical sense that it imitated religion's shape for a different object, and that its poetry achieved real devotional intensity on those borrowed terms. On Lewis's account, Romeo's pilgrim sonnet is not a trick but a fluency: the young man speaks the period's most serious language of devotion because that language is what seriousness about love sounded like, and Juliet's answering quatrains show her equally fluent in the same liturgy. The sacred register, for Lewis's tradition, dignifies its object by the only measure the culture kept in stock.

Joseph Porter's study Shakespeare's Mercutio reads the same scenes from the other end of the play's telescope. Porter argued that Mercutio is no decorative wit but the bearer of a rival philosophy, in which love is the body's business and the body's truth, and that his bawdy is the philosophy's native tongue: obscene, exact, and immune to the self-deceptions of the Petrarchan worshipper. On Porter's reading, the conjuration outside the wall is the play's counter-liturgy delivered at full strength, and its failure to raise Romeo is not a refutation but a tragedy of timing, since the philosophy it preaches is the one the lovers will need and cannot use: an unsentimental knowledge of appetite that might have slowed them, grounded them, kept them in the body's timescale instead of the shrine's eternity. Porter gives the profane register what criticism had long denied it, a doctrine. Where the older tradition heard Mercutio as noise around the love story, Porter hears the love story's most serious opposition, and the opposition's early death as the moment the play loses its ballast.

Between Lewis's liturgy and Porter's doctrine stands the editor, and the editor's evidence is a single word. The earliest printed text of the play, the quarto of 1597, gives Romeo's sonnet line as "This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this," where the fuller quarto of 1599, followed by René Weis in the Arden Third Series text quoted throughout this article, reads "the gentle fine is this." The variant is not a triviality. With sin, the kiss is a transgression the pilgrim confesses in advance, and the sonnet's theology tilts toward trespass: the worshipper knows the shrine is forbidden and proceeds. With fine, the kiss is a penalty paid and a debt discharged, and the theology tilts toward penance: the worshipper's fault is real but the rite contains its remedy. Editors who print fine, as Weis does, hand the sacred register a working doctrine of forgiveness inside the conceit; the quarto that prints sin hands the profane reading its proof text, since the lover's own first word for his act, in that version, is the word for wrongdoing. The crux shows the collision operating at the level of the document itself. The play's two registers are not merely themes an interpreter finds in the text. They are variants the text's own transmission could not decide between.

The banter scene of Act 2 Scene 4 is the test case on which the two critics can be made to fight most cleanly, since it shows Romeo fluent in Porter's register and Mercutio misreading the fluency in Lewis's despite himself. Porter can claim the scene: here is the friend restored, sociable, fencing in puns, the Petrarchan vapour dispelled by daylight and male company, exactly the cure the conjuration attempted. Lewis can claim it too: the fluency is real but its cause is the feast, the liturgy answered, the devotion requited, and the bawdy is running on energy the sacred register supplied. The scene adjudicates between them by means of its secret. Romeo's ease in the profane key is possible only because the profane is no longer his whole story; he banters like a man with somewhere else to be, and Mercutio, hearing the whole performance and missing the whole point, certifies the cure of a patient whose condition he has never once diagnosed correctly since the play began. The criticism that treats Mercutio's register as the play's ground truth has to explain why its speaker is wrong about his closest friend in every scene they share, while the criticism that treats the sacred register as self-sufficient has to explain why its hero needs the ground truth's language, at noon, in the street, to arrange his sacrament. Neither explanation is available, which is the adjudication in embryo.

The adjudication this article defends follows from the evidence of sequence assembled in the preceding sections. Porter is right that the bawdy carries a doctrine and wrong that the doctrine refutes the worship: the conjuration fails on its facts, since the Romeo it summons by Rosaline's body no longer exists, and a philosophy of appetite that cannot track appetite's own movements has no claim to ground truth. Lewis is right that the sacred language is a sincere liturgy and wrong, if his tradition is pressed, that sincerity is the end of the matter: the sonnet's worship is instrumental from its first line, spoken to obtain a kiss, and Juliet's "You kiss by th' book" marks the instrumentality from inside the rite. What neither account quite states is the play's own position, which is transactional between the two: the worship is real because the appetite audits it, and the appetite is dignified because the worship gives it forms in which another person can answer. The editor's crux then stops being an embarrassment and becomes the emblem of the whole system. Sin and fine are both true of the kiss, and the play needs a text that can hold the word for the trespass and the word for the remedy within four syllables of each other, because that doubleness is the sound of the lovers' language at every stage from the feast to the tomb.

## The Death of Bawdy: Mercutio's Grave Pun and Romeo's Defiance

The mixture's midpoint crisis arrives in Act 3 Scene 1, and it arrives as the death of the profane register's greatest speaker. Mercutio, wounded under Romeo's arm, spends his last breath in his own idiom and discovers its limit in public. The wit holds to the end: asked about the wound, he promises that anyone who asks for him tomorrow will find him a grave man (3.1.97-98), the pun carrying its two senses, solemn and buried, in a single syllable's difference. But the curse that follows abandons wit for the play's other great vernacular of imprecation: "A plague o' both your houses! They have made worms' meat of me" (3.1.99-100). The plague wish is a sacred form inverted, a prayer for pestilence addressed to no god in particular, and the worms'-meat image is the profane register's final reduction of the body it celebrated, flesh as carrion. Mercutio dies, in other words, speaking both registers in their corrupted forms at once, the curse and the carrion, and the scene's point is that his philosophy has no third term: nothing in the bawdy doctrine can say what his death means, so the meaning has to be supplied by Romeo, whose interpretation is immediate and catastrophic. The love that Mercutio mocked as softness has, in Romeo's own account, made him effeminate and blunted his valour's steel (3.1.113-115), and the remedy he applies is the feud's remedy, the sword. The play's bleakest joke is structural: the one hour in which Romeo abandons the mixture and acts on a single register, first Mercutio's contempt and then Tybalt's honour, is the hour that kills three people and banishes a fourth.

Romeo's other great single-register performance comes in Mantua, and the play frames it as a relapse to be pitied rather than a stance to be admired. Balthasar's news shatters the mixture's balance, and Romeo's answer is the sacred register weaponized against heaven itself: "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24). The defiance is magnificent and entirely sterile. Where the balcony's sacred speech was audited, answered, and corrective, the Mantuan defiance is a soliloquy hurled at an abstraction, and its practical fruit is a visit to the Apothecary, where the sacred register's vocabulary is replaced wholesale by the market's. The poison purchase is conducted as a transaction between two men who understand each other perfectly: "The world is not thy friend, nor the world's law; / The world affords no law to make thee rich; / Then be not poor, but break it, and take this" (5.1.72-74). Romeo's commentary on the gold he hands over completes the inversion of the feast. At the shrine, a fine was paid and a sin was purged; in Mantua, gold is paid and a worse poison than the poison is named: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1.80-83). The speech is the profane register's philosophy spoken by the worshipper, appetite and gold and lawlessness fused, and it is spoken with a lucidity that the play grants to despair. Romeo knows exactly what he is doing and what it costs, and he does it because the answering voice that once corrected his registers is, as he believes, dead in a vault. Mantua is the control experiment repeated at the tragedy's end: remove the respondent, and the lover's language decomposes within a scene into defiance and purchase, the two single registers of Act 1, the shrine and the street, waiting to be reunited only over a body.

## The Potion Speech: The Mixture Under Maximum Stress

Juliet's soliloquy over the vial in Act 4 Scene 3 is the mixture's severest examination, because it is conducted alone, at night, with every auditor the play has previously supplied now withdrawn. The woman who corrected Romeo's conceits, forbade his oaths, and equivocated with Paris must now drink on the strength of a language she has no partner left to audit, and the speech shows what the registers do under that deprivation. They do not separate. Her terror is profane in its materials, a catalogue of the body's horrors: waking before her time in the charnel house, the loathsome smells, the shrieks like mandrakes torn out of the earth, the terror that might make her mad and play with her forefathers' joints (4.3.44-53). Her resolve is sacred in its address, since the whole speech is a deliberation conducted before an invisible tribunal, ending in a toast that parodies the feast's courtesies: "Romeo, I come! This do I drink to thee" (4.3.58). The toast is the sonnet inverted. At the feast she drank in his worship with her eyes and corrected it; here she drinks poison with his name as the grace before it, and the correction she performs is on her own fear, which she summons, inspects, and dismisses item by item like a confessor hearing a confession she is determined to survive.

The speech also settles a question the clash reading raises in its strongest form, which is whether the sacred register is merely what the lovers perform for each other, a duet that collapses when the partner leaves the room. Alone, Juliet does not collapse into either single register. She does not pray in any straightforward sense, since no petition is offered and none is answered, and she does not relapse into the Nurse's bawdy or the market's accounting, though the speech is full of bodies, joints, smells, and bones. She does something harder to name: she reasons in the mixed idiom as in a mother tongue, testing the Friar's honesty, the potion's nature, the tomb's population, and her own nerve in a single continuous meditation that treats the spiritual and the physical as one jurisdiction. The mixture, the speech proves, has become her cognition rather than her courtship style. That is the deepest sense in which the registers complete each other in this play. They finish as a way of thinking, installed in the character least permitted to think aloud, and still functioning at the moment of maximum terror, when every borrowed liturgy and every borrowed joke has been stripped away and the thinking is all that is left.

## The Tomb: A Shrine That Becomes a Grave

The closing scene runs the collision in reverse, and the reversal is the verdict's final proof. Romeo enters the Capulet vault performing the pilgrim's office one last time, and his description of the tomb is the sonnet's vocabulary at its most exalted and most estranged: "For here lies Juliet, and her beauty makes / This vault a feasting presence full of light" (5.3.84-85). The shrine language is intact, the saint's radiance undimmed, and every word of it is addressed to a woman the speaker believes is a corpse. The sacred register, separated from the answering body that had audited it since the feast, reverts within a single scene to the condition of the Rosaline worship: fluent, fervent, and spoken into a silence that cannot correct it. The play drives the point with a stage picture. Paris, the sanctioned suitor, has been performing his own rite at the same tomb, strewing the bridal bed with flowers and promising nightly observance (5.3.12-17), a devotion every bit as liturgical as Romeo's and every bit as unanswering, and the two worshippers fight over the shrine in the dark and one of them dies at its door. Uncorrected worship, the scene argues, is not merely futile. It is lethal to bystanders.

Juliet's waking completes the reversal from the other side. She is given no sonnet, no invocation, no hymn; her last speech is the profane register transfigured into an instrument of absolute fidelity. Finding Romeo dead with the cup in his hand, she works the problem of the poison like a practitioner, kissing his lips to find whether any residue remains for her, and hearing the watch approach, she takes the play's most famous stage property and names it in the language of the marriage bed: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170). The sheath image is bawdy's oldest property, the joke of a hundred tavern songs, and in her mouth, at that moment, it carries the full weight the sacred register carried at the feast: a vow, a consummation, a sacrament performed with the only instrument the city has left her. Critics who treat the profane as the play's comic solvent and the sacred as its tragic essence have the tomb scene backwards. At the end, it is the bawdy image that performs the tragedy's most solemn act, and it is the worship that wanders the vault talking to the dead. The registers have exchanged offices, which is only possible if they were never the opposites the clash reading requires.

The fathers' closing vows then display, in civic form, what the lovers' mixture had refused. Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold, Capulet answers that Romeo's shall stand as rich beside it, and the Prince gathers the whole catastrophe into the cadence of a judgment (5.3.296-310). Statues are the sacred register with the profane correction permanently removed: images that cannot answer, cannot set terms, cannot forbid an oath or audit a kiss, offered to a city invited to worship them forever. The play's first audience, which had watched real shrines emptied within living memory, knew what a golden image of a dead beloved was and what it cost to maintain one. Verona ends the tragedy by founding the cult that the lovers, while they lived and spoke, had declined to be. The idolatry charge, hovering unspoken over the sonnet, the orchard, and the tomb, finally lands, and it lands on the city, not on the lovers, which is where the play's argument had been aiming it from the first scene.

### Why does the tomb scene return the lovers' language to ritual after the bawdy has died?

Ritual is what remains when dialogue ends. While the lovers lived, their language was a negotiation where rite and appetite corrected each other; with Mercutio dead, Romeo banished, and Juliet silent in counterfeit sleep, no negotiator is left, and speech hardens into ceremony over people who cannot answer. Its grief is of forms outliving the conversation that kept them honest.

The closing scene also admits a third and final tongue, the tongue of the document, and its arrival measures what the mixture's death costs the record. The truth of the lovers reaches the Prince not in their language but in evidence: a letter carried by Balthasar, a friar's deposition, a page's testimony, a servant's report, each item checked against the bodies in the vault. The play's most eloquent speakers end as exhibits in other people's prose. Nothing in the dossier is false, and nothing in it is adequate, which is why the Friar's long narration, accurate in every particular, sounds so thin beside the scenes it summarizes. The record register is the city's native tongue, the language of edicts and inquests, and the tragedy closes by demonstrating its sufficiency for judgment and its insufficiency for knowledge. Verona will know what happened. It will never again hear how it sounded, and the distance between those two conditions is the space the lovers' mixture occupied for five acts.

One more witness deserves hearing before the verdict closes, and it is the play's handling of Capulet in Act 4 Scene 5, when the household believes Juliet dead on her wedding morning. The father who had priced her in the marriage market now speaks the mixture fluently and without knowing he has learned it: "Death is my son-in-law, Death is my heir; / My daughter he hath wedded" (4.5.38-39). Marriage language, the sacred register's central institution, is conscripted to name the profane fact of the corpse upstairs, and the conscription is not satire. Capulet's grief is the first fully honest speech he makes in the play, and it arrives in the lovers' own mixed idiom, wedding and burial in one breath. The household that policed the registers apart, that proposed marriage as a book and desire as disobedience, ends by speaking as the lovers spoke, because grief has no other adequate language. The play does not underline the conversion. It simply lets the idiom spread, from the feast to the orchard to the cell to the patriarch's own mouth, and the spread is the nearest thing the tragedy offers to a resurrection: the language survives the lovers, in mouths that tried to silence it, and what it says in those mouths is the truth.

## The Verdict: What the Mixture Says About the Play's Vision of Love

The mixture, assembled whole, amounts to a definition. Love in this play is neither the appetite the servants brag about nor the worship Romeo sighs over Rosaline, and it is not a compromise between them, a moderate portion of each. It is the state in which each register has full possession of the other: worship so embodied that it can be frank about mansions bought and not yet possessed, appetite so devoted that it can build a shrine, accept correction, swear by the beloved's self, and keep faith past the grave's door. The play tests this definition against every alternative its world contains. The feud's love of honour speaks neither register and produces corpses. The marriage market speaks the sacred register's forms as accounting and produces a terrified girl with a vial. Mercutio's pure bawdy produces the play's best talk and a grave in the middle of the action. The Friar's pure doctrine produces warnings that events outrun. Only the mixture produces the balcony scene, the wedding-night soliloquy, and the two deaths that Verona's assembled authorities stand over in silence, unable to find a register of their own adequate to what they are seeing.

That is also why the cliché the series thesis names, the love kept holy by being kept bloodless, is such a durable misreading. A holy love extracted from the mixture flatters the sacred register into the very idolatry the play is careful to refuse, worship of an image that cannot answer, and it makes the tragedy unintelligible, since nothing in a bloodless devotion explains why a city should fear it, forbid it, or be reformed by its loss. Restoring the profane restores the stakes. The lovers are dangerous to Verona not because their feeling is pure but because their language works: it marries, it binds, it persuades friars, equips escapes, and survives cross-examination by everyone who tries to talk them out of it. The bawdy is the proof of work. A love that can afford to be joked about by its own priest, corrected by its own saint, and spoken in the market's vocabulary by its own bride is a love that has passed every audit the play's world can conduct, and the play's final judgment is that the world, not the love, failed the examination.

The last word belongs, fittingly, to neither register alone. The Prince's closing sentence abandons both worship and bawdy for the flat vocabulary of the record: some shall be pardoned, and some punished, and the tale of Juliet and her Romeo is sadder than any story known (5.3.307-310). After five acts of the richest mixed speech in English drama, the play ends in prose values, pardon, punishment, report, because the mixture's speakers are dead and the city must make do with the registers it has left. What it has left, the audience has been taught to hear as poverty. That hearing is the play's victory and its verdict: the sacred and the profane in the lovers' language complete each other so thoroughly that when they are separated at last, by death, by law, by golden statues, the separation itself is the tragedy, and everything else is only its occasion.

A word, finally, about the play's own framing voice, since the Chorus speaks neither register and the omission is deliberate. The Prologue's vocabulary is legal and astronomical, household, feud, stars, and the play's action is summarized as a traffic of two hours in which a pair of lovers take their life. Fate, in the framing, is not worshipped and not joked about; it is simply announced, as a clerk announces a case. The effect is to position the audience above both registers from the first minute, able to see what neither the worshippers nor the wits can see, that the two languages are describing one event. Every subsequent collision is thus double-exposed. When Mercutio conjures, the audience hears the rite fail because it knows the balcony is coming; when Romeo defies the stars, the audience hears the Prologue's flat announcement underneath the defiance and knows the stars will not be defied. The mixture the lovers build is never granted the framing voice's knowledge, and the framing voice is never granted the mixture's life. The play's vision is completed by that separation: the registers complete each other for the lovers, briefly, at ruinous cost, while the language that survives them, the record, the report, the sad story, is the poorer tongue of those who watched. Criticism, which is another form of the record, should take the hint. An account of the play in a single register, whether it keeps the love holy or keeps it honest, is a Prologue's account, accurate in every fact and missing the thing itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch sacred-profane map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch sacred-profane map is the table in this article that sets the play's two registers of love side by side at fifteen stations, from the servants' quarrel to the tomb, recording the sacred move, the profane move, and what their collision accomplishes at each point. The pattern it exposes is a progression rather than a standoff. The registers begin apart, each failing in its own way, appetite decaying into street violence and worship decaying into solitary sighing. They meet at the feast inside a single sonnet, are tested by parody, counsel, warning, exile, and equivocation, and end by exchanging offices in the tomb, where a bawdy image performs the tragedy's most solemn act. Read down its final column, the map shows that no pure form survives the play intact, while the mixture survives every test until its speakers are killed.

### Q: At what moment does sacred language first enter the play, and what has the profane register already claimed by then?

Sacred language arrives with Romeo's Petrarchan devotion to Rosaline in Act 1 Scene 1, where vows, sieges, and the chaste goddess Diana supply a complete liturgy of worship addressed to an absent woman. By that point the profane register has already claimed the play's opening ground and set its terms. Sampson and Gregory have defined desire as assault, named maidenheads as spoil, and turned the boast into an actual brawl that brings the Prince and his death sentence into the street. The sequence is the play's opening argument in miniature. Appetite speaks first and produces violence; worship speaks second and produces soliloquy; neither, alone, produces a conversation. The feast sonnet matters precisely because it is the first passage in which the second register answers the first inside a single exchange.

### Q: What happens to Romeo's pilgrim worship while Mercutio conjures him by Rosaline's body outside the Capulet wall?

Nothing reaches it, and the failure is the scene's meaning. Mercutio's conjuration in Act 2 Scene 1 adjures Romeo by Rosaline's eyes, forehead, lips, legs, and thighs, performing an anti-liturgy whose facts are all out of date, since the devotion it mocks has already been transferred. Inside the wall, Romeo is beginning the balcony scene, where the pilgrim register he carried at the feast is about to be audited by its addressee. The conjuration therefore demonstrates the profane register's structural limit: it can describe appetite with unmatched precision, but it cannot locate a lover whose love has changed its object and its nature. Mercutio's own later verdict, that Romeo is sociable again, repeats the error in daylight. The bawdy philosophy knows the body exhaustively and the heart only by report.

### Q: How does the word "profane" gather new charges as it travels from the first kiss to the tomb?

In Romeo's opening line at the feast, "profane" is a courtier's self-accusation, the unworthiness of a hand at a shrine, confessed so that a fine may be paid and a kiss granted. In a Protestant playhouse the word carried a second charge, since the pilgrimage it belonged to had been abolished as false worship, and the conceit borrowed abolished rites for a love scene. As the play proceeds, the charge migrates. The city's respectable order becomes the true profaner of sacred things, forcing a sacrament into secrecy, pricing a bride in a market, and finally raising golden images of the dead lovers. By the tomb, the word's weight has reversed: the lovers' supposedly profane mixture looks like the play's only intact reverence, and the profanation belongs to the world that buried them.

### Q: What does Juliet mean by "I have bought the mansion of a love, but not possessed it" in Act 3 Scene 2?

Juliet is doing the marriage market's arithmetic on her own longing, inside the play's most elevated invocation. The mansion is the marriage itself, purchased at the Friar's cell that morning, legally hers; possession is the wedding night, still hours away, which law and custom have not yet delivered. The startling feature of the lines is their register. A bride in a hymn to night reaches for the vocabulary of purchase, sale, and enjoyment, the vocabulary of Act 1's negotiations over her own person, and speaks it in the first person about her own desire. The speech is the completion thesis in a single figure: the market's language is not expelled by the hymn but absorbed into it, corrected by being claimed by the person the market traded.

### Q: How does Juliet's "Gallop apace" hold a hymn of invocation and frank desire in the same speech?

The soliloquy opens in the grammar of liturgy, invoking Phoebus's steeds, the night, and the stars with the confidence of a set prayer, and it never leaves that grammar while its content grows steadily franker. Juliet asks night to bring her Romeo, imagines his death transformed into stars, and then audits her own position as a bride sold but not yet enjoyed, in the market's plain terms. No gear change separates the hymn from the desire, which is the technical proof of the article's argument. In Mercutio's scenes the registers fight for the floor; in Romeo's early scenes they alternate; in this soliloquy, spoken by the character who has audited both since the feast, they are a single voice at a single pitch, worship and appetite indistinguishable as instruments.

### Q: Why does Friar Laurence tell the lovers to love moderately in the very scene that marries them?

The warning is the profane register's wisdom installed inside the sacrament by the sacrament's own officer. Laurence has just stated, in his garden, that every substance holds poison and medicine together and that virtue misapplied turns vice, and he brings that doctrine to the wedding as a governor: violent delights have violent ends, consuming themselves like fire and powder, while moderate love endures. The advice is sincere, orthodox, and useless, and the scene knows all three. The lovers hear the caution, are married inside thirty lines, and proceed at exactly the speed the caution forbade. The play's interest is not in mocking the doctrine but in showing its structural position: moderation is the one counsel the mixture cannot take, because the feud has abolished the time in which moderate love could be practised.

### Q: Why does Juliet answer Paris in the Friar's cell with words that are true and misleading at once?

Because equivocation is the only weapon the scene leaves her, and the sacred setting is what makes it work. Paris, the family's chosen husband, presses his claim in the church's own room, on the way to confession, and Juliet answers in forms that satisfy the claim's surface while withholding its substance: she may be his wife when she may be a wife, and a confession of love to Romeo will be worth more spoken behind Paris's back than to his face. Every sentence is true on its face and false in its effect. The scene completes the pattern begun at the feast, where sacred language served desire; here it serves refusal, wielded by the same speaker, in the same double register, against the order that owns the building.

### Q: How does Paris's strewing of flowers at the tomb turn a bridal rite into a burial rite?

Paris comes to the Capulet vault performing the observances of a bridegroom: he strews the bridal bed with flowers, promises nightly rite and tears, and keeps a lover's vigil at the door. Every element of the performance belongs to a wedding, and every element is addressed to a corpse, in a grave, by a man who never exchanged a word of courtship with the woman in her life that the audience witnessed as courtship. The scene is the sacred register's civic form exposed: rite without respondent, devotion without dialogue, the idolatry mechanism the lovers were accused of, practised respectably by the sanctioned suitor. His death at the shrine he was tending, killed by the rival worshipper, is the play's coldest comment on unanswering devotion.

### Q: How does the word "die" change its charge between the wedding-night speech and the tomb?

In Juliet's soliloquy the word carries the period's erotic double sense inside a cosmic image: when she does die, Romeo is to be cut out in little stars, so that mortality and consummation share a verb at the height of her longing. The double sense is a property of the mixture, desire speaking of death and death speaking of desire without either cancelling the other. In the tomb the charge completes its circuit. Romeo dies by a purchased poison he has just compared, unfavourably, with the gold that bought it, and Juliet's final lines take the dagger as a bridegroom's substitute and bid it rust in its sheath. The word that began as a lovers' pun ends as a stage direction. The play's bleakest register effect is that the pun turns out to have been a prophecy all along.

### Q: Why does the secret marriage depend on the Nurse's profane errands to take place at all?

Because the sacrament has logistics, and every one of them belongs to her register. The marriage requires messages carried between two guarded houses, a cover story of confession, a meeting arranged inside a friar's cell, and a rope ladder procured for the wedding night, and the only person who can perform these errands is a bawdy old servant whose speech is the play's earthiest. Her register is not decoration around the sacred event; it is the event's supply line. The play makes the dependence structural so that its verdict cannot be escaped: strip the profane from the story and the sacred never happens at all. When the Nurse later counsels surrender to the Paris match, Juliet expels her, not for her bawdy, which had served the marriage faithfully, but for aiming it against the marriage it built.

### Q: Does any character accuse the lovers of idolatry, and if not, where does the charge come from?

No character brings the charge. The Friar, the one figure with the standing and the vocabulary to make it, instead performs the marriage and confines his warnings to measure and haste. The accusation arrives from outside the fiction: from the play's first culture, in which pilgrimage had been abolished, shrines dismantled, and the cult of saints reclassified as idolatry by the state, so that an audience heard Romeo's shrine language with the era's most damning theological noun ready to hand. Juliet herself speaks the word once, as endearment, naming Romeo the god of her idolatry. The play's refusal to let any voice inside the story endorse the charge is an argument in itself, completed at the end, where the actual image-worship, golden statues vowed by the fathers, is practised by the accusers' party.

### Q: Why do editors print "the gentle fine" where the earliest quarto reads "the gentle sin"?

The quarto of 1597, the play's earliest printed text, gives Romeo's line as "This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this," while the fuller quarto of 1599 reads "the gentle fine is this," and modern editors, René Weis in the Arden Third Series among them, generally follow the later text. The choice is a small editorial act with a large theological shadow. With sin, the kiss is a confessed trespass and the sonnet tilts toward the profane reading, the lover naming his own wrongdoing as he commits it. With fine, the kiss is a penalty paid within the rite, and the sacred reading gains a working doctrine of remedy. The crux matters beyond its four syllables because it shows the play's two registers contending inside the document itself, in the transmission, before any interpreter arrives.

### Q: What does Tybalt's fury at the feast do to the pilgrim sonnet unfolding under the same roof?

It reveals the sonnet's cost in real time. While Romeo and Juliet build their shrine in one corner of the hall, Tybalt recognizes the pilgrim's voice as a Montague's and moves to profane the feast with a duel, stopped only by Capulet's hand and a public rebuke. The collision in the scene is therefore threefold: worship, appetite, and the feud's violence all occupy the same room, and the feud is the only party that objects to the mixture. Capulet's defence of his masked guest, hospitality's code overriding the feud's, is the household's one act of register tolerance, and it is withdrawn by Act 3. The feast scene plants the tragedy's mechanism inside its most beautiful moment: the sonnet's fourteen lines are spoken within earshot of the sword that will answer them.

### Q: Why does violence keep breaking in at the moments the lovers' language turns most sacred?

Because the feud experiences the mixture as a threat to its jurisdiction, and the play stages the threat response with mechanical consistency. The sonnet is interrupted by Tybalt's recognition; the wedding is followed within hours by the street fight that kills Mercutio and Tybalt; the wedding night is cut by banishment; the tomb vigil is invaded by Paris and then the watch. The pattern is not coincidence but policy, the city's order reasserting itself wherever a rival order, with its own rite, its own priest, and its own allegiance, shows signs of founding itself. Read through the map, the interruptions are the strongest evidence for the mixture's power: Verona does not send swords against compliments. It sends them against a working alternative, a language that marries across the feud's lines and therefore dissolves the feud's reason to exist.

### Q: Why does Capulet call Death his son-in-law and his heir in Act 4 Scene 5?

Because grief finally gives the patriarch the lovers' own idiom, and he speaks it without knowing he has learned it. Believing Juliet dead on her wedding morning, Capulet reaches for the institution that has organized his thinking about her for the whole play, marriage, and installs Death in the bridegroom's place: Death has wedded her, inherited her, taken the portion and the person together. The lines are the household's first honest speech about the daughter, and they arrive in the mixed register the household spent four acts policing, wedding and burial in a single breath. The play does not mock the conversion. It lets the idiom spread to the mouth that tried hardest to silence it, which is the closest thing to a resurrection the tragedy permits: the language survives its speakers.

### Q: How can an essay argue that the sacred and profane registers complete each other rather than clash?

The strongest route is sequential evidence. Show each register failing alone first: the servants' appetite producing a brawl, Romeo's Rosaline worship producing solitary decay, Benvolio's reasonable substitution doctrine producing nothing. Then show the feast sonnet, where the same worship, answered and audited by Juliet, produces the play's first real conversation. Then track the tests: the conjuration failing on its facts, the Friar building the profane warning into the wedding, the wedding-night soliloquy fusing hymn and desire in one voice, and the tomb, where the registers exchange offices. Conclude with the distribution of survival, the statues and the record, where the separated registers are shown as the city's poverty. An essay built on that sequence answers the clash reading on its own evidence rather than by assertion.

### Q: Why does Juliet call Romeo "the god of my idolatry" in the balcony scene?

Because she has audited the sacred register more honestly than any critic in the play, and she says the dangerous part aloud. Forbidden to swear by the inconstant moon, and then forbidden oaths altogether, Romeo is offered one object worthy of the vow: his gracious self, which Juliet names, in the period's most damning theological vocabulary, the god of her idolatry. The line is not a slip. It is the beloved accepting the charge that the surrounding culture would bring against the whole affair, and accepting it as a description of her devotion's intensity rather than as a refutation of its worth. The play's boldest theological gesture is placed in the mouth of the character with the least theological training and the most at stake, and it converts idolatry from an accusation into a measure: the worship is real precisely because she knows what it risks being called.

### Q: What does the mixture of registers finally claim about the kind of love the play believes in?

It claims that the play believes in a love strong enough to survive its own cross-examination. The mixture defines that love by double possession: worship so embodied that it can speak frankly of purchase, possession, and the wedding night, and appetite so devoted that it can build shrines, accept correction, and keep faith past the grave. Every alternative the play offers is a subtraction from that definition, the servants' appetite without devotion, the Rosaline worship without a respondent, the market's forms without feeling, the Friar's doctrine without time. The definition also explains the tragedy's shape. A love that has passed every audit its world can conduct cannot be refuted by that world, only outnumbered by it, and the play's closing poverty of speech is the sound of the world after the refutation it could not perform has been replaced by force.

### Q: Which register is given the play's last word on the lovers, and why does the choice matter?

Neither. The closing speech belongs to the Prince, and its register is the record: pardon and punishment, the promise of more talk, and the flat superlative that no story was sadder than this one of Juliet and her Romeo. The choice matters because the audience has been trained for five acts to hear the mixture as the full language of the subject, and the ending withdraws it. Worship survives only as the fathers' golden images, appetite only as the gold's purchasing power remembered, and the living speech that once held both is replaced by administration. The poverty of the last word is the tragedy's final measurement. It tells the audience exactly what Verona has lost, in the only terms Verona has left, and the inadequacy of those terms is the verdict.
