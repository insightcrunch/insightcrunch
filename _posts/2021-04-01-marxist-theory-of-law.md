Ask a room of students what the Marxist theory of law holds and most will give the same answer in nearly the same words. Law, they will say, is a tool of the ruling class, a set of rules written by those who own property to protect what they own and to discipline those who own nothing. The answer has the ring of a slogan because it began life as one, compressed from a pamphlet written for a revolutionary league and repeated so often since that it now passes for the whole tradition. It is not the whole tradition. It is one position among several, held in its pure form by fewer Marxist writers than the textbooks suggest, and regarded inside the tradition itself as the weakest of the accounts on offer. The sharpest objection to it came not from a liberal defender of legality but from a Marxist historian, working from the records of eighteenth-century prosecutions, who concluded that the rule of law was a genuine achievement that restrained the powerful as well as serving them. His conclusion scandalised colleagues who shared his politics. That scandal, and the argument underneath it, is the most instructive episode in the whole field, because it forces every other position to say exactly what it claims and what it cannot explain.

![A shelf of legal theory volumes beside a balance scale, representing the Marxist theory of law and its five positions, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Five positions organise the material, and keeping them separate is the first discipline this article asks of its reader. An instrumental account treats legal rules as a means by which a dominant class secures its interests, much as it might use a police force or a newspaper. A structural account grants legal institutions a measure of independence, arguing that they serve the long-run interest of capital precisely by refusing to serve each capitalist in turn. A commodity-form account derives the very shape of legality, the abstract rights-bearing person, from the shape of commodity exchange. An ideological account treats legality as a way of forming subjects who experience themselves as free and equal while substantive inequality proceeds beneath that experience. A historical account, built from archival study, insists that the forms of legality genuinely bind the hands that wield them, with a binding that cannot be waved away as decoration. These five are not stages in a single argument and they do not sit comfortably together. Some contradict each other outright. A reader who can state each one, name its weakness, and say which evidence would embarrass it knows more about Marxism and law than a reader who can repeat the slogan in three languages.

## Marxist Theory of Law: Five Positions Rather Than One Slogan

The slogan survives because it answers a real observation. Legal systems in class societies do protect property with great energy, do punish the theft of goods more reliably than the theft of wages, and do present arrangements favourable to owners as though they were the neutral order of things. Any theory that ignored those facts would be worthless, and none of the five positions ignores them. Where they differ is over the mechanism. Does a dominant class write the rules directly, the way a committee drafts a memorandum? Does the legal order reproduce class advantage through its structure, without anyone needing to intend the outcome? Does the form of the legal person mirror the form of the commodity, so that equality before the rules is the twin of equality in the market? Does legality work chiefly inside people's heads, teaching them to see a profoundly unequal order as a fair one? Or does the historical record show something more awkward for every simple theory, namely rules that bite the hands that made them? Each question opens a different research programme, a different archive, and a different quarrel.

### Do Marxists think law is just a ruling class tool?

some do, but that instrumental account is widely regarded within the tradition as inadequate, since it cannot explain law that constrains capital or law won by workers. Other positions treat law as relatively autonomous, as derived from the commodity form, or as genuinely constraining power.

That short answer carries the article's whole argument in miniature, and the rest of these pages unpack it. Notice what the answer does not say. It does not say the instrumental account is false in every particular, because plainly some rules are made by identifiable people to serve identifiable interests, and some judgments are bought, bullied, or arranged. It says the account is inadequate, which is a different and more damaging charge. A theory of law must explain the whole phenomenon: the factory legislation that owners fought and lost, the prosecutions of employers that succeed, the occasions when the highest tribunals rule against the strongest economic interests in the land and the ruling stands. An account that treats legality as a glove on the hand of power has nothing to say about the glove closing into a fist against that hand, except to call the episode a trick. Calling every inconvenient fact a trick is the point at which a theory stops explaining and starts protecting itself.

Readers arriving from neighbouring topics should know where the boundaries of this article fall. The theory of the [state](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/) is treated in its own guide, since law and state, though entangled, raise distinct questions, and the same holds for the theory of [ideology](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/), which supplies one of the five positions with its machinery but extends far beyond the legal field. The commodity-form account receives a summary here and a full treatment in the article on [Pashukanis and the commodity form of law](/2021/04/15/pashukanis-commodity-form-of-law/), because its argument is intricate enough to deserve its own ground. The criminal law, where many readers first meet these ideas in their most concrete shape, is handled in the companion piece on [Marxist criminology](/2021/06/15/marxist-criminology-explained/). What remains here is the map itself: the five positions, their evidence, their failures, and the remarkable moment when a historian inside the tradition announced that the map's most familiar road led nowhere.

One more preliminary matters for honest reading. Marx wrote remarkably little about legality as such. There is no treatise, no chapter of the major economic work devoted to the legal order, nothing comparable to the sustained attention he gave to value, rent, or crisis. What exists is a scattering: youthful journalism on a statute about gathering fallen wood, a famous sentence in a manifesto, a penetrating few pages on equal right in a critique of a party programme, and remarks dispersed through historical writings where legal forms appear as part of the scenery of class conflict. The tradition of Marxist legal theory was built by later hands from these fragments, and the builders disagreed about what the fragments implied. That is why the field contains five positions rather than one doctrine. The fragments underdetermine the theory, and each generation of writers has completed the structure in the direction its own problems pushed it. Recognising this is not a debunking move. It is the precondition for reading the field accurately, because it explains why intelligent Marxists, citing the same handful of texts, arrived at conclusions that cannot all be true at once.

The plan of the article follows the word budget the material demands. Roughly a fifth goes to Marx's own scattered writings, since every later position claims descent from them. A third goes to the five positions stated carefully, one by one. A quarter goes to the historian's challenge, which is the centre of gravity of the whole subject and the passage students are most likely to meet in examinations and seminars. A fifth goes to the unresolved question of whether a socialist society could have law at all, or whether legality belongs so completely to commodity-producing societies that it would be replaced by administration. The remainder handles routing: how neighbouring disciplines have taken up these arguments, which misreadings to avoid, and where each kind of reader should go next.

## What Marx Himself Wrote About Law

Every position in the field claims Marx, so the first task is to establish what he actually wrote, which is less than the claims require and stranger than the claims suggest. His earliest sustained engagement with material interests came not in a study of factories or markets but in newspaper journalism about a statute. In 1842, writing for a Rhineland newspaper of liberal sympathies, the young Marx published a series of articles on the criminalisation of gathering fallen wood. Custom had long allowed the poor to collect dead branches from forests, a perquisite of poverty that supplemented fuel and income in a countryside where both were scarce. A proposed measure would treat the gathering as theft, converting a customary right into a crime and the forest owner's property claim into something the criminal law would enforce against people who had exercised the custom for generations. The young journalist's fury at the measure produced some of his most revealing early prose, and he later identified the episode as the embarrassment that pushed him toward the study of economics. The significance of that retrospective remark is hard to overstate. The founder of the tradition came to political economy through a legal question, through watching a legislature redefine a practice so that what had been a right on Monday became a crime on Tuesday, with the people affected unchanged and only the classification altered.

The wood articles repay close reading because they contain, in embryo, arguments that later positions would develop in opposite directions. One strand is recognisably instrumental. Marx watched the assembly deliberate and saw private interest wearing the mask of public reason: the forest owner's advantage dressed itself as the general good, deputies argued from the standpoint of the property they represented, and the resulting measure bore the fingerprints of the interest that had commissioned it. A reader who stopped there would have the slogan ready-made. But another strand in the same articles pulls against the slogan. The young writer also appealed to the idea of law itself, to what a genuine legal order owed to those it governed, insisting that a measure which served a private interest while claiming universality was a corruption of legality rather than an instance of it. He treated custom as carrying its own rationality for the poor, a customary right grounded in the nature of the material gathered, since fallen wood had been abandoned by the tree in a way that standing timber had not. Whatever one thinks of that argument, it is not the argument of a man who believed rules to be nothing but weapons. It is the argument of a man holding a legal order to a standard internal to legality, and finding the order wanting against its own measure. Both strands descend into the later tradition, and the quarrel between the five positions is in part a quarrel about which strand deserves priority.

The second canonical text is the Manifesto of 1848, written with Engels for a small revolutionary league and destined to become the most reprinted political pamphlet of its century. Addressing the bourgeois directly, the pamphlet declares that "the will of your class made into a law for all" is the real content of bourgeois jurisprudence, a fragment of a longer sentence whose compression has made it the most quoted line in the entire field. The sentence does genuine work in its context. It belongs to a passage answering the charge that communists wished to abolish eternal truths, and its point is that what presents itself as eternal and universal in legal thought is the outlook of a historically specific class wearing universal dress. As rhetoric, it is superb. As theory, it is a beginning rather than an end, because it asserts a correspondence between class will and legal content without specifying any mechanism by which the one becomes the other. Who drafts? Who interprets? What happens when the class is divided against itself, as classes usually are? The instrumental position takes the sentence as a finished theory and spends its energy defending the assertion. The other four positions can each be read as attempts to supply the mechanism the sentence omits, which is why their proponents can all quote the same line in good faith while meaning different things by it.

The third text is the most analytical of the three and the least slogan-friendly. In 1875 Marx wrote a set of marginal notes on the programme of a German workers' party, circulated privately and published only after his death, known as the Critique of the Gotha Programme. Its discussion of equal right remains the single most penetrating passage he ever wrote on legal form. Equal right, he argued, measures unequal individuals by an equal standard, treating them as workers and nothing else, overlooking every difference of capacity, need, and circumstance that does not enter the measure. A right that applies an identical rule to people differently endowed is, in its content, "a right of inequality" rather than a right of equality, since the strong worker and the weak, the parent of five and the person without dependants, receive shares whose real value diverges precisely because the rule refused to notice the divergence. The argument matters for legal theory because it locates the problem inside the form of right itself rather than in the bad intentions of legislators. No conspiracy is required. No committee of owners needs to draft anything. The defect belongs to the instrument: an abstract equal measure applied to concrete unequal people reproduces inequality by the manner of its application, and would do so under honest administration by officials of goodwill. That insight is the seed of the ideological position, and it sits uneasily beside the Manifesto sentence, because it explains legal outcomes without any class will doing the explaining.

### Why did Marx write so little about legality?

His mature project centred on the critique of political economy, where legal forms appear as presuppositions of exchange rather than as objects of study in their own right. Property, contract, and the free labourer are the stage on which the analysis of value proceeds, assumed so that the drama of production can begin.

The brevity of the corpus has consequences that run through everything that follows in this article. Because Marx left fragments rather than a system, later writers enjoyed an unusual freedom of construction, and used it. A Soviet jurist of the revolutionary period built the commodity-form theory from a few remarks about exchange and the legal person. Theorists of the capitalist state built the structural account from hints about the relative independence of political forms. A historian built the most unsettling position of all from the archive rather than from Marx's texts at all, arguing that the evidence of how legality actually operated mattered more than fidelity to any fragment. Students should therefore be suspicious of any sentence beginning with the claim that Marx's theory of law is some single thing. The honest sentence is longer: Marx left three or four powerful fragments pointing in different directions, a method for analysing social forms historically, and a standing invitation to test theory against the record. The five positions are so many acceptances of that invitation, and their disagreements are the field.

There is a fourth body of writing worth a paragraph, the historical journalism and the studies of revolution in France, where legal forms appear constantly as instruments and as stakes of struggle. Constitutions are made and unmade, suffrage is widened and narrowed, emergency decrees govern, courts-martial sit, and the young republic's legal architecture is dismantled piece by piece by the forces it was built to restrain. What these writings show is a writer intensely aware that legal forms matter in the short run of politics, that control of them is worth fighting for, and that their content tracks the balance of class forces with some fidelity. What they do not show is a writer who thought the forms were omnipotent. Again and again the narrative turns on the gap between what a constitution promised and what the distribution of property and arms permitted, and the moral drawn is about the limits of parchment when the social ground shifts. A careful reader finds in these pages the raw material of the structural position: forms with real effects of their own, operating within limits set elsewhere, neither puppets nor sovereigns. The tradition's later builders were not inventing when they claimed this Marx. They were selecting, as all builders from fragments must, and the selections differed because the fragments genuinely support more than one completion.

## The Instrumental Account: Legal Rules as a Means of Class Rule

The instrumental account is the position the slogan describes, and it deserves a fair statement before its difficulties are counted. On this view, a legal order is an instrument through which a dominant class protects what it holds, comparable in kind to the other means at its disposal. The class that owns the means of production also commands the state, through some combination of personnel, wealth, and influence, and the state in turn produces rules whose content reflects the owners' requirements. Property is protected absolutely, contract is enforced between formally free parties whose real bargaining power may differ enormously, combination among workers is treated with suspicion while combination among owners proceeds through the ordinary law of companies, and the criminal process falls with its full weight on offences against goods while offences against persons in the workplace attract gentler instruments. The theory has an appealing directness. It names an agent, specifies an interest, and predicts an outcome, and for a great deal of legal history the prediction fits the record without strain. Enclosure measures that converted common land into private holdings by act of legislature, statutes that made breach of a labour contract by a worker a criminal matter while breach by an employer remained a civil one, measures against combination that coexisted with the free incorporation of capital: the archive supplies examples in every century, and the instrumental writer can fill a book with them.

The position also has a distinguished, if unacknowledged, ancestry in common sense. Most people, asked why a particular measure passed, reach first for the question of who benefited, and instrumentalism is that instinct systematised. Its academic form adds two refinements. The first is a claim about personnel: that legislators, judges, and administrators are drawn disproportionately from the possessing classes, educated in their institutions, and socialised into their outlook, so that no instruction needs to pass between owner and official for the owner's interest to be served. The second is a claim about resources: that wealth purchases access, expertise, delay, and publicity, so that even a formally open process delivers outcomes tilted toward those who can work it longest and best. Both refinements are empirically plausible and both are supported by ordinary observation of how legal processes distribute their costs. A theory containing them is not a conspiracy theory. It becomes one only when it treats every outcome, including outcomes the dominant class opposed and lost, as further evidence of the class's cunning.

That treatment is where the difficulties begin, and they are severe enough that writers inside the tradition have largely abandoned the pure form of the position. Consider first the phenomenon of law that constrains capital. Factory legislation limiting the working day was fought by organised employers with every resource the instrumental account attributes to them, and it passed anyway, and it was enforced, and it altered the real terms on which labour was bought. Legislation protecting combination among workers, after long periods in which combination was prosecuted, eventually placed unions inside the legal order rather than outside it. Rules of liability have been extended against enterprises in ways their owners resisted for generations. An instrumentalist can respond to each case, and the responses follow a pattern. The concession was tactical, granted to forestall worse. The measure served capital in general against the short-sightedness of particular capitals. The victory was temporary and has been eroded since. Each response contains some truth, and together they reveal the theory's weakness, which is that it can accommodate any outcome whatever. A theory that predicts both a result and its opposite, depending on which auxiliary story is attached afterwards, has stopped making predictions at all. It has become a vocabulary for redescribing results in class language, which is a much smaller achievement than explaining them.

Consider second the problem of the general interest of capital, which the instrumental account cannot state without ceasing to be instrumental. Capital is not a committee. Individual owners compete, and what each wants from the legal order frequently injures the others and the system they share. Each employer wants freedom to lengthen the working day; all employers together need a working population that survives to work. Each enterprise wants to externalise its costs onto neighbours, rivers, and the public purse; enterprises together need a predictable environment in which contracts made in one year will be honoured in the next. Each financier wants the freedom to take risks whose failure will be socialised; finance as a whole needs the belief that failure has consequences. A legal order that simply transmitted the will of owners would transmit a cacophony, and would destroy within a generation the conditions that make ownership profitable. Somebody or something must overrule particular capitals in the interest of capital as a whole, must impose the long view on the short, must protect the system from its own beneficiaries. The instrumental account has no room for that function, because its model of the state is a transmission belt and transmission belts do not overrule their inputs. The structural account exists precisely to house this function, and its emergence inside the tradition is best understood as instrumentalism discovering its own gap and growing a successor to fill it.

Consider third the occasions when legal process operates against particular powerful people in ways that are plainly not theatre. Owners are prosecuted and sometimes imprisoned. Enterprises are broken up, fined sums that alter their behaviour, forbidden practices on which their profits rested. Judgments go against governments that appointed the judges. None of this is consistent with a picture in which outcomes are arranged in advance by the beneficiaries of the order. The instrumentalist's characteristic reply is that such cases are the price of legitimacy, sacrifices offered to keep the system credible, and that reply is interesting because it concedes the entire argument of this article's centre. If legality must sometimes decide against the powerful in order to remain credible, then the constraint is real, its operation is a proper object of theory, and the question becomes how much constraint legitimacy requires and who pays for it. That question belongs to the historian's position, not to instrumentalism, and the instrumentalist who raises it has changed sides in the middle of the argument.

### What evidence would embarrass the instrumental account?

A durable body of rules, enacted over the opposition of organised owners, enforced against them for generations, and defended by officials whose careers suffered for the defence, would embarrass it gravely. Each element alone can be absorbed as tactics or theatre; the combination cannot, because theatre does not need to be expensive, permanent, and costly to its supposed directors.

None of this makes the position useless, and a fair survey should record its permanent contributions before leaving it. Instrumentalism taught the study of law to ask who benefits, a question that purely doctrinal scholarship had trained itself not to ask. It directed attention to the sociology of legal personnel, to the economics of access, to the distribution of enforcement effort across categories of offence, and to the long record of measures whose class content is written on their face. It remains the correct first approximation for certain domains, notably the criminal law of property in periods of acute class conflict, where the archive really does show legislatures acting as committees of owners and judges sentencing accordingly. Its error is imperial rather than local. It takes a genuine mechanism, one mechanism among several, and promotes it into the whole theory, and then must explain away everything the promotion cannot cover. The mature positions in the field are best read as attempts to keep the mechanism while surrendering the empire: to say where direct class instrumentality operates, where it does not, and what operates instead. That is a harder and more interesting programme than the slogan, and it is the programme the rest of this article follows.

A word should also be said about the position's popular afterlife, because students meet it more often in the wild than in the literature. In its popular form the account loses even its refinements about personnel and resources and becomes a simple refusal: every rule is a fraud, every judgment a fix, every reform a sedative. The refusal feels radical and costs nothing, which is why it spreads. Its practical consequence is quietism, since a citizen persuaded that legal process is theatre from end to end has no reason to contest a prosecution, organise for a measure, or defend a protection once won, and quietism serves whoever already holds power better than any doctrine they could purchase. The writers inside the tradition who attacked instrumentalism most sharply were often motivated by exactly this observation. They had watched movements disarm themselves with a theory, treating hard-won protections as worthless because the theory said protections were tricks, and they concluded that a bad theory of law was not an academic misfortune but a political one. The historian whose work occupies the centre of this article wrote his famous conclusion in that spirit, and its polemical heat is unintelligible unless the reader grasps that he was arguing against comrades whose theory he believed was losing them the world.

## The Structural Account: Relative Autonomy and the General Interest of Capital

The structural account begins from the gap that instrumentalism cannot fill. Some agency must protect the long-run conditions of accumulation against the short-run demands of individual accumulators, and that agency must be sufficiently separate from any particular owner or firm to overrule owners and firms when their demands threaten the whole. The state, on this view, is not a tool lying ready in the hand of a class. It is an institutional structure with its own personnel, its own procedures, its own need for revenue and order, and its own distance from the day-to-day business of profit. Legal institutions share in that distance. Courts follow procedures that no litigant controls, apply rules whose content was settled in earlier conflicts, and employ officials whose professional identity depends on the appearance, and to a considerable degree the reality, of deciding according to the rule rather than according to the identity of the party before them. The name the tradition gives to this distance is relative autonomy, and the two words of the phrase carry equal weight. The autonomy is real: legal institutions do things no owner ordered and some owners bitterly resent. The autonomy is relative: it operates within limits set by the society's economic structure, and a legal order that set itself systematically against the reproduction of that structure would find its revenue, its enforcement capacity, and eventually its personnel withdrawn.

The classic debate that fixed the vocabulary took place between theorists of the state in the middle decades of the twentieth century, one side emphasising the social composition and connections of state personnel, the other insisting that the structure of the capitalist state guarantees its function regardless of who staffs it. The second of those emphases is the structural account proper, and its legal version runs as follows. A legal order serving capital in general must appear, and partly be, universal. It must protect property as such, not the property of friends; enforce contracts as such, not the contracts of the connected; punish theft as such, not theft from the respectable. Universality is not a disguise laid over the function. Universality is the function's mechanism, because only rules that bind everyone in form can secure the predictability on which investment, credit, and long-distance exchange depend. A regime of open favouritism would be a worse instrument for capital than a regime of genuine generality, since generality is what makes outcomes calculable across millions of transactions conducted by strangers. From this it follows that legal institutions must sometimes rule against powerful litigants, not as theatre and not as concession, but because ruling for them on grounds of their power would corrode the generality that makes the system valuable to the class as a whole. The structural account thus explains, within a Marxist frame, the very phenomena that embarrass the instrumental one.

The account also explains a feature of legal orders that instrumentalism must treat as mystery: their stability across changes of government and personnel. Elections replace ministers, revolutions replace regimes, and yet the law of property, contract, and obligation persists through the turnover with its main lines intact, amended at the margins rather than rebuilt. If legal content were the will of whoever held office, turnover would rewrite it wholesale. That it does not suggests the content is anchored somewhere deeper than office, in the structure of the economy the legal order serves and in the professional and institutional inertia of the order itself. Structural writers add that this anchoring is also a limit on reforming governments. A ministry elected on a programme of transformation discovers that the legal order it inherits constrains its options: obligations entered into by predecessors bind it, courts apply settled rules to its novel measures, capital responds to legal uncertainty by withholding the investment on which employment and revenue depend. None of this requires bad faith anywhere. The constraint is structural, which is the point of the adjective, and it explains the recurring experience of reforming administrations that win office and find the state less obedient than the theory of instruments promised.

### What separates relative autonomy from simple independence?

Relative autonomy names a bounded distance: legal institutions decide according to their own procedures and can rule against any particular interest, yet the range of outcomes they can sustain is limited by the economic order they serve. Independence would mean no such limits existed, which no writer in the tradition claims.

The difficulties of the structural account are the mirror image of the instrumental one's virtues. Where instrumentalism names an agent and risks conspiracy, structuralism names a function and risks vacuity. To say that an institution exists because it serves the reproduction of capital is to offer a reason of a peculiar kind, and critics inside and outside the tradition have pressed the question of mechanism. Through what process does the function secure the institution? If a rule failed to serve accumulation, what would remove it, and on what timescale? Functional explanations in social theory are not illegitimate, but they require a feedback loop: some process by which dysfunctional arrangements are selected out and functional ones retained. Structural writers have supplied candidates, including the dependence of state revenue on private investment, the socialisation of officials, the discipline of capital flight, and the simple fact that arrangements producing chaos tend to be reformed by whoever suffers from the chaos. The account is strongest when it specifies its loops and weakest when it treats the fit between legal order and economic structure as self-explanatory, a harmony requiring no machinery. A student should learn to ask, of any structural claim, the rude question: how, exactly, and what would it look like if the claim were false?

A second difficulty concerns the degree of autonomy, which the theory asserts but cannot easily measure. Relative autonomy explains why legal institutions can defy particular capitals, and also why they cannot defy capital in general for long, but the boundary between the particular and the general is exactly where political conflict happens, and the theory gives limited guidance in advance about where a given dispute falls. Was a given factory measure an overruling of particular owners in the general interest, or the beginning of a challenge to the general interest itself? Participants at the time rarely know, and the theory, applied after the event, tends to classify outcomes by their results: whatever survived was compatible with the general interest, whatever was reversed was not. That retrospective ease is the structural version of the instrumentalist's theatre, and honest structural writers acknowledge it. The account is best used as a frame for inquiry, directing attention to the long-run conditions a legal order must maintain and to the institutional distances that let it maintain them, rather than as a machine for classifying cases. Used that way it remains the most influential of the five positions in neighbouring disciplines, where its central insight, that legal generality is a functional requirement rather than a fraud, has been absorbed by scholars who owe the tradition nothing and sometimes do not know the source of the idea they are using.

## The Commodity-Form Account: Deriving the Legal Subject from Exchange

The third position is the most original in the field and the most demanding, and it receives only a summary here because the series gives it a full article of its own, devoted to that jurist and his derivation. Its author was a jurist of the revolutionary period in Russia, writing in the nineteen twenties, who asked a question nobody else in the tradition had thought to ask. Instrumentalists asked whose interests legal rules serve. Structuralists asked what function the legal order performs. He asked why there is a legal form at all: why social relations take the shape of rights held by abstract persons, why obligation wears the dress of contract between equals, why the subject of law is a bare bearer of entitlements stripped of every concrete quality. His answer derived the form from the commodity. In exchange, products meet as values, abstracted from every useful particularity, and their owners meet as formally free and equal persons whose wills reside in the things exchanged. The legal subject, he argued, is the commodity owner raised to a universal: an abstract will, an equal standing, a capacity to hold and alienate, exactly the equipment a market requires and exactly the equipment the modern legal person possesses. Law reaches its fullest development, on this account, not where class rule is crudest but where commodity production is most general, because only there does every social relation tend to be mediated by exchange between formally equal owners.

The elegance of the derivation explains its endurance. It explains why the legal form is abstract, which neither rival position attempts. Instrumentalism can say why a rule favours owners but not why rules present themselves as relations between equal wills; a class secure in its rule might prefer open privilege, and many ruling classes in history did prefer exactly that, governing through status, caste, and personal dependence rather than through universal right. That derivation predicts the specifically modern shape of legality: the disappearance of formal status, the universality of the rights-bearing person, the centrality of contract, the treatment of the labourer as owner of a commodity he is free to sell. It also explains the affinity between legal and economic categories that lawyers themselves notice without theorising, the way doctrines of will, consent, equivalence, and alienation migrate between the two fields wearing each other's clothes. And it generates the tradition's most radical conclusion about the future. If the legal form is the form of commodity relations, then a society that abolished commodity production would not produce a new and better law. It would cease to produce law at all, replacing legal regulation with technical administration, the way a railway timetable regulates without rights, courts, or guilt. That conclusion, drawn by its author with full awareness of its severity, sets up the question of socialist law that a later section of this article examines.

The account's difficulties are as distinctive as its strengths. Historians object that legal forms recognisably similar to the modern subject appear in settings where commodity production was thin, and that the derivation risks reading a logical affinity as a historical cause. Theorists object that the account explains the form of private law, the law of property and contract between persons, far better than it explains public and criminal law, where the state confronts the individual not as an equal exchanger but as a sovereign, and where the author's own attempts at derivation strain audibly. There is also the question of the author's fate and its bearing on the theory: he recanted under political pressure in the following decade and perished in the purges of his country, and his book survived to be rediscovered by later generations who read it as the tradition's one genuinely original contribution to jurisprudence. The rediscovery was not confined to Marxists. Scholars of international law, a field where formally equal sovereigns exchange and contract without any superior to command them, found the derivation uncannily apt for their material, and the commodity-form argument now circulates in that literature detached from the politics of its author, a pattern this article meets again when it turns to uptake in neighbouring disciplines.

### Why does the commodity-form account treat contract as central?

Because exchange is the relation the whole derivation rests on, and contract is exchange wearing legal dress: two abstract wills, formally free and equal, meeting to alienate and acquire. If the legal subject is the commodity owner universalised, then the law of contract is where that subject lives most completely.

For the purposes of the five positions grid, the commodity-form account should be filed as the position that explains form where the others explain content or function. It is largely silent about why a particular rule takes the content it does, and its proponents would regard that silence as honesty, since content belongs to the history of class conflict and cannot be deduced from the form. It is correspondingly strong exactly where instrumentalism is weakest. The instrumentalist must treat the equality of the legal subject as a lie laid over domination. The commodity-form theorist treats that equality as real within its sphere, the sphere of exchange, and locates the domination elsewhere, in production, where the formally equal parties to the labour contract go on to meet as commander and commanded within the working day. That relocation matters for everything the tradition says about rights. On this account, rights are not a fraud perpetrated on the worker. They are the genuine form of his freedom to sell the one commodity he owns, and the tragedy the theory describes is that this genuine freedom is fully compatible with exploitation, requiring no fraud anywhere in the system. Students who grasp that point have grasped why the tradition's attitude to rights has always been divided against itself, revering them as real gains in one paragraph and indicting them as the form of unfreedom in the next. The division is not confusion. It is the commodity-form argument working itself out in writers who felt both sides of it.

## The Ideological Account: Law as the Making of Free and Equal Subjects

The fourth position shifts attention from what legal rules do to what they make people believe, and more deeply, to what they make people be. On the ideological account, the deepest work of a legal order is performed before any dispute reaches a hearing, in the formation of subjects who understand themselves as rights-bearing persons, free and equal, owners of property in things and in their own capacities, authors of agreements they have chosen and therefore bound by them in conscience as well as in fact. The machinery of this formation is treated fully in the guide to the Marxist theory of ideology, and only its legal application belongs here. That application starts from the Gotha insight. Equal right applied to unequal people yields unequal outcomes through the equality of its application, and the ideological account adds the psychological corollary: because the rule was equal, the outcome appears just. The worker whose wage buys less life than the owner's dividend is not, in his own understanding, the victim of an unfair rule. He is a party to a fair exchange whose result disappoints him, and the disappointment is privatised, experienced as personal failure or personal luck rather than as the output of a structure. Legality thus reconciles people to inequality not by lying about the rule, which really is equal, but by confining attention to the rule and away from the unequal endowments the rule processes so impartially.

This is a subtler claim than the crude picture of ideology as falsehood pumped into passive minds, and its proponents insist on the subtlety. Legal ideology works because its central representations are true in the sphere where they operate. Persons really are equal in the market and before the tribunal; the labourer really may refuse a contract and starve as a free man rather than starve as a serf; the owner's title really is examined by the same doctrines that examine the tenant's. The ideological effect arises from the sphere's boundaries, from the way the equality of circulation conceals the inequality of production, where the parties who met as equals in the morning meet again as buyer and bought within the organisation of the working day. A theory of legal ideology is therefore a theory of partial truth and systematic framing, of a lens that shows one region of social life in sharp focus and renders the adjoining region invisible by the same optical act. Writers in this line have extended the analysis to the rituals of legal process itself: the architecture that dwarfs the individual, the robes and forms of address that lift officials out of ordinary humanity, the insistence that coercion arrives only after reasons have been given in a language of justification. Each element teaches the same lesson, that power here is not power but order, and the lesson is taught to the officials as thoroughly as to the public, which is why the account can explain sincere belief among judges without insulting their intelligence.

The ideological account has a distinctive strength: it explains consent, which is the great unexplained fact for cruder theories. Class societies are not held together by coercion alone, since the coercive apparatus is always a minority of the population and could not police a hostile majority through a single season of harvests. They are held together substantially by the belief of the governed that the order governing them is legitimate, natural, or at least unchangeable, and legal forms are among the principal factories of that belief. Every contract signed without resentment, every verdict accepted as unfortunate but fair, every queue of citizens waiting patiently for a process they trust, is the ideological account's evidence accumulating in ordinary life. The account also explains the peculiar bitterness of legal disillusionment. When a legal order is caught acting in open bad faith, the damage exceeds the episode, because what breaks is not a rule but the representation of the whole: the discovery that the equal rule was a costume on one occasion raises the question of how many other occasions wore the same costume unseen. Movements for legal reform have always understood this instinctively, which is why they prize exposure of a single flagrant case out of proportion to its material weight.

### Does the ideological account claim that legal equality is fake?

It claims the opposite, and the distinction is the position's signature. Legal equality is genuine within the sphere of exchange and adjudication; the ideological effect lies in that sphere's boundaries, which hide the unequal endowments people bring to it and the unequal relations waiting for them in production.

Against these strengths stand two difficulties that the tradition itself has pressed. The first is that the account, carelessly used, becomes as unfalsifiable as the instrumentalism it was meant to improve. If acceptance of a verdict proves ideology's grip and rejection of a verdict proves ideology's cunning in permitting harmless dissent, then no observation counts against the theory, and it has retired from explanation into mood. Careful writers in this line therefore specify in advance what their claims predict: that legitimation should vary measurably with the perceived fairness of procedures, that exposure of systematic procedural bad faith should damage compliance beyond the cases exposed, that groups whose experience of legal process is repeatedly unequal should hold the order's legitimacy in lower regard than groups whose experience is benign. Such predictions can be wrong, which is what makes them valuable. The second difficulty is that the account can slide into treating subjects as cultural dopes, formed so thoroughly by legal representations that their own experience of fairness counts for nothing. The historical position exists partly as a protest against that slide. The historian's archive showed ordinary people using legal forms for their own purposes, appealing to rights against their betters with full tactical awareness, believing in the forms neither naively nor cynically but instrumentally and sometimes successfully. A complete theory of law and ideology must leave room for that tactical citizen, and the tension between the ideological account's formed subject and the historical account's resourceful one is among the most productive in the field, generating research rather than merely dividing opinion.

## The Historical Account: What the Archive Does to Theory

The fifth position differs from the other four in method before it differs in doctrine. Where they proceed from texts and concepts, it proceeds from records: assize files, prosecution patterns, pardon petitions, the paper trail of a legal system at work on the poor of an eighteenth-century countryside. Its founding study, published in 1975, examined a statute of notorious severity, an act that multiplied capital offences against property in a single legislative stroke, attaching the gallows to offences against trees, cattle, parks, and fishponds in a schedule that still startles readers who meet it for the first time. A straightforward instrumental reading was available and tempting. Here, surely, was the ruling class writing its will into law with unusual candour, terror legislated to protect the deer of great men against the hunger of small ones. The historian's achievement was to follow that reading into the archive and report, with evident reluctance, that the record would not sustain it in its simple form. The statute was real, the terror was real, and the class interest behind the measure was real. What the record also showed was a system that could not operate as pure terror, that acquitted against the evidence, that pardoned wholesale, that watched juries refuse the law's own categories, and that bound the prosecutors and magistrates who wielded it inside forms they could manipulate only at the cost of the authority the forms conferred.

From that record the historical account draws its characteristic claims. Legal forms, once established, acquire a logic that participants must respect to use at all. A prosecution must allege an offence the statute recognises, prove it by evidence the tribunal accepts, and survive defences the accused is entitled to raise, and each requirement is a point at which the powerful can and do lose. Discretion, which the system required in vast quantities because its penalties were too savage to apply uniformly, was exercised through petitions, patronage, and displays of mercy that staged the ruling order as paternal and just; but the staging had to be performed, and performance imposed its own script. A mercy that was never granted would have advertised the terror beneath the theatre, so mercy was granted, often, to real people whose lives were really saved by the forms. The account therefore refuses the vocabulary of mask and facade. What the archive shows is not a false front on class power but a medium through which class power had to pass, and which altered what passed through it, as a lens alters light. Power that must justify itself in universal terms, proceed by evidence, and submit to acquittals is power genuinely limited, limited not by the goodwill of its holders but by the operating requirements of its own instrument.

This is the soil from which the historian's famous conclusion grew, and the next three sections of this article give that conclusion the quarter of the whole that it has earned in the literature. Before turning to it, the historical position should be placed precisely among the five. It is not a rival mechanism on the same level as the others so much as a discipline imposed on all of them: a demand that theories of law survive contact with the record of law in action. Instrumentalism survives that contact in damaged form, true of enactment more often than of operation. Structuralism survives better, since the archive's spectacle of forms binding their makers is what relative autonomy looks like at ground level. The archive leaves the commodity-form account largely untouched, for better and worse, since its derivation operates at a level of abstraction the records neither confirm nor refute directly. The ideological account is both confirmed and corrected: confirmed, because the record is full of legitimation being manufactured through ritual, mercy, and procedural display; corrected, because the same record shows the manufactured belief escaping its manufacturers, as the poor took the proclaimed universality of the forms at face value and sued, petitioned, and appealed in its name against the very order that proclaimed it. A legitimation that can be turned against its makers is a strange kind of trick, and the strangeness is the historical position's permanent gift to the field.

### What does the historical account add that the others lack?

Evidence about operation. The other positions reason chiefly from the content of rules, the structure of institutions, or the form of legal relations, while the historical account watches a legal system deciding actual cases across decades, and reports which theories the behaviour of prosecutors, juries, petitioners, and the condemned will bear.

There is also a lesson about period and place embedded in the position, and students should extract it carefully. The founding study's legal order was one in which a small propertied class governed a large propertyless population through a criminal law of exemplary terror softened by discretionary mercy, a configuration specific enough that generalising from it requires argument rather than assumption. Later historians have tested the position against other configurations: systems of mass incarceration, systems of administrative regulation, systems where the propertyless majority holds formal political rights the eighteenth century denied. The position travels, but it travels as a question rather than as an answer. In each new setting the question is the same: what do the forms require of those who wield them here, what do the wielders actually lose by those requirements, and who else has learned to use the forms for purposes their authors never intended? Asked that way, the historical account stops being a claim about one statute and becomes a research programme, and it is in that form that socio-legal scholars with no allegiance to the tradition have taken it up most eagerly. The next sections turn to the conclusion that made the programme famous, stated in the historian's own work at the end of his study, where he paused over his evidence and wrote the sentence that his comrades have been arguing about ever since.

## The Five Positions Grid

Surveys of a contested field owe their readers a compression they can carry away, and this article's findable artifact is the five positions grid, a table that sets the positions side by side and forces each to declare what it takes law to be, what it says explains legal content, what it cannot explain, and whose name attaches to it. Tables flatten, and the prose of the preceding sections should be read as the authority where the grid and the prose differ. But flattening has its uses. A student revising at midnight, a teacher planning a seminar, a researcher checking whether a new argument is genuinely new or an old position in fresh vocabulary: each is served by a compression that makes the field's joints visible at a glance. Read down the third column especially. A position is known by its failure mode, by the class of facts it must wave away, and the discipline of naming that class in advance is what separates a theory from a temperament.

| Position | What law is | What explains its content | What it cannot explain | Principal proponent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instrumental | An instrument of class domination | The will and interests of the dominant class | Law that binds capital, victories won against owners | The Manifesto's readers, in the slogan's form |
| Structural | A relatively autonomous institution | The long-run requirements of capital as a whole | Which disputes count as particular, which as general | Theorists of the capitalist state |
| Commodity-form | The form of relations between abstract owners | The structure of commodity exchange | Public and criminal law, thin-market settings | Pashukanis |
| Ideological | A factory of free and equal subjects | The need for legitimation of unequal outcomes | Tactical, clear-eyed use of forms by the governed | Theorists of ideology |
| Historical | A medium that constrains its wielders | The operating requirements of legal forms themselves | Settings far from its founding archive, without fresh evidence | Thompson and the social historians |

Two features of the grid deserve emphasis because examinations reward them and casual reading misses them. The first is that the positions are not five answers to one question but answers to at least three different questions wearing similar dress. The instrumental and structural accounts answer a question about content and function: why do the rules say what they say, and whose situation do they improve? The commodity-form account answers a question about form: why do social relations present themselves as rights of abstract persons at all? The ideological account answers a question about belief: why do the governed consent, and what part do legal representations play in manufacturing that consent? The historical account answers a question about operation: what actually happens, across thousands of cases, when a legal system goes to work? Because the questions differ, the positions can be combined more often than their polemics suggest. A single scholar might hold that exchange explains the form, that class conflict explains much of the content, that legitimation explains the consent, and that the archive disciplines all three claims, and several of the field's strongest writers hold roughly that portfolio. The polemics arise because each position, at the moment of its founding, claimed the whole territory, and the claims have never been formally withdrawn.

The second feature is the asymmetry of the failure column. Each position fails somewhere different, and the pattern of failures is itself instructive. Instrumentalism fails upward, on law that rises above its supposed masters. Structuralism fails at the boundary, unable to classify the disputes that matter most while they are happening. The derivation from exchange fails outward, at the edges of the market's domain, in the criminal and public law where exchange is a metaphor rather than a mechanism. The ideological account fails downward, on the governed who refuse to be formed as the theory requires and who handle the forms with a strategist's cold eye. The historical account fails across distance, weakening as it is carried from the archive that nourished it into settings whose records it has not read. A reader who can recite the five strengths has learned a list. Someone who can recite the five failures, and produce an example for each, has learned the field. The grid is designed for the second reader, and the sections that follow are designed to make one episode, the historian's challenge, do the work of illustrating all five failures at once, since the controversy it provoked drew every position into print against the others.

## The Historian's Challenge: A Conclusion Nobody Expected

The study appeared in 1975 under the title Whigs and Hunters, and its author, E. P. Thompson, was by then among the most respected historians writing in English and a Marxist of long standing, formed in the communist movement of his youth and tempered by departure from its orthodoxies without departure from its allegiances. The book examined the origins and operation of a statute of the seventeen twenties that had made a schedule of offences against property capital, in a period when conflicts over forest rights, customary gathering, and the claims of great landowners against small occupiers were turning violent in the wooded districts within reach of the capital. Thompson's sympathies were never in doubt. He wrote about the resisters, the disguised night-time parties who broke the fences and killed the deer, with the warmth of a historian who had spent his career recovering the experience of the defeated, and about the statute's authors with cold contempt for men who legislated the gallows to protect their parks. Nothing in the book's first two hundred pages prepares the reader for its final pages, and that is the point. A historian of the left, studying a law of the right, in a case that seemed made to order for the instrumental account, concluded his study by declaring the rule of law itself to be "an unqualified human good". The sentence landed among his comrades like a stone through a window.

The context of the sentence matters, because it is often quoted as though it were a peroration, a flourish of liberal piety at the end of a Marxist book. It is nothing of the kind. It arrives as the conclusion of an argument conducted across the whole study and pressed hardest in its closing chapter, where Thompson reviewed what his evidence had done to the theories he had carried into the archive. He had expected, he tells the reader in substance, to find the law functioning as a straightforward instrument of class power, and in the statute's origins he had found exactly that. What he had not expected was the degree to which the instrument escaped the hands that made it once it began to operate. The forms of the law, its insistence on defined offences, admissible evidence, and procedures that even prosecutors had to observe, turned out to be not a decorative wrapping on power but a set of inhibitions on its exercise, inhibitions that acquitted the factually guilty, embarrassed the well-connected, and on recorded occasions brought the machinery of prosecution down on men of standing who had treated the forms as theirs to bend. Thompson's conclusion was drawn from that friction. The rule of law, meaning the subjection of power to rules that bind the powerful as well as the powerless, was not in his judgment a bourgeois swindle to be exposed. It was a cultural achievement of the first order, wrung out of centuries of conflict, and the left damaged itself and its own people when it taught them to sneer at it.

He was explicit about the target. The passage is addressed to Marxists, and its polemic is internal. A certain kind of left scholarship, he argued, had developed a reflex: wherever legal forms appeared, it discerned only mystification, and it treated the outcomes of cases as readable directly from the class positions of the parties, as though the hearings were ceremonies whose scripts were written elsewhere. Against that reflex he set the archive. Scripts written elsewhere do not produce acquittals that enrage prosecutors. Ceremonies do not hang poachers in one parish and free them in the next on distinctions of evidence that the ceremony theory cannot even describe. The reflex, he charged, was not merely an intellectual error. It was a disarmament practised on the very people in whose name it was performed, because movements that teach their members to regard every legal protection as a fraud will not fight to keep the protections they have, and will discover the difference between a legal order that constrains power imperfectly and a power that acknowledges no constraint only when the discovery can no longer help them. Readers who know the century in which he wrote, with its ample demonstrations of what unconstrained power does to the left first of all, will hear the sentence's urgency. It was written by a man counting the cost of a theory in the currency of movements destroyed.

### What exactly did the historian conclude at his study's end?

That the rule of law, the effective inhibition of power by rules binding on the powerful themselves, is an unqualified human good. The claim is about the form's real operation, not about any legal system's justice overall, and it was drawn from evidence that the forms acquitted, embarrassed, and occasionally prosecuted the powerful.

The passage has become the most discussed in Marxist legal theory, and its afterlife divides into three streams that the following sections take in turn. The first is the argument itself, reconstructed at full strength, since Thompson's case is tighter than its quoters usually allow and rests on a claim about legitimation that this article has named as its centre. The second is the criticism, which came promptly and from named respondents of standing, and which accused the historian of abandoning materialism for liberalism at the exact point where his evidence grew uncomfortable. The third is the assessment, half a century of scholarship later in spirit if not in this article's arithmetic, of what the argument established, what it left unproven, and why the question it opened remains the field's live question rather than a settled antique. Through all three streams one distinction must be held firm, because losing it is the commonest error in the secondary literature. Thompson did not conclude that the legal system he studied was just. He concluded that one feature of it, the constraint of power by its own proclaimed rules, was a good without qualification, a formulation chosen with a controversialist's precision to deny his critics the escape of agreeing with a qualified version. The ferocity of the response is unintelligible unless that precision is respected. His critics heard the unqualified, and answered it.

## The Legitimation Constraint: Why Law Must Sometimes Lose for the Powerful

The argument beneath Thompson's conclusion deserves a name, and this article calls it the legitimation constraint. Stated as a general claim about legal orders, it runs as follows. A legal system performs ideological work for the order it serves: it reconciles the governed to arrangements that favour the powerful, by presenting those arrangements as the outcome of impartial rules impartially applied. That work can only be performed if the presentation is credible, and credibility has operating requirements. The rules must be seen to operate according to their own logic, which means they must sometimes produce outcomes the powerful dislike, in cases the public can observe, at a rate sufficient to sustain the belief that outcomes follow rules rather than instructions. A system that decided every contested case for the stronger party would forfeit its legitimating function within a generation, because the pattern would be legible to everyone and the costume would cease to convince even its wearers. Legitimation therefore purchases belief at a price, and the price is real constraint. The powerful must sometimes lose, visibly and on matters they care about, as the running cost of an order whose other decisions will be accepted without the expense of constant coercion. The constraint is not a decoration on power. It is a limitation power accepts, or rather discovers it cannot refuse, as the condition of ruling through law rather than through terror alone.

The claim's structure repays attention because it converts the ideological account's central insight into a mechanism with teeth. Ideology, on the crude version, is a story the powerful tell the powerless, and stories are cheap. On the legitimation constraint version, the story is expensive, and its price is paid in lost cases, acquitted enemies, embarrassed officials, and precedents that bind future exercises of power in ways their authors would not have chosen. The conversion matters because it gives the theory empirical content. If legitimation requires credible constraint, then the historical record should show legal systems generating a characteristic pattern: not randomness, not equality of outcomes between strong and weak, but a systematic minority of consequential decisions against the powerful, concentrated where publicity is greatest, together with ferocious internal resistance whenever officials are caught bending the forms, since bending discovered is legitimation destroyed. Thompson's archive showed that pattern in an unlikely place, a terror statute administered by the class that wrote it, and the pattern's appearance there is what gives the argument its force. If the constraint operates even under those conditions, the presumption is that it operates wherever rule through law is attempted at all.

A useful way to fix the idea is to compare the three accounts of a single acquittal of a powerful defendant, or of a judgment against a great interest, as each position reads it. The instrumentalist reads the episode as theatre, a sacrifice staged to legitimate the rest, and is committed to the staging being cheap: the matter sacrificed must be one the powerful could afford to lose. The structuralist reads it as the general interest overruling the particular, the system protecting its own predictability against a beneficiary grown careless. The legitimation constraint reads it as the price of credibility, neither staged nor systemic in the abstract, but extracted by the operating requirements of ruling through forms in front of an audience that is watching for exactly such evidence. The readings overlap, and in many cases they predict the same episode. They diverge in what they expect next. The instrumentalist expects the powerful to be compensated elsewhere, quietly. The structuralist expects the overruling to track the long-run needs of accumulation. The constraint expects something subtler: that the rate and visibility of such episodes should respond to the system's need for belief, rising when legitimacy is scarce and contested, and that attempts to reduce the price, by capturing the process or disciplining its officials, should generate legitimacy crises out of proportion to the material stakes. Those divergent expectations are what make the three positions theories rather than vocabularies, and they give researchers something to do besides redescribe.

### How does the legitimation constraint differ from calling law a mask?

A mask costs nothing to wear and can be dropped at will. The constraint is a cost that cannot be refused without destroying the benefit it purchases: a legal order that never decided against the powerful would lose the credibility that makes its other decisions acceptable, so the losses are a genuine operating expense of ruling through law.

The argument also reframes the oldest question in the field, whether rights and legal protections are worth fighting for. On the instrumental account, they are not, except tactically, since the forms are the enemy's property and victories inside them are loans to be recalled. On the constraint account, the forms are a terrain on which real value changes hands. A protection won is a precedent, a procedure, an expectation lodged in the operating requirements of the system, and its removal costs the powerful legitimacy they must then replace by other means. This is why the historian's conclusion was a political intervention and not merely a scholarly one. It told movements that their inheritance included something worth defending with the same seriousness they brought to attacking the order that housed it, and that a politics unable to distinguish between the order and the constraint would surrender the constraint and keep the order, the worst available trade. Later writers in several traditions have generalised the point beyond its original setting. Any institution that rules by claiming to follow rules, examinations that claim to reward merit, markets that claim to reward value, inherits a version of the constraint, since the claim creates the standard by which the institution can be caught, and being catchable is a real limitation on the institution's freedom. The legal case remains the purest, because there the rules are written down, the decisions are public, and the gap between proclamation and practice can be documented by anyone with access to the record.

One clarification protects the argument from its commonest misreading, friendly as well as hostile. The legitimation constraint is a claim about a mechanism, not a claim about a quantity. It asserts that some real constraint is the price of legitimation; it does not assert that the price is high, that it is paid gladly, or that the resulting order is therefore acceptable on balance. A system might purchase enormous legitimacy with a small ration of constraint, conceding where little is at stake and holding firm wherever the structure of property is touched, and nothing in the mechanism forbids that bleak arithmetic. Whether the arithmetic is bleak, and how bleak, is an empirical question the argument itself does not answer, a point the critics seized on with justice and which the next section examines at the length it deserves. The mechanism establishes that the constraint is real. It does not establish that the constraint is enough, and the distance between real and enough is where the controversy lives.

## The Criticism: Capitulation, Say the Comrades

The response from within the tradition was swift, distinguished, and unkind, and a fair account must present it at the strength its authors gave it. The most cited reply came from the legal theorist Morton Horwitz, who took the historian's own phrase for his title and asked, in a celebrated review essay, whether the rule of law was indeed the unqualified good the conclusion proclaimed. His objection cut at the argument's historical root. The rule of law as a practice and an ideal, he argued, was entangled from its maturity with the rise of market society, and its vaunted generality carried a substantive cargo: by treating parties as abstract equals and forbidding decision-makers to weigh their unequal situations, it systematically advantaged those who arrived already advantaged, and it delegitimised in advance any attempt to use public power to correct the imbalance. Formal equality, rigorously enforced, is a decision about outcomes wearing the dress of neutrality, and a historian celebrating the form without reckoning that cargo had mistaken the market's favourite institution for humanity's. The objection does not deny the constraint. It denies the unqualified, arguing that a good purchased by entrenching substantive inequality carries its qualification on its face.

A second line of criticism came from within social history itself, from scholars who had worked the same archive and read it differently. Douglas Hay, whose study of the criminal law in the same period appeared alongside Thompson's and is permanently bound with it in the literature, had emphasised the law's ideological choreography: the majesty of its rituals, the terror of its penalties, and the mercy of its pardons combining to manufacture deference among the governed. Where Thompson saw the forms escaping their makers, Hay's account saw the forms working as designed, their apparent mercies and acquittals functioning as the most effective legitimation precisely because they were experienced as real. The two historians did not disagree about the facts of acquittals and pardons. They disagreed about what the facts showed: constraint discovered in the system's own operating needs, or legitimation achieved through them, and the disagreement is instructive because it reveals how much of the controversy turns on a distinction between a cost and an investment. What Thompson called the price of credibility, Hay's reading calls the purchase of deference, money well spent. Later writers, among them Bob Fine in a sustained treatment of democracy and the rule of law, pressed the theoretical version of the point: that a Marxism which ends by blessing the central institution of bourgeois society has not corrected its theory of law but abandoned the attempt to have one, mistaking the limits of its old theory for the virtues of the opposing camp.

Beneath the named exchanges runs the objection this article's brief requires to be stated at full strength, because it is the strongest available and because Thompson's defenders have never fully answered it. It runs as follows. An account holding that law constrains power because it must appear to do so explains too little, since the appearance can be maintained by deciding against the powerful in matters that do not affect their position, and a constraint that operates only where nothing is at stake is not a constraint. The powerful lose cases the way a casino loses hands: frequently, visibly, and within an arithmetic that guarantees the house. They lose the poacher whose prosecution embarrassed a magistrate, the official whose corruption became a scandal, the enterprise whose practice had grown indefensible even to its peers. They do not lose the structure. No accumulation of acquittals redistributes the forest, and no line of adverse precedents has yet expropriated the expropriators. If the legitimation constraint binds only at the margin, taxing power lightly for the appearance of binding it generally, then calling the result an unqualified good is not a conclusion from evidence but a change of allegiance, and the historian's comrades were right to hear it as one.

The answer available to Thompson's side begins by refusing the casino's arithmetic as a description of how precedent works. Legal constraint does not operate only in the case decided. A rule applied against a powerful party in a small matter becomes a premise available to every future litigant, and premises migrate. Doctrines forged in disputes between landowners have been turned against landowners by tenants; procedures designed to discipline the poor have been used to delay and publicise the prosecutions of the rich; the requirement that power give reasons, once conceded anywhere, becomes an embarrassment everywhere it is refused. The historical record contains episodes in which legal constraint operated against substantial interests and not merely marginal ones: measures limiting the working day against the organised opposition of the employing class, prosecutions of the well-connected that ended in ruin, judgments that destroyed business models and were obeyed. Each episode can be redescribed as marginal by a critic determined to redescribe it, since substantiality is itself contested, and at that point the argument reaches its honest resting place, which Thompson's better defenders have the candour to name. The legitimation constraint requires an empirical claim about how much must be conceded to sustain legitimacy, and neither its author nor its defenders have established that quantity. If the necessary concession is small, the critics are right in substance whatever one calls the form. If it is large, recurrent, and liable to grow under pressure from below, the historian is right and the constraint is among the most important facts about legal orders. The controversy endures because the question is genuine, measurable in principle, and unmeasured in fact, and a survey article serves its readers best by leaving it exactly there: a real mechanism, an open quantity, and a standing invitation to the archive.

### Did Thompson think he had left Marxism behind?

He denied it, in print and with some heat. His claim was that the conclusion followed from Marxist premises pursued honestly into evidence: that a materialist history which could not accommodate its own findings about legal forms was a materialism in name only, and that the sneering reflex he attacked was the departure, not his correction of it.

One further feature of the controversy explains its longevity in teaching. The dispute was never only about law. It condensed a wider argument within the left of that period about the value of liberal inheritances generally: free expression, representative institutions, rights against the state, the whole apparatus that one tradition of Marxism dismissed as bourgeois forms and another insisted on counting among the working class's own conquests, won by its struggles and held in its interest. Thompson belonged emphatically to the second camp, and his legal conclusion was the jurisprudential front of a general position. That is why the passage is quoted in arguments far from legal history, and why students meeting it should read it as a dispatch from a larger war. The five positions grid acquires a sixth shadow at this point, the question of what a post-capitalist society should want from the legal inheritance at all, and that question leads directly to the debate over socialist law, by way of a necessary detour through a school that is often misfiled as the tradition's own.

## Is Critical Legal Studies Marxist? A Correction the Record Requires

Surveys of legal theory written for students commit one filing error with such regularity that this article is required to correct it in terms. Critical Legal Studies, the movement that arose within elite legal education in the final decades of the twentieth century, is routinely presented as a Marxist school, sometimes as the Marxist school of modern jurisprudence, and its leading figures are introduced to undergraduates as though they were cadres of the tradition this article surveys. The filing is wrong, and the correction matters beyond pedantry, because absorbing the school into the tradition distorts both. The movement's relation to Marxism is partial and was disputed within the movement itself, by participants on both sides of the question, and an accurate survey states that partiality rather than smoothing it into membership.

The school's signature claim was the indeterminacy thesis: the claim that legal materials, the stock of rules, precedents, and principles available to a decision-maker, do not determine outcomes in the cases that matter, because for nearly every doctrinal path there exists a counter-path of equal professional respectability leading the other way. Decision, on this view, is choice dressed as deduction, and the dressing conceals the political commitments that actually move the chooser. The thesis plainly has affinities with parts of the Marxist field. It denies the legal order's self-presentation as a neutral machine. It treats doctrine as a field of struggle rather than a geometry. Some of its early figures had passed through Marxist politics and said so, and the movement's founding conferences overlapped in personnel and vocabulary with the broader left of the period. Those affinities are the whole truth of the filing, and they do not amount to identity. Indeterminacy is a thesis about legal reasoning, not about class, production, or history, and it is compatible with several political positions, including some sharply hostile to Marxism: a theorist may hold that doctrine underdetermines outcomes and conclude that judges should therefore exercise their freedom in a liberal direction, a conservative direction, a libertarian direction, or a feminist direction, and leading figures of the movement held various of these positions and argued among themselves accordingly. The movement's internal life was defined by that plurality. It contained Marxists, and it contained scholars for whom Marxism was one more grand theory to be dissolved by the same scepticism they applied to doctrine, and the second group was not a fringe.

The dispute was not only retrospective. Participants contested the school's identity while it flourished, with some pressing it toward a Marxist programme of class analysis and others resisting exactly that destination as a relapse into the determinism the movement existed to refuse. To file the school as Marxist is therefore to take sides in an argument the participants never settled, and to take the side the school's own centre of gravity mostly rejected. The accurate statement is the one this article's brief prescribes: the relation is partial and disputed. Some personnel, some vocabulary, and one overlapping suspicion of legal neutrality travelled from the Marxist tradition into the movement. The tradition's core commitments, to historical materialism as an account of social change, to class as the central axis of analysis, and to the critique of political economy as the foundation beneath the critique of law, did not travel with them, and several of the movement's signature methods, drawn from literary theory and post-structural philosophy, were regarded by Marxist legal theorists of the same years as solvents of the very determinacy their own tradition required. The two camps criticised each other in print, sometimes warmly, and the warmth should not be mistaken for merger.

### What did Critical Legal Studies take from Marxism, and what did it leave?

It took the suspicion that legal neutrality is a presentation rather than a fact, and a generation of personnel formed on the left. It left the critique of political economy, the theory of class, and any account of why legal materials bend in patterned directions rather than randomly, which is why its indeterminacy thesis floats free of the tradition's explanations.

The correction has a practical use for students, because the filing error propagates into examinations. A candidate who attributes the indeterminacy thesis to Marxist legal theory will be marked as confused by an examiner who knows the field, and a candidate who attributes the five positions of this article to Critical Legal Studies commits the mirror error. The clean separation runs as follows. Marxist theories of law, in all five positions, are explanatory theories about a social institution: they say what law is, where its content comes from, and why it takes its form, and they stake those claims on evidence about classes, exchange, legitimation, and the archive. Critical Legal Studies, at its centre, is a sceptical practice directed at legal reasoning itself, closer in spirit to a permanent cross-examination of doctrine than to a theory of society. Where the two meet, in the refusal to accept neutrality at face value, they meet as allies of occasion. Allies of occasion are not the same army, and the grid of this article has no sixth row.

## Can Socialist Law Exist? The Question the Tradition Cannot Put Down

Every position surveyed so far describes law in class societies that exist or have existed. The tradition's most divisive question concerns a society that has never fully existed, and it divides the positions because each gives a different answer. Can there be socialist law? The question sounds scholastic until its stakes are seen. If law is in its essence the form of commodity relations, or the instrument of a ruling class, or the ideology of abstract equality, then a society that has abolished classes and commodity production has abolished the conditions of law itself, and what remains is administration: the coordination of production and distribution by technical rules, revocable plans, and direct decision, without rights against the community, without courts standing between the individual and the collective, without the whole apparatus of the legal form. If, on the other hand, the historian's conclusion is sound, and the constraint of power by rules is an unqualified human good, then a society that abolished law in the name of liberation would have abolished one of liberation's own achievements, and the withering away of the legal form would be a loss to be mourned and guarded against rather than a destiny to be welcomed. The following article in this series takes up the debate in its own right; the task here is to map the positions and the experience that shadows them.

From the commodity-form account comes the sternest answer, and its author drew it without flinching. Law, on that account, is the form taken by relations between commodity owners, and it attains its classical purity where exchange is general. A society organising production directly, without exchange of products between independent owners, has no more use for the legal form than it has for the money form, and for the same reason: both are mediations required only where producers are separate. What replaces them is not a higher law but a different kind of regulation altogether, technical rather than juridical, concerned with the efficient arrangement of things rather than with the rights of persons. The author pressed the point into criminal law, arguing that guilt, desert, and proportionate punishment are themselves legal categories saturated with the logic of equivalence, the eye for the eye rationalised into the year for the offence, and that a mature society would replace retribution with measures of social defence and treatment as unembarrassed as medicine. The argument's consistency is its glory and its danger. It predicts that attempts to build a specifically socialist legality, neither bourgeois nor merely transitional, are attempts to square a circle, and that such attempts will either reproduce the bourgeois form under new management or dissolve legality into administrative discretion while keeping its name.

Experience supplied the debate with its grim laboratory. The revolutionary state whose jurists first posed the question passed through phases that read like the theory's table of contents performed by actors who had not read it: an early period of revolutionary legality improvised from decrees and tribunals, a theoretical flowering in which the commodity-form account was the orthodoxy of the law faculties, a reversal in which that account was denounced and its author destroyed, and a consolidation in which a doctrine of socialist legality was proclaimed, complete with codes, courts, and a jurisprudence of stability, at exactly the moment when the state's actual practice of power was least constrained by any of them. Writers across the spectrum have mined that sequence for morals. Defenders of the commodity-form account note that the proclaimed socialist legality functioned, in the period of its proclamation, as legitimation for a power that observed it selectively, which is what their theory would predict of legal forms retained after their social basis has been declared abolished: the form persists as ideology precisely because its substance has departed. Critics of the account reply that the sequence shows the opposite, that the catastrophe of the period was not too much legality but too little, that where power's subjection to rules collapsed entirely, what followed was not the free administration of things but the unfree administration of persons by terror, and that Thompson's sentence reads, against that background, less like a liberal lapse than like a prophecy addressed to his own tradition.

Between the stern answer and the historian's warning, intermediate positions have been constructed, and a survey should note their shapes. One holds that law under socialism is transitional in a precise sense: so long as distribution proceeds by contribution and scarcity persists, the equal right Marx anatomised in the Gotha critique persists with its defects, a bourgeois right surviving into the society that has abolished the bourgeoisie, and only abundance retires it. That position has the authority of Marx's own text behind it, since the Gotha notes describe exactly such a first phase in which equal right, still stamped with the birthmarks of the old society, governs distribution and is recognised as defective but unavoidable. Another holds that the question miscasts law as a single thing, and that its components have different futures: the law of property in productive assets withers with private ownership, while law as protection of the person against power, law as procedure, law as the individual's enforceable claim to be treated according to announced rules, might be retained and deepened by a society that had read Thompson and believed him. A third position, held quietly by many practising lawyers of the left, declines the theoretical question entirely and treats legality as a technology of coordination and protection whose class character lies in its content and administration rather than in its existence, so that asking whether socialism can have law is like asking whether it can have accounting. The debate among these answers is unresolved, and the tradition's honesty consists in leaving it visibly unresolved rather than papering it with a formula.

### What would technical administration lack that law provides?

Standing. Under administration, a person affected by a decision petitions for its reconsideration as a favour or an error. Under law, the same person holds a right, can demand the decision be justified against announced rules, and can win against the deciding authority in a forum the authority does not control. The whole debate turns on whether that standing is a bourgeois ornament or a human achievement.

The question also has a forward edge that students should notice, because it disciplines speculation about any future order, not only socialist ones. Whenever a reformer proposes to replace adjudication with management, rights with outcomes, or courts with algorithms of allocation, the old debate reactivates in new dress. The commodity-form tradition asks what social relations the new arrangement presupposes and whether the legal form is being abolished or merely hidden. The historian's tradition asks what constraint on power is being surrendered and who will discover the surrender first. Neither question is reactionary, and neither is optional. A legal theory that cannot ask them about arrangements it favours has confused loyalty with analysis, which is the confusion the best writers in this field, of every position, spent their careers refusing.

## Law and the State: Entangled Institutions, Distinct Questions

Readers of this series meet the theory of law alongside the theory of the state, and the two are so often taught as one subject that separating them requires a deliberate effort worth making at the start of serious study. The state, in the tradition's vocabulary, names the organised apparatus of rule over a territory: the bodies that tax, conscript, police, and administer, together with the claim that this apparatus stands above society as its neutral arbiter. Law names something both narrower and wider. Narrower, because much of what the state does, war, diplomacy, macroeconomic management, proceeds with law at its elbow rather than in its essence. Wider, because law overflows the state on every side: custom that no legislature enacted, obligations recognised between merchants across borders, the internal norms of churches, guilds, and associations that govern their members with sanctions of their own. A theory of the state can be complete while saying little about doctrine, precedent, or the legal subject. A theory of law must eventually explain why commands arrive dressed as rights, why coercion insists on wearing the costume of adjudication, and why the ruled so often answer power in power's own legal vocabulary. Those are questions the state literature borrows rather than answers, which is why the series gives the legal field its own articles and why this one links outward rather than summarising its neighbours.

The entanglement is nevertheless real, and three connections matter most. The first is enforcement. Modern law's claim to be law, as distinct from custom or morality, rests substantially on the organised force that stands behind its judgments, and that force is the state's. The tradition's theorists of the state supply the analysis of that force: its organisation as a public power separate from the population, its financing from taxation, its professionalisation into bodies of armed and administrative specialists. The legal theorist takes that analysis as a premise and asks the further question the premise raises. Why does a power possessed of overwhelming force bother with forms at all? Why try the poacher when he could be flogged by order, why hear the employer's defence when his works could be closed by decree? The answers the five positions give to that further question are the five positions, which is another way of seeing that the theory of law begins exactly where the theory of the state stops: at the mystery of force consenting to procedure.

The second connection runs through personnel and formation. Judges, advocates, and administrators are produced by institutions, universities, professional bodies, systems of appointment and promotion, that belong to the sociology of the state as much as to the theory of law. The instrumental account drew its plausibility largely from this terrain, pointing to the class origins and social connections of legal personnel as the transmission mechanism between economic power and legal outcome. The structural account answered on the same terrain that formation cuts both ways: the professional socialisation that binds a judge to the outlook of his class also binds him to the outlook of his profession, with its internal standards of craft, its horror of the visibly corrupt decision, its memory of precedent as a collective inheritance no individual member may squander. Which binding dominates in a given case is an empirical question, and the mature literature treats it as one, studying careers, appointments, and decision patterns rather than trading intuitions about robes and accents.

The third connection is fiscal, and it is the least discussed in student writing. Legal orders cost money, and states that cannot fund courts, registries, and enforcement discover that their law becomes a literature rather than a practice, admired in codes and absent in provinces. Conversely, the dependence of the state on revenue drawn from a privately organised economy is the deepest of the structural constraints surveyed earlier in this article, since it disciplines governments without a single instruction being issued: a legal order whose innovations frighten the sources of its own funding learns caution through its budget. Theorists of law who ignore the fiscal connection produce accounts of doctrine floating free of the conditions that pay for doctrine's enforcement, and the tradition at its best has refused that float. Marx's own historical writings are full of budgets, loans, and tax revolts doing the quiet work beneath constitutional drama, and the legal articles of this series follow him in treating the material maintenance of institutions as part of their theory rather than as an administrator's footnote.

### Why do legal orders survive the states that made them?

Because their content is anchored in the organisation of production and exchange, which changes more slowly than governments, and because their personnel, procedures, and records constitute a going concern that new rulers need intact. Conquerors and revolutionaries alike discover that abolishing the registry is easier proclaimed than survived.

A final distinction protects students from a confusion the examination hall punishes reliably. Relative autonomy, in the legal literature, is claimed at two levels that must not be merged. There is the autonomy of the state from the dominant class, the structuralist thesis about the political apparatus as a whole. There is also the autonomy of law from the state, the capacity of legal institutions, doctrines, and professions to develop by their own logic and on occasion to obstruct the very government that funds them. The second does not follow from the first, and evidence for one is not evidence for the other. A state might be thoroughly the committee of its possessing class while its courts, jealous of craft and precedent, frustrate that committee's impatient members weekly. A state might enjoy wide autonomy from every social class while its legal order remains a pure transmission belt for ministerial instruction. The grid's structural row asserts the first autonomy and implies a measure of the second; the historical row documents the second in a period when the first was close to nonexistent, which is precisely what made the documentation explosive. Keeping the two apart converts many apparently contradictory findings in the literature into compatible ones, and the conversion is among the most useful services a survey can perform.

## Equal Right Revisited: What the Gotha Argument Proves and What It Does Not

The short passage on equal right in the critique of the German workers' programme, treated in full in the guide to the [Critique of the Gotha Programme](/2013/09/01/critique-of-the-gotha-programme-guide/), carry more weight in legal theory than their length suggests, and they repay a second visit from the legal side because later positions have claimed them for incompatible purposes. The argument's skeleton is simple. A distributor who measures every recipient by a single standard, labour contributed, treats as equal what is not equal. One worker is stronger, quicker, or more skilled; another carries the labour of a household in his evenings; a third's contribution is consumed by dependants the measure refuses to count. The standard, precisely because it is applied impartially, converts those unmeasured differences into unequal shares of the social product, and the resulting inequality is not a malfunction of the right but its product. Marx's phrase for the arrangement, a right of inequality in content despite equality in form, is the most exact sentence the tradition produced on the subject, and it has the rare property of being quotable by reformers and revolutionaries alike, since it identifies a defect that no improvement in administration can remove while the form persists.

The commodity-form theorists claimed the passage as a proof of their derivation. Equal right, on their reading, is the exchange relation generalised: as commodities meet in the market as equivalents regardless of the labour and need embodied in them, so persons meet in distribution as equivalents regardless of everything that makes them different, and the blindness is the same blindness in both spheres. The ideological theorists claimed the same passage for the opposite emphasis. For them its lesson concerns consciousness: a distribution experienced as equal right will be experienced as just, and grievances against its outcomes will present themselves as grievances against persons, the lazy colleague, the fortunate rival, rather than against the measure. The historian's tradition makes a third claim on the passage, quieter than the others. Equal right, however defective as a principle of distribution, is a principle, and principles bind their administrators: a distributor bound to an announced measure can be caught departing from it, and the history of movements is full of the measure's victims turning the measure against the measurers, demanding the equality that was proclaimed and auditing the proclamation against the practice. All three claims on the passage are textually defensible. The passage supports each because it is genuinely about three things at once: a form, a belief, and a standard by which power may be judged. Students who learn to hold the three readings apart, and to say which writer is using which, have acquired the article's central skill in its purest exercise.

What the argument does not prove is equally important, since the passage is routinely overdrawn in polemic. It does not prove that equal right is worthless, and its author says the opposite in terms: the defective right of the first phase is recognised as unavoidable, an advance on the arrangements it replaces, to be transcended by abundance rather than denounced by rhetoric. It does not prove that unequal outcomes under an equal rule are the fault of the rule's administrators, since the whole point is that honest administration reproduces them. It does not prove that a better rule, sensitive to need, could simply be substituted by decision, since the passage's horizon is a society whose productive development has made measurement by contribution obsolete, a material condition and not a drafting choice. And it does not prove that rights in general stand condemned, because the argument's target is a specific right, the right of the contributor to an equivalent share, in a specific setting, the distribution of consumption goods in a society emerging from capitalism. Polemic that conscripts the passage against rights as such, against legal equality before tribunals, against protections of the person, is conscripting it beyond its orders, and the conscription should be refused wherever it is met, in the seminar room as firmly as in print.

### How can an equal rule produce unequal results without anyone cheating?

By measuring one dimension of people and ignoring the rest. A rule that pays strictly by hours worked is honestly administered and perfectly equal as a rule, yet it yields unequal lives because strength, health, household burdens, and dependants differ outside the dimension measured. The inequality is manufactured by the measure's blindness, not by corruption in its application.

There is a pedagogical consequence worth stating, because the Gotha passage is where many students first feel the tradition's analytical power and also where they first learn its characteristic temptation. The power lies in the relocation of explanation: an outcome that common sense attributes to character or corruption is shown to follow from a form, impersonally, reproducibly, in the absence of villains. The temptation is to perform the same relocation everywhere, to dissolve every legal phenomenon into its form and to treat the search for agents, decisions, and responsibilities as naivety. The other positions exist as correctives to that temptation. Sometimes there are villains. Sometimes a measure was written by identifiable men to injure identifiable others, and the instrumental account of it is simply true. A mature reader of the Gotha argument holds it as one instrument among five, reaches for it when the phenomenon is a form reproducing inequality under honest administration, and leaves it in the case when the phenomenon is a committee drafting a statute against the deer of the poor. Knowing which phenomenon one is looking at is most of what expertise in this field consists in, and it cannot be replaced by loyalty to any single row of the grid.

## Enforcement and Discretion: Where Legal Orders Actually Live

A statute book is a legal order's autobiography, written in the genre it prefers, and every position in this article has at some point mistaken the autobiography for the life. The historical account's enduring methodological lesson is that law lives in enforcement: in the decisions to prosecute or overlook, to charge under the severe statute or the mild one, to pursue the debt or let it sleep, to inspect the premises or to accept the assurance that nothing has changed. Between the rule as written and the rule as lived there stretches a territory of discretion, and the class character of a legal order, where it has one, is often located in that territory rather than in the text. Two offences of comparable harm may inhabit different enforcement worlds, one patrolled by dedicated agencies with powers of entry and presumptions in their favour, the other left to the initiative of victims who must fund their own proceedings and prove their own cases. The texts of the two offences may be models of impartial drafting. The lived law differs as much as if the texts had been written by enemies, and a theory that reads only texts will certify the order as neutral while the territory does the work.

Discretion is not an aberration that better drafting could abolish, and the tradition's better writers have understood its structural sources. Rules are general and cases are particular, so application always contains a residue of judgment. Resources are finite, so enforcement is always selection. Penalties severe enough to deter are often too severe to impose on the sympathetic defendant, so mercy and evasion grow in the shadow of severity, as the eighteenth-century record showed in its pardons and its juries acquitting against the evidence to avoid a hanging for a theft of trifles. Each source of discretion is also a point of entry for the pressures the theories describe. Wealth enters, because the capacity to litigate, to delay, to appeal, and to be believed is unevenly distributed. Connection enters, because discretion exercised by persons is exercised among persons known and unknown. Ideology enters, because the discretionary judgment of what is serious, what is credible, and what is a genuine misunderstanding rather than a fraud is formed inside the same representations the ideological account analyses. A complete theory of law needs an account of discretion as careful as its account of rules, and the five positions divide instructively over where they locate it. Instrumentalism treats discretion as the class's flexible grip, tightening and loosening by instruction. Structuralism treats it as the system's shock absorber, bending so the frame need not break. The historical account treats it as the medium in which legitimation is manufactured, through mercies staged and severities suspended. The differences are testable in principle against enforcement records, and the gradual digitisation of such records is quietly making this the most empirical frontier of the whole field.

### Why do severe penalties sometimes produce more acquittals?

Because the human beings who operate the system refuse its arithmetic. Jurors who judge a punishment grotesquely disproportionate to an offence acquit against the evidence, prosecutors charge lesser offences than the facts support, and judges strain interpretation toward mercy. Severity thereby manufactures the discretion that softens it, a regularity the eighteenth-century record displays on page after page.

The study of enforcement also corrects a bias built into the other positions, their tendency to treat the legal order as something done by the powerful to the powerless, full stop. The archive of enforcement shows the powerless as practitioners. They inform and withhold information, they swear complaints and withdraw them, they use the threat of prosecution as a bargaining counter in disputes the law never hears, they learn which forms protect a customary claim and recite them with precision. The criminal law of a property regime was, in the hands of village prosecutors, frequently a weapon of neighbour against neighbour in quarrels about grazing, gleaning, and debt that the statute's authors never imagined. None of this softens the order's class character at the level of its design. It complicates the level of its use, and a theory that cannot accommodate the complication will be perpetually surprised by the record. The mature position, toward which the field's centre of gravity has moved, treats law as a resource unevenly distributed and a language unevenly mastered, whose rules are written disproportionately by one side and spoken, sometimes fluently and to effect, by both. That sentence is not a compromise manufactured for peace. It is what the evidence, read across enough cases, actually supports, and each of the five positions contributes one clause of it.

## Rights: The Tradition's Divided Inheritance

No topic divides the tradition against itself more reliably than rights, and the division is not between the tradition and its enemies but down its own middle, where it has run since the founder's lifetime. On one side stands the critique. Rights, in the critical register, are the entitlements of the abstract person, and the abstraction is the problem: the rights-bearer of legal theory is a monad, separate, self-owning, related to others by contract and collision, and a society that organises itself around such bearers consecrates separation as freedom. The young Marx's essay on the Jewish question, his first extended treatment of political emancipation, reads the celebrated rights of man in exactly this register, as the rights of egoistic man, of man withdrawn into his private interest, with political community demoted to a means for protecting the withdrawal. The Gotha argument adds the distributive count: even where rights are genuine, their equal application manufactures inequality. On the other side stands the inheritance the historian defended. Rights are also, in the register of experience, the only language in which the powerless have consistently been able to say no to power and make the refusal stick: the right to combine, to publish, to assemble, to a hearing before punishment, to the vote itself, each denounced in its winning as a bourgeois formality and each missed, when lost, with a desperation that gives the denunciation the lie. Movements do not bleed for formalities. They bleed for rights, and a theory that cannot explain why they are right to do so has failed a test more important than consistency.

The sophisticated positions in the field are attempts to hold both registers without flattening either, and they deserve to be distinguished from the lazy synthesis that calls rights both good and bad and stops. The commodity-form resolution holds them by location: rights are real and valuable in the sphere of exchange and protection, and the critique is aimed not at the rights but at the social relations that confine human freedom to that sphere's shape. The ideological account holds them by function: rights legitimate the order that grants them, and simultaneously arm the granted, and which effect dominates is a historical question varying with the balance of forces. The historical account holds them by record: whatever rights have been in theory, in practice they have repeatedly been turned against their granters, and a form that can be so turned is not adequately described as its granters' instrument. The structural account holds them by system: an order serving capital as a whole requires rights that bind particular capitals, and the requirement is not a trick but a load-bearing feature, so that defending rights can be, objectively, a defence of the conditions under which challenges to capital remain possible at all. Each resolution costs something. Each preserves something the slogan destroys. A student who can state the four resolutions and their costs understands the rights debate better than most of its loud participants.

### Are human rights a bourgeois invention, on this account?

They are a historical product of the age that generalised commodity exchange and political emancipation together, and the tradition's critical side insists on the genealogy. Its other side insists that genealogy is not refutation: institutions outgrow their origins, and protections won under one order may be among the few possessions the next order should refuse to surrender.

There is a further subtlety in the founder's own treatment that students often miss, and recovering it improves examination answers measurably. The essay on political emancipation distinguishes between the rights of man, the entitlements of the separate individual, and the rights of the citizen, the entitlements of the participant in collective self-government, and its criticism falls unevenly: the citizen's rights, to assemble, to vote, to take part in the community's direction, are treated as the higher form tragically subordinated to the lower, since the political community exists, in the order criticised, chiefly to guarantee the private rights beneath it. The mature tradition's ambivalence maps closely onto that early distinction. Its suspicion has always fallen most heavily on rights as shields of property and privacy, its respect has always gathered around rights as instruments of collective action and protection against the state, and the conflation of the two families under one word is responsible for a large share of the debate's confusion. Disaggregation is the remedy the survey prescribes. Ask of any right three questions: whose separation does it protect, whose combination does it enable, and against whom may it be enforced? The answers place the right within the tradition's divided inheritance far more accurately than any general verdict on rights as such, and they convert a sterile quarrel about a word into an analysis of institutions, which is where the tradition has always done its best work.

## Property: The Legal Form at the Centre of the Field

Every legal order has a centre of gravity, and in the orders this tradition studies the centre is property: not one doctrine among others but the institution around which the law of persons, obligations, and offences silently organises itself. The point is worth a section because students often file property as a topic within private law, a chapter between contract and tort, and thereby miss the tradition's claim, which is architectural. Property, in the analysis the founder inherited and transformed, is not a relation between a person and a thing. It is a relation between persons with respect to things, a socially recognised power to exclude, enforced in the last instance by the organised force of the community, and experienced by everyone else as the shape of the world they must navigate. The fence is a legal proposition before it is a physical fact, and the proposition's enforcement is what makes the field beyond it a different country for the person who owns it and the person who gathers fallen wood at its edge. That the tradition's founding legal episode concerned exactly such an edge, the boundary between the forest owner's timber and the poor's customary gathering, is a symmetry the literature has often noted: the whole theory is latent in the question of who may pick up a fallen branch.

The five positions read property differently, and the readings organise much of their disagreement elsewhere. The instrumental account reads the law of property as the owners' charter, its doctrines of title, trespass, and theft written by the possessing for the possessing, and finds its evidence in the historical record of enclosure and criminalisation. The structural account reads the same doctrines as the operating system of a market order, valuable to capital in general precisely because they protect the small holder against the great, the creditor against the debtor's friends in office, the purchaser in good faith against the whole world, and so make holdings calculable and transferable across generations. For the commodity-form account, property is the presupposition of exchange itself: before commodities can meet in the market, their guardians must be recognised as owners entitled to alienate them, so that the legal institution of property is not a superstructure on exchange but one of its internal conditions, which is why the derivation treats the owner and the legal subject as a single figure viewed from two sides. The ideological account reads property's presentation: the representation of a historically specific and violently established distribution as the natural order of mine and thine, protected by a criminal law whose severities fall on the offences of the propertyless while the offences of the propertied are renamed as sharp practice, misfortune, or the regrettable externality of enterprise. The historical account reads the record of property's enforcement and finds the by now familiar complication, that the forms protecting the great also protected the small with real effect, that customary claims framed as rights sometimes defeated titled claims in open proceeding, and that the propertied themselves were repeatedly indicted under their own statutes by prosecutors they could not control.

### Why does the tradition treat theft as a privileged window onto law?

Because theft is where the legal order shows its priorities without embarrassment. The definition of what counts as taking, the grading of takings by the thing taken and the person taking, the energy of enforcement compared with the energy spent on harms that lack a property form: the pattern discloses what the order exists to protect more candidly than any preamble.

One analytical dividend of placing property at the centre is that it explains the otherwise puzzling intensity of legal conflict over apparently minor objects: a right of way, a boundary hedge, a customary portion of a harvest, a fishery, a well. Where property is the organising institution, a minor object may carry a major principle, since conceding the custom in one parish establishes the premise for the next, and both sides in such disputes have historically understood the stakes with a clarity that puts later commentators to shame. The tradition's historians earned their authority partly by taking those small wars seriously as constitutional events in miniature. The dividend extends to the present structure of doctrine, if the phrase may be allowed without any contemporary reference: the law's solicitude for the security of transactions, its doctrines protecting the bona fide purchaser, its reluctance to unwind completed exchanges, all express the same architectural priority, the requirement that holdings be reliable enough to circulate. A student who sees the priority can predict doctrines not yet studied, which is the mark of a theory doing work rather than a list being memorised.

## Contract and the Labour Contract: Freedom's Double Face

If property is the centre of the legal order the tradition studies, contract is its characteristic ceremony, the ritual by which separate owners meet, agree, and bind themselves, and the labour contract is the ceremony on which the entire critique turns. The founder's analysis of that contract remains the most influential application of legal-form thinking ever written, and it requires no commitment to the rest of his system to feel its force. In the market, buyer and seller of labour power meet as the legal order requires: free, equal, independent, each disposing only of his own, bound by no status and coerced by no superior. The contract they conclude is, as a legal act, impeccable, and the tradition has never claimed otherwise. Its claim concerns what the impeccable act sets in motion. The equality of the parties is an equality of the moment of exchange. What is exchanged is unlike any other commodity, since its use is labour itself, delivered over time under the buyer's direction, so that the parties who parted as equals at the signing meet again inside production as commander and commanded, and the surplus the commander appropriates arises not from cheating the contract but from fulfilling it. The legal form is not the mask of the exploitation. It is its honest doorway, and the analysis's coldness on this point is what separates the mature critique from every denunciation of fraud.

The five positions distribute themselves across this analysis in instructive ways. The derivation claims it as a home fixture, since the labour contract is the derivation's crucial case: the worker must be a legal subject, owner of his labour power as of a thing, for the system to function, and his formal freedom is a requirement of production rather than an ornament of ideology. The ideological account claims the same fixture for the opposite shelf, pointing to the experience of the contract as chosen, the signature as self-expression, the wage as the price one agreed, and the consequent privatisation of outcomes already surveyed in these pages. The instrumental account has the least to say here, and its silence is diagnostic: no committee drafted the labour contract's form, no conspiracy maintains it, and an account of law as class instrument must treat the central legal relation of capitalism as an exception to itself. The structural account contributes the observation that the form's generality is load-bearing, that a labour market in which employers could rewrite terms by status or favour would collapse into the inefficiencies of personal dependence, so that the equality of the contract is among the constraints capital in general imposes on capitalists in particular. The historical account, true to its method, asks how the ceremony was actually experienced and used, and finds workers invoking the contract's terms against masters with tactical sophistication, treating the form's promises as claims to be collected rather than as veils to be pierced.

### Why does the critique insist the labour contract is not a fraud?

Because fraud would be easier. A fraudulent system could be reformed by honesty, whereas a system that exploits through the faithful performance of free and equal contracts must be criticised at the level of the relations the contracts presuppose: who owns the means of production, and who owns nothing but the capacity to work.

The double face of contractual freedom generalises beyond labour, and the generalisation is a reliable generator of insight across the legal field. Wherever parties of radically unequal endowment meet under a form that registers only their agreement, the form will certify as free a transaction whose terms were dictated by the endowments. The tenant and the housing market, the borrower and the lender, the small supplier and the great purchaser, each pair performs the ceremony and each performance is legally genuine, and the critique's question is never whether the ceremony occurred but what the parties brought to it and what the ceremony is designed not to see. Legal reform traditions have repeatedly tried to make the form see more, through doctrines of unconscionability, implied terms, and protections that cannot be signed away, and the tradition surveyed here reads those reforms with its usual division: as concessions extracted by organisation, as the general interest disciplining the particular, as legitimation renewing its lease, or as the form's own logic maturing. The readings compete. The phenomenon they compete to explain, the double face, is the founder's permanent bequest to legal thought, acknowledged even by scholars who reject every other element of his system, and it is the surest sign that the theory of law in this tradition is a contribution to jurisprudence and not merely a politics with footnotes.

## Legal Consciousness: How Ordinary People Hold the Law in Mind

Between the legal order as institutions describe it and the legal order as it governs conduct there lies the territory of legal consciousness: the stock of beliefs, expectations, and habits through which ordinary people understand what law is, what it is for, and what someone like them may expect from it or do with it. The ideological account made this territory theoretically central, since legitimation is not an abstraction but a state of millions of minds, and the empirical study of the territory has become one of the liveliest meeting points between the tradition surveyed here and the sociology of law practised without its commitments. The findings of that study complicate every simple position in this article, which is precisely their value. People do not hold one attitude toward legality. They hold repertoires, and they switch among them by situation, by audience, and by need, with a fluency that no theory of indoctrination predicted and no theory of resistance can claim as its own.

Research in this vein distinguishes, in one influential vocabulary, three stances toward the legal order that recur across interviews and observations. In the first, people stand before the law as before a majestic and distant power, awesome, rule-bound, and not to be trifled with; they comply because the order is the order, and they experience its interventions as weather, to be endured rather than negotiated. In the second, people stand with the law as players of a game whose rules they respect instrumentally, hiring expertise, calculating odds, exploiting procedure, and accepting losses as the cost of play; the order is neither sacred nor sinister but a field of strategy. In the third, people stand against the law in small and large resistances, evading, foot-dragging, exploiting its contradictions, and treating its demands as obstacles placed by a power whose legitimacy they concede only in public. The interest of the scheme for this article is that all three stances appear in the same neighbourhoods, the same workplaces, and frequently the same person within a single week. The formed subject of the ideological account, the tactical citizen of the historical account, and the sceptic of the critical schools are not three populations. They are three registers of one population, and a theory of law adequate to the evidence must explain the switching rather than elect one register as the truth of the others.

Class enters the territory not as a slogan but as a distribution of experiences. The stances people take correlate with the legal order's biography in their lives: whether its appearances have been as protector or as pursuer, whether its promises made to people like them were kept, whether its costs fell on them as plaintiffs with resources or as defendants without. A population whose encounters with legality run through eviction, debt collection, benefit sanction, and police attention builds a different common sense about law from a population whose encounters run through conveyancing, probate, and the enforcement of contracts in its favour, and the difference requires no conspiracy and no indoctrination to explain itself. Experience is the teacher, and the lessons are rational readings of the evidence available. This is the point at which the empirical study of legal consciousness returns a verdict favourable to the tradition's deepest instinct, its insistence that consciousness is formed by material life rather than by sermons. Where the study corrects the tradition is in its portrait of the formed consciousness itself: not a veil but a toolkit, not a stupor but a set of working theories, held with varying confidence and revised under pressure of events. The governed of this literature are nobody's dopes. They are analysts with incomplete data, which is also a fair description of the theorists.

### What does legal consciousness research do to the ideological account?

It disciplines it. Legitimation survives as a phenomenon, since most people, most of the time, treat the legal order as binding and its outcomes as entitled to respect. Indoctrination does not survive, since the same people prove capable of irony, strategy, and contempt in the next breath. The account emerges narrower and stronger: an explanation of default respect, not of total formation.

There is a methodological moral here that the article collects for its later section on evidence. The study of legal consciousness proceeds by listening: by interviews, observation, and the patient reconstruction of how people narrate their encounters with landlords, officials, tribunals, and police. Its findings were invisible to the methods the five positions traditionally used, which read texts, structures, and archives but rarely asked the governed what they thought they were doing. The archive of the historical account came closest, since petitions and testimony preserve the voices of the dead in something like their own words, and it is no accident that the historian's position anticipated the toolkit portrait better than its rivals: his poachers and prosecutors alike behave in the record as strategists within a form they partly believed. A survey that ends by recommending one book above others for method could do worse than recommend the habit his work embodies, of treating every theoretical claim in this field as a hostage to the next body of evidence about what people actually did and said. The positions that survive in the literature are, increasingly, the ones that learned to issue such hostages and redeem them.

## The Legal Professions: A Layer Between Power and Form

No account of law is complete that treats rules as self-executing and officials as transparent, and the tradition's scattered remarks on the legal professions deserve gathering into the section they never received from the founder. Lawyers, judges, notaries, and administrators form a distinct social layer with interests of its own: in the complexity that makes its expertise necessary, in the procedures that constitute its craft, in the monopolies that protect its market, and in the prestige that attaches to mastery of an arcane and consequential language. That layer stands between economic power and legal form, and neither end of the relation fully controls it. Owners must hire it and speak through it, translating their purposes into pleadings whose grammar they do not command. The state must staff its courts from it and accept its professional judgments about what may credibly be argued and decided. The layer's relative independence is therefore not an idealist postulate but a feature of the division of labour, and several positions in the grid can be restated as claims about how far that independence extends and what it is used for.

The instrumental account reads the professions sociologically, through recruitment and reward. Where advocates and judges are drawn from the possessing classes, educated at their expense, and advanced by the approval of their peers and patrons, the account expects the layer's output to lean toward the interests that formed it, without any instruction needing to pass. The reading has evidence: the social composition of benches and bars across many societies and periods is skewed in ways no theory of open competition explains, and the great legal careers have historically been made in the service of great properties. The structural account answers that the same layer is the custodian of the system's generality, since its professional capital is invested in the predictability of doctrine, and a judge who sells a decision sells the asset on which his whole caste's position rests. On this reading the professions are the mechanism by which the general interest of capital polices its particular members, the priesthood that keeps the temple's rules against the temple's richest worshippers when the rules require it. The ideological account reads the layer as the clergy of the civil religion in a stricter sense: the producers of the language in which inequality is redescribed as order, the authors of the fictions, presumptions, and forms of words by which a distribution achieved by history is presented as the conclusion of reason. The historical account, characteristically, reads the record and finds the layer divided against itself: prosecutors zealous beyond their instructions and prosecutors subverting them, judges who stretched doctrine to hang and judges who stretched it to save, advocates who discovered in the forms resources for clients the forms were built to crush. The record supports a layer with real discretion and divided loyalties, which is to say it supports treating the professions as a variable in the theory rather than as a constant.

One phenomenon belongs to this section because every position must explain it and none explains it cheaply: the recurrent appearance of lawyers on the side of the powerless, not as isolated philanthropists but as an organised tendency within the profession, staffing defence committees, inventing doctrines for the protection of combination and expression, and litigating the forms against their makers across generations. Instrumentalism must call this the system's safety valve or the caste's bad conscience, explanations that sit poorly with the careers risked and sometimes destroyed in such work. The structural account fares better, since a profession invested in generality will generate members who take generality seriously against the powerful as a matter of craft honour. The legitimation constraint fares best of all, since the phenomenon is one of its mechanisms in person: the constraint on power is not an abstraction but is operated, case by case, by professionals whose formation taught them that the forms are real and who act on the teaching at cost to themselves. A theory of law that cannot see those professionals except as camouflage has failed the archive's plainest lesson, and the failure was among the historian's explicit charges against the reflex he attacked. His own formation as a historian of movements had taught him what the movements owed to such lawyers, and his conclusion about the rule of law can be read as the theoretical generalisation of a debt he had watched being paid.

### Why do legal professions resist being treated as a transmission belt?

Because their asset is credibility. A profession whose outputs could be ordered would lose the market value of its expertise and the public value of its certifications simultaneously, so its members defend the autonomy of their craft from purchasers and governments alike, not from virtue alone but from an accurate sense of where their position comes from.

The professions also supply the field's most accessible evidence about legal change, since doctrine moves when the layer's mind moves, and the layer's mind moves through education, appointment, generational succession, and the slow migration of respectable opinion. Revolutions in legal thought rarely begin in legislatures. They begin in lecture halls and law reviews, in the changing sense of what a competent lawyer may say without embarrassment, and by the time a doctrine is enacted its intellectual victory is usually a generation old. The tradition's theorists, attentive to the base beneath the superstructure, have sometimes underrated this autonomous temporality of professional thought, treating ideas within the layer as mere reflections of interests outside it. The record resists the treatment. Doctrines have conquered professions against the resistance of the interests they inconvenienced, carried by the internal logic of the materials and the ambitions of their carriers, and any materialism that cannot accommodate the relative autonomy of an idea's career inside a profession will be surprised by legal history as reliably as instrumentalism is surprised by factory acts. The mature synthesis the field approaches treats the professions as a relatively autonomous layer with class-linked recruitment, system-serving functions, craft-driven constraints, and genuine internal dynamics: four claims, one from each of four positions, all true at once, which is perhaps the pattern toward which this entire survey has been converging.

## Precedent and Legal Change: How Doctrine Moves Without Permission

A legal order must do two things that pull against each other: it must be stable enough that holdings, contracts, and plans can rely on it, and it must change as the society beneath it changes, or its rules become a museum that practice routes around. The mechanism by which the orders this article studies manage the contradiction is precedent, the discipline by which past decisions bind and guide future ones, and the tradition's analysis of that mechanism is a small masterpiece of its method at its best, since precedent exhibits in miniature every feature the five positions attribute to the whole. Formally, precedent is the rule of law's purest expression: like cases decided alike, the powerful litigant bound by the decision that bound the weak, the judge's present will subordinated to the accumulated reason of the institution. Functionally, precedent is a ratchet and a reservoir: it stores the victories of past struggles in a form that later litigants may draw on without refighting the original war, and it raises the cost of reversal, since a protection embodied in precedent must be distinguished, narrowed, or openly overruled in the light of day, each operation leaving evidence and inviting resistance. The historian's tradition prizes exactly this storage property. Movements, on its telling, should attend to doctrine as a treasury in which their gains are banked, vulnerable to inflation and to theft but not to mere forgetting, and the movements that neglect the treasury discover that gains held only in statutes and ministries evaporate with the next turn of politics.

Change within such a system has a characteristic tempo and disguise. Doctrine rarely announces revolution. It distinguishes the inconvenient precedent on facts no previous court thought material, extends a principle one analogy beyond its last resting place, revives a dissent and discovers it was right all along, or finds that a rule framed for one world has quietly acquired a new meaning in another. The tradition reads this tempo two ways. Critically, the disguise of continuity serves legitimation: change that arrives dressed as discovery provokes less resistance than change confessed as choice, and the legal order renews itself while denying that renewal is occurring, preserving the representation of law as found rather than made on which much of its authority rests. Appreciatively, the same disguise is a discipline: because change must argue from the materials, must show its working against the accumulated record, it is slower, more public, and more answerable than change by decree, and those subject to it can plan their resistance in the same language. Both readings are correct, and their conjunction is the field in one institution: a mechanism of legitimation that is simultaneously a mechanism of constraint, the legitimation constraint wearing its working clothes.

### How can precedent bind the powerful if the powerful appoint the judges?

Appointments shape a bench over decades, but the appointee inherits a treasury he did not write and a profession watching how he treats it. Decisions openly purchased or dictated devalue the currency every judge holds, so the institution's members, including the appointed, defend its forms against their appointers often enough to matter.

The tempo of doctrinal change also supplies the structural account with some of its best evidence. When economic organisation transforms, the doctrines built for the older organisation come under strain that no conspiracy organises: new forms of enterprise strain the categories of liability, new forms of property strain the doctrines of title, new concentrations of private power strain the assumption that contract joins independent parties. The strain registers first as anomalies, hard cases, dissents, and academic unease, and the system's response, spread across years and tribunals, has the appearance of adaptation without an adapter, which is what a structural explanation predicts and an instrumental one cannot easily describe. Nobody ordered the adaptation. Interests lobbied at its margins and sometimes captured its details. But the direction of travel, toward doctrines under which the new organisation of production could operate predictably, was set by the structure's requirements rather than by any committee's will. A student who learns to narrate one doctrinal transformation in this double register, interests at the margins and structure in the direction, has learned the article's method in its most transferable form, since the same double register organises the best writing in the neighbouring fields of legal history and the sociology of doctrine.

## The Wood-Gathering Debates as a Method in Miniature

The episode with which Marx's engagement began, the criminalising of wood-gathering, has been used in this article as biography. It deserves a second use, as method, because the debate it provoked contains in miniature every analytical move the later tradition would formalise, and because students who work the episode slowly acquire the field's instincts faster than students who begin with the abstractions. The scene is a legislature debating whether a customary practice of the poor, the collection of dead wood from forests, should be reclassified as theft. Around the question gather the elements the five positions would later separate. There is a material interest, plain and quantifiable, since fallen wood has value and its appropriation by gatherers is a loss to owners who might sell it. There is a customary right, old, unwritten, embedded in the practice of generations, whose existence the proposed measure does not deny but proposes to override. There is a rhetoric of universality, in which the owners' interest is presented as the interest of order, of property as such, of the forest's own preservation. And there is the young journalist, watching the elements combine, drawing conclusions that point in two directions at once.

The first conclusion he drew was the instrumental one, and the episode is the strongest evidence that instrumentalism is not a vulgar error imposed on the tradition from outside but one of its founding insights. The deputies in the debate did not hide their standpoint. They argued as owners, voted as owners, and produced a measure whose content can be read off the interest it served with unusual directness. Where a legislature is socially continuous with a propertied class, debating the criminalisation of that class's losses, the transmission belt the instrumental account describes is not a metaphor but a report. The tradition's error was never to have seen the belt. The error was to universalise the sighting, to carry a mechanism documented in a particular institutional configuration, a narrow franchise, a legislature of notables, a press still fighting for its own freedom, into a universal theory of legal orders as such, including orders whose configurations differ in every relevant particular. Method consists largely in knowing the range of one's evidence, and the wood debates teach the range by exhibiting both the mechanism and its conditions in a single scene.

The second conclusion the young writer drew has been less noticed and is the more prophetic. He defended the customary right of the poor not as a policy preference but as a right, arguing from the nature of the material: wood that the tree has shed stands in a different relation to the owner from wood the tree still grows, and the gatherer's claim rests on that difference as title rests on its documents. The argument is jurisprudential, conducted inside the forms, granting the legal order its own premises and showing that the premises condemn the measure. Here in embryo is the whole later practice of immanent critique, the method by which the tradition's best legal writing operates: not denouncing the forms from outside as frauds, but taking them more seriously than their custodians do, and indicting the practice by the standard the practice proclaims. The historian's conclusion of 1975 is this method grown old and armed with an archive. The legitimation constraint is this method stated as a mechanism. Even the ideological account, at its most sophisticated, is this method inverted, asking what the forms' proclamation does to those who hear it. A student who sees the continuity between the young journalist holding a legislature to the idea of right and the old historian holding a tradition to the evidence of its own archive has understood something about the field that no list of positions conveys: that its characteristic gesture, across a century and a half and five incompatible theories, is to refuse the choice between taking law seriously and taking power seriously, and to insist, against both the cynical and the naive, on doing both at once.

### What makes immanent critique different from denunciation?

Denunciation judges a legal order by a standard imported from outside and can therefore be shrugged off as mere opposition. Immanent critique judges the order by the standard it proclaims for itself, its own universality, its own equality, its own proclaimed rules, so that every conviction it secures is secured on evidence the order itself supplied.

There is a biographical coda the literature rightly treasures. The founder, looking back on the episode from the vantage of his mature work, described the wood debates as the occasion on which he first found himself in the embarrassing position of having to discuss material interests, and credited the embarrassment with sending him to political economy, from which everything else followed. The remark licenses a pleasing symmetry: the tradition that produced the most ambitious theory of law's place in society began when its founder watched a legislature redefine a customary right and felt the inadequacy of his philosophical vocabulary to say what was happening. Marxist legal theory is thus not an application of a finished system to a new domain. It is the scar tissue of the system's founding wound, and the five positions are five ways of remembering what the wound taught: that interests dress as universals, that forms bind their makers, that equality of measure manufactures inequality of result, that exchange shapes the subject who exchanges, and that the archive, consulted honestly, will embarrass every one of those lessons in turn.

## Criminal Law: The Sharp End Where Theories Are Tested

The criminal law is where the tradition's theories meet their hardest evidence, and the series treats its systematic study separately in the companion article on Marxist criminology. A section belongs here nonetheless, because no survey of the positions is complete without the domain in which each of them was first tested and in which several of them were born. Criminal law is the legal order's sharp end: the point at which its coercion stops being a background condition and becomes the explicit content of the proceeding, where the state names an act, proves it, and answers it with suffering deliberately inflicted. Theories of law that work comfortably for contract and property often stumble here, and the stumbles are informative. The criminal process also concentrates the field's oldest empirical regularities. The populations subjected to criminal sanction are drawn overwhelmingly from the propertyless in every society the tradition has studied. The offences defined with the greatest precision and pursued with the greatest energy cluster around property. The harms characteristic of the propertied, fraud at scale, the knowing sale of danger, the theft of wages, inhabit the criminal law's margins or live outside it altogether in the gentler jurisdictions of regulation and civil liability. Any theory of law must say why, and the five positions say it differently.

The instrumental account has its home ground here and says it most simply: the criminal law is the owners' fence with the state's dogs behind it, defined by the class whose goods need guarding and enforced in proportion to the threat the propertyless pose to the distribution of holdings. The founding archive supports the reading at the level of definition, since the statute the historian studied loaded the book with new capital crimes against property at one stroke, while offences by the powerful against the persons of the poor remained, in the main, matters for lesser instruments. The structural account refines the reading by asking what criminal law does for the order as a whole: it individuates disorder, converting the collective grievances of a class into the personal guilt of offenders, each tried alone, each sentenced for his own act, each removed as an individual pathology from a social scene the trial is forbidden to examine. Punishment, on this reading, is a machine for preventing the criminal question from becoming the social question, and its rituals of individual responsibility are the ideological account's home ground as much as the structuralist's, since the guilty subject produced by the trial is the free and equal subject of the labour market viewed from the other side: the same abstract person, bearer of a will, author of his acts, owner of their consequences. Completing the circuit, the commodity-form account contributes its most disturbing suggestion, that punishment itself is shaped by the exchange form, that the tariff of penalties, so many years for so much harm, is the equivalence principle wearing a wig, the lex talionis rationalised into a price list in which liberty is the currency and every offence has its rate. Readers who find the suggestion extravagant are invited to explain, from their own theory, why proportionality feels so natural.

Against this formidable convergence the historical account enters its familiar complication, and the complication is nowhere more important than here. The criminal process, of all legal processes, is the one whose forms most visibly constrained its operators in the record: the requirement of a defined offence excluded retrospective invention, the requirement of proof acquitted the suspected, the publicity of proceedings created a theatre the authorities did not always control, in which the condemned could speak, crowds could judge the judges, and executions intended as lessons in terror could convert, in the watching, into lessons in sympathy or disgust. The scaffold was the state's loudest megaphone and its least reliable one. Nor is the complication merely antiquarian. The protections of criminal procedure, the presumption of innocence, the exclusion of evidence improperly obtained, the right to silence and to counsel, are the points at which the legitimation constraint bites hardest in any period, because there the legal order most openly restrains its own coercive arm in the name of its proclaimed rules, and every proposal to strip them away for efficiency's sake reactivates the old argument in its purest form. The tradition's divided inheritance is nowhere more practical: the same movement may denounce the criminal law as the fence of property on Monday and defend its procedures as the patrimony of the powerless on Tuesday, and be right both times, about different aspects of the same institution.

### Why does the tradition study punishment to understand law as a whole?

Because punishment strips away the mediations that hide law's nature elsewhere. Contract presents coercion as agreement and property presents it as order, but punishment presents it as itself, deliberately inflicted suffering justified by rules, so the questions every position must answer, whose rules, what form, what belief, what constraint, appear there without costume.

A closing observation for this section concerns proportion and attention. Public argument about law is disproportionately argument about crime, and the tradition has a standing explanation for the disproportion that students should be able to state. Its trials are the legal order's most legible theatre, the place where its values are enacted in narrative form with villains, victims, and verdicts, and attention lavished there is attention diverted from the civil and administrative territories where the distribution of wealth and security is actually decided in bulk. The trial of the thief is a spectacle. The law of landlord and tenant is a climate. Societies argue about the spectacle and live in the climate, and a theory of law that follows public attention will mistake the legal order's shop window for its warehouse. The five positions, whatever their differences, agree in redirecting attention to the warehouse: to property, contract, enforcement, and form. That shared redirection, more than any single doctrine, is the tradition's methodological signature, and it is why its students keep being surprised by how much of the legal world becomes visible once the shop window is left behind.

## Law in Revolution and Transition: The Forms Under Maximum Strain

Legal orders are built for ordinary times, and their theories are usually tested there, but the tradition this article surveys was born in an age of revolutions and has always reserved a special attention for the moments when legal orders are made, unmade, and remade under maximum strain. Those moments are theoretically precious because they strip the institution to its working parts. In stable periods, the respective contributions of force, form, belief, and structure to a legal order's authority are blended beyond separation. In revolutionary periods they come apart in the hand. A new power proclaims a new order and discovers which parts of the old one it cannot do without: the registries, the procedures, the professional habits, the population's expectations of what a claim, a title, and a hearing are. A counter-revolution restores the forms and discovers which of the revolution's innovations have sunk too deep into practice to be dredged out. The five positions each predict a different pattern for such moments, and the historical record of transitions is therefore one of the few arenas in which the positions can be scored against each other with something approaching fairness.

The instrumental account predicts that revolutionary law will be candid: that a class seizing power will legislate its interests without the veils that stable orders require, and that the veils will return as the new order consolidates and needs legitimation rather than victory. There is evidence for the prediction in the early decrees of more than one revolution, documents of a directness that stable legislatures never permit themselves, announcing expropriations and abolitions in the grammar of command. The structural account predicts the opposite emphasis: that even revolutionary powers will find their legal freedom narrow, since the work of coordination, the expectations of producers, and the need for predictable dealing with a still-private economy will force the new order back toward the forms it denounced, and that the return will be misread by the revolution's enemies as hypocrisy when it is in fact structure reasserting itself through whoever holds office. The record supplies that pattern too, in the familiar sequence by which revolutionary governments, having proclaimed the end of the old legality, proceed to rebuild codes, courts, and a legal profession on lines their predecessors would recognise, usually within a generation and usually while insisting that the resemblance is superficial. A third prediction, the commodity-form account's, concerns timing: legal form revives where and when exchange revives, and withers where direct allocation genuinely replaces the market, a prediction precise enough to be checked against the sequence of economic policies in the transitions the account's author lived through, and one that his contemporaries checked it against, in his lifetime, with his participation.

The ideological and historical accounts divide over the population's role in transitions. The ideological reading expects the new order to invest enormously in the manufacture of new subjects, through oaths, rituals, renamed institutions, and the pedagogy of show proceedings, since a revolution that cannot change what people believe law is has changed only its management. The historical reading expects the population to be less malleable and more interesting: to carry the old order's legal common sense into the new one's tribunals, to frame grievances in whatever vocabulary the moment licenses while pursuing ends the vocabulary's authors never intended, and to defend, sometimes at real risk, procedural expectations the new power finds inconvenient, because those expectations are among the few assets ordinary people hold against any power at all. The record of transitions, read in bulk, honours the second expectation more often than the first, and the honouring is continuous with the historian's general conclusion: the constraint that legal forms impose is not a gift of the powerful but a deposit left by generations of the governed, who learned the forms' uses and refuse, in each new dispensation, to surrender the knowledge.

### What do revolutions reveal about law that stable periods hide?

They reveal the proportions. In stability, force, form, belief, and economic structure blend into a single authority that feels natural. In revolution they separate: decrees go unenforced for want of belief, old forms persist for want of replacements, and everyone can watch, in real time, which components of legality were load-bearing.

One further lesson of transitions deserves record because it disciplines a temptation common to all five positions, the temptation to treat legal orders as expressions of a single social logic. Transitions show legal orders as accumulations: layers of form deposited by successive regimes, each layer partly erasing and partly preserving its predecessors, so that the working law of any real society is an archaeology rather than an architecture. A doctrine of property from one dispensation serves a republic that overthrew it; a procedure designed for terror is operated by reformers for protection; a code written for an empire governs a democracy that defines itself against the empire's memory. The tradition's founder, whose historical writings are full of such survivals, of old forms inhabited by new contents and new forms haunted by old ones, would have found nothing surprising in the archaeology. His epigones have sometimes been more systematic than he was, and the transitions correct them. A legal order is not the legal expression of a mode of production in the way a shadow is the expression of a body. It is a built environment, constructed over centuries by identifiable hands for identifiable purposes, inherited by successors who did not choose it, and altered by them under constraints the building itself imposes. That sentence could stand as the historical position's whole creed, and the other four positions are best honoured by being read as accounts of particular storeys of the building rather than as rival descriptions of the whole.

## Grading the Five Positions Against the Legitimation Constraint

A survey that merely lists positions has done half its work. The other half is to say how the positions stand in relation to one another when a single test is applied to all of them, and this article has been constructing such a test since its centre: the legitimation constraint, the claim that legal orders earn their ideological authority only by deciding, with credible regularity, against the interests they serve, and must therefore sometimes rule against the powerful. Used as a grading instrument, the constraint sorts the five positions by how much of the phenomenon each can accommodate without strain. The exercise is this article's own and is offered as a reading device rather than as a verdict of the field, since the field has reached no verdict and the honest survey says so. But the exercise has a discipline of its own: a grading that cannot state what would reverse it is an opinion in costume, and the statement of the reversal condition is therefore given at this section's end, in the form this series requires of every verdict it prints.

The instrumental account grades lowest against the test, for the reason developed in its own section: it must treat every decision against the powerful as theatre, and the theatre reading becomes less credible the more the decisions cost, the longer they stand, and the more their beneficiaries organise to defend them as real. An account whose central cases require a standing conspiracy of self-deception among the powerful themselves, who must pretend to lose what they secretly win, has mistaken a sometimes-true description of tactics for a theory of the institution. The ideological account grades higher, since the constraint is built from its materials: legitimation is its home concept, and the discovery that legitimation has a price in real constraint is a development of the account rather than a refutation. Its grade is capped by its weakness on agency, its tendency to describe belief as manufactured where the record shows it negotiated. The structural account grades high on the existence of constraint and lower on its mechanism: relative autonomy predicts that legal institutions will defy particular interests, and the constraint specifies one reason they must, but the structural version of the reason runs through the requirements of accumulation, while the constraint's version runs through the requirements of belief, and the two reasons diverge in cases where accumulation would be better served by a frightened population than by a confident one. Terror, after all, also reproduces labour and protects property, in the short run at ruinous long-run cost, and the structural account needs the constraint's audience, a public whose belief is being husbanded, to explain why the ruinous short run is so rarely chosen by orders that could choose it.

The commodity-form account grades in a different dimension, since the constraint is a claim about operation and the account is a claim about form, and orthogonal claims cannot refute each other. Where they meet, the meeting is productive: the form explains why legitimation takes the shape it does, why credibility must be established through the spectacle of equal subjects treated equally, rather than through the spectacle of a wise ruler's benevolence, which was the legitimation technology of orders built on status. A patrimonial power legitimates itself by mercy and magnificence. A legal order of abstract persons legitimates itself by consistency, and the constraint is the price of consistency in a world where the powerful are also persons before the form. The historical account grades highest against its own test, which is no surprise and no scandal, since the test was generalised from its evidence. The interesting question is whether the grade survives export, whether the constraint operates in legal orders unlike the founding archive's, and that question returns the survey to the empirical frontier where the field's future work lies: in enforcement records, in studies of legal consciousness, in the comparative history of orders that chose terror and orders that chose law, and in the measurement, still rudimentary, of how much losing legitimacy actually requires.

### What would defeat the legitimation constraint argument?

Evidence that legal systems retain their legitimating force while deciding consistently in favour of the powerful. If populations continued to regard an order as legitimate, and complied with it as legitimate, across generations in which its decisions never once embarrassed a substantial interest, the mechanism this article has described would be shown not to operate, and the historian's conclusion would fall with it.

That defeater deserves a paragraph of its own, because stating it changes the status of the whole argument. The legitimation constraint, with its defeater attached, is an empirically checkable claim about how legitimacy is sustained, and it invites a specific research programme: assemble the record of consequential decisions in a legal order, classify them by the interests they favoured, measure the population's belief in the order's legitimacy by the instruments the students of legal consciousness have built, and test whether belief survives the absence of consequential losses for the powerful. The claim's defenders predict it does not. Its critics, to defeat it, must produce the contrary case, an order believed legitimate by its subjects while visibly never ruling against its masters, sustained across a period long enough for the pattern to be common knowledge. Near cases exist and are argued over: orders in which legitimation rests on charisma, confession, or dynasty rather than on legal consistency may sustain belief without the constraint, which is why the claim should be scoped, as its best defenders scope it, to orders that legitimate themselves through law. Within that scope the defeater stands unmet in the literature this survey knows, and the argument survives with its quantity question open: the mechanism established, its price unmeasured, its unqualified good still contested by honourable opponents whose strongest objection has been given its full force in these pages.

## How Neighbouring Disciplines Use These Arguments

An idea's travels are evidence about the idea, and the arguments surveyed in this article have travelled far beyond the tradition that produced them, into disciplines whose practitioners often hold no brief for Marxism and sometimes do not recognise the provenance of the tools they are using. The relative autonomy argument is now standard furniture in legal theory and the sociology of the state, where the claim that institutions develop logics irreducible to the intentions of their creators is debated on evidence rather than on allegiance. The legitimation constraint circulates in socio-legal studies and legal history as a working hypothesis about why procedural fairness matters to compliance, a literature that arrived at the historian's neighbourhood by its own road, through experiments and surveys on why people obey the law, and found his formulation waiting there with an archive attached. The commodity-form argument has enjoyed the most surprising afterlife, in international legal scholarship, where a field organised around formally equal sovereigns, binding themselves by agreements no superior enforces, discovered in the derivation a ready-made account of why its law takes the form it does: exchange among abstract equals, all the way up. Scholars of that field have built on the argument extensively, some in explicit allegiance and others in a spirit of cheerful expropriation, and the traffic has improved the argument by testing it against material its author never considered.

The travels cut in two directions, and a survey should record both. Export validates: an argument that does work for scholars with no motive to flatter it has passed a test that internal applause cannot simulate, and the five positions are, by that test, in better health than their reputation in some quarters suggests. Export also dilutes. Relative autonomy, in transit, often loses its second word's discipline and becomes a bare assertion that institutions matter, which nobody denies. The legitimation constraint, in transit, sheds its class analysis and becomes a management insight about the reputational costs of unfair process, true as far as it goes and silent about the distribution the fairness legitimates. The commodity form, in transit, risks becoming a metaphor, applied to any setting where abstraction appears, with the derivation's rigour, its grounding in the analysis of value, left at the border. Students who meet these arguments in neighbouring literatures should therefore perform the return journey: read the exported version for its evidence, then read the original for its edge, and notice what the edge was for. The tradition's versions are distinguished from their exports not by loyalty tests but by a question the exports tend to drop: the question of what the legal order's operations do to the distribution of wealth and power over time, and for whom the whole arrangement, fairness included, finally works.

### Why does international law attract the commodity-form argument so strongly?

Because its subjects are the derivation's figures at full scale: formally free and equal persons, the sovereign states, meeting without a superior, alienating and acquiring by agreement, bound only by what they have consented to. A theory built to explain why exchange produces legal subjects finds, in that field, a world built entirely of them.

There is a lesson about the sociology of knowledge folded into these travels, and it connects this article to the series' treatment of how ideas move between traditions. Arguments migrate when they solve problems their new hosts already have, and they are rebranded in migration because hosts prefer tools without instructions about the politics of their manufacture. The result is a curious double life, in which a tradition may be pronounced dead in the journals of opinion while its concepts do daily work in the journals of record. Marxist legal theory's actual condition resembles that double life more than it resembles either the triumph its partisans claim or the obituary its opponents publish. Its questions, whose interests, what form, whose belief, what constraint, are asked wherever law is studied seriously, by scholars who would give five different answers to the question of where the questions came from. A survey's final duty to its subject is accuracy about that condition: neither a mausoleum nor a throne room, but a workshop whose tools are in use on every bench in the building, some of them stamped with the maker's name and some with the name long since filed off.

## Misreadings to Correct: Five Errors That Survive Every Textbook

Fields accumulate characteristic errors the way harbours accumulate wrecks, at the same navigational points, for reasons built into the chart. The Marxist theory of law has five that this article has been correcting as it went and now collects in one place, since a reader who can name the errors is inoculated against most of the bad literature in the field, including some of the famous literature. Each error survives because it flatters a constituency: examiners who want a doctrine to test, polemicists who want an opponent to hit, and sympathisers who want a slogan to carry. Accuracy is nobody's constituency, which is why surveys exist.

The first error is the belief that Marxism has one theory of law, of which the others are footnotes or heresies. The record shows a founder who left fragments pointing in several directions and a succession of builders who completed the structure incompatibly. Treating the field as a doctrine produces the characteristic bad examination answer, in which positions are blended into a soup, instrumental about content, structural about institutions, ideological about belief, with no awareness that the ingredients quarrel. The remedy is the grid: five positions, five failure modes, and the discipline of attributing each claim to its row before evaluating it.

The second error is the promotion of the instrumental reading into the tradition's official position, usually by writers outside the tradition who find it the easiest to refute and by writers inside it who find it the easiest to chant. Within the literature, instrumentalism in its pure form has been the position most often attacked by Marxists themselves, from the structuralists who built an alternative to house its failures to the historian who called its reflex a disarmament of the movements. A critic who refutes instrumentalism has refuted the field's weakest row and should be told so; a sympathiser who defends it has chosen the same row and merits the same message in a gentler tone and at greater length.

The third error concerns the historian's conclusion, and it comes in two mirror forms. Admirers outside the tradition quote the unqualified human good as a recantation, a Marxist's deathbed conversion to liberalism, and he rejected the description angrily in print, insisting that the verdict grew out of his own school of history honestly applied to records its guardians had left unread. Critics inside the tradition accept the same description and draw the opposite moral, treating the passage as apostasy to be quarantined. Both readings agree on the false premise that the conclusion cannot be a Marxist conclusion, and the premise is the error. A materialism that cannot be corrected by material evidence is a catechism, and the passage's real provocation is its insistence that the archive outranks the catechism, an insistence whose Marxist pedigree, through the founder's own practice of revising theory against the record, is impeccable.

The fourth error is a confusion about relative autonomy, committed by friends and enemies in alternate paragraphs: the reading of autonomy as independence. Relative autonomy asserts a bounded distance, within which legal institutions follow their own logic and may defy any particular interest, while the bounds themselves are set by the order the institutions serve. Reading it as independence converts the structural position into a liberal pluralism with Marxist vocabulary, and reading it as nothing, as a verbal fig leaf over determination, converts it back into instrumentalism with extra steps. The position lives in the interval, and the interval is where the evidence is: institutions that lose for the powerful regularly and for the system never, over long periods, without anyone issuing instructions in either direction. Explaining that pattern is the position's whole assignment, and dismissing the pattern because the explanation is difficult is not criticism but abstention.

### Did Marx write a treatise on law that scholars have neglected?

He did not, and the fifth error is the supposition that somewhere in the corpus a systematic jurisprudence waits to be summarised. What exists is journalism, one celebrated manifesto sentence, some marginal notes on a party programme, and scattered remarks across the historical and economic writings. The tradition built its theories from those materials, which is why the theories are plural and why appeals to the founder settle less than partisans expect.

The fifth error, stated in the answer above, deserves its consequence drawn out. Because there is no treatise, the field's authority structure differs from fields built on a systematic text. Arguments are won by evidence and construction rather than by exegesis, and the founder's fragments function as charters, licensing enterprises they do not describe. This is why the field's internal debates have the texture of common-law argument, precedent and distinction and the distinguishing of precedents, rather than the texture of commentary on a code. It is also why the field remains open in a way that more exegetical traditions are not: a new body of evidence, a newly digitised enforcement archive, a newly studied population of legal consciousness, can rearrange the standings of the five positions without violating any text, because no text governs the rearrangement. Students sometimes experience this openness as a defect, a failure of the tradition to make up its mind. It is better understood as the tradition's fidelity to its own founding embarrassment, the moment when a young journalist discovered that the philosophy he had brought to the forest was not adequate to what the forest showed him, and chose the forest.

## Evidence and Method: How Claims About Law and Class Can Be Tested

A field that began in polemic owes its readers an account of how its claims could be wrong, and this article has attached defeaters to its central argument for that reason. The present section generalises the obligation, surveying the kinds of evidence the five positions can be tested against and the characteristic ways each kind misleads. The survey is offered in a practical spirit. Students are examined on these positions, researchers build on them, and citizens argue with them, and all three activities improve when the question shifts from which position one prefers to what observation would change one's mind. A preference is a biography. An observation is an argument, and the field's best writers, whatever their row in the grid, have been the ones who told their readers what observation they were staking their claims on.

The first kind of evidence is the text: statutes, codes, judgments, the documentary surface of the legal order. Textual evidence is indispensable and treacherous. It shows what the order proclaims, and every position except the most naive requires a distinction between proclamation and practice, since the gap between them is where discretion, ideology, and the legitimation constraint all live. Text misleads in a characteristic direction: it flatters the order's self-image, recording the rule and not the raid, the right and not the queue for its enforcement. The second kind is the record of operation: prosecutions brought and declined, cases won and lost by category of party, sentences imposed and remitted, the archive the historical account taught the field to read. Operational evidence corrects the text and misleads in its own direction, since what was recorded was selected by the recording institution, and the archive of a legal order is itself one of that order's products, preserving what the order found preservable. The historian's craft consists largely in reading such archives against their grain, and his founding study remains the field's masterclass in the technique, extracting from the prosecution records of a terror statute a portrait of the terror's limits that the records were not kept to provide.

The third kind is testimony: what participants said they were doing, in petitions, memoirs, interviews, and the modern instruments of legal consciousness research. Testimony reaches the interior of the legal order as no document can, and misleads as interiors do, through self-justification, faulty memory, and the human talent for narrating strategy as principle. The fourth kind is comparison: the same institution observed across societies whose economic structures differ, or the same society across a transformation of its structure. Comparison is the closest approach the field has to experiment, since it varies the condition the theories treat as causal, and it misleads through the thousand differences that travel with the one under study. The fifth kind is the natural experiment of transition, treated in an earlier section, where revolutions and collapses vary several conditions at once and the theories compete to explain the sequence that follows. No kind suffices alone. The field's convincing arguments are braids, text checked against operation, operation against testimony, testimony against comparison, and the braid is what distinguishes the literature's durable works from its pamphlets, a distinction that survives translation across all five rows of the grid.

### What single research habit most improves work in this field?

Stating, before the evidence is gathered, what result would count against the position being tested, and writing the statement down. The habit converts allegiance into inquiry, exposes in advance which auxiliary stories a theory is permitted to tell, and produces the rarest object in the literature: a partisan argument that its opponents must take seriously.

Two methodological warnings complete the section, each earned from the field's own history of error. The first concerns the selection of cases. Legal history written from celebrated cases is legal history written from the order's exceptions, since cases become celebrated by being unusual, and theories built on them inherit the distortion. The instrumental account's evidence base skews toward statutes of flagrant class content, which exist and matter, while the structural and historical accounts built their cases from the unglamorous bulk of ordinary operation, which is where legal orders spend their lives. A claim about a legal order should be required to say what fraction of the order's business it describes, and claims that cannot say should be read as claims about episodes, valuable and limited. The second warning concerns the direction of causal stories. The field's founding temptation is to read every legal phenomenon as an effect of the economic structure, and its second temptation, in reaction, is to read the structure as an effect of legal arrangements, since property, contract, and the corporation are legal creations without which the economy's characteristic actors could not exist. The mature literature treats the causation as reciprocal and historical: legal forms inherited from one dispensation channel the development of the next, which deposits new forms in its turn, and the interesting questions concern the channels and the deposits rather than the search for a first mover. Students who absorb that reciprocity early are spared the field's most sterile quarrel, and examiners reward the sparing with a reliability that borders on the mechanical.

## How Law Makes Classes: The Constitutive Direction of the Argument

Most of this article has followed the tradition's habitual direction of explanation, from class to law: classes exist, their interests and relations shape the legal order, and the five positions quarrel over the shaping mechanism. A complete survey must also follow the reverse direction, which the tradition's subtler writers have always acknowledged and its popularisers habitually forget. Law does not merely reflect classes formed elsewhere. It participates in forming them, by defining the categories within which people hold, work, combine, and inherit, and by distributing the capacities that class position consists in. The point deserves development because it corrects the picture of a one-way determination that hostile critics attribute to the whole tradition and then refute with satisfaction. The founder's own analysis contains the reverse direction at its core. The free labourer, the figure on whom the entire critique of political economy turns, is a legal production: a person whom the law recognises as owner of his labour power, unbound by status from any master, and equally recognised as owner of nothing else with which to live. That double freedom, to sell and to starve, was not found in nature. It was manufactured across centuries by legal acts, the dissolution of customary tenures, the criminalisation of vagabondage, the abolition of the guilds and of settlement, the long legislative labour by which a population of customary holders was converted into a population of legal persons with nothing to sell but themselves. Class, in that founding story, is not the premise of the legal order. It is among its products.

The constitutive direction operates continuously and not only in founding moments. The law of the business corporation constitutes a form of collective capital that no individual owner's will could sustain, with perpetual life, limited liability, and a legal personality that survives its members, and the class structure of modern societies is unthinkable without that constitution. The law of combination constitutes the working class as an actor, since a class that may not lawfully assemble, bargain, and withhold its labour in concert exists as a statistic but not as a force, and the long legal struggle over combination was, in the tradition's own understanding, a struggle over whether the class would be permitted to constitute itself. Inheritance law constitutes the intergenerational dimension of class, deciding what portion of an advantage survives its earner and in what form, and thereby setting the gradient that each new generation must climb or descend. Even the categories of the census and the tax code do quiet constitutive work, teaching populations to understand their positions in the vocabulary the forms supply. None of this reverses the tradition's materialism into idealism. The legal acts that constituted classes were themselves moved by interests, pressures, and structures of the kinds the five positions describe. The claim is about sequence and reciprocity: interests act through forms, forms consolidate into positions, positions generate new interests, and the analyst who freezes the circuit at any single point and calls that point the cause has mistaken a cross-section for an explanation.

### If law helps make classes, does the base and superstructure picture collapse?

It matures. The picture survives as a claim about the long-run constraints that economic organisation imposes on legal orders, and it sheds the mechanical reading in which legal forms are epiphenomenal shadows. The tradition's best writers never held the mechanical reading; its persistence in textbooks is the fifth misreading's cousin, and it is corrected the same way, by reading what the writers wrote.

The constitutive direction also reframes the article's central controversy in a way that may reconcile its parties more than either expected. If legal forms help constitute the classes whose interests they are said to serve, then the forms are not external tools that a pre-existing class picks up and might set down. They are part of the class's own constitution, and an attack on the forms is partly an attack on the constituted order of positions itself. The historian's defence of the rule of law acquires, from this angle, an additional weight he did not himself emphasise: the forms he defended were among the materials from which the subordinate classes had built their own organisations, claims, and self-understanding, so that surrendering the forms would not merely disarm a tactic but dissolve a patrimony of collective identity. Conversely, the critics' insistence that the forms entrench inequality acquires the same additional weight, since entrenchment, on the constitutive reading, is not a surface effect but a rebuilding of the positions themselves in each generation. The controversy is deepened rather than settled by the reframing, which is the usual sign that a reframing is honest. What the survey can report is that the field's centre of gravity now rests with the reciprocal picture, and that the five positions are increasingly read, by their own defenders, as accounts of phases in a circuit rather than as rival engines bolted to the same chassis.

## The Limits of Law: What Legality Cannot Do

A survey written in sympathy with its subject's critical tradition should end its analytical work by turning that tradition's favourite question on the legal order's admirers, including its own historian. If law is a real constraint on power and a real achievement of civilisation, what are its limits? What can legality not do, however perfectly it operates according to its own rules? The question matters because the tradition's contribution to legal thought is finally a doctrine of limits: of the limits of formal equality in a world of unequal endowment, of the limits of rights in a world whose production relations they presuppose, and of the limits of reform conducted entirely within the forms. Stating those limits precisely, without sliding into the cynicism the historian refuted, is the settled view toward which the whole article has been travelling, and it is the position a reader should carry away if only one is to be carried.

The first limit is distributive. A legal order operating with perfect integrity distributes according to its rules, and its rules take the existing distribution of endowments as their input. Legality can guarantee that the game is honestly refereed. It cannot, by its own operation, equalise the players, because equalisation is a political act that must be decided and imposed, usually against the resistance of those the existing distribution favours, and then defended by the legal order as one content among others. Confusing the referee's honesty with the game's fairness is the ideological error in its most respectable form, the form it takes among people who would never defend a corrupt tribunal. The Gotha argument is the tradition's permanent correction of the confusion, and it applies with full force to orders whose procedures are beyond reproach. Indeed it applies most instructively there, since a corrupt order's inequalities can be blamed on the corruption, while a clean order's inequalities must be blamed on the structure, which is where the tradition has consistently located them.

The second limit is constitutive, in the sense the previous section developed. Legal forms presuppose the social relations they regulate, and reforms that leave the relations untouched discover that the forms regenerate the old outcomes through new channels. Protect the tenant without touching the ownership of housing, and the protection is priced into the rent or evaded through the contract's redesign. Equalise the parties' rights in the labour contract without touching the ownership of the means of production, and the equality presides over the same appropriation with better manners. The tradition's name for the pattern is the reproduction of the relations through their forms, and its policy moral is austere: legal reform is real, its gains are real, and its gains are systematically bounded by the relations it declines to alter, so that a politics of legal reform alone is a politics that has decided, whether it knows it or not, to manage the consequences of a structure rather than to change it. The historian's tradition answers, correctly, that managing consequences is not nothing, that the difference between managed and unmanaged consequences is measured in lives, and that the austerity of the moral has been used to excuse the abandonment of winnable protections. Both are right. The survey's task is to keep both in view, since the field's wisdom consists precisely in the refusal to drop either.

The third limit is epistemic, and it is the one surveys most often skip. Legal process knows the world through categories, and its categories are built for its purposes: act and omission, intention and negligence, person and thing, mine and thine. Harms that fit the categories are visible to the order and may be remedied. Harms that do not fit, the slow violence of an economic arrangement, the injury distributed across a population so thinly that no plaintiff can show it, the wrong done by everyone following the rules, are invisible to the order not through corruption but through constitution, the way an eye is blind to what it has no organ to register. Much of the tradition's work can be reread as an attempt to build organs for those harms: to make the structure itself a cognisable object, first in theory and then, haltingly, in doctrine. The attempt's partial successes, doctrines that aggregate small harms into actionable wholes, liabilities that attach to arrangements rather than to intentions, are among the legal order's genuine developments, and the tradition may claim a share of the credit for the questions that produced them, having asked, earlier and more insistently than its rivals, why the warehouse of harm stood outside the shop window of crime.

### Does recognising law's limits require abandoning law reform?

The tradition's mature answer is no, and the answer distinguishes it from both its cynical and its naive caricatures. Limits define what reform can achieve and what only structural change can; they do not devalue what reform achieves within them. A protection that saves real people from real evictions is not refuted by the observation that it leaves ownership untouched.

With the limits stated, the survey can permit itself one synthetic sentence about the whole field, of the kind its five positions would each qualify and none, perhaps, would wholly refuse. Law, in the societies this tradition studies, is the form in which class power becomes durable, general, and partly self-restraining: durable, because forms outlive their makers and store the outcomes of struggles; general, because its universality is the condition of the order it serves; self-restraining, because the generality and the durability together bind the powerful to a measure of their own proclaimed rules, and the measure, though unmeasured still in quantity, is the most consequential unknown in the study of legal orders. The sentence is offered as a summary of the article and as a target for its readers, who are invited to improve it by the only method the field respects, which is to take it to evidence it has not yet met.

## Legal Language: The Order's Second Architecture

Every legal order is built twice, once in institutions and once in language, and the second building constrains the first in ways the tradition has studied under the heading of ideology but rarely under the heading of words themselves. Legal language is a technical dialect with unusual properties. It preserves archaic forms long after the vernacular has abandoned them, borrows prestige from dead languages, multiplies synonyms where the vernacular economises, and prefers the passive construction at exactly the points where an agent might otherwise have to be named. These are not ornaments or accidents. Each property does work. Archaism lends the order the appearance of antiquity, and antiquity lends authority, since a rule that has always been spoken in the same words presents itself as a feature of the world rather than as a decision somebody made. The passive construction performs the order's favourite magic trick, the disappearance of the actor: property is protected, sentences are served, possession is taken, and the grammar declines to say by whom, so that coercion arrives in the language as weather arrives, authorless and therefore unanswerable. The technical vocabulary polices the boundary of the profession, since a dialect that takes years to acquire cannot be spoken by the laity, and the laity's dependence on translators is a material fact about access to justice before it is a fact about style.

The tradition's analysts of ideology have drawn the obvious conclusion and some less obvious ones. The obvious conclusion is that legal language mystifies, wrapping decisions in a fog of precedent and Latin so that the decided-against experience the outcome as the conclusion of a reasoning process too deep for them to follow, and therefore as legitimate. The less obvious conclusion concerns the language's effect on its speakers. Officials who must justify coercion in the dialect find their justifications shaped by what the dialect can say. Reasons that cannot be phrased in the vocabulary of doctrine cannot easily be acted on, and reasons the vocabulary supplies ready-made, precedent, proportionality, the reasonable person, acquire a momentum of their own, carrying decisions in directions no one chose. The language is thus one of the mechanisms of relative autonomy, a technology by which the order's past speaks in its present, and the historian's archive shows the mechanism working in both directions: prosecutors trapped by the words of the statutes they invoked, unable to reach conduct the words did not cover, and defendants saved by definitions drafted for other purposes in other centuries. Words bind. That is not a metaphor in this field but a summary of evidence.

### Why does legal language resist plain speech reforms?

Partly from professional self-interest, since obscurity is a monopoly's fence. Partly from a genuine functional need, since terms of art compress doctrines that plain words would have to re-argue in every case. And partly because the language's strangeness is itself a legitimation device, marking the order's utterances as belonging to a realm where ordinary standards of sense are suspended.

There is a countervailing tradition inside the legal order that the survey should honour, the plain speech of the jury charge, the summing-up addressed to laypeople, the moments when the order must translate itself or lose its audience. Those moments are among the legitimation constraint's natural habitats. An order that must explain itself to the governed, in words the governed command, accepts a discipline that pure expertise escapes, and the history of legal reform is partly the history of the translation zone expanding: pleadings simplified, judgments written for the parties rather than for the profession, reasons given where silence once sufficed. The tradition reads the expansion with its usual double vision, as legitimation renewing itself by intelligibility and as a genuine transfer of power to those who can now follow, check, and answer. Both readings are available because both are true in different proportions in different episodes, and the proportions, once again, are an empirical matter that slogans cannot settle. What the survey insists on is the premise the whole section rests on: that the language of a legal order is not its clothing but part of its skeleton, and that a theory of law which treats words as transparent will misunderstand the institution as surely as a theory which treats them as everything.

## Legal Education: Where the Order Reproduces Its Mind

If the professions are the legal order's custodians, legal education is the workshop in which custodians are made, and the tradition's scattered attention to that workshop deserves a section of its own, since education is where the order's common sense is installed before it can be examined. The student of law does not primarily learn rules, which change, nor primarily learn doctrines, which date. The student learns a way of seeing: which facts of a human mess are legally salient and which are noise, which arguments sound in the profession's ear as strong and which as naive, which outcomes feel like justice and which like sentimentality intruding on craft. That formation, sometimes called learning to think like a lawyer, is the ideological account's most concrete mechanism, since it operates not by propaganda but by the patient training of perception, exercise by exercise, until the student spontaneously sees a dispute as the order sees it and experiences the seeing as professional competence rather than as a point of view. The formation's power is proved by its invisibility to the formed, who will describe their perceptions as realism long after they have forgotten the exercises that installed them.

The tradition's critique of the workshop runs on two tracks that should be distinguished. The first is sociological and concerns access: who reaches the workshop at all. Where legal education is expensive, lengthy, and culturally coded, the professions recruit from the classes that can fund the delay and read the codes, and the instrumental account's personnel argument begins its work before a single doctrine is taught. The second track is curricular and concerns content: what the exercises train the eye to see and to miss. A curriculum organised around the appellate case trains the eye on doctrine at its most abstract and on parties at their most equal, two abstractions that flatter the order's self-image. A curriculum organised around the clinic, the advice centre, and the enforcement file trains a different eye, on queues, discretion, and the gap between the rule in the book and the rule at the counter. The two curricula produce different professions from the same raw students, and struggles over legal education, which recur in every generation and are usually reported as quarrels about standards, are on this reading struggles over the order's future common sense, fought in the only place where that common sense is manufactured.

### What does a law school's case method hide from its students?

The docket's unrepresentative character. Appellate cases are the disputes that survived every filter of cost, stamina, and doctrine, argued between parties resourced enough to reach the top of the system, and a profession formed on them mistakes the system's summit for its terrain, underestimating everything that happens at the counter, the station, and the settlement table.

The historian's tradition adds its characteristic amendment to the critique, and the amendment matters for the politics of education. Formation is powerful but never total, because the workshop's raw material arrives with experience the exercises did not install and sometimes refuses the installation at the exact points where experience contradicts it. Every generation of law students contains some who entered to serve the order and left to fight it, carrying the dialect out of the temple and into the campaigns of the order's opponents, and the profession's own heroes include a disproportionate number of such defectors, whose authority in later argument derives precisely from their credentials as initiates. The workshop therefore reproduces the order's mind and, in the same process, trains a fraction of its most effective critics, a dialectic the order cannot escape without ceasing to teach its dialect at all. A survey of legal theory that ignores education leaves its readers unable to explain where theorists come from, including the five positions' own authors, every one of whom was formed in some workshop of the order they criticised and armed by the formation they criticised. The point is not a paradox for its own sake. It is the biographical form of the article's central claim, that the forms bind and arm both sides, in the schoolroom as in the courtroom, and that any politics of law which forgets the arming will be perpetually surprised by its own recruits.

## Personal Status and Dependency: The Law of the Household

Surveys of law and class habitually begin in the market and the factory, and the habit hides a territory in which legal orders have always done some of their deepest constitutive work: the household, and the law of personal status that governs who may marry, who owns what within a marriage, whose labour belongs to whom inside a family, what children owe and are owed, and which dependencies the order recognises as natural and which as legal. The tradition's attention to this territory was uneven in its classical period, a failing its later writers have named plainly, but its method transfers to the territory without alteration, and the transfer yields some of the method's most illuminating results. For the household is where the abstract legal person of the market meets his opposite: a web of statuses in which persons are not free and equal bearers of rights but wives, husbands, children, servants, and dependants, bound by relations the law treats as given by nature or sacrament and therefore as outside the sphere of contract and critique. The coexistence of the two regimes, universal personhood at the market's door and particular status at the household's, is one of the legal order's oldest arrangements, and the boundary between them has been among the most contested frontiers in legal history.

The five positions read the territory in character. The instrumental account finds legislation serving the interests of household heads as owners in miniature, transmitting property across generations and disciplining the labour of wives and children as the factory acts disciplined the labour of strangers. The structural account finds the order reproducing the conditions of labour power itself, since the household is where workers are raised, fed, healed, and returned to the market, largely by unpaid work that the legal order organises by status while declining to count it as work at all. Here the commodity-form account finds its limit and says so: relations inside the household are precisely those that exchange has not generalised, and the survival of status within a society of contract is, for the derivation, an anomaly to be explained by the household's function as the market's necessary outside, the one place where the commodity labour power is produced without being purchased. The ideological account finds the representation of historically specific arrangements as nature, the oldest ideological operation of all, performed in the vocabulary of blood, duty, and the sacred. The historical account finds, in the records of the territory, the familiar pattern of forms turned against their makers: dependants invoking the duties the status imposed on their superiors, wives pleading the obligations of husbands before tribunals that recognised the plea, children of the household using the order's own paternalist premises as claims. The pattern does not redeem the statuses. It demonstrates, in the territory where domination is most intimate, the article's recurring finding that proclaimed obligations bind the proclaimers in some measure, and that the measure is a proper subject of research rather than of rhetoric.

### Why did the classical tradition understate the household?

Because its founding analyses took the market and the workplace as the sites where the society's logic was purest, and treated the household as a residue the logic would eventually dissolve. Later writers, many of them formed by movements the founders did not live to see, showed the residue was a foundation, and corrected the map without abandoning the method that drew it.

The territory also supplies the cleanest illustration in the whole field of the constitutive argument of an earlier section. Class position is inherited before it is earned, and the inheritance is a legal event: the transmission of property, of name, of standing, of the obligation to maintain and the right to be maintained, all occur inside the law of status and succession, long before the heir meets a market. A theory that begins its analysis at the factory gate arrives after the distribution it hopes to explain has already been substantially accomplished in the nursery and the marriage settlement. The tradition's mature form knows this, and its students should be examined on the knowledge: that the legal order's class work is done as much in the registers of birth and marriage as in the statutes of trade, and that the historian's method, patient attention to the records of ordinary life, is nowhere more necessary than in the territory where the records were kept by the order's most respectable clerks and concern its least respectable secret, the manufacture of advantage inside the home.

## Legal Fictions: The Order's Acknowledged Untruths

Every mature legal order maintains a stock of fictions: propositions known by everyone concerned to be false and treated by the process as true, because treating them as true makes the system work. The corporation is a person. The reasonable man acts as no man ever acted. The parties to a standard contract have read and understood its terms. The finder of lost property in one old doctrine holds it against all the world but the true owner, in a ceremony of constructive possession nobody believes describes an event. Legal fictions embarrass the order's official philosophy, which claims that law is reason applied to fact, and their persistence across centuries of embarrassment is a datum the five positions can each claim as corroboration, which makes the fictions a useful test of the positions' discrimination: a theory that explains everything explains the fictions no better than its rivals, while a theory that explains why these fictions, in these places, with these beneficiaries, earns its keep.

The ideological account claims the fictions first, as ideology in its purest laboratory form: representations whose falsity is common knowledge among the initiated and whose function is to make an outcome reachable that the order's other commitments would forbid. The fiction of the contracting parties' informed consent legitimates the enforcement of terms neither party negotiated, by relocating the transaction's reality into a ceremony that did not occur. The structural account claims the fictions as load-bearing devices, the joints at which the order bends without breaking: where a new social form, the joint-stock company, the trust, the insurance fund, must be processed by categories built for natural persons, fiction supplies the adapter, allowing the system's generality to survive the economy's innovations without the embarrassment of legislative reconstruction. The commodity-form account claims the central fiction as its own portrait: the legal person itself, the abstract bearer stripped of body, need, and history, is the order's founding untruth, maintained because exchange requires a subject as abstract as value, and the lesser fictions are its descendants, local applications of the original abstraction to new materials. The instrumental account, characteristically, asks of each fiction the beneficiary's name, and finds the question often answerable: fictions that deem notice given, consent obtained, and delivery made have a way of favouring the party who drafted the form, and the pattern of favour is evidence no theory of neutral technique should ignore. The historical account, as reliably as ever, complicates the pattern with the record, in which fictions invented for the convenience of the powerful are discovered, generations later, in the hands of the powerless: the corporate person invoked against the corporation's creators, the deemed consent turned against the deemer, the presumption hardened into a protection its authors would have voted down. Fictions, like precedents, are a treasury, and treasuries do not ask the politics of those who deposit or withdraw.

### Why do legal orders keep fictions everyone knows are false?

Because abolishing them would cost more than maintaining them. A fiction is a treaty between the order's need for continuity and the world's refusal to fit its categories: the category is preserved, the case is decided, and the price, a known untruth on the record, is one the order has always been willing to pay for both.

The fictions reward study for a further reason, methodological rather than doctrinal. They are the points where the legal order confesses, in its own documents, that its representations and the world diverge, and confessions are the historian's favourite evidence, since they cannot be dismissed as the critic's imposition. A critic who calls the legal subject an abstraction is making an argument. A legal order that must deem, presume, and feign in order to function is making the critic's argument in its own hand, and the tradition's best polemic has always consisted of arranging such confessions in a row and letting the order testify against the naive version of itself. Students preparing for examinations should collect fictions the way the historian collected pardons, as the evidence that survives cross-examination by the other side, because the other side wrote it.

## A Comparative Glance: Legal Orders Follow Their Economies, at a Distance

The theories in this article were built largely from the experience of societies undergoing the transition to generalised commodity production, and their export to other settings is the field's standing test. A comparative glance, conducted at the level of types rather than of named places, shows both the theories' reach and their limits with unusual clarity. Consider three ideal types of legal order. In the agrarian order of status, where most production is for use and most people are bound to land and lord by custom, law is particular: rights attach to persons by birth and station, obligations run between known parties, and the abstract legal subject of the commodity-form account barely exists, because the exchange that would require him is a marginal activity conducted at fairs and borders. In the commercial order, where trade is extensive but production remains organised by household and guild, the legal subject appears in the ports and markets as a specialised figure, the merchant, governed by a law of his own that prizes speed, good faith, and the enforceability of promises among strangers, while the countryside continues under custom a day's walk from the counting house. In the industrial order of generalised exchange, where labour power itself is a commodity and most people buy most of what they consume, the legal subject becomes universal, the presumption of every doctrine and the hero of every code, and the other positions' phenomena appear at full strength: a state managing the general interest of an economy it does not direct, an ideology of formal equality processing radical inequality, a criminal law fencing the property of a propertyless majority's employers.

The comparative glance corroborates the commodity-form account's central correlation, since the legal subject's career tracks the career of exchange across the three types with a fidelity no rival explains as economically. It corroborates the structural account's insistence on distance, since the legal orders of societies at similar levels of commercial development differ enormously in doctrine, personnel, and temper, differences deposited by distinct histories that no deduction from the economy predicts. It disciplines the instrumental account, whose mechanism requires a legislature socially continuous with a possessing class, a configuration the three types exhibit in different degrees and sometimes not at all. And it sets the historical account its largest assignment, since the constraint of power by forms appears, in the comparative record, under configurations the founding archive never knew, in orders of status where custom bound lords who acknowledged no statute, and in commercial orders where the merchant's law bound princes who needed the merchants' credit more than the merchants needed the princes' protection. The legitimation constraint, generalised, may be older than capitalism and wider than law in the strict sense: any power that rules by proclaiming standards, of custom, of faith, of descent, discovers that the proclamation can be quoted back, and the quoting is the beginning of constraint. That generalisation is offered as the comparative section's gift to the field's future work, with the warning the field has earned: generalisations of that sweep are programmes for research, not results, and they stand or fall by archives not yet read.

### What does comparison do that case study cannot?

It varies the conditions. A single legal order, however deeply studied, cannot show which of its features follow from its economy and which from its accidents, because the two arrive together. Comparison separates them imperfectly but really, and imperfect separation is the only kind the social sciences have ever had.

The glance must end with its own limit stated, in the spirit of the article's method. Ideal types are instruments, and the societies of the record are mixtures, in which status and contract, custom and code, household production and wage labour coexist in proportions that shift by region, by trade, and by generation. The types earn their keep by organising questions, not by describing places, and the student who deploys them as descriptions will be corrected by the first archive consulted, which is the correct outcome and the reason archives exist. What comparison establishes, at the level this survey can certify, is a correlation robust enough to demand explanation and loose enough to forbid deduction: legal orders follow their economies the way a coastline follows its geology, faithfully in outline and unpredictably in every inlet, and the five positions are five theories of the tides.

## Sanctions Beyond the State: Custom, Shame, and the Informal Order

The legal order's monopoly of legitimate coercion is a proclamation before it is a fact, and the societies this article studies have always contained parallel systems of norm and sanction that the official order tolerates, uses, ignores, or fights, with varying success. Custom governed the gathering of fallen wood before any statute named it theft, and custom's sanctions, the opinion of neighbours, the memory of the parish, the refusal of future cooperation, enforced the customary right for generations without a single official. Shame disciplines conduct in every workplace and village more cheaply than any inspectorate. Religious obligation binds believers in forums no bailiff attends. Commercial communities police their members by reputation and exclusion, sanctions swifter and sometimes more feared than litigation. Any account of law unable to locate these informal orders in its map will misunderstand the formal one, since the formal order's real significance often lies in its relation to the informal: as their rival, their backstop, their codifier, or their undertaker.

The tradition's positions extend to the informal territory with instructive differences. The instrumental account reads informal norms as the ideology of the village, the internalised discipline by which the governed police themselves in the interest of an order they did not design, and finds its evidence in customs that sanctify hierarchy and shame the poacher more reliably than the game laws ever did. The structural account reads the informal orders as part of the total machinery of reproduction, relieving the formal order of enforcement burdens it could never carry alone, since no state can post an official in every lane, and the delegation to custom and conscience is a condition of the formal order's affordability. The ideological account claims the territory as its heartland, since informal sanction is ideology with its sleeves rolled up, the point at which representations become conduct without passing through any institution that could be petitioned or reformed. The historical account, predictably, finds the territory double-edged in the record: custom as the poor's constitution as often as the rich man's catechism, the customary right defended against the statute, the shame of the parish turned against the informer and the engrosser, the informal order as the archive in which a subordinate population stores its own theory of justice and from which, at moments of crisis, it produces that theory fully armed. The founder's first articles were written in defence of exactly such a stored theory, the custom of the poor against the statute of the owners, and the defence's jurisprudential form, an appeal from the written law to an older unwritten one, has recurred in every century since, wherever formal orders have overreached.

### When does custom defeat statute in practice?

When the statute's enforcement depends on the cooperation of the population that holds the custom. Prosecutions require witnesses, juries, informers, and officials drawn from the locality, and a custom held with sufficient unanimity starves the statute of all four, leaving it a text that visiting authority recites and the parish ignores.

The informal territory also corrects a professional deformation of legal theory, its tendency to measure an order's importance by its institutions' grandeur. Much of what governs conduct in any society is decided at the level of the tenancy committee, the chapel meeting, the union branch, the family council, and the market's whispering gallery, and the formal law's role in those decisions is often that of a distant weather system, known, allowed for, and rarely seen. The tradition's contribution to the study of that level is its insistence on asking, of every informal norm, the warehouse questions: whose interest the norm serves, what form of life it presupposes, what belief sustains it, and what happens to those who break it when the formal order is invoked against the informal one's judgment. Those questions, asked patiently of small materials, have produced some of the field's most admired local studies, and they model a scale of inquiry at which every reader of this article can work, since everyone lives inside informal orders whose workings are available to observation. The survey commends the scale. Grand theory began, in this tradition, with a young man's attention to firewood, and it renews itself wherever attention of that quality is paid to materials of that size.

## The Jury and the Appeal: Institutions That Build the Constraint In

The legitimation constraint has been treated so far as a pressure acting on legal orders from outside their design, a price extracted by the need for belief. Two institutions deserve notice as the places where legal orders have internalised the constraint, building into their own machinery the requirement that power lose sometimes, visibly, by design. The first is the jury, in the traditions that maintain it: a body of laypeople, drawn from the governed, empowered to acquit against the evidence and the wishes of every official in the room, whose verdicts cannot be punished and whose refusals cannot be appealed by the prosecution in the systems that guard the institution most jealously. The jury is the legal order's standing confession that its own officials are not to be trusted with the whole of its power, and its history in the record is a history of the confession being honoured at inconvenient moments: acquittals that nullified statutes the legislature refused to repeal, verdicts that announced, in the only language available, that a law had lost the population it claimed to govern. Legislatures have raged against such verdicts for centuries and have repeatedly shrunk from abolishing the institution that produces them, and the shrinking is the constraint in its institutional form, the powerful declining to seize a power they could seize because the seizure's cost in legitimacy exceeds the power's value.

The second institution is the appeal, and with it the whole architecture of review, by which decisions are reopened before higher authority and errors corrected in public. Appeal is easy to mistake for a technicality, a quality-control device of the profession. Its theoretical significance is larger. A system with genuine appeal proclaims that its own first word is not its last, that power exercised at the base may be disowned at the summit, and that the disowning is a normal operation rather than a scandal. The proclamation binds the summit as the forms bound the base: a reviewing body that never reversed would advertise the review as ceremony, and the advertisement would corrode the legitimacy the ceremony exists to manufacture, so reversals occur, at rates that vary and are studied, and the powerful lose in the appellate reports cases they won below, in full view of the profession. The tradition reads the architecture with its double vision once more. As legitimation, review converts grievance into procedure, channelling the disappointed into a process whose delays and costs filter out most of them and whose occasional victories certify the channel. As constraint, review is a real exposure of official power to correction by standards the officials did not write for the occasion. Both readings are true, and the proportion between them in any system is, by now the reader will anticipate the formula, an empirical question, measurable in reversal rates, in the social distribution of appellants, and in the fate of those who win.

### Why would a ruling class tolerate juries that acquit its enemies?

Because the alternative costs more. A prosecution service that always won would be understood, correctly, as an arm of the executive, and every conviction it obtained would be discounted accordingly. The jury's unpredictable mercy and unpredictable severity alike certify that outcomes are not arranged, and the certification is worth more to the order than any single verdict.

The two institutions share a feature that generalises into the section's conclusion. Both are technologies for making the legal order's losses credible: public, unpunishable, and attributable to the order's own rules rather than to accident or mercy. A pardon can be read as favour. An acquittal by one's peers and a reversal on stated grounds cannot be read as anything but the system operating, and it is the system operating against power, in the cases that certify the whole. The tradition's historian built his conclusion from a record in which such technologies were young and partial. Their maturation since, and their survival through periods that abolished much else, is among the strongest evidence that his mechanism describes something real about how legal orders persist: they persist by institutionalising their own restraint, and the restraint, once institutionalised, develops defenders, budgets, and traditions of its own, which is to say it becomes a fact of the social landscape as stubborn as any interest the instrumental account ever named.

## Ceremony and Robes: The Theatre That Binds Its Actors

Legal process is theatre, and the observation, which the critical tradition sometimes delivers as an exposure, is better treated as a description whose implications run in several directions at once. The courtroom is a stage built to a pattern: elevation for the bench, separation for the parties, costume for the officials, a script of addresses and risings that marks the proceedings as belonging to a different order of event from the disputes they decide. The audience is part of the design, since publicity is the medium in which legitimation travels, and the architecture of older tribunals, with their galleries and their processions through public streets, shows the design pursued with a candour later ages disguise. The tradition's ideological account reads the theatre as the manufacture of awe: the citizen dwarfed by the building, the official enlarged by the robe, the violence at the ceremony's end transfigured by the ceremony into judgment. There is evidence for the reading in every period, including the testimony of defendants who describe the setting's intimidation more vividly than the sentence's weight.

But theatre binds its actors as well as its audience, and the binding is the part of the phenomenon the exposure reading misses. A judge costumed as the impersonal voice of the law has accepted a part, and parts have lines that cannot be improvised without breaking the performance for everyone. The official who must give reasons in the dialect, proceed in the order, hear the other side, and pronounce in the forms discovers that the ceremony is a discipline on the ceremonialist, that the robe is heavier than it looks, and that stepping out of character, deciding by favour or by rage in view of the gallery, costs the performer the authority the costume exists to lend. Regimes that have dispensed with the ceremony in favour of direct decision have repeatedly discovered the cost of the dispensation: their decisions, unclothed, are experienced as what they are, the commands of the stronger, and must be enforced at the price commands always pay when they cannot borrow the theatre's credit. The ceremony is thus one of the legitimation constraint's oldest technologies, older than the jury and the appeal, and its antiquity matters for the comparative argument of an earlier section: wherever power has sought durability, it has costumed itself, and the costume has always chafed the wearer in ways the wearer's descendants could exploit.

### If ceremony is theatre, are its outcomes scripted?

The record answers no, and the answer is the section's point. Scripts in this theatre constrain the improvisation without determining it, as the forms of a verse constrain a poet without writing the poem. Acquittals against the evidence, judgments against the powerful, and dissents that become law are the improvisations the script could not prevent and the audience came, in part, to witness.

There is a final turn of the theatrical screw that the tradition's historians have savoured and its theorists have underused. Audiences learn. A population that watches the ceremony across generations acquires a connoisseurship of it, an ear for the false note, the hurried proceeding, the coached witness, the judgment whose reasons do not reach its conclusion, and the connoisseurship is a political force, since an order whose performances are reviewed by experts it educated itself must raise its standard or lose its house. Movements have always known this and have staged their own counter-theatre in reply: the defendant who addresses the gallery over the bench, the trial converted into an indictment of the prosecutors, the scaffold speech that outlives the sentence. The legal order's theatre is therefore not a monologue but a repertory in contention, and the contention is possible only because the forms are public property, learned by all and usable by any. That usability is the deepest version of the article's recurring finding. Law's forms, its ceremonies included, are a commons of technique enclosed by a profession and never perfectly enclosed, and the history of the field is the history of the commons being grazed by herds its enclosers did not intend.

## Law and Time: Limitation, Memory, and the Authority of the Dead

Legal orders govern time as deliberately as they govern territory, and their temporal arrangements repay the tradition's attention because they encode, in technical dress, decisions about whose claims survive, whose injuries expire, and how much of the past the present must carry. Limitation periods extinguish claims after a term of years, however just the claim and however cynical the delay that outran it. Prescription converts long possession into title, so that time itself launders origins, including origins in force, into ownership the order will defend. Precedent binds the living to the reasoning of the dead. Constitutional entrenchment, where it exists, raises the price of change for specified commitments, delivering the founders' preferences into the future with a guard of supermajorities around them. Each device is defensible in the language of repose, certainty, and the settlement of expectations, and each has distributive consequences that the tradition's questions expose at once: limitation protects the holder against the claimant, and holders and claimants are not randomly drawn from the population; prescription rewards the possessor who could hold, against the dispossessed who could not return; entrenchment delivers the victories of one generation's dominant coalition into the custody of courts, against the coalitions of later generations who must win twice, once at the polls and once against the text.

The five positions read the temporal order in character, and the readings are among their most discriminating. The instrumental account finds the dead hand congenial to its thesis in a refined form: a class that wins in one period can store its winnings in forms that bind periods in which it might lose, converting political victory into legal permanence, and the history of entrenched property protections reads, on this account, as the diary of such conversions. The structural account corrects the refinement with the observation that storage binds the storers' descendants too, that entrenched forms frustrate later coalitions of the same class when conditions change, and that the general interest of an economy may require the release of what a past generation locked, so that the temporal order becomes another site where the particular and the general interest of capital quarrel across the grave. The ideological account attends to the representation of the arrangement: the past presented not as past decisions by past interests but as the constitution, the settlement, the way things are founded, with antiquity doing for temporal arrangements what ceremony does for present ones. The historical account consults the record of the devices in operation and finds, beside the entrenchments, the escapes: doctrines of desuetude by which dead statutes die unburied, interpretations that empty entrenched texts of their founders' meanings while keeping their words, limitation periods tolled and evaded by courts unwilling to let the cynical win on the calendar. The forms bind the future. The future, assisted by the profession, picks locks the founders believed final, and the picking is itself governed by forms, which is the whole institution in one sentence.

### Is prescription, title by long possession, a legalised theft?

It is the legal order's frank admission that peace outranks pedigree. Every system of property must somewhere stop the regress of titles, since all titles traced far enough arrive at seizure, and prescription names the stopping point honestly: possession held openly for the term becomes ownership, and the order defends the result without inquiring into the first chapter.

The temporal arrangements also govern the field's own subject matter in a way worth a closing remark. The five positions are themselves artefacts in time, deposited by the situations of their authors: the instrumental account by an age of candid class legislation, the commodity-form account by a revolution that proposed to abolish law, the structural account by the administered capitalism of the postwar decades, the ideological account by the discovery of consent as the central problem of order, the historical account by an archive and a political emergency that taught its author the price of unconstrained power. Knowing the deposits' dates does not refute the positions. It explains their emphases and predicts their blind spots, and it equips the reader to ask of each new theory of law the field's most useful question: what in its moment taught it to see what it sees, and what is its moment hiding from it now?

## Open Questions: Where the Field's Work Remains Undone

A survey that has graded its positions and stated its defeaters owes its readers a final analytical service: an honest register of what the field does not know, stated as questions that research could actually answer. Five such questions have recurred through this article, and collecting them converts the survey from a monument into a worklist, which is the condition in which its subject's best practitioners would wish it left.

The first is the quantity question of the legitimation constraint. The mechanism is argued; the dosage is unknown. How much consequential losing, at what visibility, across what period, does a legal order require to sustain the belief that its outcomes follow rules? The question is empirical, the materials for answering it are accumulating in enforcement and decision records, and the belief itself can now be measured with instruments developed by researchers of legal consciousness, whose price is in question. A generation of work could connect the two series of data, and the connection would either crown the historian's argument or wound it in ways its critics have only asserted.

The second is the boundary question of relative autonomy. The structural account asserts a bounded distance between legal institutions and the interests around them, and the boundary's location in particular disputes is exactly what participants need to know and the theory does not say. Progress here is likely to be casuistic rather than grand: a body of studied episodes, classified by the conditions under which institutions defied interests and the conditions under which they folded, from which something like a map of the boundary could be drawn. The map would not satisfy theorists who want a law of the boundary. It would satisfy everyone who has to act inside one.

The third is the scope question of the commodity-form account. The derivation explains the legal form where exchange is general, and its behaviour at the margins, in public law, criminal law, and the law of the household, has been contested since its publication. The question for research is whether the margins can be brought inside the derivation by its own resources, as its author attempted with punishment and equivalence, or whether the account should accept a bounded domain, the law of private relations between owners, and cede the remainder to the other positions. Bounded theories are not failed theories. They are theories that know their address, and the field would gain from the address being settled.

The fourth is the switching question of legal consciousness. The empirical literature shows populations holding several stances toward legality at once and deploying them by situation, and neither the ideological account nor its rivals predicted the pattern in detail. What governs the switch? Which experiences move a person from standing before the law to standing against it, and which move whole populations, as plainly happens in periods when an order's legitimacy drains within a few years after standing for generations? The question connects the field to the study of political crisis, and its answer would discipline every position that trades in the currency of belief.

The fifth is the design question of socialist law, generalised. Stripped of its period costume, it asks whether the goods the historian identified in the legal form, standing, answerability, the constraint of power by announced rules, can be detached from the social relations in which they historically grew and installed in relations of a different kind, or whether the goods are the form's and the form is exchange's, so that a society beyond exchange must invent their equivalents or lose them. The question is no longer confined to the tradition that posed it. Every proposal to govern by administration, by expertise, by algorithm, or by plan re-poses it, and the field's century of argument about the question is the richest resource available to anyone who must answer it in practice.

### Which open question matters most for citizens rather than scholars?

The first. The quantity of constraint legitimacy requires decides, in practice, how much protection a population actually has, since protections beyond the required quantity are vulnerable to economy drives and protections below it are subsidised by belief the order has not earned. Knowing the dosage would tell citizens what they hold and what they merely think they hold.

A register of ignorance is an unusual way to approach an article's end, and the choice is deliberate. The tradition surveyed here began with a young man's discovery that his philosophy could not describe a legislature, and its subsequent century is the record of that discovery being repeated, position by position, archive by archive, at rising levels of sophistication. The positions that command respect in the field are the ones that published their defeaters in advance. The survey has tried to follow them in that, and it turns now to the readers it was written for, students, teachers, and researchers, each of whom will use the field differently and each of whom is owed a direct word about the use.

## Reading a Legal Order Whole: The Five Questions in Working Order

The survey closes its analytical work by converting the five positions into an instrument a reader can carry into any legal material, including material this article has never mentioned. The instrument is a sequence of five questions, one from each row of the grid, asked in an order that moves from the most concrete to the most abstract and back again. Asked of a statute, a doctrine, a procedure, or a whole legal order, the sequence produces the beginnings of an analysis that no single position could have produced, and it models the habit the article has recommended throughout: not loyalty to a row, but fluency across the grid.

The first question is the instrumental one, and it is asked first because it is the easiest to answer and the most dangerous to skip. Who benefits from this arrangement, who drafted it, whose losses does it criminalise and whose does it civilise, and what would the arrangement look like if it had been designed by its beneficiaries in a candid hour? The question's answers are often genuinely illuminating, and the discipline consists in treating them as the start of the inquiry rather than its conclusion. The second question is the structural one. What does this arrangement do for the system entire, what long-run conditions does it maintain, which particular interests does it override in maintaining them, and what would break if it were removed? The question catches the arrangements whose beneficiaries are a system rather than a set of persons, and it explains the otherwise baffling phenomenon of rules that every powerful interest complains about and no powerful interest abolishes. The third question is the commodity-form one. What does this arrangement presuppose about the persons it governs, what abstraction does it perform on them, and where does the abstraction bite, at what point does the treatment of people as free and equal owners collide with what they actually own and what they actually are? The question is the deepest of the five and the slowest to answer, and it repays the slowness by revealing connections between doctrines that share nothing but their subject.

The fourth question is the ideological one. What does this arrangement teach those it governs to believe about themselves and about the order, what does its daily operation make seem natural, and where does its representation of itself diverge from its record? The question requires evidence about belief and not merely about text, which sends the analyst to testimony, to the students of legal consciousness, and to the archive of petitions and complaints. The fifth question is the historical one, and it is asked last because it judges the other four. What does the record of this arrangement in operation actually show: whom has it constrained, whom has it served, what has it cost the powerful, what have the governed made of it that its makers never intended, and which of the first four questions' answers survive the record? The sequence is not a ritual. An analyst who finds the fifth question's evidence overwhelming the first question's suspicions has learned something, and an analyst who finds the suspicions confirmed in detail has learned something else, and the instrument's value lies in making both outcomes possible in advance, which is the definition of an instrument rather than a catechism.

### Can the five questions be used on a single court decision?

They were built for exactly that scale as well as for whole orders. A decision has beneficiaries, a systemic function, a presupposed subject, a lesson it teaches, and a place in a record of similar decisions, and the analyst who asks all five of one judgment will usually find that two do the work and three expose what the two are hiding.

The instrument also serves as the article's answer to the question with which it opened, the student's question about what the Marxist theory of law is. It is five questions, developed across fifteen decades by writers who disagreed about nearly everything except the conviction that law is too important to be left to its own account of itself. The conviction is the tradition's unity. The questions are its content. A reader who leaves with the questions, and with the historian's warning about what is lost when their answers are allowed to harden into a slogan, has what the survey was built to give, and the remaining sections address the particular uses that students, teachers, and researchers will make of the equipment.

## Why the Slogan Survives: The Reception of a Complicated Field

It remains to explain a puzzle the article has exhibited without pausing over: if the instrumental account is the weakest of the five positions, regarded as inadequate by the tradition's own leading writers for generations, why is it the version the wider world knows? The reception of the field is itself a fit subject for the field's methods, and the explanation, when assembled, draws on every row of the grid in a way that makes a fitting penultimate exercise. The slogan survives because it is portable, because it is useful to opposed constituencies for opposed reasons, and because the institutions that transmit ideas about law reward compression over accuracy with a reliability the tradition's own theory of ideology predicts.

Portability first. The five positions, stated accurately, require a grid, a set of distinctions, and an archive. The slogan requires eleven words and fits in a headline, a placard, and the first paragraph of a textbook chapter written against a deadline. Ideas in transmission are selected for their fitness in the transmitting medium, and the media of general culture, journalism, introductory teaching, and political rhetoric select for the compressible with the indifference of a sieve. The tradition is not the only victim of the sieve; every complicated field is known to the wider world by its weakest portable claim, and the phenomenon would be recognised by the field's own theorists as a case of form determining content, the channel shaping the message as surely as exchange shapes the legal subject. There is a consolation available, and it is not small: the slogan, however inadequate as a theory, performs the service of a signpost, since a large fraction of the field's serious readers arrived by way of it, having first believed it, then doubted it, then gone looking for what the tradition actually held. A signpost need not resemble the city to direct travellers there.

Usefulness second, and here the opposed constituencies enter. The slogan is useful to the tradition's enemies because it is refutable: a critic who can show one statute that bound the powerful, one judgment against a great interest, has refuted the slogan and may retire believing the tradition answered, when the tradition's own writers made the same point earlier and built four positions on the rubble. The slogan is useful to a certain kind of sympathiser for the opposite reason, because it is unanswerable in the mouths of those who hold it as a mood: every contrary case becomes theatre, every protection a trick, and the holder is relieved of the labour of distinguishing the legal order's features, a labour the historian identified as the price of an effective politics. Between the enemy who needs the tradition simple to defeat it and the sympathiser who needs it simple to chant it, the complicated field is transmitted by its two least reliable custodians, and the arrangement is stable because both custodians are satisfied. Surveys exist to break the arrangement, and their method is always the same unglamorous one: restore the distinctions, name the positions, give the strongest objection its full force, and let the reader discover that the field is more interesting than its reputation, which in this subject is not a close-run thing.

### Does the slogan do any genuine work despite its inadequacy?

It asks the first of the five questions with unmatched economy, and as a first question it is indispensable: who benefits, whose will, what interest? Its inadequacy begins only when the first question is mistaken for the last, which is why the article keeps it, demoted to its proper rank, at the head of the working sequence.

The reception puzzle has one more layer, concerning the institutions of transmission themselves, which the field's sociology of knowledge would insist on examining. Textbooks reproduce the positions of earlier textbooks under pressures of time and market that have nothing to do with the evidence, so that an error installed in one generation's survey becomes the next generation's starting point, cited to itself. Examination systems reward the classifiable, and a five-position field with a famous controversy is more classifiable than a slogan, which is why the better syllabuses have quietly adopted the grid this article prints while the slogans continue to circulate in the culture outside the examination hall. The divergence between the taught field and the popular field is therefore likely to persist, and readers of this article are now on the taught side of it. What they do with the advantage, in seminars, in print, and in the patient correction of the next person who announces that Marxists think law is just a tool, is the reception's next chapter, and it is theirs to write.

## Neighbouring Verdicts: What Liberal and Abolitionist Critics See From Outside

The five positions have been presented from inside the tradition, with their quarrels conducted in its own vocabulary. A survey gains depth by stepping outside for one section to record how the field looks from its two nearest neighbouring standpoints, since each neighbour sees a feature of the landscape that the interior view systematically misses, and each neighbour's criticism has improved the tradition when the tradition has had the sense to steal it.

The liberal standpoint, in its serious jurisprudential form, accepts much of the field's evidence and contests its framing. From that standpoint, the subjection of power to rules is not a puzzle requiring a mechanism drawn from class analysis; it is the central achievement of constitutional civilisation, explained by the internal morality of law itself, by the requirements that rules be general, public, prospective, and stable if they are to guide conduct at all. A power that rules through such requirements has accepted discipline as the price of ruling through law, and the discipline's reality needs no further foundation in legitimation or accumulation. The liberal critic therefore reads the legitimation constraint as a rediscovery, in Marxist vocabulary, of what jurisprudence's own tradition had long maintained, and asks why the rediscovery is presented as a scandal. The question is fair, and the tradition's best answer is instructive. What the internal morality explains is why rule through law constrains. What it does not explain is the distribution of the constraint's benefits and burdens, the pattern by which the discipline binds power firmly in some territories and loosely in others, the territories correlating, with embarrassing regularity, with the property relations the liberal account treats as law's neutral subject matter. The Marxist positions exist to explain the pattern the liberal account records and passes by. The two standpoints are therefore less rivals than successive questions, and the field's mature writers have increasingly treated them so, taking the liberal account of the mechanism and supplying the materialist account of its distribution.

The abolitionist standpoint, represented in the tradition's neighbourhood by anarchist and some radical criminological currents, contests the field from the other side. From that standpoint, the historian's conclusion is not a correction but a seduction: the rule of law, however genuinely it constrains, constrains in the service of an order whose basic operations, the extraction of surplus and the fencing of the commons, are the injury itself, so that praising the constraint is like praising the humanity of a well-run prison. The tradition's interior answer has already been given in these pages and need not be repeated at length: the constraint is a deposit of the governed, its surrender has historically been followed by worse, and the abolitionist's alternative, the administration of things by the associated producers, must answer the design question of the previous section before its confidence is earned. But the neighbour sees something the interior misses, and honesty requires naming it. The tradition's defence of legal form can harden, in comfortable times and comfortable hands, into a defence of the order the form serves, with the critical edge retired into academic keeping. The abolitionist's refusal is the standing reminder that the form's goods are goods within a history that also contains the fence and the gallows, and that a jurisprudence ending in reverence for its subject has stopped being the kind of theory this tradition was founded to produce. The survey leaves the two neighbours where it found them, unconvinced and indispensable, which is the correct relation between a field and its critics.

## Remedies: The Civil Side of the Constraint

The argument of this article has been illustrated mostly from criminal and constitutional materials, because those are the territories where the founding texts and the founding archive live. The constraint the article describes, however, does its largest daily work in the civil territory, in the law of remedies, where the question is never only who was right but what the loser must now do, pay, restore, or stop doing. Remedies are where legal victories are converted into facts, and the conversion is neither automatic nor free. A judgment is a piece of paper until it is executed, and execution has its own sociology: assets that can be found and assets that dissolve, defendants who pay from pride and defendants who pay never, injunctions obeyed because disobedience is priced and injunctions evaded because the pricing was done by someone who had never met the defendant's accountants. The tradition's warehouse questions apply to remedies with special force, since a legal order may distribute rights with scrupulous equality and distribute remedies with a tilt that quietly returns the advantage to the party with the deeper purse, the longer horizon, and the better advice about which obligations can be safely ignored until sued upon.

Each position finds characteristic evidence here. The instrumental account points to the pricing of disobedience: where the remedy for a profitable wrong is a payment smaller than the profit, the law has in effect sold a licence, and the sale's terms favour those who can treat liability as a cost of business. The structural account points to the opposite phenomenon, the remedy that destroys: the injunction that closes the works, the award that bankrupts the enterprise, imposed by courts on defendants whose ruin served no one's plan, because the doctrine under which they were liable had been settled in other cases for other reasons and the institution honoured its own logic over the economy's convenience. The ideological account studies the experience of remedy from below: the claimant who wins after years and discovers that winning was the smaller half of the ordeal, and whose neighbours draw the intended lesson about the wisdom of claiming at all. The historical account, faithful to its method, asks what the record of execution shows, and finds claimants of small means using remedies with an ingenuity the doctrine never foresaw, converting judgments into bargaining counters, settlements into customs, and the threat of process into a currency that circulates far beyond the courts. The civil territory thus confirms the survey's general finding in a new key. Rights are the legal order's promises. Remedies are its performance, and the gap between promise and performance, varying by the claimant's resources and the defendant's, is one of the most honest measures available of what a legal order's equality is worth in practice. A student who learns to ask of every right the remedial question, and then what, has acquired the habit that separates the study of law from the study of its brochures.

## Law's Material Culture: Buildings, Files, Seals, and Paper

A legal order is not only a set of ideas and institutions. It is a physical civilisation of courthouses and registries, of files that outlive their makers, of seals, stamps, ribbons, and bound volumes, of archives in which the state's memory of every title, conviction, and obligation is stored against the frailty of human recollection. The tradition's materialism is incomplete until it attends to this material culture, since the physical apparatus does theoretical work that the doctrines alone cannot explain. The file is the legal order's characteristic invention: a durable, portable, inspectable double of a person or a property, which can be consulted in the person's absence, transferred between offices, and produced years later as the authoritative version of events against the testimony of everyone who was present. Power that works through files has a distinctive temper. It is patient, cumulative, and forgetful of nothing it has written down, and it is also, by the same token, answerable to its own record, since the file that convicts can be reopened, the register that excludes can be audited, and the archive that serves the administration becomes, in the historian's hands, the evidence of the administration's crimes and constraints alike. The founding archive of the historical position was a material culture before it was an argument: bundles of indictments and petitions whose survival was an accident of storage, read by a scholar who understood that the paper had outlasted the power that produced it and could now be made to testify in the other direction.

The building does similar work at the scale of the body. Tribunals are housed to be found, to be entered through ceremony, to dwarf or to welcome according to the period's theory of authority, and the long movement from the palace and the church porch to the open-plan office and the remote hearing is a history of the order's changing account of itself, legible in stone and then in floor plans. The seal and the stamp compress the whole theory of the institution into an object: authority made reproducible, the same mark in every office, the decision's validity carried by its form rather than by the decider's presence. Even the profession's notorious paper, the endless copying and binding and serving of documents, has its function in the material economy of legitimacy, since a process that generates a record at every step creates the evidence by which its own fidelity can later be judged. Reforms that dematerialise the apparatus, replacing the file with the database and the hearing with the screen, do not abolish these functions. They relocate them, and the tradition's questions follow the relocation without difficulty: who benefits from the new material culture, what does its form presuppose, what does its operation teach, and what will its archive, if it keeps one, allow a future historian to prove? The last clause is not rhetorical. A legal order that ceases to keep records in durable form ceases to be auditable by the method that produced this field's greatest work, and the loss would be a loss to the governed first of all, since the powerful have never needed archives to remember their victories.

### Why do historians of law prize the archive over the memoir?

Because the archive was not written for them. Memoirs justify, plead, and arrange; files were made to operate a system, and their evidence about the system's real workings leaks from their routine entries rather than from their authors' intentions. Reading against the grain is possible only where the grain was laid down for another purpose.

## Three Hard Cases for the Grid

Instruments are tested on hard cases, and the survey concludes its analysis by running the five questions against three phenomena chosen because each embarrasses at least one position in the grid. The exercise is the article in miniature, and readers pressed for time could begin here and work backwards.

The first hard case is the prosecution of the powerful that succeeds: not the token official sacrificed to scandal, but proceedings that reach the centre of wealth or office and end in ruin, obeyed by the institutions that execute them. The instrumental account must call the case theatre and then explain why the theatre required a real ruin when a staged one was available. The structural account explains the case as the general interest disciplining a member grown dangerous to the whole, and must then say, in advance of the next case, which members count as dangerous. The legitimation constraint explains it as credibility's price paid at the highest denomination, and predicts that such cases cluster where the order's legitimacy is most contested, a prediction the record can check. A fourth reading notes the form the proceedings take, the great person reduced for the duration to an abstract subject like any other, and counts the reduction itself as the phenomenon's significance. The historical account asks for the file.

The second hard case is the protection that outlives its usefulness to everyone powerful: a right or procedure won in one era, burdensome to the interests of every subsequent era, defended across generations by no lobby that can pay for its defence, and yet unrepealed. Instrumentalism has no mechanism for the case at all, since its repeal would serve every interest the theory recognises. Structuralism must argue that the protection serves the system's generality in ways invisible to the system's beneficiaries, which may be true and is certainly convenient. The historical account was built for the case: forms acquire defenders among the governed, repeal has a legitimacy price, and the deposit left by past struggles is drawn on by people the original struggle never met. The case is the strongest single evidence for the survey's synthetic sentence, that law stores the outcomes of struggles in a currency later generations can spend.

The third hard case is the legal order of a society in rapid dissolution, where the forms persist as ritual after the power and the economy that sustained them have collapsed: courts sitting without enforcement, titles registered to properties no one can reach, judgments pronounced into a vacuum. The case embarrasses the structural account, whose functionalism predicts the forms should wither with their function, and it embarrasses the commodity-form account in the same direction. It vindicates the historical account's emphasis on the autonomy of deposits, and it teaches the field's final lesson about its subject: legal forms, once built into a civilisation's material culture and common sense, have a half-life independent of their origins, and any theory that ties them too tightly to the conditions of their birth will be surprised by their ghost, going through the motions in the ruins, sometimes as farce and sometimes as the seed from which a later order, needing forms and finding these to hand, rebuilds. The grid survives the three cases as an instrument survives use: marked, discriminated, and more valuable for the marks, since a reader now knows which row to reach for first when the phenomenon is a ruin, a survival, or a prosecution, and knows also that reaching is the beginning of the analysis rather than its end.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Do Marxists think law is just a ruling class tool?

Some do, and that view, called instrumentalism, is the one the wider world knows. Inside the tradition it is now widely treated as the weakest of the available accounts, since it has nothing convincing to say about rules that bind employers, protections that workers fought for and kept, or verdicts that destroyed powerful defendants and were obeyed. Rival accounts give legal institutions a bounded independence, trace the shape of right back to the shape of exchange, study how legality forms the people who live under it, or point to the archive and the constraints it documents. The slogan survives as a first question. The field begins when it stops being the last one, and its most damaging critic was a historian arguing from within the camp.
### Q: What did Marx write about law?

Less than the tradition built on him, and nothing systematic. His first engagement was a series of newspaper articles of 1842 defending the customary gathering of fallen wood against a statute that criminalised it, an episode he later credited with turning him toward economics. The Manifesto of 1848 contributed the famous formulation of a class's will made into law for all. His marginal notes of 1875 on a workers' party programme contributed the analysis of equal right, the doctrine that an identical measure applied to different people manufactures unequal outcomes. Around those fragments stand remarks in the historical writings on constitutions, suffrage, and emergency rule. Later theorists constructed the field's positions from this scattering, which is why the positions are plural, and why citing the founder settles so much less than partisans expect.

### Q: What is the legitimation constraint on law?

Legitimation is the belief, among the governed, that an order's outcomes deserve acceptance because they issue from rules rather than from favour. The constraint is the price of that belief. A system whose decisions could be predicted from the parties' wealth or standing would soon stop being believed in, so any order that rules through law must let its own rules defeat its own friends often enough, and publicly enough, to keep the belief alive. Those defeats are not staged generosity; they are an operating cost, and once paid they bind, because a rule used once against the powerful becomes a premise the powerless can wield thereafter. The claim is empirical and carries its defeater: show an order that kept its subjects' allegiance while never ruling against a serious interest, and the mechanism collapses.
### Q: Why is instrumentalism about law inadequate?

Because its explanatory reach stops at the cases that flatter it. It explains candidly class legislation, the criminalisation of the poor's customs, and the tilt of enforcement toward property. It cannot explain factory legislation imposed on organised employers and enforced for generations, protections for combination won against the class the state supposedly serves, prosecutions of the powerful that end in ruin, or the way legal content outlives the ministers and majorities that inherited it. Its characteristic defence, that every contrary case is tactical or theatrical, renders it unfalsifiable, since a theory that accommodates every outcome predicts none. It also lacks any account of how the general interest of capital could override the particular interests of capitalists, the function the structural position was built to house. The verdict inside the tradition is not falsity but insufficiency.

### Q: Is the rule of law a human good?

On the evidence of one famous study, yes, without qualification: that was the verdict a Marxist historian delivered in 1975 after years inside the records of a statute written to terrorise the poor, and he chose the unqualified wording deliberately. What the records showed him was a machine that kept escaping its makers: charges that failed on points of evidence, juries that would not convict, officials prosecuted under the very act they administered. His critics answered that a form which treats unequals equally is itself an engine of inequality, and that the losses power suffers under it are marginal by design. He replied that his opponents were mistaking a catechism for a method. This survey sides with the mechanism he uncovered while leaving its size, as he left it, to the evidence.
### Q: What is relative autonomy in legal theory?

The phrase names a distance that is genuine and limited at once. Legal institutions are not ventriloquists' dummies for whoever owns the economy: they keep their own procedures, honour their own precedents, staff themselves from a profession with its own standards, and can ruin a minister or a magnate in the ordinary course of business. Yet they do all this inside a perimeter. An order whose decisions systematically prevented the surrounding economy from reproducing itself would lose the taxes that pay for its courts, the compliance that makes its writ run, and eventually its personnel. Autonomy explains why capital in general can be served by institutions that refuse capitalists in particular. Independence, the absence of the perimeter, is a stronger claim, and it belongs to no position in this field.
### Q: Can socialist law exist?

The tradition is divided, and the division follows from its positions. The commodity-form account answers no in principle: if legality is at bottom the shape taken by relations between commodity owners, then a society that abolishes commodity production abolishes the conditions of law itself, and regulation becomes technical administration rather than right. The Gotha critique supports a transitional answer: while distribution proceeds by contribution under scarcity, the equal right of the old order lingers on, defects included, as a bourgeois survival inside the new society. The historian's tradition answers with a warning: whatever succeeds law must reproduce law's constraint on power, or the succession will be mourned. Intermediate positions separate law's components, expecting property law to wither while procedure and protection persist. The next article of the series gives the debate its full hearing.

### Q: What are the positions within Marxist legal theory?

Five, organised in this article as a grid. The instrumental position treats law as a means of class rule. The structural position grants legal institutions relative autonomy in the service of capital's general interest. The commodity-form position derives the legal subject and the form of right from commodity exchange. The ideological position treats law as constituting free and equal subjects whose formal equality reproduces substantive inequality. The historical position, built from the archive, holds that legal forms impose real constraints on those who wield them. The positions answer partly different questions, about content, form, belief, and operation, and can be combined, but each has a characteristic failure the grid names, and examination answers are marked on the failures as much as on the claims.

### Q: Is Critical Legal Studies Marxist?

The honest answer is that the relation is partial and was argued about by the participants themselves. The movement that formed inside elite law schools in the late twentieth century borrowed the older tradition's distrust of legal neutrality, and a number of its early members had been formed by Marxist politics. Its signature doctrine, though, the claim that legal materials underdetermine the outcomes they are said to compel, is a thesis about reasoning, not about classes, production, or historical change, and it sits comfortably with liberal, conservative, libertarian, and feminist conclusions, all of which were represented among its leading figures. Filing the school as Marxist therefore takes a side its own centre refused to take. What travelled was a suspicion and some vocabulary. What stayed behind was the critique of political economy that gives the older tradition its explanatory spine.
### Q: Where should you start on Marxism and law?

Begin with the fragments of the founder, in the order of their difficulty: the wood-gathering articles for the founding episode and the method of immanent critique, the Manifesto passage for the instrumental charter, and the Gotha critique's pages on equal right for the tradition's most analytical legal writing. Then read the historian's 1975 study of the eighteenth-century statute, whose conclusion this article treats as the field's centre, together with one named criticism of it, so that the controversy is met as a controversy. The commodity-form classic deserves its own slow reading, assisted by this series' dedicated article. Students should keep the five positions grid beside them throughout, marking which position each text presupposes, since that habit converts a shelf of classics into a map of an argument.

### Q: How does the legitimation constraint grade the five positions on law?

Apply one test to all five: how gracefully does each handle a legal order's need to defeat its own beneficiaries in public? Instrumentalism handles it worst, forced to call every expensive defeat a performance. The account centred on ideology handles it better, since the test is built from its materials, though it pictures belief as more manufactured than the record supports. The structural account predicts defeats of particular interests from the needs of the whole economy, while the test predicts them from the needs of belief, and the predictions part company over regimes that govern by fright. The commodity-form account is graded on a different axis, explaining why credibility must be performed as equality among abstract persons. The historical account, which supplied the test, meets it most fully at home and owes evidence abroad.
### Q: What should a student separate between law as a tool and law as a constraint?

Keep two propositions apart that polemic habitually fuses. The first concerns where legal content comes from: interests lobby, draft, finance, and sometimes dictate, and the evidence for it is the history of enactments. The second concerns what legal forms do once they exist: procedures, precedents, and the need to be believed combine to bind the very hands that built them, and its evidence is the record of decisions. Nothing prevents both being true of the same statute in the same decade, the first about its birth and the second about its life. Answers that merge them into a single vague claim, law is both instrument and autonomous, forfeit the marks that the separation earns. Examiners reward the candidate who assigns each proposition its proper evidence and then explains why the tension between them is the subject itself.
### Q: Why can instrumentalism not explain law that binds capital?

Picture the theory's machinery: a class holds an instrument, and the instrument does the class's work. Now present the machinery with a measure the class organised to defeat, which passed, which inspectors enforced against its members for generations, and which those members' intended victims now defend as a possession. The machinery can only respond by redescribing the episode, as a trick, a safety valve, or a disguised profit, and it must produce a fresh redescription for every new episode, each one invented after the outcome is known. A theory whose explanations are all retrospective has retired from explanation. What the binding of capital actually displays is an institution with its own custodians, its own procedures, and its own need to be believed, none of which a transmission belt possesses, and all of which the rival positions were constructed to describe.
### Q: How does the five positions grid order the legal theories?

Read the grid by its questions rather than by its chronology. Two rows interrogate content: the instrumental row asks whose will the rules express, the structural row asks what the whole economy needs the rules to secure. A third row steps behind content to form, asking why social relations arrive dressed as the rights of abstract, interchangeable persons. A fourth row turns to the audience, asking what living under these rules teaches people to believe about the order and about themselves. The fifth row leaves the study for the record office, asking what thousands of actual decisions reveal about all the other answers. The ordering matters because the rows can be combined only when their questions are kept distinct, and because each row's blind spot, printed in the grid's final column, is the next row's reason for existing.
### Q: What separates relative autonomy from independence of law?

A perimeter. Inside it, courts and kindred institutions follow their own logic with real freedom: they embarrass governments, bankrupt the well-connected, and develop doctrines along lines no outsider dictated. The perimeter itself is drawn by dependence. The institutions eat from revenues raised on an economy they do not run, enforce through apparatuses they do not command, and recruit from a society whose structure they did not choose, so a jurisprudence that set itself permanently against that society's reproduction would be dismantled, starved, or ignored. Independence would mean deciding without a perimeter, which is a description of no legal order that has existed. The adjective relative is therefore load-bearing: remove it and the structural position becomes a liberal commonplace; remove the autonomy and it collapses back into the instrumentalism it was built to replace.
