Article 370 history in India is usually told backwards, from a single week in August 2019, as if a provision that had stood untouched for seventy years was struck down in one parliamentary stroke. The documentary record tells a different story, and the record is unusually complete, because almost every step in the story was taken through a published legal instrument whose text survives. Between 14 May 1954 and 5 August 2019, the President of India issued order after order under Article 370 itself, each one carrying more of the Indian Constitution into Jammu and Kashmir, each one trimming the distance between the state's constitutional position and the position of every other state in the Union. By the time the order of 5 August 2019 applied the entire Constitution to the state and the declaration of 6 August 2019 rendered Article 370 inoperative, the provision's original content had been reduced, piece by dated piece, across sixty five years. This article defends one claim and tests it against the strongest rival readings: Article 370's long arc from 1954 to 2019 is a sixty five year constitutional erosion trajectory, and the August 2019 action consummated that trajectory rather than rupturing a settled and intact arrangement.

## Three Readings of Article 370, and the Reading Defended Here

Three readings compete for the same evidence. The first reads August 2019 as a political moment: a government with a long declared commitment, a parliamentary majority and a prepared legal route chose its week and executed a founding promise of its movement. That reading, [the political-architectural-moment reading](/2019/07/31/article-370-abrogation-august-2019/), captures agency, timing and intention, and it situates the action inside [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) through which the post 2014 governments renegotiated the founding settlement. The second reading is narrower and legalistic. It treats August 2019 as a self contained juridical event, a pair of presidential orders and a reorganisation statute, to be assessed by the validity of each step and by nothing else. The third reading, defended here, is longer and colder. It follows the paper trail of presidential orders from 1954 onward and asks what was left of the original provision by the time the final orders arrived. On that evidence, the events of August 2019 were the last entries in a ledger that had been filling for sixty five years, and any account that begins in 2019 mistakes the final entry for the whole account.

The long arc reading does not deny the political moment reading; it disciplines it. A political decision explains why the final orders came in August 2019 rather than in 2009 or 2029. A legal assessment explains whether each step in that week was open to the actors who took it, a question the Supreme Court answered in December 2023. Neither explains the precondition that made the week possible: that the distance remaining between Jammu and Kashmir and the rest of the Union had already been narrowed, order by order, until a single order applying the whole Constitution could be presented as the completion of a process rather than the invention of one. Governments of every complexion issued those orders. Congress governments issued most of them. Coalition governments issued more. The government of 2019 inherited a mechanism that its predecessors had used about forty seven times, and the mechanism's own logic, set down in 1949 and widened by the Supreme Court in 1961 and 1968, pointed toward the use that was finally made of it. The erosion thesis is therefore not a claim about one party or one leader. It is a claim about an instrument and the direction in which the instrument travelled whenever it was picked up.

The claim carries a discipline of its own, and this article observes it throughout. Erosion is a description of movement in legal content, measured by comparing the text of the arrangement at dated points: the provision as adopted on 17 October 1949, the order of 14 May 1954 that first filled it with content, the orders of the middle 1960s that carried emergency powers and a new head of state into the arrangement, the order of 1986 that carried a Union legislative override, and the orders of August 2019 that emptied the provision entirely. At each point the article states what the instrument said, who issued it, whose concurrence accompanied it, and which named writers defended or condemned it at the time or in the scholarship that followed. Where the meaning of the movement is disputed, and it is disputed at every stage, the dispute is named and each side is given in the terms its holders chose. A. G. Noorani, whose Article 370: A Constitutional History of Jammu and Kashmir (2011) assembled the fullest archive of the orders, read the trajectory as the hollowing of a solemn compact. A. S. Anand, a former Chief Justice of India writing in The Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir: Its Development and Comments (2006), read much of the same record as lawful constitutional development within the provision's own terms. Sumantra Bose, in Kashmir: Roots of Conflict, Paths to Peace (2003), read the record politically, as the documentary surface of a deeper contest over consent in the Kashmir Valley. The article adjudicates among these readings at the end, on the evidence assembled stage by stage.

One boundary must be drawn at the outset, because the locked span of this study runs from 1965 to 2019 while the trajectory itself begins in 1954. The year 1965 is not an arbitrary start. It marks the point after which the erosion ceased to be a matter of extending provisions and became a matter of restructuring the state's own constitutional offices and emergency exposure: the head of state redesignated, the Union's emergency articles carried across, the Supreme Court in Sampat Prakash confirming that the presidential power survived the disappearance of the body that was supposed to end it. The decade from 1954 to 1964 laid the foundation course. The years after 1965 built on it in one direction only. A reader who wants the political history of the single week in 2019 will find it in the companion study named above. A reader who wants to know why that week was legally and institutionally available, and what it cost the provision across the preceding half century, will find the answer in the orders themselves.

## The Original Design: What Article 370 Said on 17 October 1949

The provision began as a wartime and accession instrument, drafted for a state whose ruler had acceded under invasion. Maharaja Hari Singh signed the Instrument of Accession on 26 October 1947, as tribal fighters backed by Pakistan moved on Srinagar, and Governor General Mountbatten accepted the instrument on 27 October 1947 with a reference to a future reference to the people once order was restored. The instrument covered three subjects: defence, external affairs and communications, together with ancillary matters listed in its schedule. Every later argument about Article 370 returns to that schedule, because the provision's defenders treated the three subjects as the agreed ceiling of Union power, while the provision's critics treated accession as complete and final in the same manner as every other princely accession, with the schedule marking only the first administrative instalment. The legal text of accession was identical in form to the instruments signed by other rulers, a point the Supreme Court would restate in December 2023 when it held that Jammu and Kashmir retained no element of internal sovereignty after accession and the adoption of its own constitution. The political circumstances were not identical, and the difference produced Article 370.

### Did the Instrument of Accession limit Parliament to defence, external affairs and communications?

The instrument of 26 October 1947 ceded legislative authority over defence, external affairs and communications, with ancillary subjects in its schedule, and its eighth clause preserved the ruler's authority in all other fields. Article 370 then converted that schedule into the constitutional baseline for Union lawmaking in the state.

The drafting history sits inside [the founding triangulation context](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) and inside [the post-Partition political-architectural context](/2019/01/08/partition-constitutional-consequences-india/), where a ceasefire line, a reference to the United Nations and a war left unfinished business that the Constitution had to carry. N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar, the minister who had handled Kashmir affairs in the Union government, moved the draft provision, numbered 306A in the draft, in the Constituent Assembly on 17 October 1949. Ayyangar's speech of that day remains the founding exposition of the design. He told the Assembly that the state was not yet ripe for complete integration, that a war was in progress in its territory, that part of the state was in hostile occupation, that the United Nations was seized of the question, and that the Government of India had committed itself to ascertaining the people's will. For those reasons, he argued, the relationship had to be provisional in form: Parliament could legislate on the accession subjects, other constitutional provisions could be applied by presidential order with the state government's concurrence, and the arrangement would last until the state's own Constituent Assembly decided the final shape of the relationship. B. R. Ambedkar, as Law Minister, had declined to draft the provision, and the task had passed to Ayyangar; the episode, recorded in the Assembly's proceedings and in the correspondence of the period, already signalled that the provision was a political settlement wearing legal dress, a character it never lost.

The adopted text repays close reading, because the whole later trajectory is an argument over four clauses. Article 370(1)(b) limited Parliament's legislative power to the accession subjects, on which the state government had only to be consulted for matters specified in the Instrument, and to other subjects in the Union and Concurrent Lists with the state government's concurrence. Article 370(1)(d) allowed the President to apply other provisions of the Indian Constitution to the state with exceptions and modifications, again with concurrence for matters beyond the accession schedule. Article 370(2) required that concurrence given before the state Constituent Assembly met be placed before that Assembly for its decision. Article 370(3) allowed the President to declare the article inoperative or operative with modifications, but only on the recommendation of the state Constituent Assembly. The provision sat in Part XXI of the Constitution, headed as temporary, transitional and special provisions, although the word temporary appeared in the part heading rather than in the article's own marginal note, which read simply as temporary provisions with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir. Every clause contained a door, and every door opened one way. Concurrence could extend Union provisions; no clause provided a route by which an extended provision could be withdrawn. The Constituent Assembly's recommendation could end the article; no clause said what would happen if that Assembly dispersed without recommending. The ratchet and the silence were both original features of the design, and the next seventy years were lived inside them.

### Was Article 370 placed among the temporary and transitional provisions?

Article 370 stands in Part XXI, whose heading covers temporary, transitional and special provisions, and its own marginal heading in the original text described it as a temporary provision with respect to Jammu and Kashmir. Its temporary character rested on the expected decision of the state Constituent Assembly under clause 3.

The founding settlement also left the state free to write its own constitution, a liberty no other state possessed, and the state Constituent Assembly that convened in Srinagar in 1951 under Sheikh Abdullah's leadership exercised it. Between the adoption of Article 370 in October 1949 and the first great presidential order of May 1954, the relationship was filled in by negotiation, most formally in the Delhi Agreement of July 1952 between Jawaharlal Nehru and Sheikh Abdullah. That agreement recorded understandings on citizenship, on the state's flag, on the head of state, on emergency powers and on the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court, and its terms became the benchmark against which every later order was measured by the autonomy school. The agreement's understandings were warmer than the practice that followed. Within thirteen months of its signature, Sheikh Abdullah was dismissed and arrested on 9 August 1953, an event carried out by the Sadar-i-Riyasat, Karan Singh, on the stated ground of lost confidence, and Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad took office at the head of a government whose concurrence would be available for the orders that followed. The sequence matters to the adjudication this article must eventually make: the mechanism of Article 370 was designed for concurrence between two governments in a relationship of negotiated trust, and from August 1953 onward it operated, for long stretches, between a Union government and state governments whose own mandates were contested in the Valley. The orders were lawful instruments. The political quality of the concurrence behind them is a separate question, and the scholarship divides on it precisely there.

## The Five-Stage Map: Naming the Trajectory Before Tracing It

A long arc claim earns its keep only if its stages can be named, dated and checked, so this article fixes its map before walking it. The map is the article's findable artifact, cited here as Table 1, the Five-Stage Long-Arc Erosion Map of Article 370 from 1954 to 2019, and each stage in the text that follows is argued against the instruments listed in its row. Stage one is the founding extension of 1954, in which the first presidential order filled the provision with most of the Indian Constitution's operative content and inserted Article 35A. Stage two is the restructuring of the middle 1960s, in which emergency articles reached the state, the state's head was redesignated, and the Supreme Court confirmed the mechanism's survival. Stage three runs from the accord of 1975 to the order of 1986, in which the framework was expressly preserved on paper while its legislative balance shifted again. Stage four runs from 1990 to 2019, in which long central rule, an autonomy committee, a rejected assembly resolution and a coalition promise of status quo each tested the arrangement without reversing it. Stage five is August 2019, in which the mechanism was used to apply the entire Constitution and then to declare its own source inoperative. Readers of documentary compilations, including researchers who open the scanned orders through [an office file viewer for constitutional documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), can check every row against the Gazette texts.

The map also exposes the honest difficulty in the erosion claim, which the complication section of this article takes up in full: stage five contains two novelties that no earlier stage contains, the use of the provision to end itself and the conversion of a state into Union Territories. A trajectory can prepare an event without exhausting its meaning, and the article's final adjudication weighs exactly how much of August 2019 the sixty five year record explains and how much it does not. What the map establishes beyond serious dispute is the direction and the mechanism. No stage moved content out of the Union's reach. No order restored a withdrawn field. The one formal attempt to reverse the direction, the autonomy resolution of June 2000, was refused by the Union Cabinet within days. Direction, mechanism and the absence of any reverse gear are the facts the rival readings must accommodate, and the stages can be walked in order.

## Stage One: The Order of 14 May 1954 and the Filling of the Gateway

The Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order of 14 May 1954 is the single most consequential instrument in the entire trajectory, more consequential in content than the orders of August 2019, because it decided how much of the Indian Constitution would live inside the state for the next sixty five years. A first order of 26 January 1950 had applied a limited set of provisions as the Constitution commenced. The 1954 Order, issued with the concurrence of the state government headed by Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad after the Delhi Agreement negotiations and after Sheikh Abdullah's removal, superseded that beginning and applied the Constitution in bulk: Union citizenship in adapted form, the fundamental rights with exceptions and modifications, the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in defined fields, the authority of the Election Commission for parliamentary elections, the Union's executive reach on the accession subjects and beyond them by concurrence. The order's technique set the pattern for every successor. Rather than negotiating each extension as a discrete political act, the two governments settled a large standing application in one instrument, and later orders could then proceed by amendment of the 1954 text, a drafting convenience that steadily lowered the political visibility of each subsequent extension.

Article 35A, inserted by the 1954 Order into the Constitution as applied to the state, shows the technique at its most double edged. The article empowered the state legislature to define the classes of permanent residents and to confer on them rights and privileges in public employment, property acquisition, settlement and scholarships, from which other citizens of India could be excluded. For the autonomy school, 35A was part of the protective core of the arrangement, the demographic and proprietary safeguard without which accession on the Delhi Agreement terms made little sense to the Valley's leadership. For the integration school, 35A was an anomaly created by executive order rather than by parliamentary amendment under Article 368, a constitutional provision that had never faced the amending procedure. Both descriptions are accurate as far as they go, and the 2023 judgment disposed of the provision as a living question by upholding the orders that ended it. What matters for the long arc is the route: the most distinctive protective provision in the state's armoury entered the Constitution through the same presidential order mechanism that later removed it, and its presence in the 1954 text proved, from the very first stage, that the mechanism could create as well as extend. A gateway that can insert an article can also, in other hands and other years, amend the interpretation clauses through which the gateway itself is read. The route taken in August 2019 was signposted, in that limited sense, in May 1954.

The 1954 Order also carried the seeds of the rights asymmetry that ran through the state's constitutional life. Fundamental rights applied, but in modified form: Article 19's freedoms were qualified, preventive detention received a longer shelter through Article 35C, which immunised state detention laws from challenge for a stated period, and the right to property carried its own adaptations. The Supreme Court's jurisdiction extended to the state in appellate and defined fields, while the full writ architecture arrived in stages. Noorani's archive dwells on these modifications because they show the order working in both directions at once: extending the Constitution's reach while diluting the Constitution's protections, so that the state received more of the Union's power and less of the Union's restraint than any other state. Anand's commentary answers that the modifications were the price and the meaning of a negotiated application, each one traceable to a concurrence, and that a provision designed to work by agreement cannot be faulted for recording the agreements actually reached. The adjudication between those readings cannot be settled at stage one. It can only be noted that the direction of travel for Union power was set, that the ratchet engaged, and that the state's own Constituent Assembly, still sitting in 1954, ratified the order's concurrence framework when it proceeded to draft a constitution inside the space the order had defined.

The state constitution that emerged, adopted on 17 November 1956 and effective from 26 January 1957, is the strongest documentary support the permanence school possesses, and the long arc reading must give it full weight. Section 3 of the Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir declared the state to be and to remain an integral part of the Union of India, and Section 147 placed that declaration beyond amendment by the state legislature. The state Constituent Assembly, the very body whose recommendation Article 370(3) required for the provision's end, dispersed in January 1957 without making any such recommendation, having instead entrenched the Union relationship in the state's own text. Two conclusions were drawn from that record, and Indian constitutional law lived with both for six decades. The permanence reading held that the Assembly's silence, combined with its entrenchment of the relationship, converted a provisional arrangement into the settled terms of a compact: Article 370 survived because the only body authorised to end it had chosen a constitution instead. The temporariness reading held that silence is not a recommendation, that the provision remained in Part XXI awaiting its clause 3 moment, and that the power to end it must lie somewhere once the recommending body had ceased to exist. The Supreme Court chose between these readings in stages, in 1959, in 1968 and finally in 2023, and the middle choice, made in Sampat Prakash, is the hinge on which the whole post 1965 trajectory turns.

## The Courts Consolidate the Mechanism: Kaul, Lakhanpal, Sampat Prakash and Damnoo

Four judgments built the legal road along which the orders travelled, and each one widened the road it was asked merely to inspect. In Prem Nath Kaul v State of Jammu and Kashmir, decided in 1959, a five judge bench examined the provision's history and described the state Constituent Assembly as the body that would finally determine the constitutional relationship, language the permanence school quoted for decades. The holding itself concerned the competence of the state's ruler to legislate after the Assembly convened, but the judgment's historical passages fixed the frame: Article 370 was a bridge provision, and the bridge led to a decision in Srinagar. Read alone, Kaul supports the compact reading. Read with what followed, it marks the high point of that reading's judicial fortunes.

### Did the Supreme Court in Prem Nath Kaul describe the state's Constituent Assembly as the final arbiter?

The five judge bench in Prem Nath Kaul, in 1959, traced the accession history and treated the state Constituent Assembly as the authority that would settle the constitutional relationship finally, language that later gave the permanence school its strongest judicial support before Sampat Prakash recast the position in 1968.

Puranlal Lakhanpal v President of India, decided in 1961, took the second step. Lakhanpal challenged a presidential order modifying the application of constitutional provisions, arguing that the power to apply provisions with modifications under Article 370(1) could not extend to altering the provision's effect in substance. The Supreme Court rejected the challenge and held that modification in this setting carries its widest amplitude, extending to a radical alteration of the provision as applied. The sentence mattered far beyond its case. If the President, with concurrence, could modify a constitutional provision radically in its application to the state, then the content of the state's constitutional position was, in law, whatever the accumulated orders said it was, and the boundary between applying the Constitution and remaking it for one state had been dissolved by interpretation. Noorani treated Lakhanpal as the original judicial sin of the trajectory, the point at which the court surrendered the distinction between a gateway and a legislature. Defenders of the judgment answer that the text left the court little choice: a power to except and modify, exercised by order, either means what it says or is rewritten by the bench, and the Constituent Assembly that wrote clause 1(d) had Ayyangar's own explanation before it, in which the widest flexibility was the stated point of the device.

Sampat Prakash v State of Jammu and Kashmir, argued on the orders of 1959 and 1964 that had extended the shelter of Article 35C for preventive detention laws, was decided in 1968 and reported in 1970, and it is the judgment after which this article's locked span begins to make sense. Sampat Prakash, a detainee held under the state's preventive detention law, contended that Article 370 had spent itself when the state Constituent Assembly dispersed in 1957, so that no presidential order issued after that date could rest on it. A five judge bench rejected the argument in terms that settled the mechanism's longevity. The court held that the provision continued in force because the conditions that called it into being continued, that the Assembly had made no recommendation under clause 3, and that the presidential power under clause 1 therefore remained available, to be exercised from time to time as fresh extensions were judged necessary. The bench also confirmed, following Lakhanpal, that modification includes amendment in the widest sense. The practical consequence dwarfed the detention question before the court. The one event that might have frozen the ratchet, the disappearance of the recommending Assembly, was converted by Sampat Prakash into the ratchet's charter: the power survived its own intended terminus, answerable to no further consent event in Srinagar beyond the concurrence of whichever government held office there. Every order from 1968 to 2019, including the final pair, rests doctrinally on this judgment, and the 2023 bench, asked to treat Sampat Prakash as per incuriam for ignoring Kaul, refused the reference to a larger bench in March 2020 and reaffirmed the position in the final judgment.

Mohammad Maqbool Damnoo v State of Jammu and Kashmir, decided in 1972, closed the judicial sequence by recognising that the Sadar-i-Riyasat named in the constitutional texts had become, after the redesignation of 1965, the Governor, so that acts done in the Governor's name satisfied provisions written for the older office. The point appears clerical. Its place in the trajectory is not. The redesignation of the head of state was the most symbolically charged of the middle 1960s changes, converting an elected state head, chosen by the state legislature under the Delhi Agreement understandings, into a Union appointee holding office during the President's pleasure. Damnoo normalised the change in law, holding that the substance of the office continued through its renaming. Together the four judgments performed the legal half of the erosion: Lakhanpal widened the power, Sampat Prakash extended its life, Damnoo absorbed its institutional consequences, and Kaul remained on the record as the road not taken, available to every later petitioner and adopted by none.

## Stage Two: 1964 to 1968, Emergency Powers and a New Head of State

The orders of 1964 and 1965 changed the kind of provisions that crossed into the state, and the change of kind is why the locked span of this study opens in 1965. The extensions of the 1950s had carried rights, courts and elections. The order of 1964 carried Articles 356 and 357, the Union's emergency machinery for state failure of constitutional government, together with other provisions that deepened the Union's legislative and financial reach. Article 356 as applied allowed the President to assume the functions of the state government on the Governor's report, and Article 357 allowed Parliament to exercise the state legislature's powers during such a proclamation. In every other state these articles were part of the original constitutional bargain of 1950. In Jammu and Kashmir their arrival a decade and a half later, by order and concurrence, converted the state's insurance policy against Delhi into Delhi's instrument inside the state, and their later use was extensive: the long spell of President's Rule from January 1990 to October 1996, the longest in the state's history, ran on the authority the 1964 extension supplied, and the Governor's rule periods of 1977, 1986 and 2018 to 2019 framed the state's politics repeatedly. A provision inserted to protect the state's government from the Union became, in the decades of militancy and coalition collapse, the ordinary channel through which the Union governed the state directly. The long arc reading rests heavily on this inversion, because it shows erosion operating not by stealth but by the ordinary use of extended provisions for purposes their extenders could defend, one proclamation at a time.

The redesignation of 1965 cut closer to the founding understandings. Under the Delhi Agreement of 1952 and the state constitution of 1957, the state's head was the Sadar-i-Riyasat, elected by the state legislature for a five year term, a deliberate departure from the appointed Governors of the other states and one of the visible emblems of the accession settlement. The sixth amendment of the state constitution in 1965, carried by the state legislature under Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad's successor Ghulam Mohammed Sadiq, replaced the elected Sadar-i-Riyasat with a Governor appointed by the President, and replaced the state's Prime Minister with a Chief Minister. A presidential order applying Article 367's interpretation clause to the state aligned the Indian Constitution's references accordingly. The autonomy school has always treated 1965 as the trajectory's point of no return in institutional form: the state lost the head of state its legislature chose, and with him the office whose refusal of concurrence might have slowed the orders, since a Governor appointed from Delhi was structurally unlikely to withhold what Delhi proposed. The integration school answers that Sadiq's government proposed the alignment itself, that an elected assembly enacted the sixth amendment, and that uniformity of offices is the ordinary grammar of a maturing federation, the grammar that [the post-1956 federal-architecture context](/2019/02/10/states-reorganisation-act-1956-india/) applied to state boundaries across the Union. Both accounts name real evidence. What neither disputes is the mechanical consequence: after 1965, the concurrence that Article 370 required would be given, in periods of Governor's rule, by an appointee of the very government seeking the extension, a collapse of the two key design into one key that the orders of 1986 and 2019 would each exploit.

Between the redesignation and Sampat Prakash, the wider constitutional weather also changed, and the state's arrangement was not insulated from it. The national emergency jurisprudence of the following decade, the amendment battles and the basic structure settlement that [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/) organises, all proceeded on the footing that Jammu and Kashmir stood inside the Indian constitutional system for most operative purposes, a footing the accumulated orders had created. By 1968, when the Sampat Prakash bench confirmed the mechanism's survival, the provision's text still promised a distinct relationship, while the provision's content described a state integrated in rights machinery, emergency exposure, judicial hierarchy and headship, distinct chiefly in its land and residency protections, its own constitution's survival and the formal gateway through which further integration had to pass. That residue was real, and the next two stages show governments affirming the residue in words while the gateway continued to admit new content in fact.

## Stage Three: Preservation in Words, Movement in Law, 1975 to 1986

The accord of February 1975 between Indira Gandhi and Sheikh Abdullah is the strongest evidence against a simple erosion story, and the long arc reading must absorb it rather than step around it. Sheikh Abdullah, released and politically rehabilitated after two decades in which the state's politics had been conducted by his successors and rivals, negotiated his return to office through Mirza Afzal Beg's talks with G. Parthasarathy for the Union. The accord's terms, published as an agreed statement, recorded that the state would continue to be governed by Article 370, that the Union's legislative authority would remain as it stood, that provisions applied to the state could be altered by the state legislature in defined fields with the President's assent, and that no change would be made unilaterally in the accord's own settlement. Sheikh Abdullah became Chief Minister in February 1975, leading a Congress supported administration before reviving the National Conference and winning the election of 1977, the election widely regarded in the Valley as the freest of the state's early decades. For the autonomy school, the accord proved that the trajectory could be halted by political will: the framework was reviewed at the highest level and expressly preserved, and the state's most authoritative leader accepted office inside it rather than demanding its renegotiation. For the erosion reading, the accord's fine print carried the colder fact. Sheikh Abdullah had sought, during the talks, the restoration of the pre 1953 position in substance, the return of the elected head of state and the review of post 1953 orders, and the published accord conceded none of it. The framework preserved in 1975 was the framework as it stood after 1965, Governor, emergency articles and all. Preservation, in the accord's hands, meant freezing the trajectory at its eroded point and calling the freeze a settlement.

The decade after the accord tested both readings. Sheikh Abdullah died in September 1982 and was succeeded by his son Farooq Abdullah, whose government was dismissed in July 1984 by Governor Jagmohan after defections engineered in Srinagar politics, an early demonstration of the Governor's office operating as an instrument of the Union executive in the state. The Rajiv Gandhi and Farooq Abdullah accord of November 1986 restored the National Conference to office in alliance with the Congress, at the price of the party's remaining distance from Delhi, a price the Valley's voters recorded in the election of March 1987, whose management by the ruling alliance became a founding grievance of the militancy that followed. Between those political events sits the legal event this stage is remembered for: the presidential order of 1986 extending Article 249 to the state. Article 249 empowers the Rajya Sabha, by a two thirds resolution in the national interest, to authorise Parliament to legislate on a state subject. Its extension to Jammu and Kashmir meant that a field reserved to the state legislature could be opened to Parliament by a resolution in a chamber where the state held a handful of seats. The concurrence for the 1986 order was given not by an elected state government but by the Governor, Jagmohan, during Governor's rule, after Farooq Abdullah's dismissal and before the November accord. Noorani's criticism of the order is the sharpest in his archive: a provision enlarging Union power over the state's list was carried in on the signature of the Union's own appointee, during a spell of direct central administration, precisely the concurrence collapse that the redesignation of 1965 had made possible. The order's defenders replied that Article 370 requires the concurrence of the government of the state, that the Governor in Governor's rule is that government in law, and that the same reading would later be unavoidable for any order issued during President's Rule, including, as events proved, the most consequential orders of all. The legal argument is consistent. The political objection is also consistent. The long arc is the accumulation of exactly such consistencies, each defensible in isolation, travelling in one direction in sum.

By the end of 1986 the residue of the original arrangement had thinned to a definite list. The state retained its own constitution, its permanent resident regime under Article 35A, its land protections, the formal necessity of concurrence for further extensions and the unexercised clause 3. It had lost its elected head, its immunity from Union emergency government, its exclusive hold on the state list against a Rajya Sabha resolution, and, after the election crisis of 1987, much of the democratic credibility that might have converted the residue into a bargaining position. Sumantra Bose's account of these years, written from Srinagar interviews as well as archives, treats the legal thinning and the political discrediting as one process: each order that narrowed the state's autonomy also narrowed the class of Kashmiris willing to defend the arrangement as a genuine compact, until by 1989 the defence of Article 370 had become, in the Valley, the programme of parties competing with an armed movement that rejected the arrangement altogether. The erosion thesis, in Bose's handling, is not only a claim about texts. It is a claim about what the texts did to the constituency for the settlement itself.

## Stage Four: Strain Without Reversal, 1990 to 2019

The fourth stage is the longest and, for the trajectory, the most revealing, because it contains the one organised attempt to reverse the direction and records its failure in full. The militancy that began in 1989, the exodus of Kashmiri Pandits from the Valley in early 1990, the return of Governor Jagmohan in January 1990 and the imposition of President's Rule that July created conditions under which the state's constitutional position was administered rather than debated. President's Rule, extended by parliamentary resolution beyond the ordinary limits through the machinery the 1964 order had supplied, ran until October 1996. During those six years the concurrence mechanism slept, since no elected state government existed to give or withhold it, and the accumulated orders simply governed. The election of 1996 returned Farooq Abdullah and the National Conference with a two thirds majority won explicitly on an autonomy platform, and the new government appointed the State Autonomy Committee, chaired by Karan Singh, the former Sadar-i-Riyasat, with a membership drawn from the state's political class. The committee's report, submitted in June 1999, together with a companion report on regional autonomy associated with Balraj Puri, recommended the restoration of the state's constitutional position to its state before 1953 in most respects: the Union confined to defence, foreign affairs, communications and currency, the elected head of state restored, the jurisdiction of Union institutions rolled back to the accession terms. The report was the fullest official statement the autonomy case ever received, drafted not by pamphleteers but by a committee of the state's own government, and its recommendations amounted to an official finding that the erosion described in this article had in fact occurred and ought to be undone.

The Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly adopted the committee's report by resolution on 26 June 2000. The Union Cabinet, meeting under Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee on 4 July 2000, rejected the resolution as unacceptable, recording in its published decision that acceptance would set the clock back and reverse the process of harmonising the aspirations of the state's people with the integrity of the nation, while reaffirming a general commitment to devolution of powers to states. The speed and the phrasing both matter. The resolution was not negotiated, amended or remitted for study; it was refused, within eight days, by the cabinet of a coalition government in which the National Conference itself participated at the Union level, Omar Abdullah serving as a minister of state in that government. The autonomy school reads July 2000 as the proof that the ratchet was political and not merely textual: offered a democratic mandate, a committee report and an assembly resolution, the Union chose the accumulated orders over the founding terms, and the one constitutional channel through which reversal had been formally requested was closed by name. The integration school reads the same week as democratic normality: an assembly resolution binds no one, the pre 1953 demand would have dismantled arrangements on which two generations of law rested, and the cabinet's preference for general devolution over a bespoke rollback was a policy choice open to it. The long arc reading requires only the agreed facts. Between 1954 and 2000 the direction of the state's constitutional travel was tested once, formally and democratically, and the test produced a refusal that left every order in place.

### What did the Union Cabinet say when it rejected the autonomy resolution in July 2000?

The Union Cabinet's published decision of 4 July 2000 found the Assembly's resolution unacceptable, holding that acceptance would set the clock back and reverse the harmonising of the state's aspirations with the integrity of the nation, while affirming devolution of powers to states in general terms.

The remaining two decades of the stage added strain without movement. The People's Democratic Party, founded in 1999 by Mufti Mohammad Sayeed, carried the language of self rule into government after the election of 2002 and again in coalition with the Congress after 2008, and its self rule working papers proposed a softer version of the old demand, joint mechanisms and shared sovereignty in defined fields, which no Union government adopted. The coalition that took office in March 2015, joining the People's Democratic Party with the Bharatiya Janata Party, published an Agenda of Alliance in which the partners recorded their opposed positions on Article 370 and agreed that the constitutional position would be maintained on the status quo, the party committed to the provision's removal formally undertaking, in a government document, to leave it undisturbed. That undertaking, given by the government of Jammu and Kashmir with the concurrence politics of coalition behind it, was the provision's last political shelter. The coalition collapsed in June 2018 when the Bharatiya Janata Party withdrew, Governor's rule followed under Satya Pal Malik, the Assembly was dissolved in November 2018 after competing claims to form a government were advanced, and President's Rule was imposed in December 2018. The sequence delivered the state, by the summer of 2019, into exactly the institutional posture the mechanism required for its final use: no elected government in Srinagar, Parliament exercising the state legislature's powers under President's Rule, and a Governor in office as the Union's appointee. Stage four's lesson is therefore structural. The arrangement survived 1990 to 2019 not because any government of India affirmed it as a compact, but because reversing it required an occasion, and occasions for concurrence based action are manufactured by the state's own political calendar. When the calendar produced a dissolved assembly and central rule, the occasion had arrived, prepared by sixty five years of orders that had already done most of the work the final orders merely completed.

## Stage Five: August 2019 as Consummation, and the Two Novelties

The legal state of the provision on the eve of August 2019 can be stated in one paragraph, and stating it plainly is the erosion thesis in miniature. Article 370 stood in the Constitution. Behind it stood the 1954 Order as amended by about forty seven further orders across sixty five years, the exact count varying with the treatment of amending orders, through which the great bulk of the Indian Constitution already applied to the state. The state's own constitution survived, its permanent resident regime survived, and the formal requirement of concurrence survived. Almost everything else in the original design had been renegotiated by instrument: the head of state, the emergency exposure, the legislative balance, the judicial and electoral machinery, the financial relationship. What remained for a final action was therefore not the dismantling of a working autonomy but the disposal of a residue: apply the whole Constitution at once, retire the state constitution and the resident regime with it, and declare the gateway provision itself inoperative. The orders of August 2019 did precisely that and little more, which is why the long arc reading treats the week as a consummation. The detail of the week belongs to the companion political study; the steps matter here only as mechanism. On 5 August 2019 the President issued Constitution Order 272 under Article 370(1), with the Governor's concurrence given during President's Rule, superseding the 1954 Order and applying the entire Constitution to the state, and amending, through the Article 367 interpretation route, the meaning of the state Constituent Assembly in clause 3 to read as the state Legislative Assembly, whose powers Parliament then exercised. On 6 August 2019 Constitution Order 273, acting on the recommendation so constructed, declared Article 370 inoperative except as a spent shell recording its own end. Parliament carried the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act in the same week, dividing the state into the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir with a legislature and the Union Territory of Ladakh without one, effective 31 October 2019. Home Minister Amit Shah, moving the measures in the Rajya Sabha on 5 August 2019, defended them in the integration school's classic terms: the provision was temporary, it had sheltered corruption and separatism, it had kept the state's people from the benefits of Union law on education, health and reservation, and its end completed the accession that Sardar Patel's generation had begun. Each step used a tool the earlier stages had forged: the order power of Lakhanpal's widest amplitude, the survival doctrine of Sampat Prakash, the concurrence of a Governor during central rule on the pattern of 1986, and the interpretation clause device first aligned in 1965, the whole sequence matching [the architectural-substantive-extension framework](/2020/08/24/ir-coelho-ninth-schedule-2007/) developed for the Ninth Schedule cases, which describes the same constitutional habit from the other side: a device created for a limited purpose, extended by practice into a general instrument, until a court or a government finally uses it at full stretch.

The complication must be faced at full strength, because two elements of August 2019 have no precedent in the earlier stages and the long arc reading understates them at its peril. The first novelty is reflexivity: Article 370 was used to end Article 370. The orders of 1954 to 1986 extended other provisions through the gateway; no earlier order turned the gateway's power on the gateway itself, still less by first redefining, through an interpretation clause, the body whose recommendation clause 3 required. Critics of the 2019 action, among them Noorani in his final writings on the subject and the petitioners' counsel in the Supreme Court, described the route as a constitutional fraud on the text: clause 3's safeguard, the recommendation of a constituent body elected for the purpose, was satisfied by substituting a legislature, then a Parliament acting for that legislature, then a Governor's concurrence arranged by the beneficiary of the substitution. The second novelty is territorial: no earlier stage had contemplated the conversion of the state itself into Union Territories, a change not in the state's constitutional content but in its constitutional existence as a state of the Union, effected by Parliament under Article 3 while the state was under President's Rule and its own legislature's views were taken in the form Parliament chose. A trajectory of content erosion does not, by itself, prepare a change of that order, and an honest adjudication must record that the reorganisation was a rupture in kind even where the abrogation was a culmination in degree.

The erosion reading survives the complication, but in a qualified form, and the qualification is the article's analytical contribution. The final orders were legally loadable onto the trajectory because every doctrinal element they combined had been separately established and separately used: widest amplitude modification, survival past the Assembly, Governor's concurrence during central rule, interpretation clause alignment. Novelty in combination is not novelty in kind for each element, and the 2023 bench would later uphold the action largely on that element by element basis. What the trajectory does not explain is the will to combine the elements and the choice to add territorial reorganisation to the combination. Those belong to the political moment reading, and the two readings are therefore complementary in a definite proportion: the long arc explains the availability of August 2019, the political reading explains its occurrence, and the territorial rupture stands outside both as the week's genuinely new act. This proportion also answers the abrogation specific reading, the narrow legalism that would assess August 2019 as a self contained event. An event whose every instrument was inherited cannot be assessed in isolation from the inheritance. The correct unit of analysis for Article 370 is the sixty five years, and within that unit August 2019 is the final entry, the largest single entry, but an entry in a ledger that others had been keeping since 1954.

## The Judicial Terminus: The Constitution Bench Judgment of 11 December 2023

The Supreme Court's judgment in In Re Article 370 of the Constitution, delivered on 11 December 2023 by a five judge Constitution Bench presided over by Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud, is the arc's judicial terminus, and it is stated here by holding, as the charter of this series requires for judgments. The bench held, unanimously on the central questions, that Article 370 was a temporary provision, its placement in Part XXI and its history in the Constituent Assembly debates establishing a design meant to operate until the state's constitutional relationship was settled. The bench held that Jammu and Kashmir retained no element of internal sovereignty after the Maharaja's accession and the adoption of the state constitution, whose Section 3 declaration of integral status the court treated as conclusive of the point. The bench held that the President's power under clause 3 survived the dissolution of the state Constituent Assembly and could be exercised without that body's recommendation, the recommendation requirement being directory once the recommending body had ceased to exist, and that Sampat Prakash had been correctly decided on the mechanism's survival. The bench upheld Constitution Order 272 in its application of the entire Constitution to the state and Constitution Order 273 in its declaration, while recording that the interpretation clause route to redefining the Constituent Assembly was unnecessary to the result, since the recommendation was not a precondition. The bench upheld the creation of the Union Territory of Ladakh under Article 3. On the conversion of the state of Jammu and Kashmir into a Union Territory, the bench declined to adjudicate, accepting the Solicitor General's assurance that statehood would be restored, directed that restoration take place at the earliest, and directed the Election Commission to hold elections to the state Legislative Assembly by 30 September 2024. Justice Sanjay Kishan Kaul, concurring, added a recommendation for a truth and reconciliation commission to address the human rights violations of the preceding decades in the state. Three opinions structured the decision: the Chief Justice's opinion for himself and Justices Gavai and Surya Kant, Justice Kaul's concurrence, and Justice Sanjiv Khanna's concurrence with both.

Measured against the long arc, the judgment is the trajectory's ratification and its epitaph in one document. The temporariness holding adopts, at the end of the story, the reading that Sampat Prakash had operationalised in 1968 and that Kaul's more compact friendly language of 1959 had left open: the provision was a bridge, and a bridge is judged by the traffic it carried. The court reached the destination by the documentary road this article has walked, citing the orders, the accords and the assurances as the record of a relationship being progressively integrated, rather than as the record of a compact progressively betrayed. Where Noorani's archive reads each order as a step away from a promise, the bench read the same archive as the working out of a design whose clause 1 machinery existed precisely to be used until clause 3 closed it. The judgment does not refute the erosion description; it revalues it. Erosion, in the court's implicit vocabulary, is integration proceeding by the instrument the founders supplied, and the moral charge the word carries in the autonomy literature is discharged by the finding that the destination was written into the provision from 17 October 1949. Readers who accept the bench's premise will find the long arc a story of completion. Readers who hold the Delhi Agreement and the accord of 1975 as promises that bound the Union's honour will find the same arc a story of attrition, and will note that the bench resolved the contest by choosing the Constituent Assembly's text of 1949 over the negotiated understandings of 1952 and 1975 as the authoritative statement of the bargain. The choice is the law of the land. The dispute it settled remains the material of political argument, and the elections the court directed were held in the autumn of 2024, returning an elected government to Srinagar under the Union Territory framework the judgment left in place pending restoration.

### How many subjects could Parliament reach in Jammu and Kashmir in 1950, and how many by 2019?

At the commencement of the Constitution in 1950, Parliament's reach in the state ran to the accession subjects of defence, external affairs and communications, with a short list of applied provisions. By 2019 the accumulated orders had carried the great bulk of the Union and Concurrent Lists into the state.

## Adjudication: Erosion, Integration and the Compact That Was Argued Over

The three scholarly anchors of this article can be weighed against the assembled record. Noorani's constitutional history, published in 2011 after a lifetime in the archive, states the autonomy case at its maximum strength: the accession was conditional in circumstance if not in form, the Delhi Agreement recorded the conditions, and every subsequent extension obtained from governments of doubtful mandate in Srinagar diminished a settlement that honour required the Union to keep. His documentation is the fullest in existence, and this article's stage by stage account is indebted to it at every point where an order's date, text or concurrence is in question. Where Noorani's reading weakens is at the level of mechanism. The orders he chronicles were, with few exceptions, lawful under the provision as the courts construed it, the state legislatures that might have resisted them were repeatedly returned by elections the Valley participated in, and the one decisive judicial opportunity to freeze the mechanism, Sampat Prakash, was lost on arguments the bench answered from the text. A compact that can be lawfully dismantled by its own machinery, over sixty five years, under judicial supervision, is a compact whose terms were the machinery. Noorani proves the attrition beyond argument. The betrayal he builds on it requires a premise, that the understandings of 1952 outrank the text of 1949, which the record states but does not compel.

Anand's commentary of 2006 states the integration case in its most learned form: the provision was a transitional device in a constitution whose federal scheme always contemplated convergence, the orders were the device working, and the state's own constitution, with its unamendable Section 3, recorded the destination in the state's own hand. The strength of the reading is its fit with the documentary direction: sixty five years of instruments point one way, and Anand, who served as Chief Justice during the autonomy resolution crisis of 2000, wrote with a participant's knowledge of how the Union's institutions understood their authority. Its weakness is a corresponding blindness to the concurrence problem. A device that requires the state government's agreement works differently when the agreeing government is the Union's appointee, dismissed ministry or coalition dependent, and the commentary's confidence that form answers substance is least persuasive exactly where the trajectory turned, in 1965, in 1986 and in 2019. Bose's political history of 2003 supplies the missing register: the orders mattered because each one taught the Valley's politics that constitutional forms would not protect a Kashmiri mandate, a lesson whose tuition fees were paid in 1953, in 1984 and in 1987, and whose graduates staffed the movements of 1989 and after. Bose's evidence for the political mechanism is interview based and Srinagar centred, and his reading understates the support that integration commanded in Jammu and Ladakh, regions whose preferences the Valley centred archive habitually discounts. Weighed together, the three readings divide the truth by register: Noorani owns the documents, Anand owns the doctrine, Bose owns the consequences. The long arc claim defended here is the documentary claim, and on the documents it stands. Article 370's content was progressively transferred to the Union's side of the ledger by the provision's own machinery between 1954 and 2019, no transfer was ever reversed, and the final orders closed an account that had been running for sixty five years.

Two honest limits complete the adjudication. The first is regional: the trajectory was experienced as loss chiefly in the Kashmir Valley, as deliverance or indifference in much of Jammu, and in Ladakh as a grievance running the other way, against subordination to Srinagar, which is why the Union Territory of Ladakh in 2019 answered a demand older than the militancy. An erosion map drawn from Delhi's instruments flattens that geography, and the article's claim is a claim about constitutional content, not about a uniform popular experience of it. The second limit is the counterfactual the permanence school is entitled to press: had the state Constituent Assembly recommended the provision's end in 1957, or had Sampat Prakash gone the other way in 1968, the identical founding text would have supported a frozen and permanent special status, defended by the same courts that instead ratified its dissolution. The trajectory was therefore not inevitable in 1949. It became inevitable by accumulation, as each order raised the cost of reversal and each judgment lowered the legal risk of the next order, until by the summer of 2019 the only remaining question was the political one of timing. That is the precise sense in which the August 2019 action consummated rather than disrupted the arc. Disruption was available in 1954, arguable in 1968, attempted in 2000 and refused. By 2019 the ledger had one page left, and the government of the day turned it. The founding generation that designed a temporary provision inside [the founding settlement traced from the Constituent Assembly](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/) onward could not have foreseen the route, but the route ran on rails the founding text laid: a gateway with a ratchet, a concurrence that time made cheap, and a closing clause whose key dissolved in the hand that was meant to turn it.

## The Symbols of the Arrangement: Flag, Offices and a Separate Text

The arrangement's distinctness was never only legal. The Delhi Agreement of July 1952 recorded that the state would have its own flag, flown alongside the Union flag, and the state constitution of 1957 gave that flag constitutional standing, a red banner with a plough and three stripes for the state's three regions. The elected Sadar-i-Riyasat, the Prime Ministerial title for the head of government and the separate constitutional text completed a set of symbols that told residents, in daily administrative life, that the accession settlement was a relationship between two constitutional entities and not a simple absorption. The trajectory of the symbols tracks the trajectory of the law with uncomfortable fidelity. The Prime Ministerial title went in 1965, the elected head of state went in the same amendment, the separate flag lost its standing when the state constitution was retired in 2019, and the separate text itself, amended many times but never replaced, ended with the application of the entire Indian Constitution under Constitution Order 272. Defenders of the arrangement treated the symbols as the visible earnest of a compact. Its critics treated them as the regalia of a separateness that the accession had never licensed. The long arc reading treats them as evidence of what the arrangement was understood to be by the generation that made it, which is why their staged removal, office by office and emblem by emblem, belongs in the same ledger as the orders.

### Was the separate flag of Jammu and Kashmir recognised in the Delhi Agreement of 1952?

The Delhi Agreement of July 1952 recorded that the state would retain its own flag, to be flown alongside the Union flag, and the state constitution of 1957 gave the flag formal standing, a recognition that lasted until the state constitution was retired in 2019.

That symbolic record also disciplines the article's central word. Erosion is sometimes heard as a charge of stealth, of changes smuggled past an inattentive public. The record assembled here shows the opposite. The major changes were announced, debated, litigated and, in several cases, campaigned on: the redesignation of 1965 was enacted by the state's own legislature, the order of 1986 was attacked in print within the year by the state's most persistent constitutional critic, the autonomy resolution of 2000 was a public attempt to reverse the direction, and the action of 2019 was the declared programme of the party that proposed it, debated in Parliament across two days of August. A trajectory can be open and still be one directional. The distinction matters for the adjudication this article has defended, because it separates the descriptive claim, that content moved steadily to the Union's side of the ledger, from any claim about concealment or surprise, which the record does not support. What the record supports is accumulation under publicity: each step visible, each step lawful under the constructions then standing, and the sum of the steps visible only to a reading that takes the whole sixty five years as its unit, which is the reading this article has supplied, in the company of [the wider federal story of the Indian Union](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/) and against the background of the era that produced the provision's final political sponsors.

## Inside the Ledger: What the Orders Carried, Field by Field

A count of orders understates the trajectory unless the orders are sorted by field, because the fields arrived in a sequence that itself tells the story. The first wave, centred on the 1954 Order, carried the machinery of citizenship, courts and elections. Citizenship applied in adapted form, with the permanent resident regime layered over it by Article 35A, so that a resident of the state held Indian citizenship filtered through a state definition that controlled land, employment and settlement. The Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction reached the state, the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir was fitted into the Indian judicial hierarchy, and the Election Commission superintended parliamentary elections in the state from the 1950s, a reach that mattered because the conduct of elections became one of the standing grievances of Kashmiri politics. The Advocate General's office, the state public service arrangements and the audit authority of the Comptroller and Auditor General followed in the early applications, stitching the state's administration into the Union's accountability frame within the first decade.

The second wave carried money and services. The Union's financial provisions, the distribution of taxes, borrowing powers and the Finance Commission framework reached the state by order, so that by the middle 1960s the state's budgetary life ran inside the same fiscal constitution as every other state, a dependence that deepened with each plan period. The All India Services, the Indian Administrative Service and the Indian Police Service, were extended to the state cadre, which meant that the senior administration of Jammu and Kashmir was staffed through the same services, examinations and cadre rules as the rest of the Union. Noorani's archive treats the services extension as one of the least debated and most effective of the integrations, because it changed who governed in Srinagar at the level of the district and the secretariat without requiring any headline constitutional event. Anand's commentary treats the same extension as administrative common sense in a single civil service market. Both descriptions fit the instrument. The argument is over the description's moral, not over the order's text.

The third wave carried coercive and preventive law, and it is the wave that generated the litigation through which the courts consolidated the mechanism. Article 35C, carried by the 1954 Order, sheltered the state's preventive detention legislation from fundamental rights challenge for five years, and the orders of 1959 and 1964 renewed and lengthened that shelter to ten and then fifteen years, the renewals that Sampat Prakash challenged and the Supreme Court sustained. The Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act reached the state by a different route, through Parliament's defence competence after the state was declared disturbed in 1990, and it operated alongside the state's own Public Safety Act of 1978, a detention statute enacted by Sheikh Abdullah's own government under the state's constitution. The layering matters for an honest ledger. Not every coercive instrument in the state arrived from Delhi, and the autonomy literature's strongest passages acknowledge that Srinagar's governments used detention powers with their own hands. The trajectory claim does not require Delhi to have authored every restraint. It requires only the direction of the constitutional amendments to the arrangement, and in that ledger the entries run one way: detention shelters renewed by presidential order, emergency articles applied by presidential order, and no order in the series restoring a lapsed immunity or withdrawing an extended power.

The fourth wave carried the Union List deep into fields the accession schedule had never named. By the end of the process, counts of the applied provisions converged on a position in which the overwhelming bulk of the Union List and a large part of the Concurrent List operated in the state, with the residuary power, uniquely in the Indian federation, resting with the state legislature under the state constitution rather than with Parliament. The residuary survival is the integration school's favourite counterexample and it deserves its place in the ledger: on paper, the state's claim to legislate on unenumerated subjects outlasted almost every other element of the 1949 design. Its practical weight shrank as the lists filled around it, because a residuary claim over a shrinking unenumerated field is a claim over very little. The erosion map is therefore not a claim that nothing survived. It is a claim that what survived was, by 2019, a residue whose contents can be listed in a paragraph: a state constitution, a permanent resident regime, a land protection regime resting on that regime, a residuary legislative claim, a separate flag with constitutional standing, and the unexercised closing clause of Article 370 itself. Stage five disposed of the entire list in one week, which is the measure of how short the list had become.

## The Debate in Its Own Terms: Three Constituencies, Three Archives

The argument over Article 370 was never a two sided seminar, and the long arc can be misread if its constituencies are collapsed into a single autonomy camp facing a single integration camp. In the Kashmir Valley, the arrangement's defence was carried for two generations by the National Conference and its rivals in turn, and its emotional centre was the pledge of 1952: Sheikh Abdullah told the state Constituent Assembly, and repeated for the rest of his life, that accession had been a partnership with a secular India whose terms Delhi was bound to honour, a position his political testament and his party's manifestos restated in 1975, in 1996 and in 2000. In Jammu, the oldest organised position ran the other way. The Praja Parishad's agitation of 1952 and 1953, aligned with Syama Prasad Mookerjee's Bharatiya Jana Sangh, demanded full application of the Indian Constitution to the state under a slogan that rejected two constitutions, two heads and two flags in one country, and Mookerjee's death in custody in Srinagar in June 1953, after he entered the state defying the permit system, made the integration demand a founding martyrology of the Hindu right. The demand travelled from the Jana Sangh into the Bharatiya Janata Party's manifestos, including the manifesto of 2014 and the manifesto of 2019, which promised the provision's removal in terms. Arun Jaitley, the party's leading constitutional voice, argued in his published writings and speeches that the provision had worked against the state's own residents, freezing investment, land markets and the reach of protective Union legislation, and that its temporary drafting was the founders' own verdict on it.

Ladakh supplied the third constituency, and its archive corrects both of the others. Ladakhi organisations, including the Ladakh Buddhist Association, demanded separation from Kashmir and direct Union administration from 1989 onward, on the stated ground that the accession settlement, whatever it promised Srinagar, had delivered Ladakh into subordination to a Valley dominated state government. The demand was repeated by Ladakhi members in Parliament across parties and was met, in part, by the Ladakh Autonomous Hill Development Council Act of 1995, which created elected councils for Leh and later Kargil without touching the constitutional position. When the Reorganisation Act of 2019 made Ladakh a Union Territory without a legislature, the response in Leh was celebration and in Kargil protest, a split that itself records how little a single Kashmiri interest ever described the old state's peoples. A long arc account that treats the provision's end as a transaction between Delhi and the Valley alone misses the constituency that had asked, for thirty years, to be let out of the arrangement altogether.

The scholarly archive maps onto these constituencies without being reducible to them. Noorani wrote as the Valley's counsel in the court of history, with a barrister's fidelity to documents and a litigant's choice among them. Anand wrote as a judge of the Union's own court, for whom the state's integration was the Constitution's ordinary business and its incompleteness an administrative untidiness. Bose wrote as a political scientist of the conflict, for whom the texts were moves in a struggle over consent and the Valley's alienation the datum every text had to explain. A fourth voice belongs in the record because it unsettles the first three: Karan Singh, who as Sadar-i-Riyasat signed the dismissal of Sheikh Abdullah in 1953, who chaired the State Autonomy Committee that recommended rollback in 1999, and whose public statements across six decades defended both the accession's finality and the autonomy bargain's honour. His career is the trajectory lived by one man in both directions, and his committee's report remains the one official document in which the Indian state system, through a government of the state itself, formally adopted the erosion description and proposed its reversal. The Union Cabinet's rejection of that proposal in July 2000 is therefore not a footnote to the debate. It is the debate's only binding verdict before 2019, delivered by the political branch, on the fullest record the autonomy case ever assembled.

## The Residue Under Litigation and Pledge, 2014 to 2019

The final five years of the fourth stage placed the residue under simultaneous pressure in court and in manifesto, and the pressure explains why the political occasion of 2019 found the provision defended by fewer hands than at any earlier point. Article 35A, the permanent resident provision carried by the 1954 Order, came under direct challenge in the Supreme Court in 2014 in a petition by the organisation We the Citizens, which argued that a provision inserted by presidential order, without the amending procedure of Article 368, could not stand as constitutional text, and in a further petition in 2017 concerning the rights of women married outside the state. The hearings were adjourned repeatedly, with the Union and the state taking shifting positions, and the provision lived its last years under a judicial question mark that the court never finally answered, because the orders of August 2019 removed the subject matter. The litigation mattered beyond its pleadings. It advertised, to every reader of a newspaper in the state, that the protective core of the arrangement rested on an instrument whose pedigree the integration school had always contested, and it gave the provision's opponents a courtroom theatre in which the anomaly argument could be rehearsed at length without the risks of a parliamentary vote.

The manifesto pressure ran in parallel. The Bharatiya Janata Party's election documents of 2014 committed the party to discussing the provision's future with stakeholders, language its opponents read as a holding position, and the manifesto of 2019 stated the commitment to remove it without qualification, alongside the abrogation of the temporary provisions in the party's long standing programme. The party's coalition partner in the state government from 2015, the People's Democratic Party, had extracted the Agenda of Alliance undertaking to maintain the status quo, and the undertaking held exactly as long as the coalition held. Its collapse in June 2018, followed by Governor's rule, the dissolution of the Assembly in November 2018 and President's Rule that December, removed the last institutional obstacle whose removal the mechanism required. Read through the long arc, the years 2014 to 2019 are not a separate story of sudden radicalisation. They are the stage at which the residue, thinned by sixty years of orders to a state constitution, a residency regime and a flag, lost in sequence its judicial security, its coalition shelter and its elected government, in that order, before the final instruments were signed. The legal state of the provision on the eve of August 2019, described at the opening of the stage five account, was the product of that sequence, and the sequence is why a single week sufficed.

## The Paper Trail in the Archives: How an Order Was Made

The under cited evidence for the trajectory lies neither in the great judgments nor in the memoirs, but in the files that produced the orders themselves, the correspondence between the Union Home Ministry and the successive governments in Srinagar in which each extension was proposed, negotiated, resisted or conceded. A presidential order reads as a unilateral act, and in form it is one: the President issues, the Gazette publishes, the provision applies. The files behind the published texts, preserved in part at the National Archives of India and in the state's own archives, record a more granular reality. An extension typically began as a Union proposal, often attached to an administrative need, a service rule, a tax schedule, a court fee, an election roll. The state government's concurrence was sought by letter, and the replies in the early decades, under Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad and Ghulam Mohammed Sadiq, show a Srinagar administration that occasionally bargained over drafting and timing while conceding the substance as a matter of course. In later decades the correspondence thins, because the practice had hardened into routine: the order amending the 1954 text was prepared in Delhi, the concurrence obtained as a formality of office, and the Gazette notification closed the matter. A researcher tracing the trajectory through the files learns the same lesson the judgments teach, at the level of stationery. The mechanism's genius, from the Union's side, was that it converted constitutional change into correspondence, and correspondence has no opposition benches.

The archival record also preserves the moments when the routine broke. The negotiations behind the Delhi Agreement of 1952 generated a paper trail of drafts and reservations, including the exchanges over the head of state's election and the emergency clauses, that show both governments aware they were writing terms meant to last. The accord negotiations of 1974 and 1975 produced the Beg and Parthasarathy correspondence and the internal notes in which Sheikh Abdullah's demand for a review of the post 1953 orders was recorded and set aside. The State Autonomy Committee of 1996 to 1999 worked from the files in the open, printing the orders in its annexures and inviting the conclusion its report reached. Each rupture in the routine generated the documents by which the routine itself can be judged, which is why the autonomy archive and the integration archive, for all their opposed verdicts, cite the same paper. The long arc reading is, at bottom, a decision to let that shared paper outrank the polemics written over it: to count what the orders did, in the order in which they did it, and to treat every later speech, including the parliamentary debates of August 2019 and the Supreme Court's judgment of December 2023, as commentary on a ledger that the files had already closed.

## The State Constitution's Own Life, 1957 to 2019

One thread of the trajectory runs through Srinagar rather than Delhi, and it complicates any account in which the state figures only as the passive object of Union instruments. The Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir lived an active life of its own between 1957 and 2019, amended repeatedly by the state legislature for state purposes: restructuring local governance, extending panchayati institutions in adapted form, adjusting the terms and composition of the legislature, and, in 1965, performing the redesignation of the head of state and the head of government that the autonomy literature counts as the trajectory's darkest entry. That amendment was the state's own act in form, carried by its legislature under its constitution, and the integration school has never tired of pointing out that the elected representatives of Jammu and Kashmir voted the elected Sadar-i-Riyasat out of existence. The autonomy school replies that the legislature of 1965 sat in the political conditions created by 1953, with the state's dominant leader only lately released from detention and the ruling party's organisation fused with the Union's ruling party, so that the form of a state act clothed a Delhi preference. The dispute is genuine and it is the whole argument in miniature: at every station of the long arc, the instrument is regular and the conditions are contested, and the reader's verdict on the trajectory follows from the weight given to each.

The state constitution also carried provisions that cut against the erosion direction and survived for decades, and their survival belongs in the ledger beside the losses. The permanent resident definition, resting on the state subject notifications of 1927 and 1932 issued by Maharaja Hari Singh long before accession, was carried into the state constitution's own scheme and protected by Article 35A at the Union level, a double lock that kept land and public employment in the state closed to outsiders for the whole life of the arrangement. The state's land reforms of the early 1950s, among the most radical in India, abolishing large holdings without compensation, were shielded by the state constitution's own text and were never undone by any order from Delhi, a fact the integration school cites to show that the arrangement protected real Kashmiri choices and the autonomy school cites to show what the arrangement was for. The state legislature's residuary competence, the separate flag, the six year term of the state Assembly against the Union pattern of five, each survived into the final decade. The residue inventoried in this article was thin by 2019, but it was not fictitious, and the political fight of August 2019 was a fight over something real: a working, if narrowed, constitutional distinctness whose remaining contents had organised land, jobs, identity and electoral politics in the state for three generations. The long arc explains how the distinctness narrowed. It does not argue that what remained was nothing, and the vehemence of the final contest, in Parliament, in the Valley and in the Supreme Court across sixteen days of hearings in 2023, is unintelligible on any other footing.

## Measuring Erosion: A Note on Method

A sceptical reader is entitled to ask how erosion is measured, since the word can be made to carry any quantity a writer chooses. The measure used throughout this article has three tests, each applied to the instruments rather than to impressions. The first test is textual: at a given date, how much of the Indian Constitution applied to the state, and on whose initiative could the remainder be withheld? In 1950 the answer was a short schedule and a state veto through concurrence. By 2019 the answer was the whole document in waiting, withheld only until a single order was signed. The second test is institutional: which offices in the state owed their existence and tenure to the state's own constitution, and which to Union appointment or Union law? The head of state crossed that line in 1965, the senior administration through the services extensions, the emergency government through Articles 356 and 357, and the legislature's own survival, after the dissolution of November 2018, to the Union's discretion under President's Rule. The third test is reversibility: could a determined state government, at a given date, lawfully recover a field already extended? In 1954 the question was untested. By 2000 it had been answered by the Union Cabinet in the negative, on the one occasion when recovery was formally demanded through the channel the system provided. A reading that passes all three tests at every stage from 1954 to 2019, and finds the same direction in each, is not an impression. It is a measurement, and it is the measurement on which the claim of this article rests, open to any reader to repeat against the Gazette texts, the Constituent Assembly debates of 17 October 1949, the judgments from Prem Nath Kaul to In Re Article 370, and the scholarly archives of Noorani, Anand and Bose, which agree on the instruments even where they divide on everything else.

The method also explains the place this article gives to dates. A long arc argument lives or dies by its chronology, because a trajectory assembled from instruments can be checked only if every instrument carries its day and year: the accession of 26 October 1947, the adoption of 17 October 1949, the order of 14 May 1954, the renewals of 1959 and 1964, the redesignation of 1965, the judgment in Sampat Prakash in 1968, the accord of February 1975, the order of 1986, the committee report of June 1999, the Assembly resolution of 26 June 2000 and the Cabinet refusal of 4 July 2000, the orders of 5 and 6 August 2019 and the judgment of 11 December 2023. Where a date in the brief for this article diverged from the primary record, the record has been followed and the divergence flagged in the handoff that accompanies the article, in line with the standing practice of this series. Chronology is the argument's skeleton. The flesh is the concurrence files, the judgments and the three scholarly archives, and the verdict, that the August 2019 action consummated a sixty five year trajectory, is offered as the conclusion those materials jointly support when they are read at the scale of the whole period rather than at the scale of the final week.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Article 370?

Article 370 was the provision of the Indian Constitution, adopted on 17 October 1949 and placed in Part XXI, that governed the constitutional relationship between the Union of India and the State of Jammu and Kashmir. It limited Parliament's legislative power over the state to the subjects of the Instrument of Accession, defence, external affairs and communications, unless the state government concurred in wider legislation. It allowed the President to apply other provisions of the Constitution to the state by order, with exceptions and modifications, and it allowed the state to frame its own constitution. Clause 3 permitted the President to declare the provision inoperative on the recommendation of the state Constituent Assembly.

### Q: When was Article 370 adopted?

The Constituent Assembly of India adopted the provision on 17 October 1949, when N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar moved the draft article numbered 306A and defended it as a provisional arrangement shaped by war in the state, the reference of the Kashmir question to the United Nations and the commitment to ascertain the will of the state's people. The provision entered the Constitution as Article 370 when the document commenced on 26 January 1950, and it stood in Part XXI among the temporary, transitional and special provisions. Its marginal heading described it as a temporary provision with respect to the State of Jammu and Kashmir, a description whose weight the Supreme Court assessed repeatedly, in 1959, in 1968 and in December 2023.

### Q: What is the 1954 Order?

The Constitution (Application to Jammu and Kashmir) Order of 14 May 1954 was the presidential order, issued under Article 370(1) with the concurrence of the state government of Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad, that first applied the Indian Constitution to the state in bulk. It superseded the limited order of January 1950 and carried into the state most of the Constitution's operative provisions, including citizenship in adapted form, the fundamental rights with exceptions and modifications, and defined jurisdictions for the Supreme Court and the Election Commission. The order also inserted Article 35A on permanent residents. Every later extension proceeded by amending the 1954 text, which made the order the ledger into which the whole trajectory was entered.

### Q: How many Constitutional Orders modified Article 370?

The brief for the long arc counts approximately forty seven presidential orders between the founding order of 14 May 1954 and the final orders of August 2019 that extended provisions of the Indian Constitution to Jammu and Kashmir or amended the 1954 Order's application. Counts in the literature vary, because writers differ on whether purely amending orders, orders under related clauses and the final pair of 2019 are numbered in the same series. The variation does not affect the trajectory's shape. The orders run from 1954 through the restructuring orders of 1964 and 1965, the order of 1986 on Article 249, and the orders of the President's Rule decades, ending with Constitution Order 272 of 5 August 2019 and Constitution Order 273 of 6 August 2019.

### Q: What is Article 35A?

Article 35A was a provision inserted into the Constitution as applied to Jammu and Kashmir by the presidential order of 14 May 1954, rather than by a parliamentary amendment under Article 368. It empowered the state legislature to define the classes of permanent residents of the state and to confer on those residents rights and privileges in public employment, acquisition of immovable property, settlement in the state and scholarships, from which citizens from other parts of India could be excluded. The autonomy school treated the provision as a protective core of the accession settlement, guarding land and employment in a small border state. The integration school treated it as an anomaly created by executive order. It ceased to operate when Constitution Order 272 applied the entire Constitution in August 2019.

### Q: What was the August 2019 action?

The August 2019 action was a sequence of instruments carried through in one parliamentary week. On 5 August 2019 the President issued Constitution Order 272 under Article 370(1), with the concurrence of the Governor during President's Rule, superseding the order of 1954 and applying the entire Indian Constitution to Jammu and Kashmir, and amending the interpretation of the state Constituent Assembly in clause 3 through Article 367. On 6 August 2019 Constitution Order 273 declared Article 370 inoperative. Parliament then enacted the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, dividing the state into the Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir with a legislature and the Union Territory of Ladakh without one, effective from 31 October 2019.

### Q: How is Article 370's long-arc different from abrogation?

The long arc reading and the abrogation account describe the same history at different scales. The abrogation account concentrates on the instruments of 5 and 6 August 2019 and asks whether each step was lawful and what politically produced it. The long arc reading takes the sixty five years from the order of 14 May 1954 as its unit and asks what content remained in the provision when the final orders arrived. On the long arc evidence, the provision's substance had been transferred piece by piece to the Union through about forty seven orders, the courts had confirmed the mechanism's survival in 1968, and a formal attempt to reverse the direction had been refused in July 2000. The August 2019 action therefore appears as the consummation of a trajectory rather than the sudden destruction of an intact settlement.

### Q: Whose concurrence did a presidential order under Article 370 require?

For provisions beyond the subjects of the Instrument of Accession, Article 370(1) required the concurrence of the government of Jammu and Kashmir before the President could apply a provision of the Indian Constitution to the state or extend Parliament's legislative reach there. For accession subjects themselves, consultation sufficed. The identity of the concurring government changed the mechanism's meaning across the decades. In the 1950s concurrence came from ministries in Srinagar whose mandates were contested after Sheikh Abdullah's dismissal in August 1953. After the redesignation of 1965 the concurring authority could be a Governor appointed by the President, and during spells of Governor's rule or President's Rule, as in 1986 and in 2019, the concurrence was given by the Union's own appointee, a feature critics called the collapse of a two key design into one key.

### Q: What did Puranlal Lakhanpal hold about the presidential power to modify?

In Puranlal Lakhanpal v President of India, decided in 1961, the Supreme Court upheld a presidential order issued under Article 370(1) against the argument that the power to apply provisions with modifications was limited to minor adjustments. The court held that the word modification in this setting must be given its widest amplitude, extending even to a radical alteration of a provision in its application to Jammu and Kashmir. The holding mattered for the whole trajectory, because it dissolved the boundary between applying the Constitution to the state and remaking provisions for the state by order. A. G. Noorani treated the judgment as the point at which the court surrendered that distinction. Defenders answered that the text of clause 1(d), explained by its mover in 1949, left the bench little room for a narrower reading.

### Q: What did Sampat Prakash decide about the survival of Article 370?

Sampat Prakash v State of Jammu and Kashmir, decided in 1968 and reported in 1970, arose from a preventive detention challenge to the orders of 1959 and 1964 that had extended the shelter of Article 35C. The petitioner argued that Article 370 had spent itself when the state Constituent Assembly dispersed in 1957 without recommending its end. A five judge bench rejected the argument, holding that the provision continued in force because the conditions that produced it continued, that no recommendation under clause 3 had been made, and that the President's power under clause 1 therefore remained available from time to time. The judgment converted the Assembly's disappearance from a possible terminus into the mechanism's charter, and every later order, including the final pair of 2019, rested on its reasoning.

### Q: What changed when Articles 356 and 357 were extended to Jammu and Kashmir?

The presidential order of 1964 extended Articles 356 and 357, the Union's emergency provisions for failure of constitutional machinery in a state, to Jammu and Kashmir, from which they had been held back in the earlier applications. Article 356 allowed the President, on the Governor's report, to assume the functions of the state government, and Article 357 allowed Parliament to exercise the state legislature's powers during such a proclamation. The extension supplied the authority under which the state was governed directly from Delhi for long periods, most extensively during the President's Rule of July 1990 to October 1996, and again in the sequence that preceded August 2019. A field the founding design had reserved became the ordinary channel of central administration.

### Q: How did the Sadar-i-Riyasat become a Governor appointed by the President?

Under the Delhi Agreement of 1952 and the state constitution of 1957, the head of Jammu and Kashmir was the Sadar-i-Riyasat, elected by the state legislature for a term of five years, a deliberate mark of the accession settlement's distinctness. The sixth amendment of the state constitution in 1965, enacted by the state legislature under Chief Minister Ghulam Mohammed Sadiq, replaced the elected office with a Governor appointed by the President of India, and replaced the title of Prime Minister of the state with Chief Minister. A presidential order aligned the references in the Indian Constitution through the interpretation clause. In Mohammad Maqbool Damnoo in 1972 the Supreme Court recognised the Governor as the successor in substance of the older office.

### Q: What did Section 3 of the Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir declare?

Section 3 of the Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir, adopted by the state Constituent Assembly on 17 November 1956 and effective from 26 January 1957, declared that the state is and shall remain an integral part of the Union of India. Section 147 of the same constitution placed that declaration beyond the amending power of the state legislature. The permanence school cited the two sections as the state's own entrenchment of the Union relationship, adopted by the very Assembly whose recommendation Article 370(3) required. The integration school cited them as proof that accession was final in the state's own hand. The Constitution Bench of December 2023 treated Section 3 as conclusive evidence that Jammu and Kashmir retained no internal sovereignty after accession.

### Q: How did the accord of February 1975 between Indira Gandhi and Sheikh Abdullah treat Article 370?

The accord of February 1975, negotiated between Mirza Afzal Beg for Sheikh Abdullah and G. Parthasarathy for Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, recorded that Jammu and Kashmir would continue to be governed by Article 370 and that the Union's legislative authority in the state would remain as it stood. Sheikh Abdullah returned as Chief Minister on that basis. The autonomy school reads the accord as proof that the trajectory could be halted by political will, since the framework was reviewed at the highest level and expressly preserved. The erosion reading notes that Sheikh Abdullah had sought restoration of the pre 1953 position, including the elected head of state, and that the published accord conceded none of it, freezing the arrangement at its post 1965 state.

### Q: What did the presidential order of 1986 on Article 249 change?

The presidential order of 1986 extended Article 249 of the Indian Constitution to Jammu and Kashmir. Article 249 empowers the Rajya Sabha, by a resolution supported by two thirds of members present and voting in the national interest, to authorise Parliament to legislate on a subject in the State List for a renewable period. Its extension meant that a field reserved to the state legislature could be opened to Union legislation by a vote in a chamber where the state held few seats. The concurrence for the order was given by Governor Jagmohan during Governor's rule, after the dismissal of Farooq Abdullah's government, a circumstance that led A. G. Noorani to treat the order as the clearest case of the concurrence mechanism collapsing into a single key.

### Q: What did the State Autonomy Committee recommend in its report?

The State Autonomy Committee, appointed by the National Conference government elected in 1996 and chaired by Karan Singh, the former Sadar-i-Riyasat, submitted its report in June 1999. It recommended restoring the constitutional position of Jammu and Kashmir broadly to its state before 1953: the Union's competence confined to defence, foreign affairs, communications and allied subjects, the elected head of state restored, and the jurisdiction of Union institutions such as the Supreme Court and the Election Commission rolled back toward the accession terms. A companion report on regional autonomy, associated with Balraj Puri, addressed devolution within the state. The committee's report was the fullest official statement of the autonomy case, and it amounted to a formal finding by the state's own government that the post 1953 extensions ought to be undone.

### Q: What did the Jammu and Kashmir Assembly resolve in June 2000 and how did the Union Cabinet respond?

On 26 June 2000 the Jammu and Kashmir Legislative Assembly adopted a resolution endorsing the State Autonomy Committee's report and seeking restoration of the state's constitutional position broadly as it stood before 1953. The Union Cabinet of Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee considered the resolution on 4 July 2000 and rejected it as unacceptable, recording that acceptance would set the clock back and reverse the process of harmonising the aspirations of the state's people with the integrity of the nation, while affirming devolution of powers to states in general. The refusal came within eight days, from a coalition government in which the National Conference itself held ministerial office at the Union level, and it left every presidential order in place.

### Q: What does A. G. Noorani argue in his 2011 constitutional history of Article 370?

In Article 370: A Constitutional History of Jammu and Kashmir, published in 2011, A. G. Noorani assembled the fullest documentary archive of the provision, order by order, from the Constituent Assembly debates of October 1949 to the extensions of the following decades. His argument holds that the accession settlement and the Delhi Agreement of 1952 recorded terms that bound the Union, that the extensions were obtained from state governments of contested mandate after Sheikh Abdullah's dismissal in 1953, and that the cumulative effect was the hollowing of a solemn compact by its own machinery. Noorani treated the Lakhanpal judgment of 1961, the redesignation of 1965 and the order of 1986 as the trajectory's decisive stations. His documentation is relied on across the scholarly divide, including by writers who reject his conclusions.

### Q: How do defenders of the presidential orders answer the erosion thesis?

Defenders of the orders, represented in their most learned form by A. S. Anand's The Constitution of Jammu and Kashmir: Its Development and Comments of 2006, answer that the provision was a transitional device inside a constitution whose federal scheme contemplated convergence, that each order was issued under the text with the concurrence the text required, and that the state's own constitution entrenched the Union relationship in Section 3. On this reading the orders were the device working as designed, not a compact being betrayed, and the Supreme Court's constructions in Lakhanpal and Sampat Prakash followed the words the Constituent Assembly adopted. The answer's exposed point is the concurrence question: it treats the form of agreement as sufficient even in periods when the agreeing authority in Srinagar was the Union's own appointee.

### Q: Why did the Constitution Bench of December 2023 decline to decide the conversion of Jammu and Kashmir into a Union Territory?

In In Re Article 370, decided on 11 December 2023, the five judge Constitution Bench upheld the abrogation of the provision, held it to have been temporary, upheld the creation of the Union Territory of Ladakh under Article 3, and declined to adjudicate the validity of converting the state of Jammu and Kashmir itself into a Union Territory. The bench rested that restraint on the Solicitor General's assurance, recorded in the judgment, that statehood would be restored to Jammu and Kashmir. It directed that restoration take place at the earliest and that the Election Commission hold elections to the Legislative Assembly by 30 September 2024. Justice Kaul's concurrence recommended a truth and reconciliation commission for the violations of the preceding decades.
