Turning analysis into argument on Gatsby essays is the single skill that separates the students who describe the novel from the students who say something about it. Most essays on The Great Gatsby fail in exactly the same place, and it is not a lack of knowledge. The failing essay knows the green light, knows Daisy's voice, knows Nick's narration, knows the Valley of Ashes, and still earns a middling mark, because knowing those things is analysis, and analysis is not argument. The namable claim of this guide, the sentence worth carrying into every essay you write on this novel, is this: analysis is the evidence; argument is the case. Turning Gatsby analysis into argument means making a contestable claim that the analysis then supports, and the move is from accurate observation to defended position. The essay that only analyzes describes brilliantly while saying nothing; the essay that argues uses the same analysis to prove a point. Everything that follows is the working out of that sentence: how to tell the two apart, how to perform the conversion, how to practice it until it is automatic, and how to keep the distinction alive through a whole essay under pressure.

![A student converting Gatsby analysis notes into a structured argument outline with claim statements - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-58.webp)

The distinction needs a concrete picture before it needs a definition, because most students believe they already understand it and are wrong. Picture two essays answering the same question, an essay on Nick Carraway as narrator. The first essay observes that Nick opens by claiming he reserves judgment, then shows him judging Tom, Daisy, Jordan, and Gatsby across the chapters, then notes the contradiction, then closes by saying Nick is a complex and interesting narrator. Every observation is accurate. Every sentence is well written. The essay says nothing, because nothing in it could be disagreed with. No reader could respond, no, Nick does not judge people, because he obviously does. The second essay takes the same observations and opens with a claim: Nick's judgments are not failures of his stated method but the method itself, a performance of moral authority that lets him condemn the Buchanans while protecting Gatsby, which means his unreliability is strategic rather than accidental. That sentence can be argued against. A reader could counter that Nick's judgments are inconsistent rather than strategic, or that his protection of Gatsby is emotional rather than calculated. The disagreement is the proof that an argument exists. Both essays performed analysis. Only the second turned it into argument, and the difference in marks between them is the difference between the middle band and the top.

The complication this guide addresses honestly is the one hiding inside that comparison: mistaking thorough analysis for argument. It is the most flattering mistake a strong student can make, because it feels like excellence. The student reads closely, notices the exact wording of a line, traces an image across three chapters, connects a symbol to a theme, and believes the thoroughness itself constitutes the argument. It does not. Analysis describes what the text does; argument makes a contestable claim the analysis supports. A paragraph that explains how the green light changes meaning across its three appearances is analysis, however brilliant. A paragraph that uses that same tracking to claim that the light's meaning narrows when Daisy is present and widens when she is absent, and that this pattern proves the novel separates desire from its object, is argument, because a reader could contest it. The analysis in both paragraphs is identical. The claim is what differs, and the claim is what the examiner pays for. This article owns the conversion skill itself: the move from the observation to the position. The mechanics of building the claim sentence live in the thesis guide, and the standard the conversion must reach lives in the top-band guide; here, the work is the transformation, practiced until the hand does it without being told.

## Why Description Earns Nothing: The Analysis Trap

The analysis trap has a shape worth recognizing, because students fall into it in predictable ways and the predictability is the way out. The trap begins with a good habit, close reading, and ends with a dead essay, and the path between them is paved with sentences that explain without asserting. The typical trapped paragraph opens with a topic sentence that announces a subject rather than a position: "The green light is an important symbol in the novel." It continues with three or four sentences of accurate, even sensitive, analysis: the light appears at the end of the first chapter, it appears again when Gatsby shows Daisy his house across the bay, it returns in Nick's closing meditation, and each appearance carries a slightly different charge. It closes with a sentence that restates the topic in past tense: "Therefore the green light shows how symbols can change meaning." The paragraph is not wrong. It is inert. Nothing in it risks anything, and an examiner reading it can only tick the boxes for knowledge and understanding while leaving the boxes for argument and evaluation empty. The student who writes ten such paragraphs has written an essay of pure analysis, and the mark will sit exactly where pure analysis sits: safe, respectable, and far below what the student's ability deserved.

What makes the trap cruel is that the student inside it usually knows more than the student outside it. The descriptive writer has often done the harder reading. The arguer who earns the higher mark may have noticed fewer things. The difference is not intelligence or effort; it is the presence of a claim, and the claim is a separate act from the noticing. This is why telling trapped students to "analyze more" makes the problem worse. They analyze more, the paragraphs grow longer and more impressive, and the essay remains claimless, because analysis was never the missing ingredient. The missing ingredient is the sentence that says what the analysis proves, stated in a form a reasonable reader could dispute. Until that sentence exists, the analysis is material waiting for a purpose, and the examiner cannot award argument marks to material, only to purpose.

The description error this guide names is the paragraph that mistakes explanation for position, and it deserves its moment of honest accounting because it is the single most common ceiling on Gatsby essays. The error has tells. The topic sentence names a topic: "Daisy's voice is described in unusual terms." The sentences that follow unpack the description: the voice is full of money, it is low and thrilling, Nick returns to it repeatedly. The closing sentence summarizes the unpacking: "This shows that Fitzgerald uses Daisy's voice to characterize her." A reader who disagrees with that final sentence would have to claim Fitzgerald does not use the voice to characterize her, which is barely a position at all, more a refusal to read. Compare the argumentative version: "Daisy's voice is the novel's proof that charm is a financial instrument, which is why Nick can be seduced by it while understanding exactly what it costs." That sentence can be fought. A reader could argue the voice proves the opposite, that charm resists financial reduction, or that Nick is not seduced at all. The fight is the mark. The full catalog of ways essays slip into description, and the repairs for each, is mapped in the [guide to common Gatsby essay mistakes](/2030/08/05/gatsby-essay-mistakes/), which treats the description error as the central fault it is; this guide treats it as the starting point of the conversion, the raw material the method works on.

## Turning Analysis into Argument on Gatsby: The Conversion Method

The conversion from observation to claim is not inspiration. It is a procedure, and like every procedure it can be learned, drilled, and performed under pressure. The method has four moves, and they always run in the same order, because each move needs the output of the one before it. First, state the observation plainly, in one sentence, with the textual anchor attached: what the text does, where, in which words. Second, ask what the observation proves beyond itself, which is the question most students skip, because the observation feels complete and the mind is satisfied. Third, write the answer as a sentence someone could disagree with, which is the conversion itself, the moment analysis becomes argument. Fourth, check the sentence against the contestability test and the evidence test: could a reasonable reader dispute it, and does the analysis actually support it rather than merely accompany it. Four moves, always in order, and the essay is built by repeating them for every paragraph. The method is unglamorous. It works anyway.

The second move is where the conversion actually happens, so it deserves the closest attention. Asking what an observation proves beyond itself is a discipline of dissatisfaction. The observation "Nick claims to reserve judgment and then judges everyone" is satisfying on its own; it feels like a complete insight. The dissatisfaction asks: so what? What follows from the contradiction? Does it prove Nick is a hypocrite, or that his judgments are the novel's real moral center, or that reserving judgment was always a pose he adopts to gain the reader's trust? Each answer is a different claim, each supportable by the same observation, and the choice among them is the argumentative act. The student who stops at the observation has analysis. The student who answers the so-what question has the beginning of a claim. The habit to build is never letting an observation stand alone in a paragraph without its so-what sentence beside it. The so-what sentence is the claim in embryo, and the paragraph's job is to grow it.

The third move, writing the answer as a disputable sentence, is where most conversions die, because students write the answer as a stronger observation rather than as a claim. "Nick's contradiction reveals his complexity" is a stronger observation, but it is still undisputable, because no one would argue Nick is simple. The test is concrete: imagine a smart reader who has read the novel carefully, and ask whether that reader could sincerely say "I disagree." If the answer is no, the sentence is not yet a claim. "Nick's judgments are the novel's moral compass, and his condemnation of Tom and Daisy is the one judgment the narrative never undercuts" can be disagreed with, because a reader could point to the moments Nick excuses Gatsby's criminality as proof the compass is broken. That disagreement is not a threat to the essay; it is the essay's engine. A claim that cannot be opposed cannot be defended, and a paragraph that defends nothing is description wearing argument's clothes.

The fourth move is the quality control that keeps the method honest. The contestability test asks whether the claim can be opposed; the evidence test asks whether the analysis actually supports the claim or only sits near it. The evidence test catches the most sophisticated failure mode, the claim that is genuinely contestable but unconnected to the analysis that supposedly proves it. A student writes a fine observation about the color imagery in the party scenes, then claims the novel condemns the American Dream, and the paragraph reads as two good halves that never meet. The claim is contestable. The analysis is strong. The connection is missing, because nothing in the color imagery as analyzed actually proves the condemnation; the student has smuggled in a conclusion the evidence does not carry. The repair is to shrink the claim until the evidence can carry it, or to widen the evidence until it reaches the claim. The method demands both tests every time, because a claim that fails either one is not an argument yet.

### What drill builds the conversion habit fastest?

The two-sentence conversion drill builds it fastest: take any accurate observation about the novel, stated in one sentence with its textual anchor, and write a second sentence answering the so-what question in contestable form. Ten observations, twenty sentences, no essay around them.

That is the whole drill, and its leanness is the point. It isolates the conversion move from everything else an essay demands: no introduction, no conclusion, no paragraph transitions, nothing but the muscle being trained. The first attempts will produce pseudo-claims, sentences that sound assertive but cannot be opposed, and that failure is the point of the drill: it makes the contestability gap visible in a format too small to hide in. A student who runs this drill twice a week for a month will find the conversion starting to happen automatically during planning, which is exactly what the examination requires, since there is no time in the hall to philosophize about method.

The drill works best with a mixed set of observations, because the conversion muscle needs varied resistance. Use two observations about narration, two about symbols, two about character, two about imagery, and two about structure, and the hand learns that the so-what question has a different answer in each territory. A narration observation converts into a claim about reliability or perspective; a symbol observation converts into a claim about what the symbol's movement proves; a character observation converts into a claim about function rather than personality; a structure observation converts into a claim about design rather than sequence. The variety teaches the deeper lesson, which is that the conversion is not a formula with one output but a question with as many answers as the novel has dimensions. Students who drill on one territory, usually symbols, develop a conversion reflex that only works on symbols, and their essays argue brilliantly about the green light while describing everything else.

A realistic practice task makes the drill examination-honest: set a timer for twenty minutes, take a past essay question, and produce only the claim sentences for a five-paragraph plan, one claim per paragraph, each with its observation noted beside it. No full paragraphs, no polished prose, just the skeleton of the argument. Then apply the two tests to each claim: could a smart reader disagree, and does the noted observation actually support it. The claims that fail get rewritten on the spot. This is the closest a student can come to practicing the argumentative act under examination conditions without writing a full essay, and it takes less time than most students spend rereading their notes. The skeleton, once sound, makes the full essay almost mechanical, because every paragraph then has a job description: prove this sentence, using this evidence, against this possible objection.

### How do you test whether a claim is contestable?

Ask three questions in order: can a careful reader sincerely take the other side, does the disagreement matter to how the novel is understood, and does the analysis at hand actually bear on the dispute. A claim must survive all three to count as an argument.

First, the disagreement question: can a careful reader of the novel sincerely take the other side? Not a contrarian inventing positions, but a genuine reader with textual reasons. If the only possible objection is "that is not true," the claim is a factual assertion, not an argument; if no objection is imaginable at all, the claim is a truism. Second, the stakes question: does the disagreement matter to how the novel is understood? A claim can be technically disputable and still be trivial, like a dispute over which chapter a minor detail appears in. The test asks whether winning the argument changes the reading of the book. Third, the evidence question: does the analysis at hand actually bear on the dispute, or would the argument need evidence the paragraph does not contain? A claim that passes the first two tests but fails the third is an aspiration, not an argument, and the honest move is to narrow it until the evidence reaches it.

Apply the test to a working example and watch it do its job. The candidate claim: "The green light's final appearance proves that Gatsby's dream was always about the future rather than about Daisy." Disagreement question: could a careful reader oppose it? Yes, by arguing the final appearance universalizes the dream precisely because Daisy has been absorbed into it, making the light about her as the type of the unreachable. Stakes question: does it matter? Yes, because it decides whether the novel is about a man chasing a woman or about the structure of longing itself. Evidence question: does the analysis support it? The three appearances, tracked closely, show the light narrowing to Daisy in the middle appearance and widening beyond her in the last, which bears directly on the dispute. Three yeses, and the claim is an argument. Now apply it to "The green light is a powerful symbol of Gatsby's hopes." Disagreement: no careful reader denies it. The test kills it in one question, which is the test doing exactly what it is for.

The test also diagnoses the two most common pseudo-claims, and naming them helps students catch themselves. The first is the evaluative pseudo-claim, which asserts quality instead of position: "Fitzgerald's use of the green light is brilliant and effective." A reader can disagree about brilliance, but the disagreement is about taste, not about the novel, and no analysis of the text can settle it. The repair is to convert the evaluation into a position: what does the brilliant use prove? The second is the summary pseudo-claim, which restates the plot in assertive grammar: "Gatsby's pursuit of Daisy ultimately destroys him." Every reader knows this; the sentence is true and undisputed and therefore not an argument. The repair is to ask what the destruction proves: about the dream, about the class barrier, about the narrator who survives to tell it. The test is merciless with both types, and that mercilessness is its value. A claim that survives it can be built on.

## Four Worked Conversions: From Observation to Defended Position

Method described in the abstract stays abstract, so this section performs the conversion four times on real material from the novel, slowly enough that each move is visible. The four cases are chosen for range: a narration observation, two symbol observations, and a characterization observation, which together cover most of what a Gatsby essay ever needs to convert. Each case follows the same four moves: the observation stated plainly with its textual anchor, the so-what question asked honestly, the contestable claim written out, and the evidence marshaled to show the claim is supportable. Watch the pattern repeat, because the repetition is the teaching. The conversion is the same act in all four territories; only the material changes.

### How do you turn Nick's narration into an argument?

State the observation plainly, ask what the contradiction proves beyond itself, choose among the possible answers, and write the choice as a disputable sentence. Nick's announced reservation of judgment, set against his constant judging, becomes a claim about strategy rather than lapse.

Start with the observation in plain terms: Nick Carraway opens the novel by announcing that he is inclined to reserve all judgments, and across the chapters he judges Tom's cruelty, Daisy's carelessness, Jordan's dishonesty, and Gatsby's criminal associations with complete freedom. The textual anchors are the opening pages and the judgments scattered through the narrative, and the observation is accurate and easy to support. Most essays stop here, or one step further, noting the contradiction and calling Nick complex. The analysis is correct. It proves nothing, because no reader disputes it. The conversion begins by refusing to be satisfied with it.

Ask the so-what question and mean it: what follows from the contradiction between the announced method and the practiced habit? Three different answers present themselves, and the choice among them is the argumentative act. The first answer says the contradiction proves Nick is a hypocrite whose moral authority collapses, which would make him an unreliable narrator in the strong sense. The second says the contradiction proves the judgments are the real method and the reservation was always a pose, adopted to win the reader's trust before the judging begins. The third says the contradiction is deliberate on Fitzgerald's part, a way of showing that moral neutrality is impossible in a world this corrupt, so that Nick's failure to reserve judgment is the novel's point about judgment itself. Same observation, three claims, each contestable, each requiring different evidence. The student must choose, and the essay is the defense of the choice.

Take the second answer and write it as a claim: Nick's announced reservation of judgment is a rhetorical strategy that purchases the reader's trust, and his subsequent judgments are the actual narrative method, which means his unreliability is not a flaw in his telling but the design of it. Run the contestability test. Disagreement: a careful reader could argue the judgments are inconsistent and emotional rather than strategic, pointing to the moments Nick's feelings for Gatsby visibly warp his assessments. Stakes: the disagreement decides whether the novel has a moral center or only a moral performance. Evidence: the opening claim, the pattern of judgments, and the moments where Nick explicitly frames his own perspective, all bear on the dispute. Three yeses. The observation has become an argument.

Now marshal the analysis as evidence, which is the move the brief for this article names explicitly: analysis becomes the evidence, the marshaling of observations into a case. The paragraph that proves the claim does not merely repeat the observation; it arranges supporting observations in the order that builds the case. First, the opening announcement, read closely for its careful phrasing, the way it presents tolerance as a family inheritance rather than a choice. Second, the early judgments, Tom's aggression and Daisy's charm assessed within pages of the announcement, showing how quickly the method is abandoned. Third, the asymmetry of the judgments, the unsparing treatment of the Buchanans set against the protective treatment of Gatsby, which is the pattern a strategic reading predicts and an accidental reading cannot explain. Fourth, the closing pages, where Nick's judgments harden into the novel's final moral pronouncements, proving the trajectory was purposeful. Each observation is analysis. Arranged under the claim, the analysis is evidence. The paragraph argues because every sentence serves the sentence that can be disputed.

### How do you turn the green light into an argument?

Track the image's movement and ask what the pattern proves. The light narrows to Daisy when she is present and widens beyond her when she is gone, which becomes the claim that the novel separates desire from its object and makes longing itself the subject.

The observation in plain terms: the green light appears at the end of the first chapter, where Gatsby reaches toward it across the bay; it appears again in the fifth chapter, when Gatsby stands with Daisy and the light's significance visibly diminishes for him; and it returns in Nick's closing meditation, where it expands into a universal image of aspiration. The textual anchors are the three passages, and the tracking of the image across them is genuine analysis, the kind teachers rightly praise. The inert essay stops at the tracking, perhaps adding that the light symbolizes Gatsby's hopes and dreams. That final sentence is true, undisputed, and worthless as argument, because it restates what every reader already knows in slightly elevated language.

The so-what question, asked with real dissatisfaction: what does the movement of the image prove? The light does not merely repeat; it changes, and the change has a pattern. In the first appearance it is distant and solitary, attached to nothing but Gatsby's reaching. In the second it is present and diminished, seen with Daisy beside him, and Gatsby's reaction shows the symbol collapsing under the weight of the real. In the third it is distant again but universalized, no longer Gatsby's light but everyone's. The pattern is narrowing then widening, contraction then expansion, and the question is what that pattern proves about the novel's understanding of desire. One answer: the pattern proves that the object of desire destroys the desire by becoming real, which is why Gatsby's dream could survive only at a distance. Another: the pattern proves that desire in the novel is structural rather than personal, a condition of the world rather than a feeling about a woman. The choice is the claim.

Write the second answer as a contestable sentence: the green light's movement from personal symbol to diminished object to universal image proves that the novel treats longing as the permanent condition of its world rather than as Gatsby's private feeling, which is why the dream survives Gatsby's death in Nick's closing pages. Test it. Disagreement: a reader could argue the final expansion is Nick imposing meaning on Gatsby's private dream, making the universalizing an act of narration rather than a fact about the world. Stakes: the dispute decides whether the novel's famous ending is about Gatsby or about everyone, which is arguably the most important interpretive question the book poses. Evidence: the three appearances, read for the precise shifts in scale and attachment, bear directly on it. The claim survives, and the analysis that tracked the appearances is now its evidence, marshaled in the order the claim needs: distance, diminishment, expansion, each appearance proving one clause of the sentence.

Notice what the conversion did not do: it did not add new observations. The student who wrote the inert paragraph already had all three appearances. The conversion added only the claim and the arrangement, and those two additions are the entire difference between the bands. This is the point worth internalizing, because it reframes what essay improvement requires. The student who believes improvement means noticing more will keep adding observations to claimless paragraphs. The student who understands the conversion will take the observations already owned and put them to work. The examination rewards the second student, not because the examiner undervalues close reading, but because the mark scheme pays for what the reading proves.

### How do you turn Daisy's voice into an argument?

Resist admiring the description and ask what it proves. The voice being full of money becomes the claim that charm functions as a financial instrument in this world, which is why Nick can be seduced by it while fully understanding its price.

The observation: Daisy Buchanan's voice is described repeatedly as low, thrilling, and captivating, and Nick's most famous formulation says it is full of money. The textual anchors are the voice descriptions scattered through the middle chapters, and the analysis that unpacks them is often excellent: the synesthetic strangeness of a voice containing a substance, the way the description makes wealth audible, the recurrence that marks the voice as one of the novel's signature images. The inert essay admires the description, explains the metaphor, and concludes that Fitzgerald characterizes Daisy vividly through her voice. True, undisputed, inert.

The so-what question cuts deeper here than in the previous cases, because the observation is so seductive that most readers stop at admiration. What does it prove that the voice is full of money, rather than merely that Fitzgerald wrote a memorable line? One answer: it proves that in this novel, personal charm is not separate from economic power but is one of its forms, which means Daisy cannot be understood as a private individual apart from her wealth. Another: it proves that Nick, who hears the money in the voice and is drawn to it anyway, understands the corruption of his world without being able to resist it, which makes his narration a confession of complicity. A third: it proves the novel's larger claim that everything desirable in its world, beauty, charm, love, has been monetized, and the voice is the smallest unit of that monetization. Three claims, one observation, and again the choice is the argument.

Take the first answer and sharpen it into a claim: Daisy's voice proves that charm in the novel functions as a financial instrument, a form of capital that works like money because it is money, converted into sound. Test for contestability. Disagreement: a reader could argue the voice proves the opposite, that Daisy's charm is the one thing about her that exceeds her wealth, a human remainder that the money language fails to capture. That is a real disagreement with textual support, since the voice is described in terms of thrill and music as well as money. Stakes: the dispute decides whether Daisy is reducible to her class position or retains an irreducible personal quality that the class analysis cannot absorb. Evidence: the voice passages, read for the exact mixture of monetary and musical language, bear directly on the question. The claim holds.

The marshaling for this claim shows the evidence test doing its subtlest work, because the observation contains material for both sides. A paragraph proving the financial-instrument claim must handle the musical language honestly, not suppress it: the thrill and the music are the instrument's disguise, the reason the capital works, since charm that announced itself as money would not charm. That concession strengthens the paragraph, because it anticipates the objection and absorbs it. The counterargument is not an enemy of the claim but its finishing school. Students often fear that acknowledging the other side weakens their position; the truth is the reverse. A claim that has survived its strongest objection in the paragraph is far harder for an examiner to dismiss than a claim that pretended no objection existed. The conversion, fully performed, includes the objection and the answer, and the analysis serves both.

### How do you turn the Valley of Ashes into an argument?

Treat the setting as a structural claim waiting to be stated. The valley sits between West Egg and New York, the novel returns to it at every turning point, and its ash-grey desolation is described with a persistence that marks it as more than background. Ask what that placement proves, and the geography becomes an argument about the whole design.

The observation: the Valley of Ashes lies on the road between West Egg and the city, a stretch of grey wasteland where ash grows like wheat, and the narrative passes through it at every decisive moment, the first party trip, the confrontation, the deaths. The textual anchors are the valley passages in the early and late chapters, and the analysis that unpacks them is usually strong: the colorlessness against the novel's saturated palette, the men who move dimly through the ash, the eyes of the billboard watching over it all. The inert essay admires the desolation, notes the contrast with the parties, and concludes the valley symbolizes poverty or the underside of wealth. True, undisputed, inert, because no reader denies that a wasteland between mansions means something about inequality.

The so-what question, pressed hard: what does the valley's placement prove about the novel's argument, not just its imagery? One answer says the valley proves the glittering world is built on hidden waste, making the American Dream structurally dependent on what it pretends not to see. Another says the valley proves the novel's moral geography, with judgment located in the lowest place rather than the highest, inverting the expected order. A third says the valley proves that the story's violence originates outside the wealthy world and invades it, since both deaths that end the novel are rooted there. Each answer converts the same geography into a different claim, and the essay must choose. Take the first and write it as a sentence: the Valley of Ashes proves that the novel's world of wealth depends structurally on a zone of waste it must cross but refuses to see, which makes the American Dream in the book reliant on the very desolation it pretends to have escaped.

Test it three ways. Disagreement: a careful reader could argue the valley is incidental scenery, a realistic detail of the industrial landscape rather than a structural argument, and that reading the Dream's dependence into it overstates the case. That is a genuine opposition with textual reasons, since the valley occupies relatively few pages. Stakes: the dispute decides whether the novel's critique of wealth is structural or decorative, which changes the book from a story about rich people into an argument about an economy. Evidence: the valley's placement on the mandatory road between the two worlds, its recurrence at every turning point, and the fact that the plot's decisive violence originates there, all bear directly on the structural reading. Three yeses, and the geography has become an argument.

The marshaling here demonstrates the ordering principle at its most important, because setting analysis tempts students into travelogue order, describing the valley as the narrative passes through it. The argumentative paragraph instead orders by force. First, the mandatory road: everyone traveling between the novel's two poles must cross the valley, which establishes the structural claim in one observation. Second, the recurrence at turning points, which shows the placement is deliberate rather than incidental, answering the objection before it is fully raised. Third, the origin of the violence there, which proves the valley is not passive scenery but the place where the glittering world's consequences land. Fourth, the billboard eyes, read not as divine judgment but as commercial witness, which seals the claim that the waste is part of the economic system rather than outside it. Each observation is aimed at one clause of the claim sentence. Cover the claim and read the evidence: it proves the structural dependence, not merely the existence of poverty. That is the difference between analysis that accompanies and analysis that proves, and it is visible here in pure form because the material is geography, the territory where students most often merely describe.

Notice, across all four conversions, what never changed: the observations were already in the student's possession before the conversion began. The Nick contradiction, the three lights, the moneyed voice, the ash-grey road, none of these required new reading to notice. The conversion added only the so-what question, the disputable sentence, the arrangement, and the objection. Four additions, no new observations, and the entire difference between the bands. The student who internalizes this will stop asking "what else can I notice" and start asking "what does what I noticed prove," and that change of question is the whole of the skill this guide teaches.

## Building the Paragraph That Argues

A claim without a paragraph is a slogan, and the conversion is incomplete until the observation has been rebuilt as evidence inside a structure that proves the claim. The argumentative paragraph has a shape worth learning deliberately, because the shape is what makes the conversion visible to the examiner. It opens with the claim, stated as the paragraph's first sentence, so the reader knows what is being proved before the proof begins. It continues with the analysis, the observations arranged in the order that builds the case, each one read closely enough to do work. It anticipates the objection, the strongest reason a careful reader might resist the claim, stated fairly rather than as a straw figure. It answers the objection with further analysis, showing why the claim survives. It closes by stating what the proven claim contributes to the essay's larger position, the so-what of the so-what. Five movements, and every sentence in the paragraph can be checked against the claim: does this sentence help prove it, qualify it, or defend it? A sentence that does none of the three does not belong.

The opening claim sentence deserves its own craft, because students habitually open paragraphs with observations and bury the claim in the middle, which forces the examiner to reconstruct the argument instead of following it. Compare the two openings. The descriptive opening: "Nick's narration contains several contradictions regarding his judgment of other characters." The reader learns the paragraph's topic and waits for the point. The argumentative opening: "Nick's contradictions about judgment are not lapses but strategy, a performance of moral authority that lets him condemn the Buchanans while protecting Gatsby." The reader learns the point immediately and reads the analysis that follows as evidence for it. The difference is not stylistic; it is structural. The examiner can award argument marks from the first sentence of the second paragraph, while the first paragraph must be read to the end before its purpose emerges, and some examiners will not wait. The mechanics of constructing that opening sentence, the grammar of claim-making, the ways a thesis sentence distributes its clauses, belong to the [thesis-statement guide](/2030/04/08/gatsby-thesis-statement/), which this article deliberately does not duplicate: the claim mechanics live there, the conversion lives here, and the paragraph is where the two meet.

The middle of the paragraph is where analysis does its evidentiary work, and the arrangement matters as much as the content. Evidence should be ordered by argumentative force, not by the order it appears in the novel. The observation that most directly proves the claim comes first, so the case is established early; the supporting observations follow in descending order of strength; the observation that handles the anticipated objection comes at the point where the objection naturally arises. This ordering feels unnatural to students trained in chronological reading, who want to walk through the novel in order, but the paragraph is not a tour of the text. It is a case, and cases are organized by persuasion. A useful check: cover the claim sentence and read the paragraph's evidence sentences in sequence, then ask what claim they prove. If they prove the paragraph's claim, the arrangement is sound. If they prove a different claim, or no claim at all, the evidence is drifting and needs reordering or replacement.

The objection and answer deserve emphasis because they are the most neglected movement and the most powerful. Most student paragraphs omit the objection entirely, presenting the claim as if no reasonable reader could resist it, which is precisely what makes the claim look weak: a position that acknowledges no opposition reads as a position that has never been tested. The objection need not be long. One sentence stating the strongest counter-reading, fairly put, is enough, followed by one or two sentences of analysis showing why the claim survives it. The effect on the examiner is disproportionate to the space it takes. It demonstrates the evaluative thinking the top bands demand, it shows the student has considered the novel's complexity rather than flattened it, and it converts the paragraph from assertion into argument in the fullest sense: a case made against resistance. Students who master this movement find their marks rising across every essay, because the objection habit transfers to every subject that asks for argument.

### How do you build a paragraph that argues?

Start with the claim as the opening sentence, arrange the observations in the order that builds the case, state the strongest objection fairly, answer it with analysis, and close with what the proven claim contributes to the essay's overall position.

A paragraph argues when every sentence serves the contestable claim stated in its opening line, which means the construction starts with the claim and works backward to the evidence it needs. Write the claim first, as a single disputable sentence, and test it with the three questions: can a careful reader disagree, does the disagreement matter, and can the analysis at hand support it. Then list the observations that prove it, no more than three or four, ordered by force, and discard every observation that merely accompanies the claim without proving it. Then write the objection in one fair sentence and answer it with analysis. Then close with the contribution sentence, what this proven claim adds to the essay's overall position. The paragraph that results has no spare sentences, because every sentence was hired for a job.

The most common construction failure is the evidence pile, the paragraph that contains strong observations and a real claim but arranges the observations as a list rather than a case. The sentences are individually good; collectively they do not build. The repair is the arrangement check described above: read the evidence without the claim and see what it proves. Usually the pile proves the claim's first clause but not its second, or proves a neighboring claim the student did not intend. The fix is surgical rather than wholesale: reorder the observations, cut the one that proves the wrong thing, add the one that proves the missing clause. The paragraph does not need more analysis. It needs its analysis aimed. Students should practice this repair on their own drafted paragraphs, because the ability to diagnose a paragraph's argumentative structure is the same ability the examiner uses to mark it, and seeing one's own work through the examiner's eyes is the fastest route to the higher band.

A second construction failure is the claim-evidence mismatch, where the claim is genuinely contestable but the analysis supports a smaller or different point. The student claims the novel condemns the American Dream and offers analysis of the party descriptions, but the analysis as written proves only that the parties are vulgar, and vulgarity is not condemnation. The honest repair is to narrow the claim until the evidence reaches it: the parties' vulgarity, read closely, proves the novel finds the dream's social performance hollow, which is a smaller claim the evidence can carry. Or the repair goes the other way: keep the large claim and widen the evidence to include the Valley of Ashes, the closing meditation, the fates of the dreamers, until the case matches the ambition. Either repair is legitimate. What is not legitimate is leaving the mismatch in place and hoping the examiner will not notice, because the mismatch is exactly what the examiner is trained to notice. The evidence test from the conversion method is the paragraph-level version of this check, and it should be run on every paragraph before the essay is considered finished.

## The Level the Conversion Must Reach

Conversion is a skill, but skills have standards, and the standard for this one is set by what the highest band of marking demands. The top band does not ask for more analysis than the middle bands; it asks for a different relationship between analysis and claim. In the middle bands, analysis supports a position. In the top band, analysis is the position, developed with a conceptual awareness that the student understands what the claim implies beyond the immediate question. The distinction is subtle and it matters enormously, because students aiming at the top often try to get there by adding sophistication to their analysis, longer sentences, rarer vocabulary, more intricate observations, when the actual requirement is sophistication in the claim: a position that shows awareness of the novel's larger debates, its structural ironies, its resistance to simple verdicts. The conversion method in this guide produces claims; the top-band standard judges their conceptual reach.

A concrete comparison shows the gap. The middle-band claim: "Nick's judgments prove he is an unreliable narrator." Contestable, supported, arguable, and limited, because it stops at the label. The top-band version: "Nick's unreliability is the novel's real subject, a demonstration that moral judgment in a corrupt world always serves the judge, which makes the narrative an ethical problem rather than a story with an ethical narrator." The analysis behind both claims could be identical. The second claim reaches further because it connects the observation to the novel's design, to the question of what the book is about rather than merely what happens in it. The conversion method gets the student to the first claim reliably. The reach to the second comes from asking, after the so-what question, a further question: so what for the novel as a whole? That second question is the top-band habit, and it is a habit rather than a talent, which means it can be practiced like the conversion itself.

The full account of what the highest band requires, the conceptual moves, the evaluative language, the way top-band essays handle counter-readings and sustain a line of argument across the whole essay, is set out in the [top-band essay guide](/2030/09/30/gatsby-top-band-essay/), which defines the standard this article's skill must meet. The division of labor is deliberate and worth respecting. This article teaches the transformation of analysis into argument, the engine. The top-band guide describes the destination, the level of conceptual argument the engine must reach. A student who masters the conversion without knowing the standard will argue soundly and plateau; a student who knows the standard without the conversion will aim high and miss, because aiming is not a skill. The two together are the complete equipment. Read the standard, then drill the conversion until the claims you produce naturally reach the level the standard describes.

The conversion also sits inside a larger essay architecture, and the architecture matters because even perfect paragraphs fail inside a formless essay. The claims of the individual paragraphs must add up to an overall position, the essay's thesis, and the paragraphs must be ordered so the case builds rather than repeats. The introduction must state the overall claim and map the route. The conclusion must do more than summarize; it must state what the proven claims establish, the final so-what of the whole essay. The complete method for assembling converted paragraphs into a whole argument, planning, structuring, introducing, concluding, is the work of the [essay-writing hub](/2030/04/01/how-to-write-gatsby-essay/), which this guide feeds into rather than replaces. Think of the relationship as component and assembly: this article manufactures the components, the hub assembles them into the finished essay. Neither works without the other, and the student who understands both never again faces the blank page with only analysis and hope.

## Five Broken Conversions and Their Repairs

The conversion fails in recognizable patterns, and learning to diagnose the patterns is faster than learning to avoid each one by trial and error. What follows is a repair manual: five broken conversions, each shown in its broken form, diagnosed, and repaired, so the student can recognize the failure in a draft and apply the fix. The patterns are ordered by frequency, starting with the failure almost every student commits first. Each repair follows the same principle: shrink the ambition or widen the evidence until the two meet, and never leave a claim the analysis cannot carry.

The first broken pattern is the summary claim, the sentence that restates the plot in assertive grammar and mistakes assertiveness for argument. Broken form: "Gatsby's obsessive pursuit of Daisy ultimately leads to his tragic death." The diagnosis is straightforward: every reader of the novel knows this, so no reader can dispute it, and the contestability test kills it in one question. The repair asks what the undisputed fact proves. One repaired version: "Gatsby's death proves that the novel treats the pursuit of the past as structurally fatal, not merely unlucky, because every mechanism of his world, from Tom's violence to the ash-covered valley to the indifference of his party guests, is arranged to make the past unreachable." The fact is the same. The claim now says what the fact means, in a form a reader could oppose by arguing the death is contingent rather than structural. The repair move is always the same: take the true sentence and ask what it proves beyond itself.

The second broken pattern is the evaluation claim, which asserts quality instead of position. Broken form: "Fitzgerald's portrayal of the Valley of Ashes is powerful and vivid." A reader can disagree about power and vividness, but the disagreement is about taste, and no analysis of the text can settle a disagreement about taste. The diagnosis: the sentence evaluates rather than argues, so it cannot be proven, only felt. The repair converts the evaluation into a position by asking what the powerful portrayal proves. Repaired: "The Valley of Ashes is the novel's proof that its glittering world depends on a hidden zone of waste, which makes the American Dream in the book structurally reliant on what it pretends not to see." The admiration is gone, replaced by a claim about the novel's design that the ash imagery can actually support. Students cling to evaluation claims because they feel like praise for the author, and praise feels safe. Argument is not praise. It is a position, and positions take risks.

The third broken pattern is the double claim, the paragraph that contains two contestable sentences and proves neither. Broken form: a paragraph that opens "Nick is an unreliable narrator whose judgments reveal his class bias" and then offers some evidence about unreliability and some about class bias, with neither line of evidence developed enough to prove its claim. The diagnosis: the paragraph is trying to do two jobs and doing neither, because each claim needs its own evidence and its own objection. The repair is amputation, not addition: choose the stronger claim, cut the other, and give the survivor the full paragraph. If both claims matter to the essay, they become two paragraphs, each with its own evidence. The double claim usually arises during planning, when the student is excited by two ideas and cannot bear to discard one. The discipline is to remember that a paragraph is a unit of proof, and a unit of proof proves one thing.

The fourth broken pattern is the orphan claim, the contestable sentence with no analysis beneath it. Broken form: "The novel argues that wealth corrupts completely," followed by a paragraph that discusses the plot in general terms without a single closely read passage. The diagnosis: the claim is real, but the evidence is missing, so the paragraph asserts rather than argues. The repair is to attach textual anchors: which passages prove the corruption, in which words, and how do those words do the proving. The orphan claim is common in timed essays, where the student knows what to argue but has not practiced the analysis that supports it. The preventive drill is the one from the method section: every claim sentence written in planning must have its observations noted beside it, and a claim with no noted observations is not ready to become a paragraph. The examination rewards the student whose claims arrive with their evidence attached.

The fifth broken pattern is the drifting paragraph, where the claim is sound and the analysis is strong but the analysis proves a neighboring claim instead. Broken form: a paragraph claiming that Daisy's voice proves charm is a financial instrument, whose evidence discusses how beautiful the voice is, how Nick is captivated by it, how it recurs as a motif, all true, all proving that the voice is important, none proving the financial claim. The diagnosis is the evidence test: the analysis accompanies the claim without supporting it. The repair is to redirect the analysis at the claim: which words in the voice passages carry the monetary meaning, how does the synesthesia of a voice containing money do the argumentative work, what does Nick's simultaneous understanding and seduction prove about the instrument's operation. The drifting paragraph is the most sophisticated failure because everything in it is good except the connection. The arrangement check catches it: cover the claim, read the evidence, ask what it proves. If the answer is a different sentence, re-aim the analysis.

## Introductions and Conclusions That Argue

The conversion does not stop at the paragraph level. The essay's introduction and conclusion must also argue rather than describe, and they fail in their own characteristic ways. The introduction's job is to state the essay's overall claim, establish why the disagreement matters, and map the route the paragraphs will take. The descriptive introduction announces the topic and summarizes the novel: "This essay will discuss the role of Nick Carraway as narrator in The Great Gatsby." That sentence, or its many cousins, is the introduction equivalent of the inert paragraph. It tells the reader what the essay is about without saying what the essay argues, which means the reader must wait for the argument to emerge, if it ever does. The argumentative introduction states the position upfront: "Nick Carraway's narration is a performance of moral authority that condemns the Buchanans while shielding Gatsby, and the novel's real subject is not Gatsby's dream but the ethics of the telling itself." A reader who disagrees knows immediately what is at stake and reads the essay as a case to be judged. That is the introduction doing its job.

The introduction also establishes stakes, the reason the disagreement matters beyond the essay. Stakes are not grand claims about literature in general; they are specific statements about what the argument changes in the reading of the novel. "If Nick's judgments are strategic rather than accidental, then the novel has no innocent observer, and every moral pronouncement in it must be read as interested." That sentence tells the reader why the essay's claim is worth the pages it will take to prove. Students often omit stakes because they seem like extra work, but stakes are what convert a competent essay into a compelling one. The examiner who understands why the argument matters reads the evidence with interest rather than duty, and interest is the most underrated factor in marking. The introduction's map, the brief indication of the paragraph route, then gives the reader the structure to follow: the claim about the opening, the claim about the asymmetry of judgments, the claim about the closing pages. Three clauses, no detail, just the skeleton of the case.

The conclusion's job is the most misunderstood in the essay, because students treat it as a summary and examiners read it as the final argumentative act. The descriptive conclusion restates what the essay has said: "To sum up, Nick is a complex narrator whose judgments reveal his unreliability." The examiner learns nothing new and the essay ends with a whimper. The argumentative conclusion states what the proven claims establish, the final so-what of the whole essay: "If the paragraphs above have proved their claims, then the novel's famous moral ambiguity is not a property of its world but a product of its telling, which means the question is not whether Nick can be trusted but what his untrustworthiness is for." That sentence does not summarize; it elevates. It takes the essay's proven positions and draws the consequence, showing the examiner that the argument has a destination beyond its own paragraphs.

Two rules govern the conclusion's construction. First, no new evidence. The conclusion is not the place for an observation the essay has not yet used, because unevidenced claims in the final paragraph read as afterthoughts and undermine the case's discipline. If a late observation matters, the essay needs restructuring, not a smuggled conclusion. Second, the conclusion must answer the essay question directly, showing how the argument as a whole addresses the terms of the prompt. The student who argued about Nick's strategic unreliability in answer to a question about narration must close by stating what that argument means for narration as the question posed it. The loop back to the question is what makes the essay feel complete rather than merely stopped. Introductions promise, paragraphs prove, conclusions collect: that is the architecture, and the conversion method supplies the argumentative content for all three parts.

Transitions between paragraphs deserve a brief word because they are where whole-essay argument most visibly lives or dies. The descriptive transition summarizes the last paragraph and announces the next: "Having discussed Nick's judgments, this essay will now examine the green light." The argumentative transition shows the logical relationship between the claims: "If Nick's judgments are strategic, then the symbols he deploys deserve the same suspicion, and the green light is the test case." The second transition does real work: it tells the reader why the essay moves where it moves, which is the essay-level version of the paragraph's internal logic. Students who write argumentative transitions find their essays cohere without extra effort, because the transitions force the planning to be logical. A transition that cannot be written argumentatively is usually a sign that the paragraph order is wrong, and reordering the paragraphs is easier than faking the connection.

## Reading the Question Argumentatively

The conversion begins before the first paragraph, in the reading of the question itself, because examination prompts are written to invite argument and students routinely misread them as invitations to describe. Consider the difference between what a question asks and what it rewards. A prompt like "Discuss the role of Nick Carraway as narrator" appears to ask for a survey: say what Nick does as narrator, cover the main points, conclude. The student who answers that apparent question writes the descriptive essay and earns the descriptive mark. But the question rewards something else, because "discuss" in examination language means "consider the arguments for and against a position," and the position is hidden in the question's terms. The role of the narrator is not a topic; it is a dispute, between readers who trust Nick and readers who do not, between the novel's surface morality and its structural ironies. The student who reads the question argumentatively finds the dispute inside it and organizes the essay as the adjudication.

The technique for finding the dispute is to rewrite the question as a contestable sentence, the same conversion applied one level up. Take "How does Fitzgerald present the American Dream?" and force it into claim form: the question becomes "Fitzgerald presents the American Dream as ___," and the blank must be filled with something a reader could oppose. "As an illusion that destroys those who pursue it" is a claim; "as an important theme" is not. The rewritten sentence is not the essay's thesis yet, but it is the thesis's raw material, and the planning that follows has a direction the descriptive plan lacks. Every examination question in literature can be rewritten this way, because every such question is built on a genuine critical disagreement, even when the wording hides it. The questions are designed by people who want to see thinking, and thinking means taking a position among alternatives.

Some questions hide their dispute better than others, and the well-disguised ones deserve specific attention. "To what extent" questions wear their argument on the surface: the phrase itself demands a measured position, and the student who answers with pure description has ignored the question's grammar. "How" questions are the deceptive ones, because "how" sounds like it asks for method, for a description of technique. "How does Fitzgerald use symbolism?" answered descriptively becomes a tour of the symbols. Answered argumentatively, it becomes a claim about what the symbolism is for: "Fitzgerald uses symbolism to separate desire from its object, so that every symbol in the novel means most when its referent is absent." The "how" question, read properly, is a "what does it prove" question in disguise, and the conversion method applies to it directly. The examination never asks for a tour. It always asks for a case, and the vocabulary of the question is the map to the case it wants.

The final pre-writing step is the question audit, performed on the plan before drafting begins. Read the plan's thesis against the question's terms and check three things: does the thesis answer the question as written, or has the essay drifted toward a neighboring question the student preferred; does the thesis take a position the question's dispute makes possible, or does it sit safely outside the disagreement; and do the paragraph claims add up to the thesis, or do they prove a different essay? The audit takes five minutes and catches the most expensive examination error, the well-argued answer to the wrong question. Students who skip it often discover, too late, that their excellent conversion work served a thesis the question never asked for. The conversion is a powerful engine. The question audit makes sure it drives in the right direction.

## Verdict

The position this guide has argued can be stated in the sentence it opened with, and the sentence has earned its repetition: analysis is the evidence; argument is the case. The essay that only analyzes describes brilliantly while saying nothing; the essay that argues uses the same analysis to prove a point. That distinction is not a piece of examination gamesmanship. It is the difference between knowing a novel and thinking about one, and the examination mark scheme is only the institutional form of a distinction that matters far beyond the hall. A reader who can convert observation into claim can participate in the conversation the novel sustains; a reader who can only observe can admire it from outside. The conversion is therefore not a trick for marks but the entrance to the activity itself, and the marks follow because the activity is what they were designed to measure.

The method's strength is its portability. The four moves, state the observation, ask what it proves, write the disputable sentence, test it twice, work on any passage, any character, any symbol, any structural feature, because they do not depend on the content of the observation but on the logic of proof. The worked conversions showed the same pattern three times on deliberately varied material, and the pattern held. That invariance is what makes the method drillable: the student is not learning three conversions but one conversion with three applications, and the drill transfers to the fourth, fifth, and fiftieth case without new instruction. The two-sentence drill, the skeleton plan, the arrangement check, the objection habit: these are not separate techniques but the method's components isolated for practice, and the student who practices them separately will find them fused under pressure, which is the definition of a skill.

The method also answers the most practical question students ask about examination writing: what to do when the mind goes blank under the clock. The blank mind is usually not empty but unorganized, full of observations with no claims attached, and the conversion gives it a procedure to run instead of a void to stare into. Pick the strongest observation available, ask what it proves, write the disputable sentence, and the paragraph has its spine. The procedure works under pressure precisely because it is mechanical: it does not require inspiration, only the willingness to ask the so-what question and write down the answer. Students who have drilled the conversion report the same experience, that the examination essay starts to assemble itself once the first claim is on the page, because each proven claim suggests the next. That is not confidence as a personality trait. It is confidence as the product of a method that always gives the writer something true to do next.

Two honest qualifications close the argument, because a guide about intellectual honesty should practice it. First, the conversion cannot manufacture insight where none exists. A student who has not read closely, who brings no observations to the procedure, will produce claims without evidence, and the method will expose that emptiness rather than disguise it. The conversion multiplies the value of analysis; it does not create analysis from nothing. Close reading remains the foundation, and the student who neglects it in favor of claim-making will write assertive, empty essays. The method assumes the observations are there. Its promise is that no observation need go to waste. Second, not every claim the method produces will be true, and the contestability that makes a claim arguable also makes it refutable. A student may convert brilliantly and argue for a reading the text does not ultimately support. That is not a failure of the method; it is the risk inherent in argument, and the examination rewards the well-defended wrong answer over the undefended right one, because the skill being tested is thinking, not divination. Argue honestly, defend with evidence, concede where the text resists, and the essay will earn what argument earns even when the position is debatable.

The final word belongs to the habit, not the method. Methods are forgotten; habits persist. The student who leaves this guide with one durable change should make it the so-what reflex: never let an observation stand alone, in planning, in drafting, or in revision, without the sentence that says what it proves. That reflex, practiced until it is automatic, is the whole of the conversion in its smallest form, and everything else in this guide is the elaboration of it. The essays that earn the highest marks are not written by students with more to say about the novel. They are written by students who make everything they say about the novel prove something. Be that student, and the analysis you already own will finally start working for you.

## Study: Turning Conversion Drills into Examination Arguments

The study routine that converts this guide into results has three parts, and they should be treated as a weekly discipline in the months before the examination. First, run the two-sentence conversion drill twice a week on fresh observations, ten observations per sitting, drawn from different territories of the novel so the reflex generalizes. Keep the drill sheets, because they become a personal bank of claim sentences, and a bank of tested claims is the most valuable revision asset a literature student can own. When a past paper question arrives, the student does not start from nothing; the student searches the bank for claims that fit the question and adapts them. The drill is therefore not only practice but production: every session manufactures material the examination can use. Treat the sheets as inventory, dated and organized by territory, and review them the way the memorization guides advise reviewing quotation sets, because a claim forgotten is a claim that cannot be deployed.

Second, ground every observation used in the drills against a reliable text before the observation enters the bank, because a conversion built on a misremembered passage is a case built on sand. The so-what question can only be answered honestly when the observation is accurate, and accuracy requires verification against Fitzgerald's actual words rather than a study note's summary. The [annotated text at VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html) is the right instrument for this grounding step, showing each passage in its full context so the student converts what the novel actually does rather than what a summary claims it does. Make verification a habit at the moment of observation, not a chore deferred to revision week: the observation enters the drill sheet only with its chapter and its exact wording attached. A bank of verified observations converts cleanly; a bank of half-remembered ones produces claims the evidence cannot carry, and the evidence test will keep failing for reasons the student cannot diagnose until the text is checked.

Third, practice the full conversion under timed conditions, because drilled components and performed arguments are different achievements. The skeleton exercise from the method section, twenty minutes, a past question, one claim per paragraph with observations noted, then the two tests applied to each claim, is the bridge between the drill and the essay. For structured rehearsal of that bridge, [ReportMedic's essay practice tool](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html) provides the timed environment where conversion drills meet real prompts, which is where this guide's method proves itself. The student who runs the full cycle, observe, verify, convert, test, plan, draft, has done everything the examination can ask. The claims will be there because they were manufactured deliberately, the evidence will be attached because the method demands it, and the paragraphs will argue because every sentence was hired to prove the sentence that can be disputed. That is the complete routine, and it is enough.

When the routine stalls, the stall will take one of three familiar forms, each with its remedy. The first is the pseudo-claim plateau, where the drill sheets fill with sentences that sound assertive but fail the disagreement question, and the student cannot see the difference. The remedy is the opposition exercise: for each candidate claim, write the strongest opposing sentence a careful reader could sincerely hold. If no opposing sentence can be written, the candidate is not a claim yet, and the so-what question must be asked again, harder. The second stall is the evidence drift, where claims pass the tests but the paragraphs keep proving neighboring points. The remedy is the arrangement check applied to every drafted paragraph until the habit internalizes: cover the claim, read the evidence, ask what it proves. The third stall is the one the whole guide warns against, the slide back into description under time pressure, when the examination clock makes the safe paragraph tempting. The remedy is the planning discipline: no paragraph gets written until its claim sentence exists on the plan, tested and evidenced. The clock pressures the student who plans weakly; it serves the student whose claims are already made.

The study routine stands on its own: drill the conversion, verify the observations, practice under time, and let the companion tools carry the grounding and the rehearsal. The essay guides this article links to handle the neighboring skills, the thesis mechanics, the top-band standard, the description errors, the whole-essay assembly, each in its own territory. This guide owns the conversion, and the routine above is how the conversion becomes permanent. The student who follows it will not merely understand the difference between analysis and argument. The student will perform it, paragraph after paragraph, until the examiner can see the case being made and has no choice but to mark it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you turn analysis into argument on Gatsby?

You turn analysis into argument by making a contestable claim that the analysis then supports, which means the conversion has four moves performed in order. First, state the observation plainly in one sentence with its textual anchor: what the text does, where, in which words. Second, ask what the observation proves beyond itself, the so-what question most students skip because the observation feels complete. Third, write the answer as a sentence a careful reader could sincerely dispute, which is the conversion itself. Fourth, run the two tests: could a reasonable reader oppose the claim, and does the analysis actually support it rather than merely accompany it. Repeat the four moves for every paragraph. The method is unglamorous and it works, because it separates the two acts essays blur: noticing, which is analysis, and proving, which is argument. A student who performs the conversion on ten observations has ten potential paragraphs; a student who only analyzes has ten descriptions waiting for a purpose.

### Q: How does analysis differ from argument?

Analysis describes what the text does; argument makes a contestable claim the analysis supports. Analysis says the green light appears three times and its charge shifts across the appearances. Argument says the narrowing and widening of the light's meaning proves the novel separates desire from its object. The analysis in both sentences is identical; the claim is what differs, and the claim is what examiners pay for. The practical test is disagreement: analysis can be agreed with by every careful reader, while argument can be sincerely opposed by one. If no reader could say "I disagree," the sentence is analysis, however brilliant. If a reader could oppose it with textual reasons, it is an argument, however plainly stated. Students confuse the two because good analysis feels like an achievement, and it is, but it is a different achievement from argument. The essay needs both, in the right relationship: analysis as the evidence, argument as the case the evidence proves.

### Q: How do you move from observation to a defended position?

You move from observation to defended position through the so-what question, asked with genuine dissatisfaction and answered in contestable form. Take the observation "Nick claims to reserve judgment and then judges everyone." Ask what follows from the contradiction: does it prove hypocrisy, strategic performance, or the impossibility of neutrality? Each answer is a different defended position, and choosing among them is the argumentative act. Write the chosen answer as a sentence a reader could oppose: "Nick's judgments are not lapses but strategy, a performance of moral authority." Then defend it by marshaling the analysis as evidence, arranged in the order that builds the case, and by facing the strongest objection fairly. The defense is what makes the position defended rather than merely stated. A claim without its evidence and its answer to the objection is an assertion. The full move, observation to claim to evidence to objection to answer, is the paragraph, and the essay is the repetition of that move.

### Q: How does analysis become evidence for a claim?

Analysis becomes evidence when it is marshaled, which means selected, ordered, and aimed at the claim rather than merely placed near it. The same observations can accompany a claim without proving it, and the difference between accompanying and proving is arrangement plus relevance. Take the claim that Daisy's voice proves charm functions as a financial instrument. The analysis becomes evidence when the voice passages are read for their monetary language specifically, when the synesthesia of a voice containing money is shown doing the argumentative work, and when Nick's simultaneous understanding and seduction is presented as proof of the instrument's operation. Each observation is aimed at one clause of the claim. The check is the arrangement test: cover the claim, read the evidence sentences, and ask what they prove. If they prove the claim, the analysis is evidence. If they prove a neighboring point, the analysis is drifting and must be re-aimed. Marshaling is the least glamorous and most decisive part of the conversion.

### Q: Is thorough analysis the same as argument?

No, and mistaking one for the other is the most flattering mistake a strong student can make. Thorough analysis, the close tracking of an image across chapters, the precise unpacking of a metaphor, the careful mapping of a contradiction, is genuinely difficult work, and its difficulty is what makes the mistake so convincing: the student reasons that work this hard must be worth argument marks. It is not, because the mark scheme pays for positions, not for noticing. A paragraph that explains how the green light changes meaning across three appearances with complete accuracy is analysis, and an examiner can only award it knowledge and understanding marks. The same analysis under a contestable claim, that the light's movement proves the novel separates desire from its object, earns argument marks too, because now the analysis is proving something a reader could dispute. Thoroughness was never the missing ingredient. The claim was, and telling trapped students to analyze more only makes their paragraphs longer, not more argumentative.

### Q: Why does analysis alone say nothing?

Analysis alone says nothing because it cannot be disagreed with, and a sentence no one can oppose makes no claim on the reader's judgment. "The green light appears at the end of chapter one, again in chapter five, and finally in Nick's closing meditation" is true, accurate, and undisputed; every careful reader assents, and assent is not engagement. Argument begins where disagreement becomes possible: the moment a sentence takes a position, it invites opposition, and the opposition is what makes the essay a live piece of thinking rather than a guided tour. This is why two essays with identical analysis can sit in different mark bands. The descriptive essay's sentences all command assent, so the examiner's judgment is never engaged. The argumentative essay's sentences demand verdicts, and the examiner, engaged, can reward the thinking. Saying nothing is not the same as being wrong. The purely analytical essay is usually right about everything and proves nothing, which in examination terms is the more expensive failure.

### Q: How do you open a Gatsby paragraph so it argues instead of describes?

Open with the claim as the paragraph's first sentence, stated in contestable form, so the reader knows what is being proved before the proof begins. The descriptive opening names a topic: "Nick's narration contains several contradictions." The argumentative opening states a position: "Nick's contradictions about judgment are strategic, a performance of moral authority that condemns the Buchanans while protecting Gatsby." The examiner can award argument marks from the first sentence of the second version, while the first version forces the examiner to read to the end before its purpose emerges. Students bury claims in the middle of paragraphs out of habit, often from school writing where the topic sentence announces a subject. Break the habit deliberately: write the claim first, then the evidence, then the objection and answer. If the claim sentence cannot be written before the paragraph, the paragraph is not ready to be written, because the writer does not yet know what it proves. The opening claim is the paragraph's job description.

### Q: What is the one-sentence test that separates a claim from a summary?

The test is the disagreement question: can a careful reader of the novel sincerely take the other side? Apply it to "Gatsby's pursuit of Daisy destroys him" and the answer is no, because every reader knows it; the sentence is summary in assertive grammar. Apply it to "Gatsby's destruction proves the novel treats the pursuit of the past as structurally fatal rather than merely unlucky" and the answer is yes, because a reader could argue the death is contingent, the product of specific choices rather than structure. The first sentence reports; the second argues. The test catches the two great impostors: the summary claim, which restates plot assertively, and the evaluation claim, which asserts quality, as in "Fitzgerald's imagery is brilliant." Both fail because neither can be opposed with textual reasons. Run the test on every topic sentence before drafting the paragraph. A sentence that fails it must be repaired by asking what it proves beyond itself, and the repair is always available, because every true sentence about the novel proves something further.

### Q: How do you make the eyes of T. J. Eckleburg prove a claim?

You make the eyes prove a claim by aiming the analysis at a contestable position about what the watching means, rather than inventorying the appearances. The inert version notes the billboard, the fading paint, the moments characters notice the eyes, and concludes they are an important symbol. The conversion asks what the watching proves: one strong claim is that the eyes prove the novel's moral witness is commercial and decaying rather than divine, which is why judgment in the book always arrives too late and changes nothing. The evidence is then marshaled to that claim: the eyes are an advertisement, their paint fades, they watch the valley where the worst damage happens, and no character is saved by being seen. The objection is faced fairly: a reader could argue the eyes retain genuine moral weight, since Wilson treats them as the eyes of God. The answer: Wilson's reading is his, not the novel's, and the narrative never confirms it, which actually strengthens the claim about absent judgment. The symbol proves the claim because every observation was aimed at it.

### Q: Why do paragraphs that begin "This shows" usually fail to argue?

Paragraphs that begin "this shows" usually fail because the phrase announces a demonstration without stating a position, which lets the writer smuggle in a truism as if it were a conclusion. "This shows that Fitzgerald uses symbolism effectively" demonstrates nothing a reader could dispute; it restates that the symbol exists and praises it. The phrase is a grammatical trick: it borrows the authority of proof while delivering only summary. The repair is to replace the phrase with the actual claim, stated plainly: "This proves that the light's meaning depends on Daisy's absence, which is why the symbol collapses when she is present." Now the sentence can be opposed, which means it can be defended, which means the paragraph argues. Students lean on "this shows" because it feels like the language of analysis, and it is, but analysis is not argument. Search every draft for the phrase and treat each occurrence as a diagnostic: the sentence after it is almost always a description wearing the grammar of proof. Rewrite it as a claim or cut it.

### Q: How do you connect two observations into a single argument?

You connect two observations by finding the claim they both prove, which means the connection runs through the position, not through the observations themselves. Students try to connect observations directly, with transitions like "similarly" or "likewise," and produce paragraphs where two good analyses sit side by side without a shared purpose. The method instead asks the so-what question of each observation separately, then looks for the overlap in the answers. The green light's diminished second appearance proves desire collapses when its object becomes real; Daisy's voice being full of money proves charm is a financial instrument. The shared claim: the novel argues that desire cannot survive contact with its object because the object is always already monetized. Both observations now serve one sentence, and the paragraph has a single job. If no shared claim emerges, the observations belong in different paragraphs. Forcing a connection where none exists produces the drifting paragraph; respecting the absence of connection produces better planning.

### Q: What is the difference between a theme and an argument about a theme?

A theme is a subject the novel treats; an argument about a theme is a contestable position on what the novel says about that subject. "The American Dream" is a theme, and an essay organized around it will describe the dream's appearances: Gatsby's parties, the green light, the Valley of Ashes. An argument about the theme takes a position: "The novel argues that the American Dream is structurally dependent on the waste it pretends not to see, which is why the Valley of Ashes sits at the story's geographical and moral center." The first essay inventories; the second proves. The practical difference shows in the topic sentences. Theme-essay topic sentences name aspects: "The parties show the dream's glittering surface." Argument-essay topic sentences state positions: "The parties are advertisements, not celebrations, which proves Gatsby understands wealth as signaling rather than enjoyment." Students who organize by theme often produce five descriptive paragraphs and wonder why the marks stall. The fix is not to abandon themes but to convert each one: for every theme in the plan, write the contestable sentence about it before drafting.

### Q: How do you keep a paragraph's evidence from drifting off its claim?

You keep evidence on its claim with the arrangement check, applied during drafting and again in revision: cover the claim sentence, read the evidence sentences in sequence, and ask what claim they prove. If they prove the paragraph's claim, the evidence is aimed. If they prove a neighboring claim, or no claim at all, the paragraph is drifting. Drift usually happens for one of two reasons. The first is chronological habit: the student walks through the novel in order, and the evidence follows the story instead of the argument. The repair is to reorder by argumentative force, strongest proof first, regardless of where it appears in the book. The second is affection for an observation: the student loves a particular reading and includes it though it serves a different point. The repair is amputation, moving the beloved observation to the paragraph whose claim it actually proves. A paragraph is a unit of proof, and every sentence in it must help prove the opening sentence, qualify it, or defend it against objection. Sentences with no job get cut.

### Q: What role does the counterargument play in a Gatsby essay?

The counterargument is the claim's finishing school: stating the strongest opposing reading fairly and answering it with analysis is what converts assertion into argument in the fullest sense. Most student paragraphs omit it, presenting the claim as if no reasonable reader could resist, which paradoxically makes the claim look weak, a position that has never been tested. The counterargument need not be long: one sentence stating the best objection, followed by one or two sentences of analysis showing why the claim survives. The effect is disproportionate. It demonstrates the evaluative thinking the highest bands demand, it shows the student has weighed the novel's complexity rather than flattened it, and it preempts the examiner's own reservations. Students fear that conceding ground weakens their case; the reverse is true. A claim that has absorbed its strongest objection inside the paragraph is far harder to dismiss than one that pretended no objection existed. Practice writing the opposition sentence for every claim in the plan, because the habit of imagining resistance is the habit of argument itself.

### Q: How do you write a conclusion that argues rather than summarizes?

You write an argumentative conclusion by stating what the proven claims establish, the final so-what of the whole essay, instead of restating what the essay has said. The summarizing conclusion, "To sum up, Nick is a complex and unreliable narrator," teaches the examiner nothing new and ends the essay with a whimper. The arguing conclusion draws the consequence: "If the paragraphs above have proved their claims, then the novel's moral ambiguity is not a property of its world but a product of its telling, which means the question is not whether Nick can be trusted but what his untrustworthiness is for." Two rules govern the construction. First, no new evidence: the conclusion introduces no observation the essay has not used, because unevidenced late claims read as afterthoughts. Second, loop back to the question: show how the argument as a whole addresses the prompt's terms, so the essay feels complete rather than merely stopped. Introductions promise, paragraphs prove, conclusions collect. The conclusion is the last proof that the essay argued.

### Q: How do you choose which analysis to keep when a paragraph gets crowded?

You choose by the claim's needs: keep the observations that prove the claim most directly, and cut or relocate the rest, however good they are. A crowded paragraph usually contains five or six strong observations proving two or three different points, which means it is really two paragraphs compressed, or one paragraph with passengers. The selection test is ruthless and simple: for each observation, ask which clause of the claim sentence it proves. Observations that prove no clause get cut from this paragraph, not from the essay; they may belong elsewhere. Among observations that prove the same clause, keep the strongest, the one with the most precise textual anchor, and cut the weaker duplicate. Students resist cutting because every observation cost effort to produce, but the sunk cost is irrelevant to the paragraph's job. Three observations aimed at one claim persuade more than six observations aimed at three claims. The arrangement check doubles as the selection tool: read the evidence without the claim, and whatever does not serve the claim's proof is excess, however brilliant.

### Q: Why does "Fitzgerald shows" weaken an argument?

"Fitzgerald shows" weakens an argument because it attributes agency to the author instead of stating what the text proves, which turns a claim about the novel into a report about the novelist's intentions. "Fitzgerald shows that wealth corrupts" is a sentence about Fitzgerald, and it invites the dead-end question of what he meant rather than the live question of what the passage proves. The argumentative version removes the author as intermediary: "The Valley of Ashes proves the novel's glittering world depends on hidden waste." Now the sentence is about the text, and it can be opposed with textual reasons, which means it can be defended. There is a second weakness: "shows" is doing the work that the analysis should do. It asserts a demonstration without performing one, which is why sentences built on it so often end where the proof should begin. Replace the construction with the claim itself, then supply the analysis that earns it. The author can return in the essay where intention genuinely matters, but the default sentence of argument is about the text, not about the man who wrote it.

### Q: How do you handle a quotation that supports two different claims?

You handle it by choosing the claim the essay needs and aiming the quotation's analysis at that claim, while acknowledging the alternative reading if it is strong enough to threaten your position. A versatile quotation, "Her voice is full of money," can serve the claim that charm is a financial instrument or the claim that Nick understands his world's corruption without resisting it. Using it for both claims in the same essay is usually a mistake, because repetition wastes the quotation's force and suggests the planning was thin. Choose the stronger fit, the claim where the quotation's specific words do the most work, and find the other claim a different quotation. If the alternative reading is one a careful examiner might raise against you, do not hide from it: state it as the paragraph's objection and answer it, showing why your reading of the line is the stronger one. The quotation that survives its alternative is more persuasive than the quotation deployed in innocence. Versatility is an asset in planning and a liability in drafting; decide, commit, and defend.

### Q: What is a claim map and how does it organize an essay?

A claim map is the essay plan reduced to its argumentative skeleton: the overall thesis at the top, one contestable claim per paragraph beneath it, each with its observations noted and its objection sketched, arranged in the order that builds the case. It organizes the essay by making the logic visible before any prose is written. The map exposes the failures planning usually hides: the paragraph with no claim, the two paragraphs proving the same point, the claim with no observations, the essay whose paragraphs do not add up to the thesis. Building the map takes twenty minutes and saves hours, because every structural problem is cheaper to fix in skeleton form than in finished prose. The map also disciplines the drafting: with the claims fixed and tested, writing becomes the execution of decisions already made, and the examination clock, which punishes the student who thinks while writing, rewards the student who planned the argument first. Keep old maps; a bank of tested claim maps is a bank of essay skeletons, adaptable to new questions.

### Q: How do you revise a descriptive draft into an argumentative one?

You revise a descriptive draft by converting each paragraph in place, working from the existing analysis rather than starting over, because the observations are usually sound and only the claims are missing. The revision pass has four steps per paragraph. First, read the paragraph and write its implicit claim, the sentence it seems to be proving; usually this reveals the paragraph proves nothing disputable. Second, ask the so-what question of its best observation and write a genuine contestable claim. Third, replace the topic sentence with the claim and reorder the evidence by argumentative force, cutting observations that serve the old descriptive purpose. Fourth, add the objection and answer, one sentence each, facing the strongest resistance fairly. Work through every paragraph this way before touching the introduction and conclusion, which must then be rewritten to match the new argument: the introduction states the overall claim the converted paragraphs now prove, and the conclusion draws the consequence. The draft that emerges is usually shorter than the original, because description is wordy and argument is economical, and almost always stronger.
