The Consumer Protection Act 1986 did something no Indian statute had done before it: it built a court-like machine that an ordinary buyer could operate without a lawyer, without a court fee proportionate to the claim, and without waiting a decade for a decree. In thirty-one sections it created three tiers of redressal agencies, declared six consumer rights, defined the wrongs a buyer could complain about, and listed the reliefs a forum could grant. What it did not create was a regulator. No official under the 1986 Act could investigate a trade practice on his own motion, order a product off the shelves across the country, penalise a misleading advertisement, or mediate a dispute by statutory authority. For thirty-three years, Indian consumer protection consisted of individual complaints decided one at a time, after the harm had already been done.

![The Consumer Protection Act 1986, showing the three-tier consumer forums and the rights the statute declared - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-33.webp)

That single structural fact explains both the statute's reputation and its replacement. The Act, formally Act 68 of 1986, was praised across the political spectrum as the most citizen-facing legislation of its decade, and the praise was earned: the forums it created decided grievances that the ordinary courts would never have reached in usable time. Yet the same statute left every systemic practice untouched unless a victim came forward, proved a case, and won an order that bound only the parties before the forum. The argument of this article is that the Consumer Protection Act 2019 is best understood not as a revision of the older statute but as the addition of the four things the older statute lacked: a regulator with investigative and recall powers, a product liability regime, statutory mediation, and a treatment of electronic commerce. Everything else in the 2019 Act is refinement. Those four additions are the verdict on what the 1986 Act was missing.

This article treats the 1986 statute as it operated: its text, its definitions, its machinery, its amendments, its judicial expansion, and the transition that carried its pending cases into the era of its successor. The head-to-head comparison of the two statutes belongs to a companion article in this series and is not attempted here. The successor statute has its own pillar article. What follows is the complete working model of the repealed Act that a reader with an older matter, an examination to prepare for, or a historical question to answer actually needs.

## The legal landscape the Act entered

Before 1986, a buyer who had been cheated had remedies on paper and very little in practice. The Indian Contract Act, 1872 allowed a suit for breach of contract, and the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 supplied rules about conditions and warranties, but both routes ran through the ordinary civil courts, where a claim worth a few thousand rupees would attract court fees, counsel's fees, and a queue measured in years. The cost of enforcing a right routinely exceeded the value of the right itself, and traders knew it. A defective pressure cooker, a short-weight consignment, or a builder who simply stopped building could be met with a legal notice and very little else.

The regulatory statutes of the period did not fill the gap, because they were built on a different logic. The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 created a commission that could inquire into restrictive and unfair trade practices, and after its amendment in 1984 it could award compensation on application, but its proceedings were slow, its docket was dominated by large industrial questions, and it was never designed as a walk-in remedy for a household buyer. The Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 and the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 were penal statutes enforced by inspectors; they punished the seller, but they returned nothing to the buyer who had paid for the adulterated or spurious product. The Essential Commodities Act, 1955 controlled supply and price of listed goods in the interest of availability, not of the individual purchaser.

The consumer movement supplied the political pressure that converted this dissatisfaction into legislation. Voluntary consumer organisations, of which the Consumer Guidance Society of India in Mumbai and the Consumer Education and Research Centre in Ahmedabad were among the most prominent, had spent years documenting the pattern: standard-form contracts that excluded liability, advertisements that promised what the product could not do, and after-sales service that evaporated once payment cleared. The international climate mattered as well. The United Nations General Assembly adopted its Guidelines for Consumer Protection in 1985, and consumer legislation was moving through parliaments in several comparable economies. India enacted its statute the following year, and the speed of the passage reflected a rare consensus that the existing machinery had failed a class of litigants who could not fight in the ordinary courts.

### Why was the Consumer Protection Act 1986 treated as an innovation when it was enacted?

Because it replaced the civil suit with a specialised three-tier system of forums that charged no court fee proportionate to the claim, required no lawyer, prescribed short timelines for disposal, and gave the lowest forum power to order refund, replacement, compensation and the withdrawal of hazardous goods, all in a statute of thirty-one sections.

The innovation deserves to be stated precisely, because its elements are often run together. First, the Act created adjudication outside the court hierarchy: District Forums, State Commissions and a National Commission, staffed by a judicial president sitting with lay members drawn from fields such as economics, commerce, accountancy, industry and public affairs. Second, it made access cheap: a complaint was a written allegation, not a plaint, and it carried no ad valorem court fee. Third, it made the procedure summary: the forums followed the statute's own procedure, took evidence on affidavit, and borrowed only specified powers of a civil court. Fourth, it set the forum's remedial menu in the statute itself, so a complainant knew before filing what could be asked for. Fifth, it declared the rights it existed to protect in the text, giving the whole scheme a charter against which its working could be judged.

## The statute in outline

The Consumer Protection Bill was passed by both Houses of Parliament and received the assent of the President on 24 December 1986. It became Act 68 of 1986. Section 1 left commencement to notification, and the Act was brought into force in two stages. Chapters I, II and IV, which contain the definitions, the consumer protection councils and the miscellaneous provisions, came into force on 15 April 1987. Chapter III, which creates the redressal agencies themselves, followed on 1 July 1987. The staging is a small fact with a practical consequence that still matters for older matters: a cause of action arising in the gap between April and July 1987 met a statute whose councils and definitions existed but whose forums did not yet function.

The Act as enacted extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, a territorial exclusion carried in many central statutes of the period and removed from the legal landscape by later constitutional change. Its long title and preamble state its purpose without decoration: the better protection of the interests of consumers, and for that purpose the establishment of consumer councils and other authorities for the settlement of consumers' disputes and for matters connected with them. Section 1(4) applied the Act to all goods and services save as otherwise expressly provided by notification of the Central Government, so coverage was universal by default and exclusion was the exception requiring an executive act.

The architecture is lean. Chapter I contains the short title, the definitional section on which everything turns, and the provision that the Act operates in addition to other laws. Chapter II creates the Central, State and District Consumer Protection Councils and assigns them their objects. Chapter III builds the three redressal agencies, prescribes who may complain and how, sets out procedure, findings, appeals, limitation, enforcement and penalties. Chapter IV gathers the miscellaneous provisions: protection for action taken in good faith, the power to remove difficulties, rule-making powers for the Central and State Governments, and the laying of rules before Parliament and the state legislatures. The whole statute, as enacted, ran to thirty-one sections.

Two features of the drafting shaped the Act's life. The first is that the operative concepts are concentrated in a single definitional section, Section 2, so that the reach of the Act stands or falls on the construction of a handful of words: consumer, complaint, defect, deficiency, service, trader, unfair trade practice and restrictive trade practice. The second is that the statute is enabling and remedial rather than regulatory: it defines wrongs and attaches a forum and a remedy to each, but it creates no inspectorate, no licensing power, and no standing machinery of enforcement apart from the forums' own orders and the penalty for disobeying them. The sections that follow take these features in turn.


## The miscellaneous chapter and the power to remove difficulties

Chapter IV repays a paragraph of attention because it contains the Act's own account of its fragility. Section 28 protects members of the forums and commissions, and officers acting under their direction, from suit, prosecution or other legal proceedings for anything done or intended to be done in good faith under the Act, a standard shield without which lay members drawn from commerce and public life would not have served on benches that decided against powerful traders and departments. Section 29 empowered the Central Government, for two years from the commencement of the Act, to make provisions by order in the Official Gazette, not inconsistent with the Act, for removing any difficulty in giving effect to it, with every such order to be laid before Parliament. The two-year fuse on the power marks it as a start-up instrument, a concession that a new species of tribunal planted in every district would meet situations the drafters had not foreseen, and that the executive rather than the courts should be able to smooth the first difficulties, temporarily and under parliamentary sight. Section 29A, inserted by the amendment of 1993, provided that no act or proceeding of a District Forum, State Commission or National Commission shall be invalid by reason only of a vacancy among its members or a defect in its constitution, a small provision that saved a large number of orders from technical annihilation when benches were later shown to have sat short-handed or imperfectly appointed during the system's chronic vacancy years. The miscellaneous chapter thus quietly records the three risks the drafters and the 1993 amenders actually feared: that members would be harassed for deciding, that a novel scheme would jam on an unprovided case, and that the appointments pipeline would fail in ways that should not be allowed to void the work the surviving members had done. All three fears were borne out, which is its own commentary on the realism of the drafting.

## The six rights the Act declared

Section 6 sets out the objects of the Central Consumer Protection Council, and in doing so it performs the Act's most quoted act: it declares six rights of consumers that the councils exist to promote and protect. The rights are the right to be protected against the marketing of goods and services which are hazardous to life and property; the right to be informed about the quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price of goods or services, so as to protect the consumer against unfair trade practices; the right to be assured, wherever possible, access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices; the right to be heard and to be assured that consumers' interests will receive due consideration at appropriate forums; the right to seek redressal against unfair trade practices or restrictive trade practices or unscrupulous exploitation of consumers; and the right to consumer education.

The placement of these rights matters for understanding what they were. They appear in the chapter on councils, as the objects of promotional bodies, not in a chapter of enforceable entitlements. A consumer could not file a complaint alleging, in the abstract, a violation of the right to be informed; the complaint had to be fitted into the complaint categories of Section 2(1)(c), such as an unfair trade practice adopted by a trader or goods suffering from a defect. The rights therefore functioned as the statute's declared philosophy and as an interpretive aid, and only one of them, the right to seek redressal, was wired directly into machinery the Act itself supplied. Critics of the Act made this point from the beginning: the statute proclaimed a right to safety and a right to information, but the duty-bearers for those rights, the manufacturer of a dangerous product or the advertiser of a false claim, could be reached only through the definitional gateways and only at the instance of an individual complainant.

Defenders answered, with some force, that declaratory rights in a statute of this kind are not inert. The six rights structured the work of the councils, informed the rule-making of governments, gave consumer organisations a statutory text to campaign with, and were repeatedly invoked by the forums when construing ambiguous definitions in the consumer's favour. When the Supreme Court expanded the definition of service to cover housing and medicine, it reasoned in the climate these declarations created. The successor statute kept the six rights and restated them in its own definitions, which is itself evidence that the declaration was judged worth preserving even by the Parliament that repealed the rest of the Act.

The District Councils complete the picture of Chapter II. The Central Council, chaired by the Minister in charge of consumer affairs in the Central Government, was to meet at least once a year. State Councils, chaired by the corresponding state minister, were to meet at least twice a year, and a District Council for every district was provided for by the amendment of 1993, which inserted the district tier into the council structure that the original Act had limited to the Centre and the States. The councils were promotional and advisory: they could study, publicise and recommend. They could not summon a trader, decide a complaint, or issue a direction. That division of labour, promotion in Chapter II and adjudication in Chapter III, is the first place a reader sees the statute's defining limitation: everything in the Act that could act on a market, as opposed to on a dispute, was confined to advice.

## The definitions on which everything turned

Section 2(1)(d) defines the consumer, and the definition is the gate through which every complaint had to pass. For goods, a consumer is a person who buys goods for a consideration which has been paid or promised, or partly paid and partly promised, or under any system of deferred payment. The definition includes a user of the goods other than the buyer, where the use is made with the buyer's approval, which is how a member of a household who did not pay for an article could still complain about it. For services, a consumer is a person who hires or avails of services for a consideration on the same pattern, and the definition includes a beneficiary of the services other than the person who hired them, again where the benefit is taken with approval. Parents who took a child to a hospital, and a family that occupied a flat bought by one member, stood inside the definition through these extensions, and the forums and courts applied them generously.

The exclusion is where the definition earned its litigation. A person who obtains goods for resale, or for any commercial purpose, is not a consumer. The resale limb was straightforward: a dealer who bought stock for his shelves could not convert a trade dispute with his supplier into a consumer complaint. The commercial purpose limb was not straightforward, because almost every small business buys goods it uses in earning money. Parliament addressed the hardest case in an explanation added to the clause: commercial purpose does not include use by a person of goods bought and used by him exclusively for the purpose of earning his livelihood by means of self-employment. The tailor who bought a sewing machine and worked it himself, the taxi owner who drove his own vehicle, the photographer who bought a camera and shot weddings with it: these buyers remained consumers, because the goods were the instruments of their own labour rather than inputs into an enterprise run at scale.

The definition of defect, in Section 2(1)(f), is deliberately wide. A defect is any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard of goods which is required to be maintained by or under any law, or under any contract express or implied, or as is claimed by the trader in any manner whatsoever in relation to the goods. The third limb is the telling one: a trader's own claim set the standard, so a product that failed to match its advertising was defective by the measure the advertiser had chosen. Deficiency, in Section 2(1)(g), performs the same work for services: any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance which is required to be maintained by or under law, or which has been undertaken to be performed by a person in pursuance of a contract or otherwise. A bank that delayed a loan disbursement, an insurer that sat on a claim, a carrier that delivered late: each was measured against the legal standard or against what the provider had undertaken, and the shortfall was a deficiency.

Service itself is defined in Section 2(1)(o) as service of any description which is made available to potential users, with an inclusive list running through banking, financing, insurance, transport, processing, supply of electrical or other energy, board or lodging, entertainment, amusement and the purveying of news or other information. Two exclusions close the clause: a service rendered free of charge is not a service under the Act, and neither is a service rendered under a contract of personal service, the master and servant relationship being left to other law. The free-of-charge exclusion produced one of the Act's biggest cases, discussed below, because it raised the question whether a government hospital that treated some patients without charge and others for payment was inside or outside the Act. The inclusive list was expanded by amendment in 1993 to name housing construction expressly, a change that interacts with the housing case law in a way this article examines when it reaches the courts.

An unfair trade practice, under Section 2(1)(r), is a trade practice which, for the purpose of promoting the sale, use or supply of goods or the provision of a service, adopts any unfair method or unfair or deceptive practice, and the clause then enumerates the practices it has in mind. The list covers false representations that goods are of a particular standard, quality, quantity, grade, composition, style or model; false representations about the standard of services; passing off rebuilt, second-hand, renovated or old goods as new; representations that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, performance, characteristics, accessories, uses or benefits which they do not have; false claims of affiliation by the seller; misleading representations about the need for or usefulness of goods or services; warranties or guarantees not based on an adequate or proper test; bargain-price advertising for goods never intended to be sold at the bargain price; gifts and prizes offered with no intention of providing them; the conduct of promotional contests and lotteries; the sale of goods that do not comply with prescribed safety standards; and hoarding, destruction or refusal to sell where the object or effect is to raise prices. A restrictive trade practice, defined separately, is a practice which requires a consumer to buy, hire or avail of goods or services as a condition precedent for buying, hiring or availing of other goods or services: the tied sale, in statutory form. These definitions did the work of a consumer-protection code in miniature, but with a limitation that returns throughout this article: the forums could respond to these practices when a complainant brought them a case, and award the reliefs the Act listed, but no authority could open an inquiry into a practice on its own motion across a market.


## The rules beneath the Act, and the price of the door

A short statute that must operate in every district of the country lives on its subordinate legislation, and the 1986 Act divided the rule-making labour in Section 30 between the Central Government and the State Governments along the lines of their respective establishments. The Centre made rules for the matters within its charge, including the composition and procedure of the Central Council and the National Commission and the salaries and conditions of its members; the States made rules for the State Councils, the District Forums and the State Commissions within their territories. Section 31 required the Centre's rules to be laid before Parliament and the States' rules before their legislatures. The Consumer Protection Rules made under the Act supplied the operational detail the sections omit: the manner of appointment processes, the conduct of meetings, the form and accompaniments of complaints and appeals, and the fee, where a fee was charged.

The fee position illustrates the Act's economics and is worth stating exactly, because it is often misstated in both directions. As enacted, the Act did not impose a court fee proportionate to the claim, and nothing in its scheme ever introduced ad valorem charging of the kind that prices a civil suit. A small fee, fixed by rules and graded mildly by the value of the claim, accompanied complaints in the system's developed practice, and the amendment of 2002 wrote the fee into the statute itself by providing in Section 12 that every complaint shall be accompanied with such amount of fee, payable in such manner, as may be prescribed. The amounts remained nominal against the values in dispute, in the tens and hundreds of rupees where a suit on the same claim would have attracted court fees in the thousands and rising with the claim. The design choice was the policy: Parliament priced the door to the forum at the cost of a bus journey rather than a percentage of the grievance, and accepted the consequence, volume, as the price of access. Any account of the forums' arrears that omits this choice has missed the legislature's own contribution to the docket it later reproached the forums for carrying.

Below the rules sat the councils' promotional work, the least litigated and least examined part of the Act. State and District Councils were charged with promoting the same six rights Section 6 declares, and their instruments were the meeting, the recommendation, the awareness campaign and the consumer education programme in schools and through voluntary organisations. Their record was uneven in the pattern this article has already identified in the appointments story: where a State funded its council's secretariat and met on schedule, the council functioned as a genuine forum for trader, official and consumer representatives to settle recurring friction, in billing, weights and measures, and local market practice, without a case being filed. Where it did not, the council existed in the statute and in the annual report and nowhere else. The variability is itself a datum about the Act's theory of change: its promotional tier depended entirely on executive energy, while its adjudicatory tier depended on complainants, and the difference in reliability between the two tiers across thirty-three years is the difference between machinery that runs on citizens and machinery that runs on departments.

## Who could complain, and what a complaint could allege

The definition of complainant in Section 2(1)(b) is wider than the disappointed buyer, and the width was a deliberate policy choice. A complaint could be made by a consumer as defined; by any voluntary consumer association registered under the Companies Act or any other law; by the Central Government or any State Government; and, in the form the clause took after amendment, by one or more consumers where there are numerous consumers having the same interest. The class limb mattered in practice: purchasers of flats in a single stalled project, or policyholders hit by the same repudiation practice, could move together through representatives rather than filing hundreds of identical complaints. Section 12, which prescribes the manner of making a complaint, repeats the cast in procedural form and adds that a recognised consumer association may complain whether or not the affected consumer is a member of it, and that one or more consumers may proceed with the permission of the forum on behalf of all consumers so interested.

The complaint itself, under Section 2(1)(c), is an allegation in writing that one of a closed list of wrongs has occurred. The list runs: an unfair trade practice or a restrictive trade practice has been adopted by a trader; the goods bought, or agreed to be bought, suffer from one or more defects; the services hired or availed of, or agreed to be hired or availed of, suffer from deficiency in any respect; a trader has charged a price in excess of the price fixed by or under any law, or displayed on the goods or the package; and goods which are hazardous to life and safety when used are being offered for sale in contravention of legal requirements about displaying information regarding their contents and the manner and effect of their use. Two features deserve emphasis. The complaint is a written allegation, not a pleading: no court fee stamp, no verification in the form of a plaint, and no requirement that the complainant frame the case in legal language. And the list is exhaustive as to categories while being generous within them, because defect and deficiency between them covered nearly every way a transaction could disappoint.

Standing under the Act therefore solved a problem the civil courts had never addressed: the diffuse grievance. A trader who overcharged every customer by a trivial sum was, in the ordinary courts, immune in practice, because no individual loss justified a suit. Under the 1986 Act, a registered association could aggregate the grievance, a government could complain in the public interest, and the forum could order the practice discontinued. The forums were also open to the opposite party's perspective through the same door: a complaint found frivolous or vexatious could be dismissed under Section 26 with an order that the complainant pay costs to the opposite party up to a ceiling of ten thousand rupees, a small but real discipline against using a cheap forum as an instrument of harassment.


## Who stood on the other side: the opposite party

The Act's defendant is described functionally rather than by a single label. A complaint about goods ran against the trader, defined in Section 2(1)(q) as a person who sells or distributes any goods for sale, including the manufacturer of the goods and, where goods are sold or distributed in package form, the packer. The definition of manufacturer in Section 2(1)(j) reaches three figures: the person who makes or manufactures goods or parts of them; the assembler who does not make the parts but puts them together and claims the end product as his own manufacture; and the person who puts his own mark on goods made by another and claims them as goods made by himself. The brand owner who manufactured nothing was therefore within the Act on the same footing as the factory, a deliberate choice that prevented the most visible name on the packet from disclaiming the packet's contents. The branch office definition, added by the amendment of 1993, completed the net by treating an establishment carrying on the same or substantially the same activity as the head office as a branch, which anchored both territorial jurisdiction and service of process in the place where the consumer had actually dealt.

A complaint about services ran against the provider of the service, whoever that was: the bank, the insurer, the carrier, the hospital, the professional, the development authority, the electricity undertaking. The Act drew no distinction between the private provider and the state provider, and no distinction between the company and the individual practitioner, though the relief had to be framed against the person whose undertaking had failed. Complaints were entertained against companies and firms as entities, against their branches where the transaction had occurred, and in suitable cases against the officers through whom the deficiency had occurred, with the forums working out in practice the familiar rules about whom an order could bind and against whom it could be executed.

Death and disappearance were provided for with increasing completeness. The amendment of 2002 applied the provisions of Order XXII of the Code of Civil Procedure, with the necessary verbal modifications, to proceedings under the Act where a complainant who was a consumer, or the opposite party, died during the pendency of the proceeding, so that legal representatives could be brought on record and the complaint neither abated for want of a mechanism nor survived against an estate without representation. The detail sounds technical and mattered in fact: consumer litigation is heavily populated by elderly complainants, insurance nominees and family businesses, and a remedial statute that lost its parties to mortality without a substitution rule would have failed a visible share of its own docket. The pattern is the same one visible in the interim order power and the ex parte set-aside power that the same amendment supplied: the 2002 legislature spent much of its effort not on new rights but on the procedural completeness of a system that had been running for fifteen years on a leaner text, closing the gaps that practice had exposed.

One boundary of the opposite party concept should be marked, because examinees and litigants both trip on it. The Act fastened on the parties to the consumer transaction and those the definitions brought in: the seller, the manufacturer, the packer, the provider. It did not create a general jurisdiction over everyone whose conduct contributed to a consumer's loss. The advertiser who composed a misleading claim for another's product, the testing house whose certificate a trader displayed, the financier standing behind a hire purchase: each had to be brought within a definition, as a trader, a service provider or a party to the transaction, before the forum could proceed against it, and complaints that arrayed the whole supply chain without that work were pruned at admission. The successor statute's product liability chapter redrew this boundary deliberately, distributing responsibility along the chain by rule. Under the 1986 Act the boundary was the definitions, and the definitions were the case.

## The three tiers of forums

Chapter III opens with Section 9, which establishes the three agencies: a Consumer Disputes Redressal Forum, to be known as the District Forum, in each district; a Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, to be known as the State Commission, in each State; and the National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, established by the Central Government. The names encode the design. The district body is a forum, the state and national bodies are commissions, and all three are agencies of a special statute rather than courts of the ordinary hierarchy, though the Act clothes them with selected powers of a civil court and deems their proceedings to be judicial proceedings.

Composition followed a consistent pattern. Each District Forum consisted of a President who is, has been, or is qualified to be a District Judge, and two other members of ability, integrity and standing with knowledge or experience of problems relating to economics, law, commerce, accountancy, industry, public affairs or administration, one of whom was to be a woman. The State Commission was presided over by a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court, appointed after consultation with the Chief Justice of that High Court, sitting with at least two such members. The National Commission was presided over by a person who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court, appointed after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, sitting with at least four members. Appointments ran through selection committees that combined the president of the next higher body with the law and consumer affairs secretaries, a structure introduced and refined by amendment to take the choice partly out of departmental hands. Members held office for a term of five years or until a prescribed age, sixty-five for the District Forum, sixty-seven for the State Commission and seventy for the National Commission, without eligibility for reappointment.

Pecuniary jurisdiction sorted complaints among the tiers by a single measure: the value of the goods or services together with the compensation, if any, claimed. The sorting changed twice, and the reader dealing with an older order needs the right era's figures, so the three positions should be held distinctly. As enacted, the District Forum entertained complaints where the value and compensation claimed did not exceed one lakh rupees; the State Commission took complaints exceeding one lakh but not exceeding ten lakh rupees; and the National Commission took complaints exceeding ten lakh rupees. The amendment of 1993 raised the bands to five lakh rupees for the District Forum, above five lakh and up to twenty lakh rupees for the State Commission, and above twenty lakh rupees for the National Commission. The amendment of 2002, brought into force on 15 March 2003, raised them again: the District Forum up to twenty lakh rupees, the State Commission above twenty lakh and up to one crore rupees, and the National Commission above one crore rupees. Those were the limits that stood when the Act was repealed, and orders that recite them belong to the post-2003 period of the Act's life.

The measure itself deserves a sentence of analysis, because it drove behaviour. Jurisdiction turned on value plus compensation claimed, not on consideration paid, so the complainant's own pleading placed the case. A claim pitched high to reach a preferred forum carried the risk that an inflated compensation claim would be examined at the relief stage and cut down, and the forums regularly warned against valuation games. The successor statute redesigned the measure entirely, keying jurisdiction to the consideration paid, precisely because the claim-based measure invited that inflation; the comparison article in this series owns that discussion, and it is enough here to register that the 1986 measure was the Act's own and explains many of its jurisdictional disputes.

Territorial jurisdiction under Section 11(2) let a complaint be instituted where the opposite party actually and voluntarily resides, carries on business, has a branch office, or personally works for gain, or where the cause of action wholly or in part arises. The branch office limb, clarified by amendment, meant that a national seller with a local branch could be sued where the buyer lived and transacted, which was the provision that converted the system from a paper scheme into one a buyer could actually use without travelling to a manufacturer's home city.

### Could a complaint be filed where the buyer lived, or only where the seller was based?

It could be filed where the opposite party resided, carried on business, had a branch office or personally worked for gain, and also where the cause of action wholly or in part arose. The branch office limb of Section 11(2) let a buyer sue a national seller in the district of purchase.

Above original jurisdiction sat the appellate and revisional structure. Each State Commission heard appeals against the orders of District Forums within the State, and could call for the records of any dispute pending before or decided by a District Forum where the forum appeared to have exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it, failed to exercise a jurisdiction vested in it, or acted illegally or with material irregularity. The National Commission held the corresponding appellate and revisional powers over State Commissions. Section 24 made every order final if no appeal was preferred. The scheme thus reproduced, in miniature and at speed, the hierarchy of trial, appeal and revision that the civil courts supplied, while stripping out the interlocutory traffic that made the ordinary hierarchy slow.

### Did the Act reach government departments and statutory authorities?

Yes. The definitions speak of persons and services without exempting the state, and the Supreme Court confirmed that a statutory development authority selling plots and houses renders a service for consideration, so housing boards, development authorities, railways, banks, insurers and electricity suppliers all stood within the forums' reach when they acted for consideration.

## Procedure under Section 13

Section 13 contains the Act's procedural code, and its design is a study in borrowed simplicity. On receipt of a complaint relating to goods, the District Forum referred a copy to the opposite party, directing it to give its version of the case within thirty days, extendable by no more than fifteen days. If the opposite party denied or disputed the allegations, or simply failed to respond, the forum proceeded to settle the dispute by the routes the section lays down. Where the complaint alleged a defect that could not be determined without analysis or test, the forum obtained a sealed and authenticated sample of the goods from the complainant, referred it to an appropriate laboratory, which was directed to report within forty-five days whether the goods suffered from the defect alleged or any other defect, and then gave both parties a hearing on the report, including any written objections to its findings or methods. Where the complaint related to services, or to goods for which the testing procedure could not be followed, the forum decided on the basis of the evidence brought to its notice by the complainant and the opposite party, or on the complainant's evidence alone if the opposite party defaulted.

The forums were armed with the powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 for specified purposes: summoning and enforcing the attendance of any defendant or witness and examining witnesses on oath; discovery and production of documents and material objects; receiving evidence on affidavits; requisitioning the report of an analysis or test from an appropriate laboratory or other relevant source; issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses; and any other prescribed matter. Every proceeding was deemed a judicial proceeding within the meaning of Sections 193 and 228 of the Indian Penal Code, and the forum was deemed a civil court for the purposes of Section 195 and Chapter XXVI of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. Section 13(3) barred any challenge to proceedings on the ground that the principles of natural justice had not been complied with where the statutory procedure had been followed, a clause that protected the summary character of the process against procedural appeals.

A party could appear in person or through an authorised representative, and legal practitioners could and did appear; the Act simply never made representation a condition of being heard. That distinction, between permitting lawyers and requiring them, is one of the most consequential design choices in the statute. It kept the door open to professional help in heavy cases while ensuring that the unrepresented buyer whose grievance was worth five thousand rupees was not priced out of the room by the cost of a vakalatnama.

### Were the consumer forums courts, and whose procedure did they follow?

They were statutory agencies with court-like powers for specified purposes, deemed to be civil courts for particular provisions and judicial proceedings for the law of perjury and contempt-adjacent purposes, but they followed the procedure of Section 13 and the rules under the Act, not the Code of Civil Procedure as a whole.

The amendment of 2002 added the Act's only express disposal timelines. A new sub-section required that an endeavour be made to decide every complaint within three months of the date of receipt of notice by the opposite party, extended to five months where the complaint required analysis or testing of commodities. Adjournments were not to be granted ordinarily, and where one was granted the forum was to record reasons and could order costs. The same amending Act supplied interim-order power during the pendency of a proceeding, and applied the machinery of Order XXII of the Code of Civil Procedure, with modifications, where a complainant or opposite party died. These were the changes of a legislature that had read fifteen years of criticism: the forums were deciding, but too slowly, and the statute answered with an endeavour clause whose soft wording is itself worth noticing. Parliament said endeavour, not shall decide, and the gap between the three-month aspiration and the arrears that accumulated is examined in the assessment below.

### How quickly was a consumer forum expected to decide a complaint?

The statute's own answer, after the amendment of 2002, was an endeavour to decide within three months of the opposite party receiving notice, or five months where the goods had to be sent for laboratory analysis or testing, with adjournments to be granted only for recorded reasons and possibly on costs.

## Section 14: the reliefs the forums could grant

If the definitions are the Act's gate, Section 14 is its engine room. After the proceeding under Section 13, if the District Forum was satisfied that the goods complained against suffered from any of the defects specified in the complaint, or that any of the allegations about the services were proved, it issued an order directing the opposite party to do one or more of the things the section lists. In its final form the menu ran as follows. The defect pointed out by the appropriate laboratory could be ordered removed from the goods. The goods could be ordered replaced with new goods of similar description, free from any defect. The price, or the charges paid by the complainant, could be ordered returned. Compensation could be awarded for any loss or injury suffered by the consumer due to the negligence of the opposite party, with a proviso, inserted by the amendment of 2002, empowering the forum to grant punitive damages in such circumstances as it deems fit. Defects or deficiencies in services could be ordered removed. An unfair trade practice or a restrictive trade practice could be ordered discontinued and not repeated. Hazardous goods could be ordered not to be offered for sale, and goods already offered could be ordered withdrawn. The amendment of 2002 added three further directions: to cease manufacture of hazardous goods and desist from offering services which are hazardous in nature; to pay a sum, where loss or injury has been suffered by a large number of consumers who are not conveniently identifiable, of not less than five per cent of the value of the defective goods sold or services provided, the amount to be credited and utilised as prescribed; and to issue a corrective advertisement to neutralise the effect of a misleading advertisement, at the cost of the opposite party responsible for it. Adequate costs to parties closed the list.

Read the menu chronologically and the Act's growth rings show. The original list of 1986 was transactional: repair, replace, refund, compensate, and, through the clauses added by the amendment of 1993, remove deficiencies in services, discontinue unfair or restrictive practices, keep hazardous goods off the market and withdraw them, and pay costs. The 1993 additions converted a repair-and-refund scheme into one that could also police practices and dangerous products at the level of the individual case. The 2002 additions reached further: punitive damages marked the wrong rather than merely the loss; the five per cent direction created a rough class remedy for diffuse injury, routing money to prescribed purposes where the victims could not be conveniently identified; and the corrective advertisement attacked the precise mechanism of a misleading claim by compelling the advertiser to fund its own contradiction. Each addition was a legislative response to a demonstrated shortfall of the previous menu, and each still operated only inside a case that a complainant had brought and proved.

### Did compensation under the 1986 Act require proof of negligence?

Compensation under Section 14(1)(d) was expressly tied to loss or injury suffered due to the negligence of the opposite party, so the head of compensation in terms required negligence, even though the other reliefs, including refund, replacement and removal of defects or deficiencies, turned on the defect or deficiency itself and not on fault.

One structural observation about the menu is worth making before the appeals architecture. Every relief in Section 14 is directed to the opposite party in the case. None of it runs against an industry, a product line in other hands, or a practice by traders who were not before the forum. A forum could order a manufacturer to withdraw its hazardous goods from being offered for sale, and that direction bound that manufacturer in that proceeding; it could not recall a product from every shelf in the country, and it could not bind a competitor running the identical practice next door. The ceiling on the Act's systemic effect was drafted into its remedial section, and it is the textual root of the namable claim this article presses at the end.

Costs under the Act completed the remedial scheme in both directions, and the direction against the complainant is the half usually forgotten. Section 14 empowered the forum to provide for adequate costs to parties, which in practice meant the successful complainant recovered a modest sum toward the expense of the proceeding rather than anything resembling a civil court's costs schedule. Section 26 supplied the mirror discipline: a complaint found frivolous or vexatious was to be dismissed for reasons recorded in writing, with an order that the complainant pay the opposite party such costs, not exceeding ten thousand rupees, as the forum specified. The ceiling dates the provision to the statute's early scale of values, and the forums used the power sparingly, conscious that a cheap forum chilled by costs orders would deter the genuine complainant along with the vexatious one. Between the two provisions the Act struck the balance its whole design assumes: entry nearly free, abuse mildly expensive, and the merits rather than the money deciding who used the room.


## Appeals, deposits, enforcement and penalties

The appellate ladder ran District Forum to State Commission under Section 15, State Commission to National Commission under Section 19, and National Commission to the Supreme Court under Section 23, each appeal to be filed within thirty days of the order, with power in the appellate body to entertain a late appeal on sufficient cause shown. One trap caught generations of examinees and some practitioners: appeals lay only against orders made in original jurisdiction. The State Commission's revisional orders, and the National Commission's revisional orders, were not appealable under these sections, a limit written into the words of Sections 19 and 23 themselves, which confine the appeal to orders made in exercise of the original complaints power.

From 15 March 2003, when the amendment of 2002 took effect, an appeal carried a pre-deposit. No appeal by a person required to pay any amount in terms of an order of the District Forum would be entertained by the State Commission unless the appellant had deposited in the prescribed manner fifty per cent of that amount or twenty-five thousand rupees, whichever was less. For an appeal to the National Commission the figures were fifty per cent or thirty-five thousand rupees, whichever was less, and for an appeal to the Supreme Court, fifty per cent or fifty thousand rupees, whichever was less. Before that amendment, the Act imposed no deposit at all: an aggrieved party could carry an order upward on the strength of the thirty-day window and a sufficient-cause application if late. The change was aimed at the judgment debtor who appealed to postpone payment, and the ceilings kept the barrier low for small orders while scaling it for large ones. Readers comparing the deposit with the successor statute's rule, which requires fifty per cent of the amount without the same monetary ceiling structure, will find that comparison developed in the comparison article; here it is enough that the 1986 Act, in its final form, asked for half the amount subject to a modest cap.

Enforcement stood on two legs. Under Section 25, in the form substituted by the amendment of 2002, every order could be enforced by the issuing body in the same manner as if it were a decree of a court in a pending suit, and where the body could not execute it, the order could be sent to the court within whose jurisdiction the company's registered office or the person's residence, business or workplace lay, to be executed as a decree sent for execution. Under Section 27, non-compliance was also an offence: a trader or person, or a complainant, who failed or omitted to comply with an order was punishable with imprisonment for a term not less than one month and up to three years, or with fine not less than two thousand rupees and up to ten thousand rupees, or with both, subject to a proviso allowing a lesser sentence where the circumstances of the case so required. The forums exercising this penal power acted as judicial magistrates of the first class for the purpose, a conferment made express by the amendment of 2002, and Section 27A, inserted by the same amendment, provided an appeal against a conviction under Section 27 to the next body in the ladder, notwithstanding the finality language elsewhere in the Act. The combination mattered: a decree route for the money and a criminal route for the defiance, both inside the same statute.


## Finality, and the forums' limited power over their own orders

Section 24 declares that every order of a District Forum, State Commission or National Commission shall, if no appeal has been preferred against it, be final. The Act as enacted contained no power of review: a forum that had made an order could not reopen it because a party returned with a better argument or a document that had been available all along. The Supreme Court settled the position in Rajeev Hitendra Pathak v. Achyut Kashinath Karekar, reported at (2011) 9 SCC 541, holding that the District Forums and State Commissions have no power to recall or review their own orders, the statute having conferred finality without the correcting jurisdiction that the Code of Civil Procedure gives a court. The forums were, in the language lawyers use, functus officio on pronouncing their orders, subject to the appeal the Act provided and to the constitutional writ jurisdiction of the High Courts, which remained available against forum orders throughout the Act's life and was regularly exercised on jurisdictional and natural justice grounds.

Two qualifications kept the finality rule workable. The amendment of 2002 armed the National Commission with a set of housekeeping powers it had lacked: Section 22A empowered it to set aside its own ex parte orders where the respondent or applicant showed sufficient cause for the earlier non-appearance, Section 22B empowered it to transfer cases between State Commissions, Section 22C provided for circuit benches, and Section 22D protected proceedings from invalidity by reason of a vacancy in the office of President. An ex parte order, passed against a party who had not been heard, was thus corrigible by the body that made it, while a contested order, passed after hearing, was correctable only upward. The distinction is principled and was maintained in practice: finality protected the heard case, the set-aside power rescued the unheard one, and parties who confused the two routes, filing review applications dressed as restoration applications, generated a steady corrective jurisprudence of their own. For the practitioner with an older matter, this is one of the points on which the text must be read exactly: the date of the order, the tier that made it, and whether it was ex parte determine which, if any, corrective door stands open, and the general information in this article is no substitute for that reading.

## Limitation and administrative control: Sections 24A and 24B

For its first six years the Act contained no limitation period of its own, and the forums borrowed and argued. The amendment of 1993 repaired the omission by inserting Section 24A, which provides that a District Forum, State Commission or National Commission shall not admit a complaint unless it is filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action has arisen. The same section permits a complaint to be entertained after that period if the complainant satisfies the forum that there was sufficient cause for not filing within it, with the discipline that the forum must record its reasons for condoning the delay. The two-year period, running from the cause of action rather than from the transaction, generated its own jurisprudence on continuing causes, recurring deficiencies in services such as insurance and housing, and the date from which time ran where a trader kept promising performance. What matters for a working model of the Act is the architecture: a short, uniform limitation for every tier, a condonation power modelled on sufficient cause, and a reasons requirement that made condonation a reviewable judicial act rather than an indulgence.

The same amending Act inserted Section 24B on administrative control. The National Commission was given administrative control over all State Commissions in specified matters: calling for periodical returns regarding the institution, disposal and pendency of cases; issuing instructions for the adoption of uniform procedure in the hearing of matters, prior service of copies of documents produced by one party on the other, furnishing English translations of judgments written in any language, and speedy grant of copies; and generally overseeing the functioning of the State Commissions and District Forums to ensure the objects of the Act were served, without interfering with their quasi-judicial freedom. State Commissions held corresponding control over the District Forums in their jurisdiction. The provision is easy to skim past and worth pausing on, because it is the closest the 1986 Act ever came to system management: the apex body could count the arrears, standardise the paperwork and circulate instructions, and the statute expressly fenced that power off from the merits of any case. Counting pendency is not the same as clearing it, and oversight that may not touch outcomes is oversight of form rather than of substance. Section 24B therefore illustrates, inside the adjudicatory chapter itself, the same limitation the councils showed in Chapter II: the Act could supervise its own machinery in administrative terms while possessing no instrument to act on the market whose conduct filled the dockets.

## Section 3 and the architecture of concurrent remedies

Section 3 is thirteen words of operative text with an outsized effect: the provisions of the Act shall be in addition to and not in derogation of the provisions of any other law for the time being in force. The clause placed the consumer forums alongside the civil courts rather than above or instead of them. A buyer could sue on the contract in the ordinary courts, complain to the forum, invoke a sectoral regulator where one existed, or pursue a criminal complaint for cheating, and the choice of one route did not, by force of Section 3 alone, extinguish the others, though the ordinary doctrines against double recovery and parallel proceedings disciplined the practice. The Supreme Court read the clause in Fair Air Engineers Pvt. Ltd. v. N. K. Modi, holding that the remedies under the Act are in addition to and not in derogation of other remedies, and that the forums are to be approached notwithstanding an arbitration agreement between the parties, because the public law character of the consumer remedy took it outside the private bargain to arbitrate. That reading made the forum a genuinely additional door and explained much of its early caseload: claimants voted for the forum with their feet because it was faster, cheaper and, after that decision, confirmed to survive their own contracts' arbitration clauses.

Section 3 also explains a recurring pattern in the Act's litigation: because the forums were additional, every other legal regime kept intersecting with them. Tenancy law, co-operative society law, electricity law, insurance law and medical negligence law all supplied the substantive standards against which defect and deficiency were measured, while the forum supplied the procedure and the relief. The Act was, in this sense, a remedial shell fitted over the whole body of Indian private law, which is why its definitions could be so short and its reach so long. It is also why disputes about the boundary of the definition of service, rather than disputes about the forums' powers, dominate the case law: once inside the shell, the machinery worked simply; the fights were all at the gate.

## The judicial expansion: housing

The statute's text did not obviously contemplate the two fields that came to dominate its dockets, housing and medicine, and the courts put both there. The housing line begins with the definition of service, whose inclusive list named banking, financing, insurance, transport and other facilities but said nothing, as enacted, about the construction of houses. Development authorities and builders argued that selling a plot or constructing a flat was a transaction in immovable property, not a service, and that allottees' grievances about delay, escalation and defective construction belonged to the civil courts or to the authorities' own grievance cells.

Parliament moved in 1993, when the amending Act inserted the words "housing construction" into the definition of service with effect from 18 June 1993. The Supreme Court moved within the same year. In Lucknow Development Authority v. M. K. Gupta, decided in November 1993 and reported at (1994) 1 SCC 243, the Court considered the complaint of an allottee against a statutory development authority and held that the construction of a house or flat for consideration is a service within the Act, that a statutory authority rendering such a service is amenable to the forums, and that the Act's protective purpose required its definitions to be construed in favour of the consumer the statute existed to protect. The decision did two further pieces of work that shaped everything after it. It confirmed that compensation under the Act could be awarded for the mental agony and harassment a consumer suffers, treating the suffering caused by an authority's arbitrary and delaying conduct as a compensable injury and not merely a grievance, and it used the occasion to describe the misfeasance of public authorities in strong terms, fastening the award to the conduct of the authority rather than to the public exchequer in principle. Builder litigation became the single largest category of consumer litigation for long stretches of the Act's life, and the doctrinal bridge by which homebuyers later reached the real estate regulator was built on this holding: anybody advising a buyer with an older matter will find the housing analysis continued in [the comparison of the forums open to homebuyers under the consumer statute and the real estate statute](/2019/02/15/rera-vs-consumer-protection-homebuyers/).

The housing holding also set the pattern for the Act's treatment of public bodies generally. A government department or statutory authority that charged for what it did was a service provider like any other, and the forums entertained complaints against insurers owned by the state, nationalised banks, the railways, electricity boards and telephone departments in very large numbers. Sovereign immunity arguments found no purchase in a statute whose definitions made no exception for the state, and the practical consequence was considerable: for many citizens, the consumer forum was the first adjudicatory body before which a public authority had to answer, promptly and in person, for the quality of a service it had supplied for payment.

## The judicial expansion: medicine

The medical line was harder, because the service definition excluded services rendered free of charge and under a contract of personal service, and the profession argued that the doctor and patient relationship was personal, fiduciary and unsuited to summary adjudication by a mixed bench. The question reached a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court in Indian Medical Association v. V. P. Shantha, reported at (1995) 6 SCC 651. The Court held that the rendering of medical services falls within the definition of service, that a medical practitioner consulted for a fee is not rendering a contract of personal service of the kind the clause excludes, and that doctors and hospitals are therefore amenable to the forums for deficiency in service, with negligence judged by the standards of medical negligence law.

The free of charge exclusion was resolved by dividing the field into categories, and the categories are the operative legacy of the case. Where a doctor or hospital rendered service free of charge to every patient, the service stood outside the Act. Where a charge was levied, the patient was a consumer in the ordinary way. The contested middle case, hospitals that charged some patients and treated others free, was resolved against exclusion: the Court held that patients treated free in such an institution were beneficiaries of a service hired for consideration by the paying patients as a class, so the free patient was also a consumer and could complain. Government hospitals fell to be classified on the same test, by whether charges were levied, rather than by ownership. The practical consequence was that medical negligence acquired a second forum across the country, cheaper and faster than the civil court, and a substantial jurisprudence on standards, informed consent and expert evidence grew up inside consumer commissions, a field whose wider health-law setting is mapped in [the guide to health laws in India](/2015/06/01/health-laws-india-complete-guide/).

The profession's objections were heard and answered rather than ignored, and the exchange is a model of the stance this series takes toward contested readings. The Indian Medical Association argued that summary forums would push doctors into defensive medicine, that negligence in a discipline of judgment and emergency could not be assessed by lay members, and that the threat of complaints would burden a scarce profession. The Court answered that the forums possessed civil court powers for expert evidence, that the standard applied remained the medical standard of the reasonably competent practitioner, and that a patient injured by negligence was not to be denied an affordable forum because the defendant belonged to a learned profession. Later decisions refined the working rules: the forums leaned on medical literature and expert testimony, the Supreme Court in cases such as Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab, which was a criminal prosecution, restated the threshold for criminal negligence of doctors in terms the forums also drew upon, and compensation awards in the largest cases were calibrated in decisions that treated the consumer commissions as competent to handle complex quantum. The point for the working model is simpler: after Shantha, the two largest service sectors in middle-class life, housing and healthcare, were inside a statute whose text had named neither.


## The services docket: insurance, banking, transport and the professions

Although housing and medicine supplied the famous cases, the forums' daily bread was the ordinary services economy, and a working model of the Act should show how the single concept of deficiency disciplined sector after sector. Insurance generated the largest single stream. The complaint pattern recurred with variations: a claim repudiated for suppression of a material fact, a claim closed as no-claim for want of a document the insurer had never demanded, a settlement voucher pressed on a policyholder for a fraction of the sum insured. The commissions' settled approach treated the policy as a contract whose exclusions the insurer had to prove, required the insurer to establish that a fact said to be suppressed was material and was within the proposer's knowledge, and treated a repudiation letter that merely recited a clause, without applying it to the disclosed facts, as itself a deficiency. Fire, motor, marine and mediclaim dockets together taught a generation of insurers that the repudiation letter was a litigable document, and claims practice changed in response, which is the closest the complaint model ever came to market-wide effect: not an order against an industry, but a thousand orders whose reasoning an industry could not ignore.

Banking and finance supplied the second stream: loans sanctioned and not disbursed on the promised schedule, fixed deposit receipts dishonoured at maturity on technical objections, lockers and safe custody failures, excessive or unauthorised charges, and the long-running grievance of the small borrower against the recovery practices of lenders. Transport and carriage supplied the third: consignments lost or damaged by carriers, railway claims for non-delivery and for the condition of reserved accommodation, courier failures, and the deficiency analysis applied to each on the undertaking the carrier had given. Electricity and telephones, the monopoly utilities of the Act's first decade, supplied a fourth: wrong billing, defective meters, disconnection without due notice, and interminable delay in providing a connection that had been paid for, complaints in which the forums functioned, in effect, as the only accessible regulator of a state monopoly's counter behaviour. Professional services beyond medicine followed the Shantha logic at lower volume: the forums entertained complaints about deficient services by other professionals where a consideration was charged, while continuing to mark the boundary of the contract of personal service that the definition excludes.

Two features of this docket matter for assessing the Act. The first is that deficiency is a standard of performance, not a schedule of offences, so the forums' real work was standard-setting by accumulation: each sector's decided cases converged on what a competent insurer, bank or carrier was expected to do, and the expectation, once settled at the National Commission level, was applied by District Forums across the country without any rule ever being made. The Act thus produced a body of service standards through adjudication that a regulatory statute would have produced through regulation, more slowly, less uniformly, and only after someone had been harmed, but recognisably. The second feature is the direction of travel: as the economy liberalised after 1991 and private providers multiplied in insurance, banking, telecom, education and health, the forums' jurisdiction expanded automatically with the market, because the definitions were indifferent to ownership and asked only whether a service had been hired for consideration. A statute drafted for the economy of state monopolies became, without a word being amended, the consumer court of the liberalised economy, and the strain recorded in the assessment section of this article is in large part the strain of that unplanned inheritance.

## The commercial purpose exclusion, argued both ways

No provision of the Act defeated more claims at the gate than the commercial purpose exclusion, and the brief of this article requires both readings to be put at strength, because both are respectable and the courts oscillated between them for three decades.

The narrow reading, favouring consumers, begins with the explanation Parliament itself wrote: goods bought and used by a person exclusively to earn a livelihood by self-employment are not bought for a commercial purpose. On this reading the exclusion aims at trade, at the buyer who purchases for resale or feeds goods into a profit generating operation of scale, and not at the individual whose purchase is the tool of his own work. The livelihood buyer and the trading buyer both spend money hoping to earn, but the statute's protective purpose, its fee structure and its summary procedure were built for the party who lacks bargaining power and legal resources, and that description fits the self-employed purchaser even though his purchase is economic. Supporters of this reading note that the exclusion speaks of the purpose of the purchase at the time it is made, so the inquiry is into the buyer's object and scale of use, not into whether the goods in the abstract are capable of commercial deployment.

The wide reading, favouring suppliers, begins with the ordinary meaning of the words and the design of the forum. A commercial purpose is a purpose of commerce, and a dispute between two businesses about a machine, a vehicle fleet or a printing press is a trade dispute, turning on specifications, correspondence, trade usage and accounting that the summary procedure of Section 13, built for affidavit evidence and short timelines, was not designed to digest. On this reading, routing business disputes to the forums distorts both systems: it floods consumer dockets with commercial litigation between parties of comparable strength, and it deprives the commercial defendant of the full trial, discovery and appellate structure that the civil courts supply. The exclusion, its supporters say, is not a trap but a boundary, and it means what it says.

The Supreme Court's leading attempt to hold the line between these readings is Laxmi Engineering Works v. P. S. G. Industrial Institute, reported at (1995) 3 SCC 583. The Court held that the question is whether the goods are bought for use in an activity engaged in for generating profit on a large scale, closely and directly connected with the purchase, or whether the buyer uses the goods himself in an activity by which he earns his livelihood. A lathe bought by a small workshop owner who works it himself fell on the consumer side of the line; machinery bought for a commercial establishment run as a business fell outside it. The Court treated the inquiry as fact-specific, directed at the buyer's purpose and the scale of the operation, and warned against converting the exception for livelihood into a general passport for business purchases.

Two later developments completed the statutory position. The amendment of 2002 extended the exclusion to services: a person who avails of services for any commercial purpose was placed outside the consumer definition for services as the definition had long placed the commercial buyer of goods, subject to the same livelihood explanation. And the successor statute carried the exclusion and the explanation across in materially identical terms, so the argument described here continues under the new Act on the same words, which the comparison article examines. For the 1986 Act itself, the honest summary is that the exclusion did the work its wide-reading supporters wanted at the top of the market, keeping substantial business disputes in the civil courts, while the explanation did the work its narrow-reading supporters wanted at the bottom, keeping the self-employed buyer inside the forum. The contested middle, the small firm buying equipment it would operate through employees, produced most of the litigation and most of the divergent orders, and no formulation the courts produced dissolved that middle entirely.

## What the Consumer Protection Act 1986 had and lacked

Its left column lists the machinery and remedies the statute provided; its right column lists the four elements it did not contain, which are exactly the four elements the replacement statute added. The right column is the argument of this article in compressed form.

| A three-tier system of District Forums, State Commissions and a National Commission deciding consumer disputes by a summary statutory procedure | A regulator with power to investigate a trade practice on its own motion, search, seize, or act against a practice across a market; in practice the gap was left unfilled, because the trade practices commission under the monopolies statute worked slowly on a different docket and no consumer authority could open a market-wide inquiry |
| Declared consumer rights in Section 6, including safety, information, choice, hearing, redressal and education | A product liability regime fixing responsibility for harm caused by a defective product on manufacturers, service providers and sellers by a self-contained chapter; in practice an injured buyer had to press the case into defect, deficiency and negligence under Section 14 and the general law of tort, with the burden and the gaps that route carried |

## The four gaps, and how each was managed while the Act lasted

The regulator gap came first in time and in importance. Every other consumer system India built in the same decades contained some body that could initiate: the securities regulator could investigate a listed company, the telecom regulator could set and enforce standards, the food authority could license and seize. The consumer statute alone relied wholly on victims. The consequence was a specific and predictable pattern of enforcement. Harms that were large per victim, a flat delivered years late, an operation negligently performed, generated complaints, because the loss justified the effort. Harms that were small per victim and enormous in aggregate, a misleading claim printed on a million packets, a charge quietly added to every bill, generated almost none, because no single loss repaid the filing effort and the association complaints that might have aggregated them were few. The forums' orders, read across three decades, are therefore a biased sample of Indian consumer harm: rich in individual catastrophe, thin in systemic practice. That bias is not a criticism of the forums, which decided the cases brought to them, and decided many of them well. It is a criticism of a statute that gave the system no other intake.

The product liability gap was doctrinal rather than institutional. A buyer injured by a defective product had to construct the claim from the concepts the Act supplied: the goods suffered from a defect, the seller or manufacturer was the opposite party, and compensation under Section 14(1)(d) required loss or injury due to the negligence of the opposite party. There was no chapter allocating responsibility along the supply chain, no statutory list of the defences available to a manufacturer, no treatment of product sellers who were not manufacturers, and no rule for harm caused by a product that was dangerous although made exactly as intended. Forums did award compensation for injuries caused by exploding appliances, contaminated products and defective medical devices, and they borrowed negligence principles from the general law to do it, but each case was an improvisation on the same spare text. The absence showed most in the pleading: complainants habitually framed product injuries as deficiency in service wherever a service element could be found, because the service limb of the Act was wider and better litigated than any product limb the statute offered.

The mediation gap was the quietest. The Act assumed adjudication: a complaint, a version, evidence, an order. Settlement happened, as it happens everywhere, in the corridor and by consent, and consumer matters were also referred in large numbers to Lok Adalats organised under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, where a compromise could be recorded as an award. But the statute itself contained no mediation chapter, no panel of mediators, no provision for a settlement reached in mediation to be recorded and enforced as an order, and no stage of the proceeding at which mediation was the designed next step. The forums' timelines, and the endeavour clause of 2002, pressed parties toward early disposal without giving them a structured alternative to a contested hearing. For disputes whose real content was a continuing commercial relationship, a delivery schedule, or a repair that both sides wanted supervised rather than ordered, the statute offered a verdict and nothing else.

The electronic commerce gap was one of vocabulary that became a gap of jurisdiction and proof. The Act was drafted for a world of shops, printed advertisements, cash memos and goods that could be sealed and sent to a laboratory. It said nothing about distance selling, online marketplaces, digital products, or the intermediary that displays a seller's goods without ever holding them. When electronic commerce arrived, the forums coped, as they had coped before, by construction: a marketplace could be a trader or a service provider on the facts, an online misdescription could be an unfair trade practice, and a payment gateway's failure could be a deficiency in a banking or financing service. But the coping was case-by-case, territorial jurisdiction strained where the seller, the platform, the server and the buyer sat in four different places, and the statute gave no rule for the liability of the intermediary as such. A reader assembling the materials for examination study may find it useful to keep these notes and the statute extracts together in [an office file viewer for working through the Act and the amendment texts side by side](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), since the four gaps are clearest when the 1986 text is read physically beside its replacement.

## A remedy without a regulator: the namable claim

The claim this article wants a reader to carry away can be stated in one sentence: the 1986 Act gave the consumer a remedy and gave nobody a regulator, and the history of Indian consumer protection between 1987 and 2020 is the history of that asymmetry. A remedy answers harm after it occurs, one complainant at a time, on proof, with relief confined to the parties. A regulator answers harm before or while it occurs, across a market, on its own information, with relief that binds strangers to any individual case. The 1986 Act contained the first instrument in an unusually accessible form and the second instrument not at all. Its councils could only advise. Its forums could only decide cases brought to them. Its apex commission could call for returns about pendency and instruct on procedure, and was expressly barred from interfering with quasi-judicial freedom, which is to say from the substance of what the system did. No provision allowed any authority to investigate a trade practice unfolding in the market, to order a product recalled from every shelf, to penalise a misleading advertisement as an advertisement, or to require a class of traders to change a standard term.

The series to which this article belongs has repeatedly found that the character of a statute is set by whether it creates an enforcement body or only a cause of action, and the consumer statute is the cleanest instance in the entire set. Compare the design choices Parliament made elsewhere in the same era: the securities legislation built a board with licensing, inspection and penalty powers; the competition legislation that replaced the monopolies statute built a commission that could inquire on its own motion; the food safety legislation consolidated enforcement in an authority with licensing and recall. In each case the statute's authors concluded that private complaint alone could not police a market. In the consumer statute of 1986, Parliament either did not draw that conclusion or drew it and preferred the cheaper institutional build, and the consequence was thirty-three years in which the state's response to consumer harm was entirely reactive. The 2019 Act, whatever else is said about it, accepted the conclusion the 1986 Act had deferred: it created the Central Consumer Protection Authority with investigation, recall and penalty powers, and thereby conceded, in legislative form, the claim made here.

One refinement of the claim is owed in fairness. The absence of a regulator was partly a function of the Act's moment. In 1986 the Indian state regulated markets mainly by licensing entry and controlling prices, and a consumer statute built around adjudication fitted the era's instruments: it is the same Parliament that ran industrial policy through licences and trade practices through a commission of inquiry. The consumer movement that pressed for the Act asked for forums, because forums were what its members' grievances needed, and it got them. The verdict of this article is therefore not that the Act was misconceived at birth, but that it was a first-generation answer whose limits were structural and visible from the start, and that the long delay before the second generation arrived, a full working lifetime for the statute, allowed the four gaps to become the settled shape of the field.

## The honest assessment: what worked and where the Act strained

What worked should be recorded before the failures, because the failures are more often quoted. The forums democratised a remedy. They entertained complaints in the tens of lakhs across the Act's life, in districts where the civil court was a byword for delay, and they did so at a cost structure that let a pensioner litigate a repudiated insurance claim and a farmer litigate a defective seed lot. They generated, through the National Commission's appellate discipline, a recognisably national consumer jurisprudence on insurance repudiation, medical negligence, housing delay and banking deficiency that practitioners could cite and traders could price. They disciplined public authorities in individual cases in a way no earlier forum had. And they kept their procedure recognisably summary for most litigants most of the time: a complaint, a version, affidavit evidence, a hearing, an order.

The strain showed in the two places the statute could not reach: time and vacancies. The disposal timelines were an endeavour from 2002 and an aspiration before it, and the gap between the three-month standard and actual disposal widened as the dockets grew. The causes were structural, and the brief for this article is right to insist that they be assigned correctly. The shortfall arose from vacancies in the benches, from appointments made late or not made, from infrastructure that in many districts amounted to a room and a clerk, and from case volume that grew with the economy the Act served, rather than from any defect in the drafting of the procedure itself. A District Forum whose President's post lies vacant cannot sit, and a State Commission carrying years of unfilled member posts cannot clear an appellate docket, whatever the statute says about endeavour. Governments that praised the Act in principle funded it in arrears in practice, and the National Commission's annual returns, called for under the administrative control the Act itself supplied, recorded the consequence in pendency figures that climbed while disposal in the functioning benches remained respectable.

A second strain was doctrinal overload. The forums became the default destination for categories of dispute the Act had never been shaped to carry at volume: builder litigation involving projects worth hundreds of crores and hundreds of allottees, medical cases requiring layered expert evidence, insurance cases turning on policy construction of real subtlety. The summary procedure bent under the weight, cross-examination and expert contest crept in by necessity, and the larger State Commissions and the National Commission came to resemble the courts they were built to bypass, in everything except their arrears. Critics who said the forums had become slow were describing a symptom; the cause was that the Act's success in attracting exactly the grievances the civil courts handled worst had delivered those grievances in quantities its institutional budget never matched.

A third strain, quieter still, was the ceiling on precedential effect. An order bound the parties; a practice discontinued against one trader continued under every other trader's letterhead; a misleading campaign ended, where it ended, after the injury had run its course. This is the regulator gap restated from the receiving end, and it is recorded here because the assessment section, not the polemic, is where the article's verdict belongs: the Act's machinery for deciding disputes was sound and often admirable, its machinery for improving markets did not exist, and the distance between those two sentences is the distance between the 1986 Act and its replacement.

Anyone relying on the position described in this article for a live matter should treat it as general information about a repealed statute and not as advice on an individual case, since limitation, the savings provisions and the facts of the matter govern what a forum will do, and those are unforgiving in detail.


## The borrowed definition: unfair trade practices and the monopolies statute

The enumerated list in the definition of unfair trade practice was not drafted from a blank page. When the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 was amended in 1984, Parliament inserted into that statute a definition of unfair trade practice in Section 36A, listing false and misleading representations, bargain-price advertising, unmeant gifts, non-compliant safety standards and hoarding in recognisably the same sequence. The consumer statute of 1986 lifted the concept and most of the list into Section 2(1)(r), and the borrowing is instructive about how the two regimes divided the work. Under the monopolies statute, an unfair trade practice was something a commission inquired into in the public interest, and an individual consumer's compensation claim before that commission was an appendage of the inquiry. Under the consumer statute, the same practice was a ground of complaint by the person it had harmed, and compensation was the centre of the proceeding rather than an appendage.

The overlap produced a decade of forum questions: could a complainant pursue both, did a finding in one bind the other, and which body should take a practice that was at once an individual fraud and a market-wide pattern. Section 3 answered part of it by making the consumer remedy additional, and the forums proceeded on the footing that the monopolies commission's existence did not oust them. The deeper answer is the one this article has pressed throughout: the two statutes represented two theories of enforcement, inquiry from above and complaint from below, and the consumer statute chose complaint from below and built nothing else. When the monopolies statute was itself repealed and its functions folded into the competition framework, the unfair trade practice list survived in the consumer statute as the working catalogue for individual cases, a genealogy that explains why the list reads like a regulator's checklist in a statute that has no regulator.

Restrictive trade practices followed the same path in miniature. The consumer statute's definition, the tied sale imposed as a condition precedent, is narrower than the monopolies statute's elaborate catalogue of restrictive practices, and deliberately so: the consumer forum was not asked to police distribution agreements or resale price maintenance across an industry, only to relieve the buyer who had been told that the goods he wanted came only with goods he did not. The narrowing is a small drafting decision that reveals the statute's consistent instinct, to convert market concepts into individual grievances and to leave the market concepts themselves to other law.


## The associational complaint and the public interest door

One of the Act's quiet radicalisms sits in the complainant definition: a registered voluntary consumer association could complain whether or not the consumer affected was its member, and the Central and State Governments could complain in their own names. The provision converted consumer protection from a purely private remedy into a field with institutional plaintiffs. Organisations such as Common Cause in Delhi, the Consumer Guidance Society of India in Mumbai and the Consumer Education and Research Centre in Ahmedabad, which had spent the years before 1986 documenting grievances they could not litigate, acquired a forum in which a documented pattern could be filed as a case. Their complaints characteristically took the second and third discourse forms the Act invited: the practice complaint, directed at a standard term or a billing method used against thousands, and the representative complaint, brought on behalf of numerous consumers with the same interest under the class limb of Section 12, with the permission of the forum, borrowing the representative suit machinery of Order I Rule 8 of the Code of Civil Procedure as Section 13(6) directs.

The representative device deserves its own analysis, because it was the closest the Act came to a class action and it exposes both the ingenuity and the limit of the design. A flat buyers' association could file one complaint for every allottee of a stalled project, prove the common deficiency once, and take an order that ran for all, with individual compensation computed on the common findings. Insurers' standard repudiation letters, a development authority's uniform escalation demand, a transport undertaking's standard refusal: each could be met once, collectively, instead of a thousand times. What the device could not do was reach beyond the consumers represented to the practice in the market. The order bound the opposite party toward the class before the forum; it did not bind the trader's competitors, did not create a fund from the wrongdoer's gains except through the narrow five per cent route added in 2002 for unidentifiable victims, and did not publicise itself. The contrast with the regulator model is again the point: aggregation of victims is a way of making complaint viable at scale, but it remains complaint, reactive and party-bound, and the associational plaintiffs who used it best understood its ceiling most clearly, since they spent their other hours campaigning for precisely the investigative authority the Act withheld.

The government complaint, by contrast, was a door barely opened. In principle the Central and State Governments could file against a practice harming consumers at large, with the resources to prove a technical case and no fee or limitation anxiety. In practice the power was used sparingly across the Act's life, for reasons that are structural rather than accidental: the governments that owned the power also owned many of the largest service providers against whom complaints ran, the public sector insurers, banks, transport undertakings and development authorities, and a state does not readily litigate against its own instrumentalities in a forum designed for citizens. The councils of Chapter II, chaired by the ministers responsible for consumer affairs, were the governments' chosen instrument instead, promotional where the complaint power would have been adversarial. A reader weighing the Act's design should register the irony without overstating it: the one complainant with standing, resources and a public mandate to attack systemic practices was the one least placed to use the power, which is a further reason the system's intake stayed individual even where its standing rules were collective.

## A complaint's life: two passages through the machinery

The working model is clearest when it is run. Consider first the defective goods complaint that Section 13 was written around. A buyer purchases a refrigerator that fails within its warranty period; the seller's service network attends twice and the cooling failure recurs. The buyer files a written complaint in the District Forum of the district where the seller carries on business, alleging a defect within Section 2(1)(f), measured both against the standard the law requires of such an appliance and against the performance the manufacturer claimed for it in its own literature. The forum refers a copy to the opposite party, which must give its version within thirty days, extendable by fifteen. The seller disputes the defect and attributes the failure to voltage fluctuation and misuse. Because the defect cannot be determined without analysis or test, the forum obtains a sample of the goods, in practice the appliance itself or its sealed compressor unit, authenticates it as prescribed, and refers it to an appropriate laboratory with a direction to report within forty-five days. The laboratory reports a manufacturing defect in the sealed unit. The forum forwards the report to the opposite party, receives written objections to the method of testing, hears both sides on the report and the objections, and, being satisfied that the goods suffer from the defect specified, makes an order under Section 14: replacement with a new appliance of similar description free from defect, or refund of the price, with compensation for the loss and inconvenience proved, and costs. If the seller does not comply, the buyer has the decree route of Section 25 and the penalty route of Section 27, and if the seller appeals, the appeal lies to the State Commission within thirty days and, after March 2003, only on deposit of half the amount ordered or twenty-five thousand rupees, whichever is less.

The services passage runs on the other limb of Section 13. A mediclaim policyholder's claim is repudiated by the insurer on the ground of a pre-existing disease said to have been suppressed in the proposal form. The complaint alleges deficiency in service: the repudiation, if wrongful, is a failure in the manner of performance the insurer undertook. There is no sample to seal and no laboratory to consult; the forum decides on the evidence the parties bring, the proposal form, the policy, the medical records, the repudiation letter, taken on affidavit. The recurring doctrinal questions of this docket were worked out across thousands of such cases: the insurer that alleged suppression had to prove it, an exclusion clause had to be proved and construed as written, and a repudiation made without applying the policy's own terms to the disclosed facts was a deficiency even where the underlying claim was arguable. The forum's order, where the complaint succeeded, directed payment of the claim amount as compensation for the loss caused by the deficiency, frequently with interest and with compensation for the harassment of a policyholder made to litigate a claim bought for peace of mind. The two passages share the same skeleton, notice, version, evidence, satisfaction, order, and differ in the evidentiary apparatus the section deploys, which is exactly the design Section 13 announces: one procedure with a testing branch for goods and an evidence branch for everything else.

## Evidence and burdens inside the forums

Because the forums were not bound by the whole of the civil procedure and evidence codes, their evidentiary practice was built from the statute, the rules and the appellate commissions' discipline, and a working account of the Act should describe it. The complainant bore the initial burden of establishing the ingredients of the complaint: the transaction and the consideration, the defect or deficiency alleged, and the loss for which compensation was claimed. Evidence was ordinarily taken on affidavit, with cross-examination available where the forum considered it necessary, and the standard of persuasion was the civil standard of a preponderance of probabilities rather than proof beyond reasonable doubt. For defects that turned on the internal condition of goods, the laboratory route of Section 13 was the forum's answer to the expertise problem, and the report, once the parties had been heard on it, ordinarily decided the technical question.

Compensation required its own proof discipline. Section 14(1)(d) ties the award to loss or injury suffered due to the negligence of the opposite party, and the commissions insisted that compensation be related to the loss established, neither a windfall for the complainant nor a token for a proved injury. The mental agony head, opened by the housing cases, was applied where the conduct of the opposite party, a repudiation maintained in the teeth of the record, a possession withheld for years, made distress a foreseeable and proved consequence rather than a formula recited in every pleading. Quantum in the largest medical and insurance cases was reasoned from the claimant's circumstances in the manner of the general law of damages, and the appellate tiers corrected awards that were punitive in everything but name before the 2002 amendment made punitive damages an express power. Interest on awarded sums, granted from dates the commissions fixed by reference to when the loss crystallised, did much of the practical work of compensating for delay in payment, and the pattern of interest awards is part of what a reader of older orders must understand to make sense of their totals.

The opposite party's burdens were equally structured. A trader who said the goods conformed to the standard had the laboratory report to meet. An insurer who said the claim fell within an exclusion had to bring the case within the clause, and exclusions were read as written and not enlarged by implication against the insured. A seller who said the buyer was not a consumer because the purchase was commercial had to establish the character of the purchase, after which the buyer claiming the livelihood explanation had to bring himself within it. These allocations were rarely announced as doctrine in a single case; they accumulated from the appellate commissions' correction of forums that had placed a burden on the party who could not, in the nature of the transaction, discharge it. That accumulation, unglamorous and case-bound, is a large part of why the system's output became predictable enough to settle most grievances without a hearing at all: traders and insurers who knew what a forum would do with a file priced their offers accordingly.


## The human machinery: appointments, tenure and the cost of a vacancy

A statute of forums is only ever as strong as its appointment pipeline, and the 1986 Act's machinery chapters are where its history of delay was actually written. The design gave the District Forum a judicial President and two members, the State Commission a High Court judge as President with members, and the National Commission a Supreme Court judge as President with members, each body mixing judicial and lay strength so that the law would be applied by a bench that contained both legal discipline and experience of commerce, accountancy, industry and public affairs. Members served five-year terms or until the age ceiling of their tier, and were ineligible for reappointment, a rule meant to secure independence by removing the hope of renewal from the member's calculations. Appointments were routed through selection committees, and the Presidents of the two commissions were appointed after consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court or the Chief Justice of India respectively.

The working weakness was not the design but the supply. Posts fell vacant at the end of fixed terms on dates known years in advance, and replacements were repeatedly appointed late, because the process required governments to convene committees, courts to consult, and departments to notify, with no statutory consequence for letting a bench lapse. A District Forum cannot sit without its President, and a Commission short of members cannot multiply benches, so every vacancy converted directly into pendency, and pendency converted into the public verdict that the forums were as slow as the courts. The Act's own administrative machinery counted the damage: Section 24B returns on institution, disposal and pendency made the arrears visible to the National Commission, which could circulate instructions on procedure and copies and translations, and could do nothing at all about the empty chairs that were causing them.

This is the honest location of the Act's famous delay problem, and the article has insisted on it because the alternative explanation, that the summary procedure was badly drafted, does not survive reading the procedure. Section 13 is short, sequenced and workable; Section 13(3A) set a three-month endeavour that functioning benches in well supplied districts met or approached for ordinary cases. What the drafting could not supply was a bench, a courtroom, a stenographer and a budget line, year after year, in every district of every State. The lesson the repeal carried forward is institutional rather than textual: the successor statute reproduced much of the 1986 design, commissions, selection committees, fixed terms and all, because the design was not the failure. The failure was a political economy in which proclaiming consumer rights cost nothing and staffing consumer forums cost money, and the 1986 Act's thirty-three years are the record of that arithmetic working itself out.

## The repeal and the transition

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019, Act 35 of 2019, received the assent of the President on 9 August 2019. Its substantive provisions were brought into force in two tranches, on 20 July 2020 and 24 July 2020, and on its commencement the 1986 Act stood repealed by Section 107 of the new Act. The full account of what the replacement kept, discarded and added belongs to [the pillar article on the Consumer Protection Act 2019](/2020/12/01/consumer-protection-act-2019-explained/), and the systematic comparison of the two statutes belongs to [the article comparing the Consumer Protection Act 1986 with the Act of 2019](/2020/12/15/consumer-protection-1986-vs-2019/). This article's business with the repeal is narrower: what happened to the world the old Act had built, and in particular to its cases.

Section 107 carried a savings scheme whose core is that the repeal does not disturb the previous operation of the repealed Act or anything duly done under it. The working rule that emerged, and that the Supreme Court confirmed, is that complaints instituted under the 1986 Act before the commencement of the 2019 Act continue to be governed by the old Act and are decided by the corresponding commissions as if the repeal had not displaced the law under which they were filed. In Neena Aneja v. Jai Prakash Associates Ltd., decided in 2021, the Supreme Court rejected the argument that a complaint pending under the 1986 Act had to migrate to the jurisdictional scheme of the 2019 Act, holding that the consumer who had invoked the old statute retained the benefit of its framework, including its pecuniary limits, and that the change of law did not rewrite the forum for a case already instituted. The practical consequence is the one the discourse around the Act predicted: a very large body of cases continued to be decided under the 1986 Act for years after its repeal, by benches that bear the new Act's names but apply the old Act's text, its limits, its deposit rules and its reliefs to everything filed before the changeover.

The transition also preserved the forums' institutional memory in a second sense. The commissions of the 2019 Act are the successors of the forums and commissions of the 1986 Act in building, staff, docket and jurisprudence, and the case law built on the shared words of the two statutes, above all the definition of consumer with its commercial purpose exclusion and livelihood explanation, carried across because the words carried across. Where the new Act deliberately changed a rule, as it did for pecuniary jurisdiction by moving from the value-plus-compensation measure to the consideration paid, the old authorities remain the map of the old territory and are still cited for matters the repeal saved.


## Reading an older order: dating the law inside the document

Because the Act changed its figures twice and its remedial menu twice, an order made under it carries its own date in its contents, and a reader who knows the markers can place any order in the Act's life without being told when it was made. An order that takes jurisdiction over a complaint valued at three lakh rupees in a District Forum belongs to the period after the amendment of 1993 and before the amendment of 2002 had it been valued above five lakh, and to any period if the value sits below the era's ceiling; the recital of the ceiling in the jurisdictional paragraph is the surest guide, because forums recited the band they were applying. An appeal order that says nothing about a deposit belongs, in the ordinary course, to the years before 15 March 2003; an appeal order that records a deposit of half the amount or twenty-five thousand rupees, whichever is less, at the State Commission stage is speaking the language of the amended Act. A relief paragraph that awards punitive damages, orders a corrective advertisement, or directs payment of a percentage of the value of defective goods toward unidentifiable consumers cannot have been written before the amendment of 2002 took effect, because the words were not in Section 14 before then. A limitation discussion that cites Section 24A belongs after June 1993; a complaint of the Act's first six years that was entertained long after the transaction will be found arguing limitation without a statutory text, from the forums' practice of the period.

This is more than a parlour skill. The saved proceedings under the repeal continue to be governed by the law as it stood for the case, execution and appeal run on the figures of the era that produced the order, and an advocate or a litigant who misreads the vintage of an order will misstate the deposit on appeal, the band that conferred jurisdiction, and the reliefs that were available to be granted. The converse also holds for the researcher: the aggregate of orders from a period is evidence of the period's law in action, and reforms that are invisible in the statute book, the moment a new relief began to be used, the moment a deposit rule began to bite on appellants, are legible in the orders themselves. The 1986 Act rewards this kind of reading because its amendments were surgical rather than substitutive: the text accreted, and each accretion left a datable layer in the documents the system produced.

## Two working questions the repealed Act leaves behind

A repealed statute with a savings clause generates the same two questions in every consultation, and the article should answer them in terms a litigant can use.

The first question is which statute decides a given dispute. The answer turns on institution, not on the date of the underlying transaction alone. A complaint filed before the commencement of the replacement in July 2020 proceeds under the 1986 Act: its forum is selected by the old pecuniary bands, its relief is drawn from the old Section 14, its appeal carries the old deposit ceilings, and its limitation is the two years of Section 24A with condonation on recorded reasons. A complaint filed after the changeover is a creature of the new Act even where the purchase, the policy or the booking predates it, because what the savings scheme preserves is proceedings and accrued positions, not a general option to elect the older code. Boundary disputes arose, as boundary disputes always do, over complaints filed in the changeover window and over execution proceedings that straddled it, and they were resolved by asking what had been instituted, and under what, before the repeal took effect.

The second question is whether an order of the old forums can still be executed and appealed as if nothing had happened. Substantially, yes. The savings of the repeal, reinforced by the general savings doctrine of the General Clauses Act, 1897 for repealed enactments, keeps orders made under the 1986 Act enforceable through the Section 25 decree route and the Section 27 penalty route, and keeps the appellate ladder available for orders of the saved proceedings. The commissions that execute and hear those appeals are the successor bodies, applying the repealed Act to the saved matter. A reader holding an order of a District Forum from the Act's last years therefore does not hold a historical document; the order lives, and the machinery that enforces it is the machinery this article has described, continued under new signage.


## The Act in public memory

Statutes rarely acquire a place in ordinary speech, and this one did. The date of its assent, 24 December, is observed as National Consumer Rights Day in India, and the consumer awareness campaigns of the Department of Consumer Affairs, of which the slogan Jago Grahak Jago, wake up consumer, wake up, is the best known, built their public messaging on the rights the Act had declared and the forums it had created. The phrase consumer court entered household vocabulary as the name of the District Forum, although the Act never called its agencies courts, and the idea that a buyer could, without a lawyer and without a heavy fee, summon a manufacturer, an insurer or a government department before a bench in the district town became a settled piece of civic knowledge. For the historian of the field, that cultural deposit is part of the Act's record: it changed what purchasers believed they were entitled to expect, and traders' behaviour, in documentation, warranty practice and after-sales organisation, adjusted over time to the existence of a forum that sellers might actually have to attend. Measuring that adjustment is beyond the evidence any single article can marshal, and this one does not attempt figures. The qualitative point is safe and sufficient: a remedial statute that is widely known to exist disciplines conduct in transactions that never come near it, and the 1986 Act was known.

## Verdict

The Consumer Protection Act 1986 should be judged as what it was: the statute that taught Indian law to treat the buyer as a litigant worth designing for. It took a grievance class that the legal system had priced out of its own courts and gave it a forum, a vocabulary and a remedial menu, in thirty-one sections that a layperson could read. Its definitions were litigated for three decades because they were the load-bearing words of a working system, and its two great judicial expansions, housing in Lucknow Development Authority v. M. K. Gupta and medicine in Indian Medical Association v. V. P. Shantha, were possible because the text was written generously enough to bear them. Against that record stand the structural findings of this article: no regulator, no product liability chapter, no mediation, no word for electronic commerce, and a remedial section whose orders bound only the parties before the forum. The four gaps are not a list of drafting oversights. They are the outline of the 2019 Act, which added exactly those four things and refined much of the rest.

For the reader's next step, the field as a whole, including the sectoral statutes that share the consumer's territory and the forums that divide the work, is mapped in [the complete guide to consumer law in India](/2020/11/01/consumer-law-india-complete-guide/). The successor statute and the comparison have their own articles, linked above at the points where this article hands over to them. What remains here is the pillar's closing judgment. A statute that creates only a cause of action can vindicate the complainant who arrives. It cannot change the market that produced the complaint. The 1986 Act vindicated complainants in numbers no one had predicted in 1986, and the market waited thirty-three years for its regulator.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Consumer Protection Act 1986 establish?

It established a three-tier system of consumer disputes redressal agencies: a District Forum in each district, a State Commission in each State, and a National Commission at the Centre, together with Consumer Protection Councils at the Central, State and later district levels. Enacted as Act 68 of 1986, with Chapters I, II and IV in force from 15 April 1987 and Chapter III from 1 July 1987, it gave consumers a specialised forum operating by a summary statutory procedure, without a court fee proportionate to the claim and without any requirement to engage a lawyer, for complaints about defects in goods, deficiencies in services and unfair or restrictive trade practices.

### Q: What consumer rights did the 1986 Act recognise?

Section 6 declared six rights, stated as the objects of the Consumer Protection Councils: the right to be protected against the marketing of goods and services hazardous to life and property; the right to be informed about the quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price of goods or services; the right to be assured, wherever possible, access to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices; the right to be heard and to be assured that consumer interests receive due consideration at appropriate forums; the right to seek redressal against unfair or restrictive trade practices or unscrupulous exploitation; and the right to consumer education.

### Q: What reliefs could the forums grant under the 1986 Act?

Under Section 14 a forum satisfied that goods were defective or allegations about services were proved could direct the opposite party to remove the defect, replace the goods with new goods of similar description free from defect, return the price or charges paid, pay compensation for loss or injury caused by negligence, including punitive damages after the amendment of 2002, remove defects or deficiencies in services, discontinue an unfair or restrictive trade practice and not repeat it, stop offering hazardous goods for sale and withdraw them, cease manufacturing hazardous goods, pay a sum of at least five per cent of the value of defective goods or services where numerous unidentifiable consumers were injured, issue a corrective advertisement at the opposite party's cost, and provide adequate costs to parties.

### Q: Were doctors and builders covered by the 1986 Act?

Yes, in both cases through interpretation and amendment rather than the original text alone. The amendment of 1993 inserted housing construction into the definition of service, and in Lucknow Development Authority v. M. K. Gupta the Supreme Court held that construction of a house or flat for consideration is a service and that a statutory development authority is amenable to the forums. In Indian Medical Association v. V. P. Shantha, the Court held that medical services rendered for consideration are a service under the Act, except services rendered free of charge to every patient; a hospital charging some patients while treating others free brought even its free patients within the definition, as beneficiaries of a service hired for consideration.

### Q: What pecuniary jurisdiction did the consumer forums originally have?

As enacted, jurisdiction was measured by the value of the goods or services plus the compensation claimed. The District Forum entertained complaints where that combined figure did not exceed one lakh rupees. The State Commission took complaints exceeding one lakh rupees but not exceeding ten lakh rupees, and the National Commission took complaints exceeding ten lakh rupees. The bands were raised twice: the amendment of 1993 moved them to five lakh and twenty lakh rupees, and the amendment of 2002, in force from 15 March 2003, moved them to twenty lakh rupees for the District Forum, above twenty lakh and up to one crore rupees for the State Commission, and above one crore rupees for the National Commission.

### Q: What deposit was required to appeal a consumer forum order?

In the Act's final form, after the amendment of 2002 took effect on 15 March 2003, an appeal by a person required to pay an amount under an order would not be entertained unless the appellant deposited fifty per cent of that amount or a stated ceiling, whichever was less: twenty-five thousand rupees for an appeal to the State Commission, thirty-five thousand rupees for an appeal to the National Commission, and fifty thousand rupees for an appeal to the Supreme Court. Appeals had to be filed within thirty days, with power to condone delay for sufficient cause. Before that amendment, the Act required no deposit at all for an appeal.

### Q: What did the earlier consumer statute not provide for?

Four things, which are exactly the four things the Consumer Protection Act 2019 added. It provided no regulator with power to investigate a trade practice on its own motion, recall a product or penalise a misleading advertisement across a market; the councils could only promote and advise. It provided no product liability regime allocating responsibility for harm caused by defective products along the supply chain. It provided no statutory mediation as a mode of settling consumer disputes. And it contained no treatment of electronic commerce or distance selling, having been drafted for shops, printed advertisements and goods that could be sealed and sent to a laboratory for testing.

### Q: What happened to consumer cases pending at the repeal?

They continued under the 1986 Act. Section 107 of the Consumer Protection Act 2019 repealed the earlier Act with a savings scheme preserving its previous operation and proceedings under it, and in Neena Aneja v. Jai Prakash Associates Ltd. the Supreme Court held that a complaint instituted under the 1986 Act before the new Act commenced in July 2020 remains governed by the old Act, including its pecuniary limits, and is decided by the corresponding commission as if the repeal had not displaced the law under which it was filed. A large body of cases was therefore decided under the repealed statute for years after the repeal.

### Q: Who could file a complaint under the Consumer Protection Act 1986?

Four classes of complainant had standing. A consumer who bought goods or hired services for consideration; any voluntary consumer association registered under the Companies Act or any other law, whether or not the affected consumer was its member; the Central Government or any State Government; and one or more consumers acting, with the permission of the forum, on behalf of numerous consumers having the same interest. Section 12 prescribed the manner of filing in the same terms. This breadth was deliberate: it let associations aggregate grievances that were small for each buyer and large in total, and let groups such as the allottees of one housing project proceed together instead of filing hundreds of separate complaints.

### Q: What was the limitation period for filing a complaint under the 1986 Act?

Two years from the date on which the cause of action arose. The Act as enacted contained no limitation period; Section 24A, inserted by the amendment of 1993, barred every District Forum, State Commission and National Commission from admitting a complaint filed later than two years after the cause of action. The same section allowed a delayed complaint to be entertained where the complainant satisfied the forum that there was sufficient cause for the delay, with the discipline that the forum record its reasons for condoning it. The period ran from the cause of action, so continuing deficiencies, as in delayed housing possession or a subsisting insurance repudiation, raised distinct questions about when time began.

### Q: What penalty did the 1986 Act impose for disobeying a consumer forum order?

Section 27 made non-compliance an offence. A trader or person against whom a complaint was made, or a complainant, who failed or omitted to comply with an order of a District Forum, State Commission or National Commission was punishable with imprisonment for a term of not less than one month and up to three years, or with a fine of not less than two thousand rupees and up to ten thousand rupees, or with both, subject to a proviso permitting a lesser sentence where the circumstances of the case so required. Alongside the penalty, Section 25 made every order enforceable as if it were a decree of a civil court, and Section 27A, added in 2002, provided an appeal against orders under Section 27.

### Q: Did the 1986 Act replace remedies under other laws or add to them?

It added to them. Section 3 provides that the provisions of the Act shall be in addition to and not in derogation of the provisions of any other law for the time being in force. A buyer could therefore pursue the forum, the civil courts, a sectoral regulator or a criminal remedy for the same transaction, subject to the ordinary bars on double recovery. In Fair Air Engineers Pvt. Ltd. v. N. K. Modi, the Supreme Court held that the remedies under the Act are additional in this sense, and that the existence of an arbitration clause in the parties' contract did not bar a consumer complaint, because the statutory remedy has a public character that a private agreement cannot contract away.

### Q: When did a commercial purpose take a buyer outside the definition of consumer?

A person who obtained goods for resale or for any commercial purpose was excluded from the definition of consumer for goods. An explanation to the clause preserved one case: use of goods bought and used by a person exclusively for earning a livelihood by means of self-employment is not a commercial purpose. In Laxmi Engineering Works v. P. S. G. Industrial Institute, the Supreme Court directed the inquiry to the buyer's purpose and scale: goods bought for a profit generating activity on a large scale, closely connected with the purchase, were bought for a commercial purpose, while goods a buyer worked himself to earn his livelihood were not. The question was one of fact in each case, turning on the closeness of the link between the purchase and large-scale profit activity.

### Q: What is the difference between a defect and a deficiency under the 1986 Act?

A defect concerns goods; a deficiency concerns services. Section 2(1)(f) defines a defect as any fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard of goods required to be maintained by or under law, under an express or implied contract, or as claimed by the trader in any manner. Section 2(1)(g) defines a deficiency as any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature and manner of performance of a service required to be maintained by or under law, or undertaken to be performed under a contract or otherwise. The two definitions are mirror images, and each complaint had to be framed in the correct limb, because the testing procedure of Section 13 applies to defects in goods.

### Q: How did a consumer forum decide whether goods were defective?

By the laboratory procedure in Section 13, where the defect could not be determined without analysis or test. The forum obtained a sample of the goods from the complainant, sealed and authenticated it in the prescribed manner, and referred it to an appropriate laboratory with a direction to analyse or test the goods and report within forty-five days whether they suffered from the defect alleged or any other defect. The forum could require the complainant to deposit the laboratory fee. The report was forwarded to the opposite party, either side could file written objections to the findings or the methods used, and the forum heard both parties on the report and the objections before making its order under Section 14.

### Q: Could a consumer forum award compensation for mental agony and harassment?

Yes. Section 14(1)(d) empowered the forum to award compensation for any loss or injury suffered by the consumer due to the negligence of the opposite party, and in Lucknow Development Authority v. M. K. Gupta the Supreme Court confirmed that the injury compensated under the Act includes the mental agony and harassment inflicted by a service provider's arbitrary or delaying conduct, there a development authority's treatment of its allottee. The commissions applied the head where distress was a proved and foreseeable consequence of the deficiency, as in wrongful repudiation of insurance claims and prolonged withholding of possession, and required it to be established rather than merely recited, relating the amount to the conduct and the suffering proved.

### Q: What did the Consumer Protection Councils do, and could they decide a complaint?

They could not decide complaints. The Central, State and District Consumer Protection Councils, under Chapter II, were promotional and advisory bodies chaired by the minister in charge of consumer affairs at the corresponding level, charged with promoting and protecting the six rights declared in Section 6. Their instruments were meetings, recommendations, awareness campaigns and consumer education. Adjudication belonged entirely to the separate line of agencies in Chapter III, the District Forums, State Commissions and the National Commission. The division is structurally important: everything in the Act that could act on a market rather than on a dispute was confined to advice, because no council could summon a trader, hold an inquiry into a practice, or issue a binding direction.

### Q: Did a complainant need a lawyer before a consumer forum under the 1986 Act?

No. The Act nowhere required representation by a legal practitioner, and a complainant could file a written complaint, appear in person, and conduct the case without counsel, which was one of the design features that made the remedy usable for small claims. Parties could also appear through an authorised representative, and advocates could and did appear, particularly in heavy State Commission and National Commission matters. The distinction the statute draws is between permitting professional representation and requiring it. Procedures were built to be workable by the unrepresented: the complaint was an allegation in writing rather than a plaint, evidence was ordinarily taken on affidavit, and the forum itself directed the testing and reference steps that a litigant in a civil court would have had to arrange.

### Q: What fee did a complainant pay to file a complaint under the 1986 Act?

No court fee proportionate to the claim was ever imposed, and that absence was a central design feature. The Act as enacted contained no ad valorem fee; in the system's developed practice a small fee prescribed by rules, graded mildly by the value of the claim, accompanied a complaint, and the amendment of 2002 wrote the requirement into Section 12 by providing that every complaint be accompanied with such amount of fee, payable in such manner, as may be prescribed. The prescribed amounts remained nominal, in the tens and hundreds of rupees, against civil court fees on the same claim that ran to thousands and rose with the amount claimed. Parliament priced access to the forum as a fixed trivial sum rather than a percentage of the grievance.

### Q: Did the 1986 Act cover services obtained for a commercial purpose?

In its original form, the commercial purpose exclusion in the definition of consumer was expressed for goods: a buyer of goods for resale or commercial purpose was excluded, with the livelihood explanation for the self-employed. Services stood differently at first, because the services limb of the definition carried no matching exclusion. The amendment of 2002 closed the asymmetry by extending the exclusion to services, placing a person who avails of services for any commercial purpose outside the definition, subject to the same explanation for services availed exclusively for earning a livelihood by self-employment. The successor statute retained the exclusion and the explanation in materially identical terms, so the line of authority built on them, beginning with Laxmi Engineering Works, continues to govern the question.
