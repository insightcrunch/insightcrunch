The two most famous family names in English drama were not invented by a playwright, a novelist, or a poet of love. Montecchi and Cappelletti enter literature in a political poem, in a single line of invective, spoken by Dante Alighieri in the sixth canto of the Purgatorio, where the names stand for the ruin of Italian civic life and nothing else. Dante gives the feud of Romeo and Juliet its names roughly two centuries before the tale of the lovers exists, and he gives them without lovers, without Verona as a setting for romance, without a balcony, a friar, a potion, or a tomb. He gives two houses held up to an emperor as evidence: this is what faction does to a city and a country. Everything the names later acquire, the star-crossed love, the secret marriage, the double suicide, is an accretion laid over a foundation that is purely, coldly political.

The verdict of this study can be stated at once, because the evidence for it is short, checkable, and decisive. The names behind Montague and Capulet are not Verona love-lore and not Shakespearean coinage. They are the names of real medieval Italian factions, preserved in the most read Italian poem of the age, detached from their politics by a Vicentine soldier-novelist who needed surnames for a pair of invented lovers, and carried through French and English retellings until they reached a London stage wearing anglicized spellings. Restoring Dante to the head of that chain changes what the feud in the play is. The "ancient grudge" of the Prologue is not a fairy-tale premise. It is the residue of a documented political culture in which great houses consumed themselves and their cities, a culture Dante diagnosed with these very names. The borrowed names contribute weight that the plot alone could never supply: they make the feud feel older than any character, grounded in a real Italy of parties and exiles, and they let the play's silence about the feud's cause ring as historical truth rather than as a gap in the writing.

That claim requires care at every joint, because the Dante connection attracts legend the way the play itself does. Antiquarians turned Dante's pairing into a real Veronese feud between two households, chroniclers and guidebooks turned the feud into a true story, and the true story hardened into a tourist geography. Each stage of that hardening is a misreading, and each can be corrected against the texts. What follows separates the three things that lazy accounts merge: the historical factions, two party names attached to different cities; the literary names, carried by Luigi da Porto into fiction; and the fictional households, Shakespeare's Montague and Capulet, whose enmity the play refuses to explain. The chain that joins them is set out in full in the InsightCrunch names chain below, and the map of the whole descent, from Ovid to the London stage, belongs to [the family tree of the play's sources](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), which places Dante's contribution at the root where it belongs: a contribution of names, not of story.

## Where in the Purgatorio Does Dante Name the Montecchi and Cappelletti?

The names occur once, in Purgatorio 6, at line 106, inside the most famous political outburst in the Comedy. The canto is set on the second terrace of ante-Purgatory, among the late-repentant who died by violence, and its engine is an embrace. Virgil meets Sordello, the Mantuan troubadour, and the two poets, strangers until the name of their shared city is spoken, fall into each other's arms: Mantua is enough. Dante watches two dead men united by nothing but a birthplace and turns on the living Italy that cannot manage as much. The apostrophe that follows, opening "Ahi serva Italia, di dolore ostello" (Purgatorio 6.76), "Ah, slavish Italy, hostel of grief" in Robert Hollander's translation for the Princeton Dante Project, is a sustained indictment of a peninsula without a ruler, of cities at war with themselves, of citizens who sharpen their swords on their neighbors. The names Montecchi and Cappelletti arrive near the outburst's climax, as exhibits summoned before an absent judge.

The passage runs, in the text established by Giorgio Petrocchi's critical edition of the Commedia, as follows: "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti, / Monaldi e Filippeschi, uom sanza cura: / color già tristi, e questi con sospetti!" (Purgatorio 6.106-108). Hollander renders the three lines: "Come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, the Monaldi and the Filippeschi, heedless man: the first already downcast, the others full of dread." The address is imperative and contemptuous. Dante orders the Emperor Albert of Habsburg, who has never crossed the Alps to claim his Italian kingdom, to come and look at the wreckage his neglect licenses. Four houses are named in two pairs. The Monaldi and Filippeschi were the rival factions of Orvieto, Guelph and Ghibelline. The Montecchi and Cappelletti stand first, and the grammar that follows spreads across all four: some are already ruined, the rest live in fear of ruin. No cause is given, no quarrel narrated, no opponent specified for any house. The lines work by accumulation, the way a prosecutor reads a list of the dead.

Placement inside the canto matters as much as wording. Canto 6 of each cantica carries Dante's political teaching: Inferno 6 gives Ciacco's prophecy of Florence's factional bloodshed, Paradiso 6 gives Justinian's history of the Empire, and Purgatorio 6 gives the invective against Italy and the absent emperor. A reader who meets the Montecchi and Cappelletti here is being taught political science, not genealogy for a romance. The pairs of houses are data in an argument about empire and faction: Italy's cities devour themselves because no imperial authority stands over them, and the great families, the natural instruments of order, have become the instruments of disorder instead. Dante returns to the theme wherever the poem touches his own exile, driven from Florence in 1302 by the victorious Black Guelphs, and the bitterness is personal as well as doctrinal. The names are chosen by a man whom faction had made homeless.

### Why does Dante address the Emperor in the lines that name the houses?

The address turns a list of ruined families into an accusation with a defendant. Albert of Habsburg, King of the Romans who never came to Italy, is summoned as the negligent guardian whose absence leaves the peninsula to its factions, and the named houses are entered as evidence against him.

By ordering the "uom sanza cura," the heedless man, to come and see the Montecchi and Cappelletti, Dante frames the ruin of the families as the visible cost of an empty throne, and the naming itself becomes an act of prosecution. The houses are not introduced for their own sake. They are exhibits.

The canto's machinery reinforces the prosecutorial frame. Justinian's eagle, the Roman law, the whole Dantean doctrine of empire as God's remedy for human greed stands behind the three lines, and the paired houses are the doctrine's negative proof. Where the emperor does not come, the Montecchi fall and the Cappelletti tremble. Scholars of the Comedy, among them Prue Shaw in Reading Dante, stress that the invective is constructed as a formal rhetorical performance, an apostrophe in the high style, and that its lists are meant to be heard as representative rather than exhaustive: four houses stand for a hundred. The Montecchi and Cappelletti therefore enter literature already functioning as symbols, examples of a general condition. That symbolic readiness is part of what made the names portable. A name that already stands for faction itself can be lifted, centuries later, onto any feud a storyteller needs.

One more feature of the passage deserves emphasis before the names are followed into history: its brevity. Dante spends three lines on four houses. He does not say where the Montecchi held power, does not name a single member of either family, does not mention Verona at all in connection with them, and does not so much as hint that the two houses had ever encountered one another. The Cappelletti are never mentioned again in the poem. The Montecchi are never mentioned again either. Everything that later readers claim Dante says about a Veronese feud between the two houses is read into a silence, and the first duty of source study is to measure that silence accurately rather than to fill it with the play.

## What Dante Gives the Feud, and What He Refuses to Give It

Set against the later story, the inventory of Dante's contribution is startling in its narrowness, and precision about that narrowness is the whole value of the passage for source study. Dante gives two surnames. He gives their association, in the minds of his first readers, with factional strife and with the condition of houses already falling or afraid to fall. He gives them a pairing, a rhythm, a place in a famous line that every literate Italian of the following centuries knew. He does not give a city shared by both houses. He does not give a quarrel between them. He does not give a single individual, let alone a pair of lovers, a marriage, a death, or a reconciliation over corpses. The Romeo and Juliet story, whose earliest assembled form belongs to the novella tradition of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, receives from Dante exactly what a mason receives from a quarry: dressed stone, cut for another building.

The refusal is as instructive as the gift. Dante had every opportunity to attach narrative to the names if narrative was what the names carried in his culture. The Comedy is crowded with feuds narrated in detail: the Buondelmonte murder that split Florence, told by Cacciaguida in Paradiso 16; the vendettas of the Romagna lords paraded through Inferno 27 and 28; Count Ugolino's treason and starvation in Inferno 32 and 33. When Dante wants a story, he tells it, usually through the mouth of a damned or penitent participant. For the Montecchi and Cappelletti he tells nothing, because in his design there was nothing to tell that the names did not already say. The ruin of the one house and the dread of the other are predicates, not plots. Later centuries mistook the predicates for the summary of a plot, and a plot was duly invented to explain them, in Verona, with lovers attached. The genealogy of that mistake is traced in a later section; the point here is that the mistake begins by ignoring the grammar of line 108.

That grammar deserves a slow reading. "Color già tristi, e questi con sospetti": the former already sorrowful, the latter with suspicions, that is, living in apprehension. The standard commentary tradition, from the fourteenth-century glosses collected in the Dartmouth Dante Project to Charles Singleton's edition and Hollander's notes, distributes the predicates across the pairs: the Montecchi and the Monaldi already brought low, the Cappelletti and the Filippeschi still standing but afraid. The distribution matters less than the tense structure. Dante describes consequences and expectations, never origins. The houses are seen from the far side of their disasters. A reader hunting for the feud's cause in Dante finds the same thing a reader hunting for it in Shakespeare finds: an effect without a stated cause, enmity as a condition rather than an event. The play's famous refusal, its "ancient grudge" with no grievance attached, is often treated as a dramatist's shortcut. In the light of Purgatorio 6 it looks more like an inheritance. The names arrived in the story already trailing unexplained ruin, and the story kept the ruin unexplained.

### Do the Montecchi and Cappelletti ever meet inside Dante's poem?

They do not meet, speak, fight, or share a scene, a city, or a sentence of narrative. The two names occur inside a single line of a catalogue, yoked by meter and rhetorical pairing, and each belongs to a different urban world: Verona for the Montecchi, Cremona for the Cappelletti.

Nothing in the Comedy stages an encounter between the houses or reports one from the past, as the earliest commentators, who assigned the families to their separate cities, well understood. Their conjunction is Dante's rhetoric, not Dante's history: two symptoms named in one breath because one disease, faction, produced both. Any account that describes a Dantean feud between the families has imported the feud from the later novella tradition and projected it backward onto a list.

The pairing itself is the seed of the whole later confusion, and it is worth understanding why Dante pairs them at all. Rhetorical catalogues in the invective proceed by representative accumulation, and pairs give the accumulation speed and balance: Montecchi and Cappelletti balanced against Monaldi and Filippeschi, north against center, one pair sinking, one pair trembling. The pairing is musical and argumentative. But a pair of names in a famous line behaves, in cultural memory, like a pair of opponents. Readers who knew the line by heart, and generations of Italians knew the whole invective by heart, carried the two names together as a unit, the way English speakers carry "Montague and Capulet" as a unit, and the unit demanded a story of opposition to justify its unity. Da Porto eventually supplied the story, setting the pair at war in Verona. The demand was created by Dante's rhetoric; the supply was a novella writer's invention. Between demand and supply lies the entire prehistory of the feud.

## The Montecchi of Verona: A Great House Already Falling

Behind the first name stands a documented Veronese dynasty. The Montecchi took their name from Montecchio Maggiore, the fortified hill town west of Vicenza whose castle commanded the road between Vicenza and Verona, and by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the family ranked among the leading houses of Verona itself, holders of offices, lands, and military followings in the city and its contado. Their century was the century of Verona's agony and greatness: the age when the commune tore itself between parties, when Ezzelino III da Romano ruled through terror as imperial vicar, and when, after Ezzelino's fall in 1259, the Della Scala family climbed from the podestà's office to hereditary lordship. The Montecchi moved through that world as principals. Their power was of the kind Dante's political writing understood best, the power of a casa, a house with clients, towers, armed retainers, and a party.

Party is the operative word. Thirteenth-century Verona's factions aligned, as factions across northern Italy aligned, with the two great supralocal causes: Guelph, the party of the papacy and the Angevin interest, and Ghibelline, the party of the empire. The Montecchi were identified in the Veronese struggles with the Ghibelline cause in the era of Ezzelino, and their fortunes rose and fell with the imperial party's. The Della Scala, who consolidated the signoria after 1277 under Alberto I, built their regime by balancing and then suppressing the great factional houses, and the Montecchi, too large to be clients and too compromised to be partners, were progressively squeezed from the city they had helped to govern. By the opening years of the fourteenth century, when Dante was composing the Purgatorio, the house's Veronese power was broken or breaking: exiles, confiscations, and the long fade of a family whose strongholds passed to others. "Già tristi," already downcast, is not a poet's flourish. It is a status report.

Dante knew Verona at first hand, which sharpens the report. The poet spent part of his exile under Della Scala protection, a guest of Bartolomeo I della Scala in the first years of the century and later, more famously, of Cangrande I, to whom tradition assigns the dedication of the Paradiso. The Verona of the Scaligeri court was a place where the memory of the Montecchi was recent and concrete: older men at that court had served with the house or against it. When Dante writes the Montecchi into the invective as a house already ruined by the factional order of Italy, he writes as an insider of the successor regime, a pensioner of the very signoria that profited from the old houses' fall. The line carries the authority of proximity. It also carries a warning aimed at the Scaligeri themselves, implicit in the pairing with the still-standing Cappelletti: what has happened to the Montecchi is the standing future of every house that lives by faction, patrons included.

The afterlife of the historical Montecchi is brief and dim, which is itself evidence. Unlike the Cappelletti, who persisted as a great Cremonese name, the Montecchi of Verona dwindle in the record during the fourteenth century, their castle and claims absorbed, their name surviving chiefly in documents and in Dante's line. Historiography has therefore always read the family backward from two sources: the archival traces of Veronese politics, and the Comedy. The archival traces establish a Ghibelline-aligned magnate house broken in the Della Scala consolidation. The Comedy establishes the meaning Dante assigned that breaking: not local accident but Italian system. Modern historians of Verona, working from chronicles such as Giovanni Villani's Nuova Cronica and the Veronese annalistic tradition, confirm the outline the commentators sketched: a real house, a real fall, a fall legible to Dante's first audience without a word of explanation. The explanation was unnecessary precisely because the audience knew. Six centuries of readers who do not know have filled the gap with the play.

### Why does Dante give no cause for the houses' ruin?

Because causation, in the invective's argument, sits one level above any house's private grievance. The cause of the Montecchi falling and the Cappelletti fearing is the condition of Italy itself: an emperor absent, cities sovereign and savage, parties armed. A narrated quarrel would shrink the indictment to gossip.

Dante's silence about origins is therefore deliberate and doctrinal. The houses are symptoms, and symptoms are described, not explained. The play inherits the pattern almost exactly: an ancient grudge, vigorously enacted, never sourced. In both works the missing cause is the meaning, the assertion that faction is a state of being before it is a sequence of events, and that states of being do not need a first insult to be real.

## The Cappelletti of Cremona: A Party Name Mistaken for a Veronese House

The second name leads away from Verona entirely, and following it honestly dissolves half the legend. The Cappelletti were a great family of Cremona, the Lombard city on the Po, some hundred and fifty kilometers west of Verona, and in Cremona's factional history the name did double duty: it denoted the lineage and, by extension, the party that gathered under the lineage's leadership. Cremona's medieval politics ran on the rivalry of two organized parties, the Cappelletti and their opponents the Barbarasi, whose struggle for the commune fills the city's chronicles. To name the Cappelletti in Dante's Italy was to name Cremona's dominant faction-formation, a political machine as much as a family. The distinction matters for everything that follows. A party name is portable in a way a household is not: it can be quoted, remembered, and eventually borrowed, precisely because it already functions as a sign rather than as a roster of kin.

Cremona's factions had earned their place in an imperial indictment. The city sat on the great Lombard road, changed hands and allegiances through the wars of Frederick II and his heirs, and suffered the full cycle of factional government: party exiles, party returns, towers thrown down, councils packed. The Cappelletti, aligned in the city's struggles with the imperial, Ghibelline interest in the age of Frederick II and his vicar Oberto Pelavicino, whose Lombard lordship rested heavily on Cremona, rose to lead the commune's dominant party, and their ascendancy was of the kind that makes enemies permanent. When Dante pairs them with the ruined Montecchi under the predicate of dread, "questi con sospetti," the still-standing house afraid of its turn, the Cremonese reading is exact: a party in power, armed and watchful, aware that the wheel that lifted it is still turning. The early commentators, who gloss the names within a generation of Dante's death, assign the Cappelletti to Cremona without hesitation. Jacopo Alighieri, the poet's son, and the Lana and Ottimo commentaries all locate the family there, and the fourteenth-century tradition is unanimous enough that the modern commentary consensus, Singleton and Hollander among it, treats Cremona as settled.

The Veronese Cappelletti, by contrast, do not exist. No Cremonese-style party, no magnate house of that name, appears in Verona's records as a rival to the Montecchi. The notion that the Cappelletti were a Veronese family feuding with the Montecchi in Verona is a late growth, fed by two sources: the novella tradition, which set its lovers in Verona and needed both families resident there, and local antiquarianism, which preferred a hometown feud to a borrowed literary device. Once da Porto placed the Cappelletti in Verona as a household at war with the Montecchi, the placement read backward into Dante, and Dante's line was misquoted ever after as evidence for the fiction it had accidentally enabled. The correction is simple and total: in Dante, the Cappelletti are Cremonese; the conjunction with Verona begins with the novella, around two centuries later.

Understanding the Cappelletti as a party first and a family second also corrects a subtler error in the standard account of the names' journey. Popular summaries say that da Porto borrowed "two feuding families" from Dante. Da Porto borrowed two names that signified faction as such, one the name of a fallen magnate house, the other the name of a ruling party. The blend is a gift to a storyteller: between them, the names cover the whole life cycle of faction, the house that faction has destroyed and the house faction is preserving in fear. When the names become households in fiction, that whole political semantics comes with them, compressed into surnames. The feud in the play feels systemic, larger than any insult exchanged onstage, partly because its names were systemic before they were domestic: one of them had already named a party, a machine for organized enmity, in a real Italian city.

## Guelphs, Ghibellines, and the Names Behind the Names

The historiography of the two houses cannot be written without the two great party words of medieval Italy, and the words themselves explain why Dante's catalogue works. Guelph and Ghibelline began as German battle names, Welf and Waiblingen, carried into Italy during the Hohenstaufen wars of the twelfth century, and hardened over the thirteenth century into the master division of Italian public life: Guelph for the party of the Church, Ghibelline for the party of the Empire. By Dante's lifetime the labels had detached from their origins and become hereditary identities, worn by cities, houses, and parties within cities, structuring everything from council votes to marriage alliances to the cut of a hat plume or the merlons on a tower. Florence split into Black and White Guelphs and expelled Dante in the process. Verona's struggles pitted faction against faction under the same banners, and Cremona's Cappelletti and Barbarasi mapped, imperfectly as local parties always map, onto the great division.

Three cautions govern any honest use of the party labels for the Montecchi and Cappelletti. First, the alignments were real but unstable: houses changed sides across generations, and local hatreds wore imperial or papal colors as convenience dictated. The Montecchi's Ghibelline color in the Ezzelino era and the Cappelletti's imperial alignment in the age of Pelavicino are the alignments the tradition reports, and both families' histories include the shifts and splits that make party labels treacherous as genealogy. Second, Dante's own relation to the labels was one of disgust. The invective of Purgatorio 6 is not a Ghibelline pamphlet cheering one party against another; it is an imperial argument against party itself, and the named houses are exhibits of a disease that infects both colors. Third, and most consequential for the play, the labels explain the emotional physics of the borrowed names. A surname that has been a party badge is not a neutral label for a household. It is a flag. Servants fight under it, strangers take sides by it, marriages are blocked by it, exactly as the play's Verona behaves in its opening scene, where the quarrel ignites between serving-men trading insults under their masters' names before any principal appears: "Gregory, on my word, we'll not carry coals" (1.1.1). The party logic survived the journey into fiction intact, even though the party words did not.

The chroniclers supply the connective tissue between Dante's line and the documented world, and their testimony cuts both ways. Giovanni Villani's Nuova Cronica, the great Florentine history completed in the generation after Dante, records the factional careers of northern houses as matters of public record, and the annalistic traditions of Verona and Cremona preserve the local detail: offices held, exiles imposed, towers razed. Read against the chronicles, Dante's three lines are a headline over a documented file. Read without them, as the line was read by the later literary tradition, the same three lines look like the summary of a story that must exist somewhere. The historiographical lesson generalizes beyond this article's subject: a famous catalogue in a famous poem will generate pseudo-history wherever its references outlive common knowledge of its referents. The Montecchi and Cappelletti are a textbook case, two real names whose real histories were gradually replaced by a fictional history that explained their conjunction better.

### Why are the Monaldi and Filippeschi named in the same breath as the Veronese and Cremonese houses?

The second pair proves that the catalogue is an argument rather than a feud report. Monaldi and Filippeschi were the Guelph and Ghibelline houses of Orvieto, locked in that city's own struggle, with no connection to Verona or Cremona at all. Dante names four houses from three cities because the subject is Italy's condition.

The ruin pattern repeats from place to place, interchangeable in its personnel. The Orvietan pair also fixes the grammatical reading of the passage. Predicates of ruin and dread spread across all four names alike, which shows the lines describe a general epidemiology of faction, not a bilateral war between the first two names. The pairing of Montecchi with Cappelletti is a pairing of examples, like the pairing of the Orvietans, and nothing in the rhetoric makes the first pair opponents while the second pair are merely neighbors in a list.

## How the Commentators Turned a Pairing into a Feud

The mistake that built the legend can be dated and watched in the commentary tradition, and watching it is the best inoculation against it. The earliest glosses on Purgatorio 6, written within decades of Dante's death in 1321 by men who shared his political world, identify the houses soberly: Montecchi of Verona, Cappelletti of Cremona, factions noted for their falls and fears. Jacopo della Lana's commentary, among the earliest complete readings of the poem, treats the names as political references requiring geographical identification, not narrative. The fourteenth-century commentators lived amid functioning parties and had no need to invent a story to explain why a poet would name two factional houses in one line. The conjunction explained itself to them, because conjunctions of faction names were the daily language of Italian politics.

The slippage begins when that daily language dies. As the party system of the communes faded into the signorie and then into the regional states of the Renaissance, readers of Dante lost the instinct that two faction names in one line needed no story between them. Antiquarian scholarship, proud and local, began supplying the story. Verona, which by then possessed the novella's lovers as a civic treasure, annexed the Cappelletti: if the play's feud was Veronese and Dante named both houses, then Dante must record a Veronese feud, and the Cappelletti must have been Veronese. The argument is circular, and its circularity went unnoticed because each half flattered the other: the poem dignified the legend, the legend localized the poem. By the time of the great Veronese historiography of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the supposed feud had acquired the patina of record, and later popular accounts, guidebooks, and prefaces to the play repeated the feud between Montecchi and Cappelletti as Dante's testimony. Modern Dante scholarship rejects the construction on the Cremona evidence, but the construction survives wherever the play's sources are summarized carelessly.

The deeper mechanism deserves naming, because it operates across the whole Romeo and Juliet tradition. Readers mistook a genealogy for a plot. Dante hands down names with relationships of juxtaposition: two houses in one line, grammatically parallel, rhetorically paired. Genealogy in that compressed form looks like narrative waiting to be unpacked, and the literary tradition obligingly unpacked it: first into a feud, which explains juxtaposition as opposition, then into lovers, who give the opposition a human cost, then into a true story, which gives the lovers an alibi in fact. Every stage inverted the actual order of invention. The plot came last, the genealogy first. Keeping the order straight is the difference between source study and mythology, and it is why the Dante passage, three lines long, supports an article of this length: those three lines are the point where the whole tradition's characteristic error can be caught in the act.

## The InsightCrunch Names Chain: From Dante's Factions to da Porto's Families to Shakespeare's Houses

The descent of the two names crosses three languages, four countries of the mind, and nearly three centuries, and at every stage the names carry less politics and more story than they carried at the stage before. The chain is set out stage by stage in the table below, the InsightCrunch names chain, which this study treats as its central exhibit: each row names a bearer of the names, the form the names take, and the transformation the bearer performs on them. Read down the rows and the pattern is unmistakable. Nothing is added to the names except fiction; everything subtracted from them is history.

| 1 | Dante Alighieri, Purgatorio 6, composed in the years around 1310 | Montecchi and Cappelletti | Two real faction names, Verona and Cremona, paired in an imperial invective as exhibits of Italy's civic ruin; no lovers, no shared city, no feud between the houses |
| 2 | Luigi da Porto, Giulietta e Romeo, written in the years around 1520 and published posthumously in the early 1530s | Montecchi and Cappelletti | The names become two households of one city, Verona, at feud; a Romeo Montecchi and a Giulietta Cappelletti are attached to them as lovers; the feud gains a plot and loses its politics |

The chain's pivotal row is the second. Luigi da Porto of Vicenza, a soldier of the Venetian wars left crippled by a wound received in 1511, composed the novella of Romeo and Giulietta in retirement and presented the tale as one heard from an archer in his company on the march. Whatever the frame's truth, the naming decision on the page is his: the lovers receive the surnames Montecchi and Cappelletti, and the houses receive Verona as their common city and a standing enmity as their common condition. Da Porto knew Dante's poem as every educated Italian knew it, and the borrowing has the marks of deliberation. He needed, for a Veronese tale of divided houses, names already heavy with faction; Dante's line offered the two heaviest names in the language, one of them genuinely Veronese. The Cappelletti's Cremonese identity was the price of the pairing, and da Porto paid it without comment, moving the party name into Verona as a household. The full account of that appropriation, the archer Peregrino, the Scaliger frame, and the novella's own inventions belongs to [the study of Luigi da Porto and the birth of Giulietta](/2025/10/03/luigi-da-porto-giulietta-origin/); here the act is registered at chain length, as the moment genealogy becomes plot.

From da Porto onward the names travel inside the story's luggage and change only by erosion. Bandello, retelling the tale in 1554 with expansions of dialogue and scene, keeps the Italian forms. Boaistuau's French version of 1559, the bridge to England, bends the spellings toward French mouths. Brooke's English poem of 1562 and Painter's prose of 1567 complete the anglicization, and Shakespeare's stage fixes the result: Montague, with its faint suggestion of a sharp mountain in Norman French, and Capulet, diminished by its ending into something almost like a little cap. The erosions are not innocent. Each respelling launders the names further from the factional Italy that minted them, until an English audience could hear Montague and Capulet as pure inventions, pretty labels for a pretty tragedy. Source study reverses the laundering. Set the seven rows side by side, as the table sets them, and the names recover their weight: three centuries of readers have been watching a Ghibelline-era casualty list mistaken for a dramatis personae. For teaching and seminar work the chain repays being laid out visually, row by row, and readers assembling the stages into a presentation can open and arrange the deck in a browser with the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), checking each row against the texts it names.

One discipline must be kept while the chain is admired: it is a chain of names, and names are not the story. The plot of the lovers descends through a different line, assembled from Masuccio's Sienese tale of Mariotto and Ganozza and the novella tradition that da Porto himself drew on, and conflating the two descents produces the standard error of the popular accounts, in which Dante is said to have originated the Romeo and Juliet legend. Dante originated its surnames. The distinction sounds pedantic and is in fact the whole subject: a story can be invented in the sixteenth century and still wear thirteenth-century names, and the wearing changes the story's meaning, because audiences who knew the names, as Italian audiences did, heard the feud with historical ears.

## What the Critics Settle, and Where They Genuinely Disagree

The brief for this study names three authorities, and the three map neatly onto the chain's three questions: where the names came from, what Dante meant by them, and what the play does with them. Geoffrey Bullough, whose Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) remains the standard collection and settlement of the play's source questions, gives the answer on origin that subsequent scholarship has not displaced: the family names descend from Dante through da Porto, the novella writer being the first to attach them to the lovers, and Shakespeare receiving them, already anglicized in tendency, through Brooke and Painter. Bullough prints the relevant texts, including the Dante passage in his discussion of the tale's ancestry, and treats the naming as a solved problem of transmission. On the Dantean side, Prue Shaw's Reading Dante stands here for the modern scholarly reading of the invective: the names function as rhetorical exhibits in an imperial argument, chosen for recognizability, and their pairing is a figure of speech before it is anything else. On the play's side, René Weis, editor of the Arden Third Series Romeo and Juliet (2012), traces the names in his introduction through the same chain and notes what the play preserves of their charge: an enmity presented as immemorial, civic, and causeless, which his notes on the Prologue connect to the households' matched dignity and matched guilt.

The genuine disagreement lies one level deeper, over the status of the legend behind the names, and it has a distinguished pedigree. Olin H. Moore, in a series of studies in the 1930s culminating in The Origins of the Legend of Romeo and Juliet, argued that the tale was no novella writer's invention but a piece of older folk tradition, possibly rooted in real Veronese events, which da Porto merely recorded and regularized. On Moore's account the names in Dante could be read as traces of that older complex: if a Veronese story of feuding houses circulated before Dante, the poet's line might glance at it, and da Porto's naming would be restoration rather than borrowing. The hypothesis had the romance of all lost-source theories and the weakness of all of them: no text, chronicle, or document earlier than the novella tradition has ever been produced that joins the two names to lovers, to a feud between the houses, or to Verona as their shared city. Bullough's settlement answers Moore by accumulation of the documentary record: every datable witness puts Dante first, da Porto second, the feud and the lovers later still.

The adjudication this study reaches follows Bullough, with Shaw's reading supplying the reason Moore's theory was always unnecessary. A rhetorical pairing in a famous invective is itself a sufficient cause for the names' later conjunction: the line taught Italy to say the two names together, and a storyteller needing paired houses for a Veronese feud had the pair ready-made in the most memorized political poem in the language. No lost Veronese saga is required to explain what the Comedy's own circulation explains. The verdict extends to the occasional modern revival of the true-story claim: the burden of proof sits on anyone who would move the feud earlier than da Porto, and the evidence offered, Dante's three lines, does not mention a feud, a city shared by the houses, or a single lover. What Dante's lines do prove is more interesting than the legend they are asked to prove. They prove that the names were already, around 1310, the readiest shorthand in Italian for houses consumed by faction. Da Porto did not borrow two surnames. He borrowed a diagnosis.

Agreement among the three authorities on the central transmission should not hide a second, quieter divergence, over how much of Dante survives inside the play itself. Bullough's concern is documents, and his method stops at delivery: the names arrive, their work in the source chain done. Weis, editing the play, presses further into effect, treating the Prologue's "ancient grudge" and the Prince's closing judgment as structures that behave the way Dante's invective behaves, distributing ruin and dread across matched houses. Shaw's Dante offers the standard by which to judge the pressing: in the poem, the houses are exhibits in an argument about political order, and any survival in the play would have to be argumentative, not verbal, since not a syllable of Dante's wording crosses into English. This study sides with Weis, carefully. The survival is structural. Shakespeare's feud, alone among the novella tradition's versions, is stripped of every originating incident his sources might have supplied and presented as pure condition, two houses "both alike in dignity" (Prologue 1), locked in a grudge whose antiquity is its only explanation: "From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue 3-4). That is the invective's grammar translated into drama. The play even closes in Dante's distribution of predicates: the houses ruined together, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3.291-292), and the Prince's summary sentence, "All are punished" (5.3.295), which is "color già tristi" expanded into a scene.

## What the Borrowed Names Do Inside the Play

The contribution of the names to the feud's resonance can now be stated with the precision the chain makes possible, and it operates on three levels at once. At the level of the first audience, the names did quiet work in the 1590s: Montague and Capulet sounded Italian, aristocratic, and old, and old was the operative quality. A feud needs ancestors, and invented names have none; borrowed names smuggle ancestors in. The Prologue exploits the smuggling at once, presenting the households before any character, as institutions with a past the audience is presumed to respect: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene" (Prologue 1-2). The dignity is Dante's contribution in embryo. His houses were great houses, powers in their cities; the play's houses enter with greatness as a premise, and the tragedy measures itself by the fall of the great.

At the level of structure, the names enforce the play's studied symmetry. Montague and Capulet are a pair in the mouth, near-equal in length and weight, impossible to say in one order as naturally as in Dante's order's descendant, and the drama built on them is a drama of mirrors: matched households, matched servants, matched griefs, a son and a daughter, two corpses, two statues of gold. Dante's rhetorical pairing, two names balanced in a line so that ruin and dread can be distributed across them, becomes in the play an architecture. Readers who feel that the feud is the play's true protagonist, a single two-headed organism destroying itself, are responding to a feeling the names were designed, in another poem, to produce. The full anatomy of that organism, its brawls, edicts, and escalations, is mapped in [the explainer of the feud between the Capulets and Montagues](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/), and the social machinery beneath it, the matched rank that makes the feud a war of equals, is dissected in [the study of the two households and class power in Verona](/2022/04/22/two-households-class-power-verona/). The Dantean point is prior to both: the pairing precedes the play, and the play thinks in the pairing because the names arrived already paired.

At the level of meaning, the names do the work the brief for this study assigns as its verdict question, and the verdict is affirmative with a qualification. The names contribute the feud's resonance of reality: not the reality of a true story, which is legend, but the reality of a true condition, which is history. When Juliet discovers the name she has fallen for, "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (1.5.136-137), the horror of the line depends on names being destinies, labels that precede persons and overrule them. That is exactly how party names worked in Dante's Italy. A Montecchi child was born into an enmity he did not choose, against opponents he might never meet, in a quarrel whose origin no one alive could state. Shakespeare cannot have known the Cappelletti's Cremona or the Montecchi's fall; the chain had laundered the knowledge away. But the thing the names had meant survived their laundering, carried in the story's structure like a recessive trait: enmity as inheritance, causeless because older than memory, curable only by the deaths of the heirs. The play's Verona is Dante's Italy with the politics forgotten and the condition intact. Restoring the names' origin does not diminish the romance. It explains why the romance had to be set against that feud and no other: only names born as faction badges could make a surname itself the tragic obstacle, so that the lovers die, in the end, of a genealogy.

## Dante the Exile: Why the Montecchi's Fall Was Personal Knowledge

Dante did not compile the invective's catalogue from books. The politics of Purgatorio 6 is the politics of a man whose life had been dismantled by the system the canto condemns, and the two family names carry the pressure of that biography. Dante Alighieri, Florentine prior in 1300, was condemned in 1302 by the Black Guelph regime that Charles of Valois's entry into Florence installed, and he spent the remaining nineteen years of his life as an exile, dependent on the courts of Italy's lords. The Comedy is, among many other things, the exile's audit of the peninsula that produced him: city by city, house by house, Dante settles accounts with the factional order. Purgatorio 6 sits near the audit's center, and its named houses are entries in a ledger the poet had personal reasons to keep accurately.

The Verona entries in that ledger were written from inside the successor state. Dante's first refuge after the final condemnations was Verona itself, where Bartolomeo I della Scala received him in the first years of the century, a hospitality the poet repaid with the gratitude recorded in the Convivio's praise of the first Scaliger lord he knew. He returned to Verona in later years under Cangrande I, the most brilliant of the Della Scala, soldier, patron, and the hoped-for imperial vicar of Dante's political dreams. The Verona of those sojourns was a city whose regime defined itself against the factional past: the Della Scala signoria presented its rule as the cure for the age of parties, and the Montecchi were the age of parties' most conspicuous local casualty. To name the Montecchi at that court was to name the defeated alternative, the great house whose fall the regime's legitimacy quietly presupposed. Dante's line performs a delicate double service. It mourns the house as Italy's symptom while confirming, before the house's supplanters, that the fall was faction's work and not the Scaligeri's crime. The exile knew the value of such formulations; his survival depended on patrons whose power had factional biographies of their own.

Cacciaguida, Dante's crusader ancestor, prophesies the exile in Paradiso 17 and frames its cause as the corruption of Florence by party, and the same canto blesses the refuge Verona provided: the great Lombard, Bartolomeo, is named as the exile's first shelter. Reading Purgatorio 6 beside Paradiso 17 shows the invective's catalogue for what it is, one panel of a triptych in which personal exile, civic pathology, and imperial doctrine are the same subject at three scales. The Montecchi mattered to Dante because the Montecchi were what Florence's factions had made of Dante: a name detached from power, surviving as memory. The poet who wrote "color già tristi" about the Veronese house was describing the condition he expected for his own. That identification explains the passage's strange tenderness inside its contempt. The invective scourges Italy through the houses, but the houses themselves are pitied, exhibits and victims at once, and the pity is an exile's recognition of kinship across the party divide that had ruined them all.

There is a further biographical irony that source study should register, because it completes the names' Dantean meaning. Dante died at Ravenna in 1321, still an exile, and Florence spent centuries trying to reclaim his bones. The Montecchi likewise survived chiefly as a name in a poem. When da Porto lifted the names two centuries later, he was lifting them from the work of a writer whose own house had been extinguished by faction in his lifetime and whose fame was posthumous. The names arrived in the novella already doubled in pathos: they signified fallen houses, and they came from a fallen house's poet. None of that survives consciously into the play, but the novella's instinct for the names, the sense that Montecchi and Cappelletti were the right surnames for a tragedy of inherited enmity, was an instinct trained by the Comedy's pathos as much as by its politics.

## Montecchio and Cappelletti: What the Surnames Themselves Carry

Surnames in Dante's Italy were recent, unstable, and legible, and the two names in the line repay the kind of attention a contemporary audience gave them automatically. Montecchi is a toponymic plural, the family of Montecchio, one of several fortified places of that name in the Veneto and Emilia, the relevant one being Montecchio Maggiore in the Vicentine hills, where the family's castle anchored its power between Vicenza and Verona. A toponymic surname is a claim staked in geography: the house is its stronghold, and the name promises that wherever the house's members go, the castle goes with them in reputation. The pathos of the Montecchi's fall is written into the form of their name. A family named for a place it no longer commands is a contradiction walking, and Dante's audience, fluent in such names, heard the contradiction without assistance. The play's Montague keeps a ghost of the toponym: English ears hear a mount in it, a height, fitting for a house whose dignity the Prologue insists on, though the English form owes more to French spelling habits than to the Vicentine hill.

Cappelletti resists a single derivation, and the resistance is informative. The name is a diminutive plural built on cappello, hat, or cappella, chapel, and Italian onomastics offers both a nickname origin, an ancestor marked by headgear or by a chapel office, and a party-badge origin, the little hats by which a faction knew its own. What is certain is the name's collective feel: Cappelletti sounds like a set, a company, a party, and in Cremona that is what the word functionally was, the label under which a coalition of families and clients acted. Between the two surnames, Dante's line thus contains the two basic forms of factional identity in his Italy: the house rooted in a place, and the party gathered under a badge. The novella tradition flatten both into households, and the flattening has a cost that criticism rarely counts. Shakespeare's Capulets are a family, with a count's kinship to the Prince and a marriageable daughter; Dante's Cappelletti were closer to a political party wearing a family's name, and the feud they belonged to was a contest for a commune, not a quarrel between neighbors. The play's feud, domestic in scale, civic in effect, sits exactly at the midpoint of that transformation, which is why its violence keeps escaping the household and flooding the streets: the party keeps breaking out of the family.

A comparison sharpens the point. The other pair in Dante's line shows the same two forms in Orvieto, and across the Comedy the great factional names, Donati and Cerchi in Florence, Colonna and Orsini in Rome, behave as parties wearing genealogies. Dante's political imagination treats the casa and the parte as phases of one substance: a house grows until it is a party, a party decays until it is only a house, and both phases end in the invective's predicates, ruin or dread. The names chain's first row therefore records more than two families. It records two specimens of the substance that the whole tradition, novella and play alike, would spend three centuries converting into a private quarrel. The conversion is the story of Italian literature's long retreat from public to private tragedy, and the surnames are its fossil record.

## Cremona's Parties in the Chronicles: The Cappelletti and the Barbarasi

The Cappelletti's own city supplies the control case for everything claimed about the names, because Cremona's factional history is documented with unusual fullness and because none of it involves Verona, lovers, or the Montecchi. Cremona in the thirteenth century was a prize of the Lombard plain, rich, fortified, and positioned on the routes between Milan, the Po crossings, and the imperial roads south. Its commune lived the full factional cycle. The party of the Cappelletti, gathering the city's imperial-leaning families and their clients, contended with the Barbarasi, a party whose name likewise began as a family's and became a coalition's. Control of the podestà's office, of the councils, of the city's towers and gates, passed between the coalitions with the century's larger tides: the ascendancy of Frederick II, the Lombard lordship of his vicar Oberto Pelavicino, who made Cremona a seat of his power after 1249, the Angevin and papal reactions after the Hohenstaufen fall, and the slow conversion of party government into signoria that reached Cremona, as it reached Verona, in the early fourteenth century.

Within that cycle the Cappelletti's trajectory matches Dante's predicate with documentary precision. A party that had ridden the imperial ascendancy faced, after the imperial cause in Lombardy collapsed, the long insecurity of a faction whose protector was gone and whose local enemies were patient. Exiles and returns mark the record; so does the watchfulness of a party in possession that expects dispossession. "Questi con sospetti," these with suspicions, living in dread, is the exact emotional register of a Lombard party between protectors, and it comes from a poet who had watched Florentine parties pass through the same register twice in a decade. Modern historians of Cremona, working the chronicle tradition that runs from the city's medieval annals through its early modern historians, confirm the outline and add the institutional texture the poem omits: the party as a sworn association, with its own meetings, its own strongpoints, its own marriage policy. That texture matters for the names chain because it defines what da Porto actually borrowed when he borrowed the name. He borrowed the label of a sworn association built for enmity, a machine whose members were bound to a quarrel most of them had not chosen. Stripped of Cremona, of the Empire, of the Barbarasi, the label still carried the machine's logic into the fiction, where it reappears as the Capulet household's readiness to fight on sight, a readiness the play presents, accurately to the name's original world, as reflex rather than decision.

The chronicle evidence also settles, by silence, the question that antiquarianism answered by invention. Cremona's rich record of its parties names the Barbarasi as the Cappelletti's opponents across generations of documentation. No Cremonese source opposes the Cappelletti to the Montecchi; no Veronese source opposes the Montecchi to the Cappelletti. Two cities, two archives, two complete factional histories, and the supposed feud between the houses appears in neither. The feud exists in exactly two places: in Dante's line, as a conjunction misread, and in the novella tradition, as an invention downstream of the misreading's possibility. Source study rarely gets a negative this clean, and the cleanliness is the argument. Where records are dense and the event is absent, the event did not happen.

## From Commentary to Guidebook: How the Feud Legend Was Built

Between Dante's commentators, who knew the Cappelletti were Cremonese, and the modern guidebook tradition, which plants the feud in Verona, stands a sequence of identifiable builders, and naming the stages is the surest way to dismantle the structure. The first builder is the novella itself: da Porto's decision to house both families in Verona under Bartolomeo della Scala converted a rhetorical pair into neighbors, and every later confusion flows from that conversion's narrative convenience. The second builder is historiography in the novella's service. Girolamo dalla Corte, compiling his Istoria di Verona in the later sixteenth century, admitted the lovers' tale into the city's history as fact, assigning it to the Scaliger past with a chronicler's confidence, and his authority gave the story a civic passport: what a historian of Verona prints as Veronese history, Verona thereafter owns. The third builder is the antiquarian commentary on Dante, which, confronted with a famous local legend and a famous line naming both houses, resolved the tension in the legend's favor and began glossing the Cappelletti as Veronese, manufacturing the Dantean feud that the fourteenth-century glossators had never heard of.

The fourth builder is the tourism of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, which required the feud to have addresses. A city selling a true story needs houses to point at, and Verona duly produced them: a Cappelletti house identified by tradition and wish, a courtyard, a balcony assembled from a sarcophagus's lines, a tomb. The present study touches that material only to mark its place in the chain of misreading, since Verona as a constructed literary place has its own study later in this series; the names' part in the construction is the relevant thread here. The Cappelletti's borrowed Veronese address is the feud legend built in stone: a Cremonese party, naturalized as a Veronese household, given a house, and the house then cited as proof of the household. Each builder added the layer the next one needed, and the completed structure has the self-sealing quality of all successful legends, in which every element verifies every other and only the documentary record, Dante's commentators and Cremona's chronicles, stands outside the seal.

Against that seal, the scholarly counter-tradition has a clear line of its own. The great age of Dante commentary in the nineteenth century, and the critical editions that followed, restored the Cremona identification to the gloss on Purgatorio 6.106 where the earliest commentators had put it, and source scholars of the play, Bullough preeminently, rebuilt the names' descent on that restoration. The result is a rare case in literary history where the correct account is also the more interesting one. The legend asks readers to believe that a real feud in one city generated a famous story. The record shows something stranger and better: a line of poetry generated a feud, a story, a civic mythology, and a tourist economy, in that order, across six centuries, and the line's author intended none of it. The Montecchi and Cappelletti are the most consequential three-word accident in the prehistory of English drama, and the accident was a comma's worth of rhetoric: two names, one breath, in an invective about something else entirely.

## Montague and Capulet: The English Afterlife of Two Italian Names

The last transformation in the chain is phonetic, and it completes the laundering that the earlier stages began. Italian Montecchi, pronounced with its hard c and its doubled consonant's weight, becomes in French mouths and English spelling a name that an English audience could mistake for native stock: Montague, indistinguishable in sound and spelling from the great English family of that name, the Montagus who had been earls and soldiers in England for centuries. The accident of resemblance naturalized the foreign house overnight. A London audience of the middle 1590s heard "Montague" not as an exotic import but as a name with English resonance, old, noble, faintly Norman, and heard "Capulet" as its lighter counterweight, a name with a diminutive's spring in its step. The tragedy's naming thus performs in miniature the play's whole relation to Italy: the material is Italian, the finish is English, and the finish conceals the join.

Editors have long noticed the consequences in the play's text itself. The early quartos waver in the forms of the names, and the dramatis personae traditions settle into the spellings the stage still uses, while the name Capulet occasionally draws the older Italianate form in speech prefixes and stage directions, traces of the chain fossilized in the printing house. Weis's Arden edition records the variants in the textual notes, and they are worth a reader's pause, because they show the anglicization still in progress at the moment of the play's first printing: the names had not finished becoming English when the compositors set them. A name in transit is a name with its history still audible, and the quartos preserve the transit the way a riverbed preserves a current.

The afterlife since the stage fixed the spellings runs in the direction Dante would have found grimly familiar: the names have become a party badge again. Montague and Capulet now function in English as the generic labels for any paired antagonists, invoked for feuding families, rival firms, opposed political camps, and divided households of every kind, usually by speakers who could not say where the names came from and assume Shakespeare coined them for the purpose. The wheel has come full circle. Names that began as the badges of real factions, were borrowed as the labels of fictional households, and were laundered by translation into pretty inventions, end as the language's standard shorthand for faction itself. Dante used the Montecchi and Cappelletti as shorthand for exactly that, in exactly that spirit, in 1310 or thereabouts. The shorthand outlasted every one of its referents because the condition it names has never gone out of production. Source study's task, performed by the names chain and by this article, is simply to restore the shorthand's footnotes: Verona and Cremona, empire and party, a fallen house and a frightened one, and a poet in exile who saw, in two names, the whole disease of his country and his age.

## Reading the Three Lines Closely: A Philological Pass

The names sit inside a longer sequence of commands, and the sequence is the unit of meaning. From line 106 to the canto's end at line 151, Dante addresses Albert in a barrage of imperatives built on a single verb: vieni a veder, come and see. Come and see the Montecchi and Cappelletti, the Monaldi and Filippeschi. Come and see the Santafiore country pressed under violence. Come and see the neglected garden of the empire, the widowed Rome, the river Arno's corrupted people, the whole peninsula arranged as a gallery of exhibits for an absentee landlord. The rhetoric is juridical and visual at once: the emperor is summoned not to hear an argument but to inspect evidence, as a judge inspects a damaged estate. Within that gallery, the four houses form the first exhibit, and their position at the head of the inspection is a ranking. Before landscapes, before Rome herself, come the families: the households are where Italy's ruin is most legible, because the household was Italy's basic political cell, and its corruption was the disease's first symptom.

The grammar of the three lines has been parsed for six centuries, and the parsing teaches the same lesson at every scale. "Uom sanza cura," heedless man, stands in apposition to the unspoken subject of the imperative, and its placement, breaking the list of names in two, makes the emperor's negligence the hinge on which the whole catalogue turns: the names before the address belong to the past's ruin, ruin and dread divided between them, and the address explains both. "Color già tristi, e questi con sospetti" distributes its two predicates with an economy that commentators from the fourteenth century to Singleton and Hollander have unpacked the same way: the first-named pair of each couplet already sunk, the second still afloat and afraid. The distribution gives the catalogue a temporal arc inside three lines, a before and an after of faction, the Montecchi showing the Cappelletti their future as surely as the Monaldi show the Filippeschi theirs. A storyteller reading the passage with a dramatist's ear finds a complete tragic structure in miniature: two matched houses, one falling, one trembling, an absent ruler, and a chorus of one drawing the moral. Da Porto needed only to set the miniature in Verona and give the trembling house a daughter.

Translation choices in the passage carry interpretive weight that English readers should register, because the feud legend feeds on loose renderings. "Tristi" is sadder and lower than "ruined": it denotes the downcast condition of the disgraced, grief as a social state. "Con sospetti" is with suspicions, in a state of wary fear, not yet fallen. Hollander's "already downcast" and "full of dread" catch the register well. What no reputable translation produces is any word for enmity between the pairs: no hatred, no war, no feud noun or verb appears in the three lines or their immediate context. Translators from Cary in 1814 through Longfellow, Singleton in prose, and the modern verse versions agree on the absence, because the Italian gives them nothing to translate. The feud enters the passage only in paraphrases written by people summarizing the play backward into the poem, a small, checkable fact that dissolves a surprising amount of published commentary. The philological verdict stands with the transmission verdict reached through Bullough: Dante attests the names, their pairing, and their predicates of ruin and dread. Everything else is later, and later means invented.

One refinement of the reading deserves record, because it shows the commentary tradition working at its proper task. Commentators have debated the exact distribution of the predicates in line 108: whether "già tristi" belongs to the first name of each pair and "con sospetti" to the second, so that Montecchi and Monaldi are the fallen and Cappelletti and Filippeschi the fearful, or whether the sorrow and the suspicion spread across all four houses as a common condition described from two angles. Singleton's notes lay out the evidence for the distributive reading, which the early glosses support and which this study adopts: the parallel structure of the two couplets, each pairing a ruined house with a trembling one, is too exact to be accident, and it gives the catalogue its miniature tragic arc, a before and an after of faction in a single breath. Hollander's rendering preserves the distribution without forcing it. The debate matters beyond grammar. On the distributive reading, Dante is not merely listing sufferers; he is staging a sequence, showing one house its future in another's present, and the staging is precisely the effect the borrowed names later produce in the play, where Capulet and Montague are shown each other's grief in the final scene and recognize it too late. Philology, pursued far enough, arrives at the same place as source study: the line contains a structure, the structure is portable, and the play is where the structure went.

## The Names in the Comedy's Wider Politics: Buondelmonte, Marco Lombardo, and the Two Suns

Purgatorio 6 is one panel of the Comedy's sustained argument about faction, and placing the names among their companion passages shows how deliberately Dante chose them. The poem's most famous feud narrative is the Buondelmonte story, told retrospectively by Cacciaguida in Paradiso 16: the young Florentine who broke a marriage contract with the Amidei to wed a Donati, and was murdered at the foot of Mars's statue in 1216, an act Florentine tradition blamed for the birth of the city's parties. Cacciaguida wishes the line of Buondelmonte had ended childless at the Ema rather than bringing its blood into Florence, and the wish defines Dante's feud theory in one image: feuds begin in marriage politics, in the household's alliances, and metastasize into the city's constitution. Set beside Buondelmonte, the Montecchi and Cappelletti change valence. Buondelmonte is a feud with a narrated origin, a wedding and a murder; the pairs of Purgatorio 6 are feuds whose origins Dante refuses to narrate. The contrast is the doctrine. Origins are accidents, weddings and insults and broken contracts; the condition is structural, and the structural exhibits are the ones given without stories. Shakespeare's play, which keeps the marriage politics of Buondelmonte and the originlessness of Purgatorio 6 in a single action, unites what Dante kept in separate cantos: a wedding at the center, and around it an enmity no one can source.

Marco Lombardo's discourse in Purgatorio 16 supplies the invective's missing premise and should be read as its commentary. Asked why the world has gone wrong, Marco rejects the astrological answer, the heavens made us do it, and assigns the ruin to failed government: Rome once had two suns, pope and emperor, lighting the road of the world and the road of God, and the two have quenched each other. The paired houses of canto 6 are the two-suns doctrine at street level. Where no sun shines, houses become each other's weather. The doctrine also disciplines the play's most famous phrase from an unexpected direction. The Prologue calls the lovers star-crossed, ascribing the catastrophe to the heavens; Marco's canto is the Comedy's formal refutation of that ascription, its insistence that ruin flows from misgovernment and willed corruption, not from stars. A reader who brings Dante's politics to the play finds the Prologue's astrology indicted in advance by the very poem that supplied the feud's names: the names arrive with an anti-fatalist argument attached, and the argument survives, as the feud's causelessness, in the play's structure even where its language of stars survives in the speeches.

The wider politics also supplies the names' final Dantean context, the roll of Italy's lords and houses that the Comedy keeps revising from canto to canto: the Della Scala praised and warned, the Malaspina honored for hospitality to the exile, Manfred of Sicily remembered in Purgatorio 3 as a victim of party hatred pursued beyond the grave by a bishop's spite. Manfred's episode is the invective's emotional twin. The excommunicate king, twice wounded in the battle that killed him, tells the pilgrim how his bones were dug up and scattered by order of the Church his party defied, and the episode proves that in Dante's Italy enmity did not end at death, reconciliation did not follow corpses, and the feud consumed even burial. The play's ending, in which the houses reconcile over the corpses and promise golden statues, is from a Dantean standpoint the fantasy element, the wish the real Italy of the names never granted. Da Porto and his successors softened the names' world by giving it a reconciled ending. Dante's world, the world the names were minted in, ended feuds by exhaustion and extinction: the Montecchi fading from the record, the Cappelletti watching the wheel. The play's statues gleam brighter against that darkness, and the darkness is documentary.

## Why the Borrowed Names Persuaded: Dante in Print and the Recognition Effect

A borrowing succeeds when the audience recognizes it, and the mechanics of recognition in da Porto's Italy explain why these two names, of all the Comedy's faction names, were the ones available for borrowing. By the early sixteenth century Dante was not merely read but institutionalized: the Commedia had been among the first books printed in Italy, in 1472, and Cristoforo Landino's great commentary of 1481, produced in Florence with civic pride behind it, fixed the poem as a school and study text whose every proper name arrived with an official gloss attached. Purgatorio 6 was among the most excerpted and memorized stretches of the poem, its invective a set piece of Italian letters, quoted by political writers as a standing rebuke to disunion. A novella writer of da Porto's formation, a gentleman soldier of the Veneto with humanist schooling, did not need to research the Montecchi and Cappelletti. The names were in the air of educated speech, proverbial for faction, and the Cappelletti's Cremonese address was exactly the kind of detail that proverbial status erases: proverbs keep the names and drop the footnotes.

The recognition effect cuts in two directions at once, and both directions served the novella. For readers who knew the Comedy well, the borrowed names announced the tale's register before its first incident: this would be a story in which houses destroy each other and the young pay for the old, because Dante's names could not headline a comedy. For readers who knew the names only as proverb, the effect was antiquity without effort: the surnames felt inherited, rooted, half-remembered from somewhere authoritative, and the tale borrowed their authority the way a forged charter borrows a seal. Da Porto's frame, which presents the story as an old tale heard from a veteran archer and set in the days of Bartolomeo della Scala, cooperates with the names to produce the same sensation from three sides: Dante's century, the Scaliger setting, the oral frame. The sensation is manufactured history, and its manufacture is so complete that four centuries of readers mistook the product for a record. The names chain resists the manufacture by reattaching each name to its documents, which is why the chain, and not the tale, is the proper place to begin a study of the feud.

One more mechanism completes the explanation: Verona's convenience. Da Porto set the tale in Verona for reasons the novella's frame makes plain, the Scaliger court, the archer's itinerary, the author's own regional world, and Verona happened to own one of the borrowed names outright. The Montecchi anchored the fiction in the city with documentary truth; the Cappelletti, dragged west from Cremona by the pairing, acquired Veronese citizenship by association with their anchored partner. Every subsequent stage inherited the arrangement unexamined, because each reteller received the names as a pair from a predecessor and had no occasion to audit the pair's geography. The chain's audit, performed in this study, is therefore the first many readers will have encountered, although the facts it rests on, Cremona for the Cappelletti, a pairing without a feud, have stood in the Dante commentaries since the fourteenth century. The lag between available knowledge and received legend, nearly seven centuries in this case, is the measure of how completely a good story can outrun its own footnotes.

## The Verdict Defended: Objections to the Dantean Reading Answered

A verdict-led study owes its readers the strongest case against itself, and the Dantean account of the names attracts three objections worth answering in the open, because answering them sharpens the account rather than merely protecting it. The first objection is geographical modesty: da Porto lived and soldiered in the Veneto, the Montecchi were a Veronese name with local traditions attached, and the novella writer may have taken the surnames from regional memory rather than from a book, in which case Dante's line is a parallel witness, not the source. The objection fails on the Cappelletti. Regional memory in the Veneto could supply Montecchi unaided; it could not supply Cappelletti, a Cremonese party name with no Veronese history, paired with Montecchi as a ready-made couplet. The pairing is the fingerprint, and the pairing exists in exactly one place before the novella: Purgatorio 6.106. A borrower of local tradition gets one name and must invent a partner. A borrower of Dante gets the couple. Da Porto has the couple.

The second objection is the argument from silence reversed: Dante's line, the objector notes, does not say the houses feuded, so the line cannot be the feud's origin, and crediting it with the feud's ancestry commits the same error as the antiquarians, reading the story into the poem. The objection misunderstands the claim. The Dantean account credits the poem with the names and their pairing, full stop; the feud between the houses is da Porto's invention, and the account says so at every stage. What the poem contributes beyond the bare names is not a feud but a frame: ruin and dread as the names' predicates, faction as their element, and a rhetorical coupling strong enough to demand narrative completion. Frames are causes in literary history, slower and less visible than plots, and denying them because they are not plots leaves the central fact unexplained, which is why this particular poem's particular pair, and not some chronicler's longer and duller lists of houses, stands at the head of the chain.

The third objection concerns the play directly: Shakespeare's audience knew nothing of Dante, the English forms of the names had shed every Italian association, and a resonance no hearer could hear is no resonance at all. The first half of the objection is true and the conclusion does not follow. A work's meanings are not limited to its first audience's knowledge, a point the whole source-study discipline presupposes: the play's debt to Brooke is a fact about the play although most of its first hearers had not read Brooke either. The names' Dantean history operates in the play as structure rather than as allusion, in the causeless antiquity of the grudge, the matched dignity of the houses, and the distribution of ruin across both, and structure is hearable by audiences who cannot name its source, exactly as a key signature is hearable by listeners who cannot name the key. The modern reader who learns the names' origin does not import a foreign meaning into the play. The reader recovers the reason the play's feud has always felt older, heavier, and less explicable than its incidents warrant: because its names were old, heavy, and unexplained when the story was young. The objections, answered, leave the verdict where the evidence put it. The cliché says Shakespeare invented the world's most famous feud names out of a poet's fancy. The record says a greater poet minted them as political evidence, a soldier-novelist borrowed them as a matched pair, and the fancy, where it operated, operated on history.

## A Note on Method: How Source Study Weighs a Name

The argument of this study rests on a method worth stating plainly, because the method is what separates the names chain from the legend it replaces. A name is evidence when it is rare, datable, and transmitted with company. Montecchi and Cappelletti pass all three tests. The names are rare: neither is a generic label that storytellers might coin independently, and their conjunction is rarer still. The conjunction is datable: it appears in a poem composed around 1310, in a chronicle-dark age whose records, where they survive, can be searched for rivals, and no earlier conjunction has been found. And the names travel with company: the pairing, the predicates of ruin and dread, and eventually the Veronese setting form a package whose stages can be tracked from text to text, each stage's form of the names datable by its spelling. Against that standard, the legend's evidence, a feud no archive records, a love story no early text tells, a Veronese Cappelletti no document knows, fails every test at once. Source study does not ask readers to choose between a dull truth and a charming story. It asks readers to notice that the truth, properly weighed, is the stranger artefact: two names that walked out of an invective, crossed three languages, and colonized the imagination of the world.

The same method disciplines the use of this study's verdict in teaching. A classroom that repeats the true-story claim because the claim is charming teaches students that charm outranks evidence, and the lesson survives long after the play is closed. A classroom that works the names chain instead, setting Dante's three lines beside da Porto's frame and the play's Prologue, teaches a transferable skill: how a text's smallest elements, two surnames, can be dated, sourced, and weighed, and how an inherited story can be audited rather than merely inherited. The names are an ideal training case precisely because the stakes are low and the evidence is complete. Nobody's grade depends on Cremona. Everybody's habits of reading are formed by whether Cremona is checked.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch names chain, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch names chain is the seven-stage descent of the feud's surnames from Dante's Purgatorio to Shakespeare's stage, set out in the table in this study. It begins with Dante's Montecchi and Cappelletti, real faction names of Verona and Cremona paired in an imperial invective around 1310, passes through da Porto's novella, where the names become feuding households in one city, and continues through Bandello's Italian, Boaistuau's French, and the English versions of Brooke and Painter, arriving at the anglicized Montague and Capulet. The pattern the chain exposes is a steady exchange: at every stage the names lose history and gain fiction. Politics drains away, plot accumulates, spellings soften, and surnames minted as evidence in a political argument end as the labels of a love tragedy. Reading the chain downward shows how the legend was manufactured. Reading it upward restores the names' original weight.

### Q: In which canto of the Purgatorio do the Montecchi and Cappelletti appear, and at what line?

The names appear in Purgatorio 6, at line 106, inside Dante's invective against Italy and its absent emperor. Canto 6 of each part of the Comedy carries a political teaching, and the Purgatorio's panel is the poem's great statement on faction: Italy enslaved, its cities at war with themselves, its houses ruined or afraid. The passage runs "Vieni a veder Montecchi e Cappelletti, / Monaldi e Filippeschi, uom sanza cura: / color già tristi, e questi con sospetti" (Purgatorio 6.106-108), naming the two houses alongside the Monaldi and Filippeschi of Orvieto. The names occur nowhere else in the Comedy, and the canto mentions no lover, no Verona story, and no quarrel between the houses. The single line is the feud's entire Dantean dossier, and its brevity is the point: Dante cites the names as evidence his first readers needed no help to read.

### Q: What exactly does Dante say has happened to the Montecchi and Cappelletti?

Dante says that one condition has overtaken both houses, at two different stages. In Robert Hollander's translation, the houses named first in each pair are "already downcast," while the others are "full of dread": the Montecchi's ruin is accomplished fact, and the Cappelletti's is anticipated, feared, and therefore already governing their lives. The Italian predicates, "già tristi" and "con sospetti," describe states, not events. Dante reports no battle, insult, murder, or marriage behind either condition, and names no opponent for either house. The grammar is the earliest form of the play's own device: an enmity presented as a standing condition, older than any narratable cause, whose effects are visible everywhere and whose origin is nowhere stated. Later readers who wanted a story behind the predicates had to invent one, and the novella tradition eventually obliged.

### Q: Who is the heedless man Dante orders to come and see the two houses?

The heedless man is Albert of Habsburg, King of the Romans and emperor-elect, who never crossed the Alps to take up the government of Italy. Dante addresses him as "uom sanza cura," the man without care, and spends the rest of Purgatorio 6 ordering him to inspect the peninsula his neglect has abandoned: the ruined houses, the oppressed territories, widowed Rome calling for her Caesar. The address converts the catalogue of families into a legal exhibit. Albert is not being informed about the Montecchi and Cappelletti; he is being charged with their condition, on the Dantean doctrine that faction flowers where imperial authority sleeps. The emperor's failure is the invective's explanation for every exhibit in it, which is why the passage names consequences and skips causes. Causes, in Dante's political theology, reduce to one: the empty throne, and the heedless man who would not fill it.

### Q: What event in the canto prompts Dante's outburst against Italy?

The outburst is prompted by an embrace between two dead poets. On the terrace of the late-repentant, Virgil meets Sordello, the Mantuan troubadour, and when the shared name of Mantua is spoken the two strangers fall into each other's arms, united by a birthplace and nothing else. Dante watches the dead achieve in an instant the solidarity that living Italians refuse, and turns on the living with the apostrophe that opens "Ahi serva Italia," ah, slavish Italy, hostel of grief. The contrast is engineered to shame: if a mere city name can reconcile shades who never met, the failure of Italy's cities and houses to reconcile is a chosen vice, not a fate. The Montecchi and Cappelletti enter the poem as exhibits in that shaming, instances of houses that chose faction over the solidarity Sordello and Virgil model by instinct.

### Q: Where were the historical Montecchi based, and what stronghold gave the family its name?

The historical Montecchi were based in Verona, and the family took its name from Montecchio Maggiore, the fortified hill town in the Vicentine whose castle commanded the road between Vicenza and Verona. The name is a toponymic plural, the people of Montecchio, and it functioned as a territorial claim: the house was its stronghold. By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the Montecchi ranked among Verona's leading magnate families, holding offices, lands, and armed followings, and moving through the city's passage from commune to signoria as principals. Their alignment in the factional struggles ran with the Ghibelline, imperial cause in the age of Ezzelino da Romano, and their power broke in the Della Scala consolidation that followed. Dante, who knew Verona as a guest of that successor regime, could write the house off as already downcast knowing his audience would register a documented fall, not a poetic invention.

### Q: In which city did the Cappelletti lead a faction, and who were their rivals?

The Cappelletti led a faction in Cremona, the Lombard city on the Po, and their rivals were the Barbarasi. Cremona's medieval politics ran on the struggle between these two party coalitions, each named for a leading family and each gathering clients, allied houses, and sworn adherents under its label. The Cappelletti's ascendancy belonged to the city's imperial, Ghibelline ascendancy in the age of Frederick II and his vicar Oberto Pelavicino, who made Cremona a seat of Lombard power. The earliest commentators on Dante assign the family to Cremona without disagreement, and Cremona's own chronicle tradition documents the Cappelletti-Barbarasi rivalry in detail. No Veronese record knows a Cappelletti house. The family's Veronese residence begins with the novella tradition, which needed both of Dante's names in one city and moved the Cremonese party name into Verona as a household.

### Q: Did Dante connect the two names to lovers or to any love story?

Dante connected the two names to nothing but politics. In Purgatorio 6 the Montecchi and Cappelletti appear as exhibits in an argument about empire and faction, named to prove that Italy's great houses are ruined or trembling under a negligent emperor. The passage contains no lovers, no young people, no marriage, no Verona setting for the pair, and no hint of a story attaching to either name. Dante's poem is full of narrated feuds and punished lovers elsewhere, from Buondelmonte's fatal marriage politics in Paradiso 16 to Paolo and Francesca in Inferno 5, and the absence of any such material at Purgatorio 6.106 is therefore conspicuous: where the poet wants narrative, he supplies it. The lovers arrive roughly two centuries later, when Luigi da Porto attaches the borrowed names to an invented pair in Verona. The love story is the novella's addition. Dante's contribution is the surnames and their charge of civic ruin.

### Q: Is there documentary evidence that the Montecchi and Cappelletti ever fought each other?

No documentary evidence places the two houses in conflict with each other. The negative is unusually strong because both cities kept dense records of their factional wars. Verona's tradition documents the Montecchi's struggles within Verona's own politics, against rival Veronese interests and the rising Della Scala. Cremona's tradition documents the Cappelletti against the Barbarasi across generations. Neither archive names the other city’s house as an opponent, and no chronicle, letter, or legal record from any Italian archive reports a Montecchi-Cappelletti encounter of any kind. The supposed feud rests entirely on two later constructions: Dante's rhetorical pairing of the names in one line, misread as a pairing of opponents, and da Porto's novella, which set both households in Verona for narrative convenience. Antiquarian writers fused the two constructions into a pseudo-record. Where documentation is this full and the event is this absent, the event did not happen.

### Q: Were the Montecchi and Cappelletti Guelphs or Ghibellines?

Both names carried the imperial, Ghibelline color in the struggles Dante's line remembers, though party labels in medieval Italy were worn loosely and shifted across generations. The Montecchi were identified with the Ghibelline cause in Verona's Ezzelino era, and their fortunes fell as the Della Scala built a signoria that suppressed the old factional houses. The Cappelletti's ascendancy in Cremona belonged to the imperial ascendancy of Frederick II and Oberto Pelavicino. The shared color is part of what made the pair legible to Dante's audience as a unit: two imperial-party names, one fallen, one fearing the fall, in the wreckage of the imperial project in Italy. Dante's invective, however, is not a Ghibelline pamphlet. Its argument condemns party as such under an absent emperor, and the named houses serve as exhibits of a general ruin that spared neither color, as the Guelph Monaldi, named beside them, prove.

### Q: How did Luigi da Porto change the two names' meaning when he took them up?

Luigi da Porto changed the names from political evidence into fictional households. In Dante, Montecchi and Cappelletti are faction names attached to different cities, cited as symptoms of Italy's disorder. In da Porto's Giulietta e Romeo, composed around 1520, the same names become two families resident in one city, Verona, bound in a standing enmity, with a son of one house and a daughter of the other attached as lovers. Every element of that arrangement is the novella's invention: the shared city, the feud between the houses, the lovers themselves. What da Porto preserved was the names' charge. His first readers knew the surnames from the most famous political passage in Italian poetry, and the borrowing told them, before any incident, that the tale's houses carried the weight of real factional ruin. The novella keeps the charge and spends it on a love story, which is the founding transaction of the whole Romeo and Juliet tradition.

### Q: How did Montecchi and Cappelletti become Montague and Capulet in English?

The names were anglicized by erosion across the story's journey through French. Da Porto's Italian forms passed intact through Bandello's Novelle of 1554, then entered Pierre Boaistuau's Histoires tragiques of 1559, where French spelling habits bent them toward French mouths. Arthur Brooke's English poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose Palace of Pleasure of 1567 completed the process, and the London stage fixed the results as Montague and Capulet. The respellings laundered the names' history. Montague became indistinguishable from the old English noble surname, sounding native and aristocratic to London ears, while Capulet's diminutive ending made the house sound lighter than its Cremonese original. By the time Shakespeare's audiences heard the names, nothing in their sound announced Dante, Cremona, or the party system. The anglicization is the final stage of the names chain: history fully converted into atmosphere.

### Q: What did Geoffrey Bullough conclude about the origin of the play's family names?

Geoffrey Bullough concluded that the family names descend from Dante through Luigi da Porto, and his Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957) remains the standard settlement of the question. Bullough prints and examines the source chain, treats the Dante passage as the names' point of entry into the tradition, and assigns da Porto the decisive act: the novella writer first attached the Montecchi and Cappelletti to lovers and to a feud between their houses. Shakespeare, on Bullough's showing, received the names at the chain's end, already eroded toward their English forms by Brooke and Painter, with no independent access to their origin. Later scholarship has refined details of the transmission without displacing the settlement, because the documentary sequence supports it at every link: Dante's line is the earliest datable conjunction of the names, da Porto's novella the earliest datable attachment of the names to the lovers, and no earlier witness for either has ever been produced.

### Q: What did Olin Moore argue against the Dante-to-da Porto account, and why has it not prevailed?

Olin H. Moore argued that the Romeo and Juliet legend was older than the novella tradition, a piece of folk tradition possibly rooted in real Veronese events, which da Porto recorded rather than invented. On Moore's account, Dante's naming of the Montecchi and Cappelletti might glance at that older complex, and the feud between the houses might precede the poem rather than follow the novella. The argument, developed across studies in the 1930s, has not prevailed for a single decisive reason: evidence. No text, chronicle, or document earlier than the novella tradition joins the two names to lovers, to a feud between the houses, or to a shared Veronese residence. Moore's lost tradition is an inference from the story's later fame, and fame is not a document. Bullough's account requires no lost witnesses, since Dante's line, the most memorized political passage in Italian, explains the pairing's availability to da Porto directly.

### Q: What do the earliest commentators on Dante say about the two houses?

The earliest commentators identify the houses geographically and politically, and say nothing about lovers or a feud between them. Glosses written within a generation of Dante's death in 1321, including the commentary of the poet's son Jacopo Alighieri, the Lana commentary, and the Ottimo commentary, assign the Montecchi to Verona and the Cappelletti to Cremona, and explain the predicates of line 108 as the conditions of factional houses, one fallen and one afraid. The commentaries collected in the Dartmouth Dante Project show a tradition consistent on the essentials because the commentators shared Dante's political world, in which two faction names in one line needed no story to connect them. The Veronese feud enters the gloss tradition only later, after the novella had created it, when antiquarian readers began harmonizing Dante with the legend. The early glosses are therefore double evidence: for the houses' identities, and for the fact that Dante's own century heard no feud in the line.

### Q: Why do the names matter to the play if the feud's cause is never explained?

The names matter because they are the explanation the play withholds in every other form. The Prologue offers an "ancient grudge" with no grievance attached, and the drama never repairs the omission. Borrowed names fill that silence with felt history. Montecchi and Cappelletti arrived in the story already signifying houses ruined and trembling under inherited faction, and although the factual content of that significance faded through translation, its shape survived: enmity as a birthright, older than any living memory, binding people who did not choose it. When Juliet cries "My only love sprung from my only hate" (1.5.136), the horror depends on a name being a destiny imposed before its bearer acts. That is precisely how party names worked in Dante's Italy, where a child of the Montecchi inherited opponents sight unseen. The names teach the audience how to feel the feud: not as a quarrel awaiting its reason, but as a condition awaiting its victims.

### Q: Did Shakespeare know that the names came from Dante?

No evidence shows that Shakespeare knew the names' Dantean origin, and the transmission makes such knowledge unlikely. The playwright received Montague and Capulet through Arthur Brooke's poem and William Painter's prose, the English links of the source chain, in forms already anglicized and detached from the Comedy. No English translation of Dante existed in Shakespeare's lifetime, the first full English Comedy being Henry Boyd's of 1802, and the play contains no verbal echo of Purgatorio 6. What survived the journey to the London stage was not knowledge but structure: the matched dignity of the houses, the causeless antiquity of their grudge, and the distribution of ruin across both families at the close. Those features behave the way Dante's invective behaves, and source study can trace the behavior to the names' origin without crediting the playwright with the scholarship. The chain transmitted effects its final bearer could not have explained, as chains of transmission commonly do.

### Q: How do translators render Dante's lines about the houses, and does any translation mention a feud between them?

Translators render the lines as a summons to inspect two ruined or fearful houses, and none produces a feud between them, because the Italian contains no word of enmity connecting the pairs. Robert Hollander's version, the standard scholarly Englishing of the Princeton Dante Project, reads "Come and see the Montecchi and the Cappelletti, the Monaldi and the Filippeschi, heedless man: the first already downcast, the others full of dread." Earlier versions, from Henry Francis Cary's of 1814 and Longfellow's of 1867 to Charles Singleton's prose, agree in substance: a list, an address to the heedless emperor, and two predicates of condition. The verbs are verbs of seeing, the nouns are names, and the only relationship the syntax asserts among the houses is juxtaposition inside one inspection. Feud language enters the passage only in loose paraphrase by writers summarizing the play backward. Checking any reputable translation against the play's account is the quickest test of the whole legend.

### Q: Where did the claim that the feud was a real Veronese event come from?

The claim was built in stages, none of them documentary. The first stage is da Porto's novella, which housed both families in Verona and set them at feud, converting Dante's rhetorical pairing into neighbors. The second is Veronese historiography: Girolamo dalla Corte's Istoria di Verona, in the later sixteenth century, admitted the lovers' tale into the city's history as fact under the Scaliger past, giving the fiction a chronicler's passport. The third is antiquarian commentary on Dante, which resolved the tension between the famous line and the famous legend in the legend's favor and began treating the Cappelletti as Veronese. The fourth is tourism, which gave the feud addresses, a house, a balcony, a tomb. Each stage borrowed authority from the previous one, and the completed structure cites itself. Against it stand the records the builders skipped: Dante's earliest commentators placing the Cappelletti in Cremona, and Cremona's chronicles naming the Barbarasi as their rivals.

### Q: How can an essay use Dante's naming of the houses to argue that the play's feud is political before it is personal?

An essay can argue from the names' documentary history to the play's structure in three moves. The first move establishes origin: Montecchi and Cappelletti enter literature in Purgatorio 6 as faction names, exhibits in an argument about civic order, with no lovers and no shared city. The second move tracks the borrowing: da Porto converts the political pair into feuding households, and every later version inherits a feud whose names were minted as party badges, one of them, Cappelletti, having named a political coalition outright in Cremona. The third move reads the play against that inheritance: the Prologue's causeless ancient grudge, the servants fighting under their masters' names before any principal speaks, the Prince's civic language of scourge and punishment, all behave like party conflict wearing domestic dress. The conclusion writes itself. The feud feels political because it is political in its materials: the play privatizes a public pathology, and the surnames are the surviving evidence of the pathology's public origin.
