The question the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act answers is a practical one. In a federal system built around chapter 44 of title 18, with definitions in section 921, prohibitions in section 922, and transfers conducted through licensees and a national background check system, what did Congress change when it enacted the first federal statute making significant changes to firearms law in nearly three decades? The answer is specific and bounded. Congress created two freestanding offenses, extended an existing prohibition to a new class of relationship, deepened and lengthened the check for one age group, rewrote the phrase that decides when a repetitive seller must hold a license, added a grant purpose for state crisis intervention proceedings, and appropriated money across three departments for mental health, school, records, and related programs. Each change operates through language inserted into law that predated the act, and each limit in the enacted text is as much a part of that answer as each operative provision.

That bounded character follows from the way the act entered law. The text traveled as Senate Amendment 5099 to the House amendment to S.2938, a bill introduced to designate a federal courthouse in Tallahassee, Florida, after a framework announced on June 12, 2022 by 20 senators and text released on June 21, 2022. The Senate concurred on June 23 by 65 to 33, the House passed the measure on June 24 by 234 to 193, and President Joseph R. Biden signed the measure on June 25, 2022, making it Public Law 117-159 at 136 Stat. 1313. Division A carries the amendments, definitions, program provisions, and standards. Division B carries the appropriations. The firearms provisions sit in Title II of Division A, sections 12001 to 12005, while mental health program provisions sit in Title I and other matters sit in Title III.

The operative changes reward attention to statutory detail. Section 932 of title 18 reaches a knowing purchase made for another person under stated knowledge conditions concerning that person's status or intended use, and section 933 of title 18 reaches a transfer or receipt under stated knowledge concerning felonious use, carrying, possession, or receipt. Section 12005 reaches convictions against dating partners entered on or after June 25, 2022, and pairs that extension with a restoration provision that applies only within the new category after five years. Section 12001 requires three contacts in the jurisdiction where a buyer aged 18 to 20 resides and allows up to 10 business days where further investigation is required, with the contact requirement expiring on September 30, 2032. Section 12002 replaces a livelihood and profit test with a predominantly earn a profit standard. Section 12003 and Division B then move money through grants, rather than through mandates addressed to states.

Reading the act against its framework clarifies each of those moves. [the complete guide to the Gun Control Act of 1968](/2021/04/01/gun-control-act-1968-complete-guide/) supplies the framework the act amends, including the definitions, prohibitions, licensing structure, and transfer rules in chapter 44 that sections 12001 to 12005 altered in place. [the Brady Act profile](/2021/04/15/brady-act-1993-guide/) supplies the background check history behind the default transfer rule the act narrows for buyers under 21, under which a licensee may proceed at discretion after three business days without a final determination. With that framework and that default rule in view, the provisions that follow can be read in the order the act presents them, offense by offense, definition by definition, and appropriation by appropriation, with the omissions stated with the same precision as the enactments.

## What the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act Is

The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act is a federal statute of the 117th Congress. Its formal citation is Public Law 117-159, and its text begins at 136 Stat. 1313. President Joseph R. Biden signed the measure on June 25, 2022. The short title names the act as a whole and applies across provisions that amend the firearms provisions of title 18 of the United States Code, authorize and fund mental health and school related programs, appropriate money to three federal departments, and address other subjects collected in a final title. Its operative effect lies less in a freestanding code of its own than in amendments inserted into existing law, definitions rewritten in place, new offenses added to an established chapter, and appropriations made for stated fiscal years. Each rule can be traced to the place the act assigned it in that structure.

The legislative vehicle explains a feature that can appear puzzling on a first reading of the opening sections. The measure traveled as S.2938. As introduced, S.2938 was a bill designating a federal courthouse in Tallahassee, Florida. The substantive text arrived late in that journey, offered as Senate Amendment 5099 to the House amendment to S.2938 and released on June 21, 2022. The enacted act preserves that history in its own text. The act's first two sections retain the designation provisions its vehicle carried, the federal courthouse designation in Tallahassee, Florida, and a post office designation in Petaluma, California. Those designations sit at the front of the public law, before the divisions carrying the substantive provisions. It records the parliamentary route by which a bill already moving through Congress became the instrument for an agreement assembled in June 2022, with text released on June 21 and enactment following within days.

The architecture of the act has two divisions. Division A contains the authorizing and amending provisions. Title I of Division A, titled Children and Family Mental Health Services, runs from section 11001 to section 11005. Title II of Division A, titled Firearms, runs from section 12001 to section 12005 and contains the provisions that amend title 18. Title III of Division A, titled Other Matters, runs from section 13101 to section 13401 and collects provisions on prescription drug policy, a Medicare fund, school safety administration, and the use of elementary and secondary education funds. Division B contains appropriations, in three titles providing money connected with the Departments of Justice, Health and Human Services, and Education. The structure separates two kinds of legislative action performed at the same time. Division A changes rules, definitions, programs, and duties. Division B supplies budget authority for specified purposes and periods.

### What kind of statute is the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act is a federal statute enacted by the 117th Congress and codified principally as amendments to chapter 44 of title 18 of the United States Code, combining new firearms offenses and background check procedures with mental health, school, and appropriations provisions in two divisions.

Codification gives the firearms provisions their durable legal home. Chapter 44 of title 18 contains the federal definitions for firearms transactions, the categories of persons prohibited from receiving or possessing firearms, the conditions on licensed importers, manufacturers, dealers, and collectors, and the background check system through which licensees transfer firearms. The act works principally through section 921 of title 18, which supplies the definitions, and section 922 of title 18, which supplies the unlawful acts. Alongside amendments to existing sections, the act adds three new sections to chapter 44, sections 932 to 934 of title 18. Section 932 addresses straw purchasing of firearms. Section 933 addresses trafficking in firearms. Section 934 addresses forfeiture connected with convictions under the two preceding sections. The act did not create a parallel firearms code outside title 18. It rewrote parts of the existing code, inserted new offenses into it, and left the surrounding framework of definitions, prohibitions, licensing, and transfer procedure in place around the insertions.

The first substantive element is a pair of new federal offenses, enacted in section 12004 of the act under the heading of the Stop Illegal Trafficking in Firearms Act. Section 932 of title 18 makes it unlawful for any person to knowingly purchase, or conspire to purchase, any firearm in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce for, on behalf of, or at the request or demand of another person, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the other person meets the criteria of one or more paragraphs of section 922(d) of title 18, intends to use, carry, possess, or sell or otherwise dispose of the firearm in furtherance of a felony, a federal crime of terrorism, or a drug trafficking crime, or intends to sell or otherwise dispose of the firearm to a person described in either of those categories. Section 932(c) of title 18 provides a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years. Where the violation is committed knowing or with reasonable cause to believe that a firearm involved will be used to commit a felony, a federal crime of terrorism, or a drug trafficking crime, the maximum term rises to not more than 25 years.

Section 933 of title 18 creates the trafficking offense in parallel terms. It makes it unlawful for any person to ship, transport, transfer, cause to be transported, or otherwise dispose of any firearm to another person in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce, if the person knows or has reasonable cause to believe that the use, carrying, or possession of the firearm by the recipient would constitute a felony. It also makes it unlawful to receive a firearm if the recipient knows or has reasonable cause to believe that the receipt would constitute a felony, and it reaches an attempt or conspiracy to do either act. The penalty is a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years. Section 934 of title 18 attaches forfeiture of property constituting or derived from the proceeds of the offense and of property used or intended to be used in the offense, and a fine of up to twice the gross profits or other proceeds of the offense, to a conviction under section 932 or section 933. Violations of section 932 and section 933 were also made predicate offenses under the federal wiretapping, racketeering, and money laundering statutes. The new offenses replaced a baseline in which straw purchasing was prosecuted principally as a false statement offense, under section 922(a)(6) of title 18 and section 924(a)(1)(A) of title 18, a mismatch federal prosecutors had long identified.

The second element extends an existing domestic violence prohibition to dating partners. The prohibition at section 922(g)(9) of title 18, with the transfer bar at section 922(d)(9) of title 18, reaches persons convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence, a category added in 1996. Before the act, the definition at section 921(a)(33) of title 18 reached offenses against a spouse or former spouse, a person sharing a child with the victim, a cohabitant or former cohabitant, a parent or guardian, or a person similarly situated. Dating partners as such were outside that definition. Section 12005(a)(1) of the act inserted into section 921(a)(33)(A)(ii) offenses committed by a person in a dating relationship with the victim. A new definition at section 921(a)(37) of title 18, added by section 12005(a)(2), describes a dating relationship as a relationship between individuals who have or have recently had a continuing serious relationship of a romantic or intimate nature, determined by considering the length of the relationship, the nature of the relationship, and the frequency and type of interaction between the individuals. Section 12005(b) provides that the amendments do not apply to any conviction entered before June 25, 2022. A restoration provision applies only within the new category. Under section 921(a)(33)(C) of title 18, added by section 12005(c)(2), a person whose disqualification rests on such a conviction against a dating partner, who has no other such conviction and is not otherwise prohibited, is no longer treated as convicted once five years have elapsed after whichever is later, the judgment of conviction or the completion of any custodial or supervisory sentence. Restoration is unavailable after a subsequent disqualifying conviction and is not extended to the categories covered before 2022, and records accessible to the background check system must be updated to reflect a restoration.

The third element strengthens the background check for buyers under 21 years of age. Section 12001 of the act, titled Juvenile Records, addresses prospective transferees under 21, in practice buyers aged 18 to 20, since licensees may not sell a handgun to a person under 21 or a long gun to a person under 18. For such a buyer, the act requires the national instant criminal background check system to contact immediately three sources in the jurisdiction where the buyer resides: the pertinent state, tribal, or territorial repositories of criminal history record information and juvenile justice information, the state, tribal, or territorial custodians of mental health adjudication records, and local law enforcement agencies. The act also bars the transfer of a firearm to a person whose juvenile records contain a potentially disqualifying adjudication or mental health determination. Before enactment, such records were not routinely queried in a federal firearms background check. Where further investigation is required, the eligibility determination may take up to 10 business days, against the ordinary rule under section 922(t) of title 18 under which a licensee may proceed with a transfer at its discretion after three business days without a final determination. The requirement that the system contact these authorities expires 10 years after enactment, on September 30, 2032. The Attorney General was directed to upgrade the system to make these contacts, and Division B provided records funding in support of that direction.

The fourth element clarifies who must hold a federal firearms license as a dealer. Section 921(a)(21)(C) of title 18 defines a dealer in firearms as a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business through the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms. Section 12002 of the act struck the qualifying phrase that tied that definition to the principal objective of livelihood and profit and substituted language directed to predominantly earning a profit. A new definition at section 921(a)(22) of title 18 defines the substituted term as an intent underlying the sale or disposition that is predominantly one of obtaining pecuniary gain, as opposed to other intents, such as improving or liquidating a personal firearms collection, and transfers made to improve or liquidate a personal collection are excepted from the licensure requirement. At the precision used by the Congressional Research Service, persons who buy and resell firearms repetitively and predominantly for profit must hold a federal firearms license, with the recordkeeping and background check duties that attach to licensees, and some intrastate private transfers by such persons are brought inside those duties. Occasional sales and genuine collecting remain outside the requirement.

The fifth element is funding, joined to a grant purpose in Division A. Section 12003 of the act adds a ninth authorized purpose to the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program, the Byrne JAG program at sections 10151 and following of title 34, stated as state crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs or initiatives, which may include drug courts, mental health courts, veterans treatment courts, and extreme risk protection order programs. Statutory standards for funded extreme risk protection order programs include due process rights, the right to be represented by counsel, evidentiary standards at least equal to those applying to a similarly situated litigant in federal or state court, and penalties for abuse of the programs. The act funds state programs through grants and does not require any state to establish such a program. For the Department of Justice, Division B provided 1.4 billion dollars to the Office of Justice Programs for fiscal years 2022 through 2026, comprising 750 million dollars for crisis intervention programs under the Byrne program, 250 million dollars for a community violence intervention and prevention initiative, 200 million dollars for grants under the STOP School Violence Act of 2018, and 200 million dollars for grants to upgrade criminal and mental health records for the background check system, with a further 100 million dollars for school security grants and 100 million dollars for the Federal Bureau of Investigation. The Congressional Research Service describes the Department of Justice grant funding in the act as 1.5 billion dollars in total.

For Health and Human Services, Division B provided 800 million dollars to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, available through September 30, 2025, comprising 250 million dollars for the Community Mental Health Services Block Grant, 240 million dollars for Project AWARE, 150 million dollars for the National Suicide Prevention Lifeline for fiscal year 2022, 120 million dollars for mental health awareness training, and 40 million dollars for the National Child Traumatic Stress Initiative, plus a further 190 million dollars, available through September 30, 2026, for pediatric mental health care access, primary care training and enhancement, and grants to states supporting school based health services under section 11003. For Education, Division B provided 1 billion dollars at 200 million dollars per year for fiscal years 2022 through 2026, divided equally between School Based Mental Health Services Grants and Mental Health Services Professional Demonstration Grants, both directed at increasing the number of mental health professionals providing services in schools with demonstrated need, and 1.05 billion dollars in school improvement funding available through September 30, 2025. Title I of Division A supplies the matching program provisions, extending the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic demonstration in section 11001 and addressing telehealth guidance, school based Medicaid services, a review of Medicaid screening and treatment requirements, and pediatric mental health care access in sections 11002 to 11005.

Title III adds provisions with no direct connection to firearms transactions. Section 13101 extends the moratorium on implementation of the Medicare Part D prescription drug rebate rule to January 1, 2027, a delay the Congressional Budget Office estimated would save 20.9 billion dollars from fiscal years 2022 to 2027, making it the principal budgetary offset in the act. Section 13201 sets the amount available in the Medicare Improvement Fund at 7.5 billion dollars for services furnished during and after fiscal year 2022. Sections 13301 to 13305, the act's school safety subtitle, require the Secretary of Homeland Security to establish a Federal Clearinghouse on School Safety Evidence Based Practices, published through SchoolSafety.gov, with notification duties and a review of federal grant programs within a year of enactment. Section 13401 amends the Elementary and Secondary Education Act to prohibit the use of its funds to provide any person with a dangerous weapon or training in the use of a dangerous weapon.

A complete statement of what the act is also requires a brief statement of subjects debated alongside it that the enacted text does not contain. The act contains no provision regulating any category of firearm, no definition or prohibition directed at semiautomatic rifles or any other class, and no revival of the 1994 ban that expired in 2004. The act contains no limit on the capacity of ammunition feeding devices. The act does not require a background check for private transfers between unlicensed persons as such. The federal check continues to attach to transfers by licensees, and the engaged in the business amendment reaches only persons whose repetitive selling predominantly for profit requires a license under the amended definition. The act creates no federal licensing or registration system for firearm owners and no federal registry of firearms or owners. The act does not require any state to adopt a crisis intervention or extreme risk protection order program, because the funding is offered by grant to states that operate qualifying programs.

Measured against the period that preceded it, the act holds a distinct place in the sequence of federal firearms statutes. It is the first federal statute making significant changes to firearms law since the 1990s, ending a period of nearly three decades in which no comparable measure passed both chambers. The preceding comparable enactments are the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act of 1993, which supplied the background check framework that section 12001 later amended for younger buyers, and the 1994 federal assault weapons ban, which expired in 2004 and which the act did not revive. Its firearms title added offenses, extended a prohibition, lengthened a check for a defined age group, and rewrote a licensing definition, while its other divisions funded mental health, school, and crisis intervention purposes at the levels stated in Division B. That combination, enacted as Public Law 117-159 on June 25, 2022, is the statute whose provisions the remaining sections take up one by one.

## How the Act Moved Through Congress

The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act reached enactment through a compressed sequence of dated steps in June 2022, each step recorded in the actions on S.2938 in the 117th Congress. A framework was announced on June 12, legislative text was released on June 21, the Senate acted on June 23, the House acted on June 24, and the measure was presented to the President and signed on June 25, becoming Public Law 117-159. Between the first of those dates and the last, the work changed character at each stage. The negotiations in the spring of 2022 produced agreement among a defined group of senators. The framework converted that agreement into a public statement of shared terms. The release of text converted the terms into statutory language that could be offered as an amendment and put to recorded votes. The House then considered the measure as the Senate had passed it, and a second recorded vote completed congressional action. Presentation and signature followed on the day after House passage.

The negotiations that preceded the framework took place in the Senate during the spring of 2022. The participants who matter for the legislative history are the senators who announced the framework and the smaller group who led the negotiations. Four senators led the work. Chris Murphy of Connecticut and John Cornyn of Texas stood at the head of the group, with Kyrsten Sinema of Arizona and Thom Tillis of North Carolina completing the group of four lead negotiators. Two of the four were Democrats and two were Republicans, a balance that matched the composition of the larger group that later announced the framework. The lead negotiators performed the function that lead negotiators perform in a Senate negotiation of this kind. They conducted the exchanges through which separate positions were tested against one another, identified the points on which agreement could be recorded, and carried responsibility for reducing those points to a form that other senators could accept or reject as a package.

### What a Framework Agreement Meant for the Negotiations

A framework agreement is an agreed outline of terms reached before statutory text is drafted and released. It records the points on which the negotiators have settled, in general form, and it leaves the conversion of those points into operative legal language for a subsequent drafting stage. For that reason the interval between a framework and a released text forms a distinct stage in the passage of a measure, and the passage record preserves the two dates separately. The framework in this instance was announced on June 12, 2022, and the text was released on June 21, 2022, nine days afterward. The nine day interval was the drafting stage of the act, during which the announced terms were rendered as an amendment that the Senate could take up through the vehicle described below.

The group that announced the framework on June 12 numbered 20 senators, divided evenly between the parties, with 10 Democrats and 10 Republicans. The even division was itself part of the announcement, because it described the coalition in arithmetical terms before any floor vote tested that coalition. The four lead negotiators sat inside that larger figure, so the announcement presented two nested numbers at once. The smaller number, four, identified the senators who had led the negotiations and who could speak for the terms from direct participation in forming the understanding. The larger number, 20, identified the senators prepared to attach their names to the framework on the day of announcement.

The release of the bill text on June 21 changed the status of the agreement. From that date the terms existed as statutory language in a form that could be offered formally, read against the sections of title 18 that the language proposed to amend, and assessed by senators and representatives who had taken no part in the negotiations. The passage record identifies the text by the procedural form it took rather than by a freestanding bill number of its own. The substantive text took the form of Senate Amendment 5099 to the House amendment to S.2938, and that identification matters because it explains how a measure of this scope reached passage within four days of the release of its text. The speed of the final stage rested on the use of a bill that already stood partway through the legislative sequence, carrying an amendment from the other chamber to which a further amendment could be offered.

### How the Senate Used an Existing Bill as the Vehicle

S.2938 had been introduced as a bill designating a federal courthouse in Tallahassee, Florida. By the time the substantive text of the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act reached the Senate, the bill had acquired a House amendment, which placed the measure in the posture in which one chamber has acted and the other chamber holds the measure with the first chamber text before it. In that posture the Senate had more than one course available. The Senate could concur in the House amendment as it stood, could concur with a further amendment of its own, or could decline to concur. The course taken was concurrence with an amendment, and the amendment was Senate Amendment 5099, which carried the substantive text released on June 21. The act as enacted preserves a trace of the vehicle inside its own opening sections. Sections 1 and 2 of the act retain the courthouse designation that had given S.2938 its original subject and a post office designation in Petaluma, California, so the enacted statute opens with the designations attached to the vehicle before its substantive divisions begin. It explains how text released on June 21 could be before the Senate in amendable form on June 23, how the Senate vote took the form of concurrence in a House amendment with an amendment rather than passage of an original Senate bill, and how House action on June 24 could complete the process without a further round of amendment exchange between the chambers.

Concurrence with an amendment also shaped the motions that the Senate had to dispose of before final action. The question before the Senate was framed as a motion to concur, and the procedural record contains a separate vote on cloture on that motion before the vote on concurrence itself. Cloture is the Senate procedure by which debate on a pending question is brought to a close by recorded vote, allowing the chamber to move from consideration to decision. Between the release of text on June 21 and the Senate votes on June 23, two days elapsed. Between the framework on June 12 and Senate passage on June 23, eleven days elapsed. The chronology at the end of the process was therefore much tighter than the chronology at its beginning. The negotiations occupied a season, described in the record as the spring of 2022. The formal stages that converted the product of those negotiations into law occupied less than two weeks from framework to signature, and less than one week from released text to signature.

### How Close Were the Votes on the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

The Senate passed the act by 65 to 33 on June 23, 2022, and the House passed it by 234 to 193 on June 24, 2022. In each chamber the winning margin exceeded the number of votes needed for passage, and in each chamber support came from both parties.

The Senate took two recorded votes on June 23. The first was the vote on cloture on the motion to concur, Record Vote 240, on which cloture was agreed to by 65 to 34, with one senator not voting. The second was the vote on passage, recorded as concurrence in the House amendment with an amendment, Record Vote 242, on which the measure passed by 65 to 33, with two senators not voting. The figure for the affirmative side stood identical across the two votes, at 65 in each instance. The figure for the negative side moved by one, from 34 on cloture to 33 on passage, and the number of senators not voting moved correspondingly, from one on the cloture vote to two on the passage vote. The total number of votes cast fell from 99 on cloture to 98 on passage, while the size of the majority in favor held constant. That pattern separates two questions that a single passage figure would merge. The cloture vote measured support for bringing debate to a close and deciding the question. The passage vote measured support for the measure itself. On June 23 those two measures of support produced the same affirmative total, so the coalition prepared to end debate and the coalition prepared to enact the text were, in aggregate number, the same coalition.

The composition of the Senate passage majority gives the figure of 65 its full meaning. The 65 yeas consisted of the 50 members of the Democratic caucus and 15 Republicans. The 33 nays were all Republicans. No member of the Democratic caucus voted against concurrence, and the opposition recorded on the Senate floor came entirely from one party, while the majority in favor drew from both. Expressed as shares of the votes cast, the 65 yeas represented two thirds of the 98 votes recorded on passage, against 33 nays representing one third. Expressed as shares within the Republican votes cast, the 15 Republican yeas stood against 33 Republican nays, so Republicans who voted divided roughly one to two between concurrence and opposition, with the larger share against. Set against the framework announced on June 12, the Senate result shows how a negotiating coalition differs from an enacting coalition. The announcing group had contained 10 Republicans and 10 Democrats. Senate passage added 5 Republican votes beyond the Republican contingent of that group and recorded the full Democratic caucus of 50 in favor, five times the Democratic contingent of the framework group. The framework had been an evenly divided coalition of 20 senators. The enacting coalition numbered 65 and was unevenly divided, with one party supplying its entire caucus and the other supplying a minority of its voting members. The distance between those two figures, 20 and 65, quantifies the work between demonstrating that an agreement across the party line existed and carrying a recorded vote in which every member of the chamber could be counted.

The House acted on the day after Senate passage. On June 24 the House passed the measure by 234 to 193, recorded as Roll No. 299. House passage completed congressional action, because the House took the measure in the form in which the Senate had passed it, and no further amendment exchange between the chambers followed. The sequence across the two days therefore ran in a straight line. The Senate concurred in the House amendment with Amendment 5099 on June 23, the House passed the measure so amended on June 24, and the completed bill stood ready for presentation. The House margin, measured in raw votes, was 41, the difference between 234 yeas and 193 nays. The Senate margin on passage, measured the same way, was 32, the difference between 65 yeas and 33 nays. The House total of votes cast was 427, the sum of the yeas and nays recorded, a figure that reflects the larger size of that chamber.

The composition of the House majority paralleled the composition of the Senate majority, with one difference in degree. The 234 House yeas consisted of all 220 Democrats and 14 Republicans. The 193 nays were all Republicans, and no Democrat voted nay. In the House, as in the Senate, the majority combined the whole of one party with a minority of the other, and the opposition came entirely from the second party. The Republican division in the House was far more lopsided than the Republican division in the Senate. House Republicans who voted divided 14 in favor against 193 against, a ratio in which the nays outnumbered the yeas by nearly fourteen to one. Senate Republicans who voted had divided 15 in favor against 33 against, a ratio of a little more than two to one.

### What the Two Roll Calls Show About the Enacting Coalition

Read together, the two roll calls describe a coalition with three arithmetical properties, each visible in the figures and none dependent on any account of individual motive. The first property is completeness on one side. Across 270 Democratic and Democratic caucus votes recorded in the two chambers, comprising 50 in the Senate and 220 in the House, every vote was cast in favor, and the number of Democratic nays across both roll calls was zero. The second property is division on the other side. Across the Republican votes recorded in the two chambers, 29 were cast in favor, 15 in the Senate and 14 in the House, and 226 were cast against, 33 in the Senate and 193 in the House. Republican votes therefore supplied the entire opposition in both chambers and a minority of the support in both chambers at the same time. The third property is the difference between the chambers in the balance of that division. In the Senate the Republican yeas amounted to just under one third of the Republican votes cast, and in the House the Republican yeas amounted to about one fifteenth of the Republican votes cast.

The margins also answer the question of closeness in a more precise sense than the word close usually allows. A vote is close in the arithmetical sense when a small movement of votes from one side to the other would change the outcome. Measured against that standard, neither vote turned on a handful of members. In the Senate, the 65 yeas exceeded the 33 nays by 32 votes, so the outcome would have survived the loss of a substantial bloc of supporters before the two sides drew level. The closeness question can also be measured against the framework and the cloture vote. Had the Senate vote reproduced the framework coalition alone, only 20 senators would have stood in favor, far short of the votes cast on passage, and the enacting majority of 65 exceeded the announcing group more than three times over. The cloture vote supplies a standard internal to June 23 itself, because the same affirmative total of 65 was recorded on the procedural vote to end debate and on the substantive vote to concur, so the two votes corroborate one another rather than showing a majority assembled for procedure and reduced on the merits. In the House, the 234 yeas exceeded the 193 nays by 41 votes, a margin of the same character. The majority that ended debate and the majority that concurred were both recorded at 65, and the stability of that figure across two votes on the same day is part of what the record shows about the coalition at the moment of enactment.

Presentation and signature closed the process with the same speed that had marked the floor stages. The measure was presented to the President and signed on June 25, 2022, one day after House passage and two days after Senate passage. Signature by President Joseph R. Biden converted the passed bill into Public Law 117-159, cited at 136 Stat. 1313. Between Senate passage and signature, three days elapsed. Senate passage on June 23, House passage on June 24, and presentation and signature on June 25 form an unbroken three day sequence at the end of the process. From the framework announcement on June 12 to signature on June 25, the formal stages covered 13 days in all, and from the release of text on June 21 to signature, four days, so the largest part of the legislative calendar belonged to the spring negotiations and the smallest part to the floor votes that completed the work. The full span of formal action, from the announcement of the framework on June 12 to signature on June 25, covered 13 days. Within that span, the stages fell in an order that the record states plainly. Negotiations in the spring of 2022 among senators led by Murphy, Cornyn, Sinema, and Tillis produced a framework announced by 20 senators on June 12. Drafting produced a text released on June 21. The Senate took up the House amendment to S.2938, agreed to cloture on the motion to concur by 65 to 34 on June 23, and concurred with Amendment 5099 by 65 to 33 on the same day. The House passed the measure by 234 to 193 on June 24. Presentation and signature followed on June 25. Each stage depended on the stage before it, the framework on the negotiations, the text on the framework, the amendment on the text, the votes on the amendment, and the signature on the votes, and the passage of the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act is fully described by that chain of dated procedural steps and by the arithmetic of the two roll calls that carried the measure through Congress.

## The New Offenses for Straw Purchasing and Trafficking

Before June 2022, federal law contained no freestanding offense of straw purchasing and no freestanding offense of trafficking in firearms. The conduct those terms describe, a purchase made for another person who could not or would not buy in his or her own name, and the movement of firearms toward persons barred from having them or toward unlawful use, was prosecuted principally as a false statement. The transaction papers, rather than the transfer of the firearm itself, supplied the operative wrong. A buyer who answered the required questions as if the firearm were for himself or herself had lied to a licensee or on a form, and that lie became the case the government could bring.

The first provision that carried that practice was section 922(a)(6) of title 18, which made it unlawful to make a false statement to a licensee about a fact material to the lawfulness of a transfer, and which carried imprisonment for not more than 10 years. Its elements directed attention toward the accuracy of what was said at the point of sale. The statement had to be false, it had to be made to a licensee, and the fact misstated had to be material to lawfulness. The identity of the true recipient and the use intended for the firearm entered the case, if at all, as background to a misrepresentation rather than as elements the government had to prove.

The second provision was section 924(a)(1)(A) of title 18, which covered false statements in the records a licensee must keep and carried imprisonment for not more than 5 years. Its focus was narrower, the integrity of the paperwork a transfer generated. Together, the two provisions attached the federal offense to the documentation of a sale, while the sale itself, undertaken for a prohibited recipient and with knowledge of that recipient, had no offense of its own. Prosecutors had to frame the acquisition of a firearm for another person as an offense about truthfulness, and the penalty followed the framing rather than the substance.

### What did prosecutors have to charge before straw purchasing had its own offense?

Before June 2022, prosecutors charged straw purchasing principally as a false statement offense. Section 922(a)(6) covered a false statement to a licensee about a fact material to lawfulness, and section 924(a)(1)(A) covered false statements in licensee records, carrying maximum terms of ten years and five years respectively.

Federal prosecutors had long identified the mismatch between the records offense charged and the conduct involved. That assessment, attributed to prosecutors, was structural. A records offense asks whether a statement was false and whether the fact misstated mattered to lawfulness. It does not ask, as an element, for whom the firearm was bought, what that person intended to do with it, or whether the buyer understood the firearm was destined for a felony.

The consequence ran in both directions. A prosecutor with strong evidence about destination and intended use held evidence the offense did not require, because the elements stopped at the counter and at the form. A prosecutor who could prove only the false statement could secure a conviction without proving destination or intended use at all. The law punished the lie uniformly whether the recipient feared paperwork or planned a felony. The grading of the offense bore no relation to the grading of the risk.

Section 12004, cited as the Stop Illegal Trafficking in Firearms Act, answered that disjunction by writing the conduct itself into chapter 44 of title 18. It added sections 932, 933, and 934 of title 18 in sequence. Section 932 addresses the straw purchase at acquisition. Section 933 addresses trafficking, the transfer and receipt of firearms under specified knowledge. Section 934 attaches forfeiture and a fine to a conviction under either offense, and companion amendments made sections 932 and 933 predicate offenses under other statutes. The center of the offense moved from the statement to the transaction and from the record to the recipient.

Section 932 makes it unlawful for any person to knowingly purchase, or conspire to purchase, any firearm in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce for, on behalf of, or at the request or demand of any other person, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that such other person falls within one of three paragraphs. Every phrase carries an element. The purchase, the mental state, the commerce connection, the relationship to another person, and the knowledge about that person must all be present. The absence of any one places conduct outside section 932, even where another provision of chapter 44 might apply.

The purchase element requires a knowing purchase of a firearm. The conspiracy language reaches an agreement to carry out such a purchase, joined with the mental states the section requires, without waiting for a completed acquisition. That reach matters because a straw purchase is rarely solitary. It presupposes a buyer who appears and another person for whom the buyer appears, and often arrangement and payment agreed in advance. By placing conspiracy inside the offense definition, section 932 keys preparatory agreement to the same purchase and the same knowledge that a completed violation requires.

The commerce element requires that the purchase be in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce. That language follows the pattern of chapter 44 and ties the offense to the jurisdictional basis on which the chapter rests. It also marks a boundary, because a purchase wholly outside that connection is not a section 932 offense. Its presence confirms that the provision amends an existing federal scheme rather than displacing state law for purely local transactions.

The substitution element requires that the firearm be bought for, on behalf of, or at the request or demand of any other person. A person who buys for himself or herself does not commit the offense on that ground alone. The buyer must have acted, at the point of purchase, as a stand in for another. The three alternatives cover arrangements from a purchase financed and directed by the other person to a purchase made as a favor at that person's asking. What unites them is displacement of the named buyer by an unnamed principal, the displacement the false statement provisions captured only indirectly through the answer given about being the actual transferee.

The knowledge element does the principal grading work. The purchase must be made knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the other person falls within one of three paragraphs. Actual knowledge satisfies the standard, and reasonable cause to believe satisfies it as well. That alternative reaches a buyer who received no explicit admission of status or intent but possessed facts from which status or intent could reasonably be inferred. A certainty standard would invite the defense that nothing was said aloud in transactions arranged in private between persons who know each other. Reasonable cause permits assessment of what the buyer saw, heard, and understood, while retaining a threshold above mere proximity or relationship.

Paragraph (1) addresses status. The other person must meet the criteria of one or more paragraphs of section 922(d) of title 18, which lists the categories of persons to whom a transfer is barred. The cross reference incorporates that list rather than restating it, borrowing disqualifications established elsewhere in chapter 44. The paragraph requires no proof of intended use and no proof of a planned felony. The recipient's barred status, the buyer's knowledge or reasonable cause to believe that status, and the purchase as a stand in complete the wrong, because the purchase defeats a transfer prohibition that would have operated directly had the recipient appeared in person.

Paragraph (2) addresses intended use. The other person must intend to use, carry, possess, or sell or otherwise dispose of the firearm in furtherance of a felony, a federal crime of terrorism, or a drug trafficking crime. Use, carrying, and possession describe the firearm in the recipient's hands. Sale or other disposition describes the firearm passed onward. Any one, joined to the specified furtherance, satisfies the paragraph. A buyer with reasonable cause to believe the person behind the purchase wants the firearm for a felony faces liability under paragraph (2) even where that person has no status based disability under paragraph (1). One paragraph turns on who the recipient is, the other on what the recipient means to do.

Paragraph (3) addresses onward transfer. The other person must intend to sell or otherwise dispose of the firearm to a person described in paragraph (1) or paragraph (2). The provision reaches a chain of two transfers contemplated at the moment of purchase. The immediate recipient may be neither barred nor intending a felony, and the purchase may be a section 932 offense where the buyer knows or has reasonable cause to believe that recipient intends to pass the firearm to a barred person or to a person intending the uses paragraph (2) describes. Without paragraph (3), a clean intermediary inserted between counter and ultimate recipient would defeat the first two paragraphs. Knowledge of the intended onward disposition is therefore an independent basis of liability.

Taken together, the three paragraphs show that section 932 is not a ban on buying a firearm for another person in the abstract. A purchase for a person fully eligible to receive it, where the buyer has no knowledge or reasonable cause of the kind described, falls outside the text, because the knowledge object is indispensable in every case. Congress combined status, intended use, and intended onward transfer as three routes to one conclusion, that the buyer knowingly served as the instrument by which a firearm would reach hands or purposes the law sought to keep apart from firearms. The false statement disappeared from the elements, and a section 932 case stands or falls on purchase, substitution, and knowledge.

The penalties under section 932(c) of title 18 confirm that design. Generally, a violation is punishable by a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years. That maximum is half again the 10 years under section 922(a)(6) and three times the 5 years under section 924(a)(1)(A). By setting the general term at 15 years for an offense requiring knowledge of the recipient's status or intended use, Congress graded the straw purchase as a serious felony in its own right, rather than as a regulatory misstatement aggravated by circumstance, and placed its exposure alongside the trafficking offense created in the same section of the act.

A second tier stands above the general tier. Where the violation is committed knowing or with reasonable cause to believe that a firearm involved will be used to commit a felony, a federal crime of terrorism, or a drug trafficking crime, imprisonment may extend to not more than 25 years. The enhancement uses the same alternative knowledge form as the underlying offense and tracks the gravest purposes in paragraph (2). Its effect divides straw purchases by the danger the buyer understood the firearm to present. A purchase for a barred person under paragraph (1), without proof of intended felony use, carries the 15 year maximum. A purchase made with the specified knowledge about future use carries the 25 year maximum. Severity follows the evidence about intended use rather than the accident of which form was falsified.

Section 933, the trafficking provision, begins where section 932 leaves off. Section 932 centers on a single acquisition made for another. Section 933 centers on movement, the disposition and receipt of firearms by persons who know or have reasonable cause to believe that the movement will place a firearm in hands whose use, carrying, possession, or receipt of it would constitute a felony. Its three paragraphs mirror the positions a participant can occupy. A person can send a firearm onward, a person can receive it, and a person can attempt or conspire to do either. A common knowledge standard applies across all three positions.

The first paragraph of section 933 makes it unlawful to ship, transport, transfer, cause to be transported, or otherwise dispose of any firearm to another person in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce, if the person knows or has reasonable cause to believe that the use, carrying, or possession of the firearm by the recipient would constitute a felony. Shipping and transporting describe movement across distance. Transfer and other disposition describe a change of possession or control. Causing a firearm to be transported reaches a person who arranges movement carried out by others, a role organizers characteristically occupy without handling the firearms themselves. The knowledge object differs from section 932. The question is whether the recipient's use, carrying, or possession would itself constitute a felony, a formulation that captures a recipient barred from possession altogether, for whom possessing is the felony.

The second paragraph makes it unlawful to receive a firearm from another person, if the recipient knows or has reasonable cause to believe that the receipt would constitute a felony. Trafficking requires a source and a destination, and a statute addressed only to transferors would leave the knowing recipient to other provisions whose elements differ from receipt in a trafficking chain. Section 933 treats receipt with the requisite knowledge as an offense of the same character as transfer with the requisite knowledge. The knowledge object is personal. The transferor must know or have reasonable cause to believe something about the recipient's use, carrying, or possession. The recipient must know or have reasonable cause to believe something about his or her own receipt. Each side is judged on awareness of the felonious character of the possession the transaction creates.

The third paragraph makes it unlawful to attempt or conspire to do either act. Attempt reaches a transfer or receipt begun and not completed. Conspiracy reaches the agreement to move a firearm to a recipient under the specified knowledge, before movement occurs. Together with the conspiracy language in section 932, the paragraph ensures the new offenses are not confined to completed transactions. Trafficking is organized conduct planned in arrangements that precede movement, and a definition attaching only upon delivery would require a transaction to reach its conclusion before its elements were complete. Attempt and conspiracy liability, keyed to the same knowledge a completed offense requires, permits the offense to attach at agreement and effort.

The penalty under section 933 is a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years. No higher tier comparable to the 25 year tier in section 932 appears, and the absence carries information about the two designs. Section 932 grades upward where knowledge of intended felony, terrorism, or drug trafficking use is an aggravating fact distinct from the knowledge making the purchase unlawful. Section 933 builds the felonious character of the resulting possession into its basic definition, since every violation requires knowledge or reasonable cause to believe that use, carrying, possession, or receipt would constitute a felony. Less grading work remains for a second tier. The single 15 year maximum, equal to the general maximum under section 932, places knowing movement into felonious possession well above the records offenses that preceded it.

A single course of conduct can implicate both sections without collapsing them into one. A purchase for a barred recipient violates section 932 at purchase, and a later transfer to that recipient, with knowledge or reasonable cause to believe possession would constitute a felony, implicates section 933. A participant who receives firearms knowing receipt would constitute a felony is reached by section 933 without any purchase. For prosecutors, two direct theories replace a single indirect theory built on a false statement, selectable according to a defendant's role and the evidence about what that defendant knew.

Section 934 supplies the financial consequences of a conviction under section 932 or section 933. A convicted person is subject to forfeiture of property constituting or derived from the proceeds of the offense, and of property used or intended to be used in it. Proceeds are what the offense produced. Property used or intended to be used is what the offense employed, the means by which firearms were bought, moved, or paid for. Forfeiture of proceeds strips the gain, and forfeiture of instrumentalities reaches the means. Section 934 adds a fine of up to twice the gross profits or other proceeds of the offense, tying financial punishment to the scale of an operation as reflected in its returns rather than to a fixed ceiling indifferent to scale.

The act also made violations of sections 932 and 933 predicate offenses under the federal wiretapping, racketeering, and money laundering statutes. Predicate status writes the new offenses into the lists on which three other bodies of federal law depend. For wiretapping, it bears on the offenses in connection with which that statute's procedures may be invoked. For racketeering, it places straw purchasing and trafficking among acts that can form part of the pattern that statute requires. For money laundering, it makes the proceeds of those offenses the kind of proceeds whose handling those statutes address. Congress connected the new offenses to provisions reserved for offenses capable of being committed as part of sustained, financed, and coordinated activity.

Against that structure, the character of section 12004 can be stated plainly. This is the prosecutors' provision, the least publicly discussed change in the statute. Provisions addressed to background checks, to dating partners, and to grants for state programs drew the attention during passage. The new offenses answered a complaint from the practice of prosecution itself. Federal prosecutors had long identified the mismatch between the records offense charged and the conduct involved, and had long worked where acquiring a firearm for a barred or dangerous recipient, with knowledge of that recipient and that purpose, was punishable principally as a lie on paperwork carrying 5 or 10 years. Section 932 names the purchase for another and grades it by status and intended use, at 15 years and at 25 years where intended felony use is known or reasonably believed. Section 933 names the movement and the receipt. Section 934 names the money. The gap was identified for decades by the officials charged with bringing these cases, defined by the distance between the offense on the books and the offense in the world.

For that reason, the new offenses may prove more consequential in practice than any provision that received attention at enactment. The claim rests on the structure of the text, on the conduct reached and the tools supplied. Attention at enactment fell on provisions operating at the perimeter of the transfer system, adjusting who must be checked or what records must be consulted. Sections 932 and 933 operate at the center of the illicit market the transfer system exists in part to frustrate, where eligible buyers lend eligibility to ineligible recipients and intermediaries move firearms toward felonious possession for profit. Before June 2022, that market was reachable federally only through a false statement, with penalties that did not distinguish a buyer for a barred recipient intending a felony from a buyer who misstated a lesser fact. The act made that market the subject of two defined offenses, three knowledge objects, two penalty tiers for purchasing, forfeiture, a fine at twice the gross profits, and predicate status under three statutes. Confined to the hedge the text supports, the prosecutors' provision may outlast the attention other provisions commanded, because it was written for the cases prosecutors had been bringing, in the form in which those cases arise.

## The Dating Partner Extension

The dating partner extension operates inside a prohibition that predates the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act by more than two decades. Section 922(g)(9) of title 18 makes it unlawful for a person who has been convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence to engage in the conduct that provision covers, and section 922(d)(9) of title 18 supplies the companion bar on the transfer side, making it unlawful for any person to sell or otherwise dispose of a firearm or ammunition to a person who falls within the same category. The two provisions work as a pair. One addresses the person under the disability, and the other addresses anyone who would transfer a firearm or ammunition to that person. Section 12005 of the act leaves the structure of section 922(g)(9) and section 922(d)(9) intact and instead amends the definition at section 921(a)(33) of title 18, the definition that determines which misdemeanor convictions count as a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence for purposes of the chapter.

The category itself dates from 1996. Before the act, the definition at section 921(a)(33) reached offenses against a defined set of persons. It covered a spouse or former spouse of the victim. It covered a person who shared a child with the victim. It covered a cohabitant or former cohabitant of the victim. It covered a parent or guardian of the victim. It also covered a person similarly situated to a spouse, parent, or guardian of the victim. Each element of that list describes a relationship marked either by a formal legal bond, by a shared household, by a shared parental tie, or by a position of care and authority over the victim. The relationship element did independent work, and the boundaries of the list therefore determined the boundaries of the prohibition that rested on it.

Dating partners as such stood outside that list. A person in a romantic or intimate relationship with the victim, who had never married the victim, who shared no child with the victim, who had never cohabited with the victim, and who stood in no parental or guardian relationship to the victim, was not brought within the definition by the dating relationship alone. The relationship might have been serious, and it might have lasted for a considerable period, but the pre-amendment definition supplied no category that captured it on those facts. Any qualifying conviction had to be fitted to one of the listed relationships, and where none of those relationships existed, the conviction did not count under section 921(a)(33), however the underlying offense was characterized under the law of the jurisdiction that entered it. The Congressional Research Service records that this omission "has been referred to as the boyfriend loophole". That sentence states the provenance of the label and nothing more.

Section 12005(a)(1) closed that space by a targeted insertion into the existing definition. The amendment inserted into section 921(a)(33)(A)(ii) offenses committed "by a person who has a current or recent former dating relationship with the victim". The placement of the insertion matters. Congress did not create a separate prohibition for dating partners, and it did not enact a freestanding offense. It expanded one clause of the definition that already supplied the relationship element for the misdemeanor crime of domestic violence category, so that the expanded definition flows into every provision that uses that defined term. The consequence is that a qualifying misdemeanor conviction involving a dating partner is treated through the same machinery as a qualifying conviction involving a spouse, a former spouse, a cohabitant, a co-parent, a parent, a guardian, or a person similarly situated. Section 922(g)(9) supplies the disability, section 922(d)(9) supplies the transfer bar, and section 921(a)(33) supplies the class of convictions to which both apply. The amendment changed the third of those elements and left the first two to operate on the enlarged class.

That design also means the extension inherits the limits built into the underlying category. The conviction must be a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence within the meaning of the definition as amended. The extension does not reach conduct that produced no conviction, because the defined category is a category of convictions. What it changes is whose relationship counts. After the amendment, the dating relationship described in the statute counts alongside the relationships that the definition had listed since before the act, and a conviction that satisfies the rest of the definition is a qualifying conviction by reason of that relationship.

An insertion of that brevity required a definition of its own, because the phrase dating relationship does not carry a self-executing boundary. Section 12005(a)(2) therefore added a new definition at section 921(a)(37) of title 18. Under that definition, a dating relationship is a relationship between individuals who have or have recently had a continuing serious relationship of a romantic or intimate nature. The definition then directs how qualification is determined. Whether a relationship qualifies is determined by considering the length of the relationship, the nature of the relationship, and the frequency and type of interaction between the individuals involved. The definition is built in two layers. The first layer states the character of relationship that counts, using the combined qualifiers of continuity, seriousness, and a romantic or intimate nature. The second layer states the considerations by which a particular relationship is measured against that character.

Each consideration performs distinct work within that assessment. The length of the relationship addresses duration. Length alone does not decide the question, because the definition joins duration to the other considerations and to the threshold description of a continuing serious relationship. A long association of a nonromantic character would not satisfy the definition by duration alone, and duration is weighed as part of a combined judgment rather than as a fixed minimum period. The statute sets no number of days, weeks, or months that a relationship must have lasted.

The nature of the relationship addresses character. This consideration directs attention to what kind of relationship the individuals had, measured against the statutory description of a romantic or intimate nature. It is the consideration that connects the factual inquiry to the first layer of the definition, because the nature of the relationship is where the romantic or intimate character is established or fails to be established. The consideration also bears on seriousness, since the character of a relationship is part of what distinguishes a serious relationship from a slight one. As with length, the statute supplies no catalog of conduct or declarations that establish the required nature, and it assigns the assessment to the circumstances of each relationship rather than to a prescribed form.

The frequency and type of interaction between the individuals addresses contact. Frequency concerns how often the individuals interacted, and type concerns the form those interactions took. Together they give the definition an evidentiary footing in observable dealings between the two persons, rather than leaving qualification to rest on a characterization offered by one side. The consideration is written in the conjunctive, so both the regularity of contact and the character of the contact enter the inquiry. Frequent interaction of a kind unrelated to a romantic or intimate relationship carries different weight than interaction of a kind that reflects such a relationship, and the definition requires both dimensions to be considered rather than treating a count of contacts as decisive.

Read together, the three considerations produce a case-by-case determination. The definition states a standard and identifies the considerations that govern its application, and it leaves the application of that standard to the facts of each relationship. No single consideration is made dispositive. No numerical threshold is set for any of them. No relationship is brought within the definition by a formal status, in the way that marriage brings a spouse within the older categories without inquiry into the quality of the relationship, and no relationship is placed outside the definition by the absence of a formal status, which was the defect in the prior list that the amendment addressed. The inquiry under section 921(a)(37) asks whether the relationship, viewed through its length, its nature, and the frequency and type of interaction between the individuals, amounts to a continuing serious relationship of a romantic or intimate nature. The contrast with the older categories is structural. The spousal, cohabitant, parental, and guardian categories turn on statuses that can ordinarily be established as discrete facts. The dating relationship category turns on an assessment of a relationship against a described character, guided by the three stated considerations.

### Does the dating partner prohibition reach convictions from before the act?

The amendments do not reach convictions entered before June 25, 2022. Section 12005(b) limits the dating partner extension to convictions entered on or after the date of enactment, so a qualifying offense against a dating partner that produced an earlier conviction does not create a disqualification under section 922(g)(9).

Section 12005(b) states the prospectivity rule directly. The amendments made by section 12005 do not apply to any conviction entered before June 25, 2022, the date the act was signed and became Public Law 117-159. The operative date is the date the conviction was entered, and the rule turns on that date rather than on the date of the underlying conduct, the date of the relationship, or the date on which a question about the conviction later arises. A conviction entered before the statutory date is outside the amendments even if the offense involved a relationship that would satisfy section 921(a)(37) in every other respect. A conviction entered on or after that date is within the amendments if the relationship element and the remaining requirements of the definition are met.

The choice matters to the provision's reach in several connected ways. First, it confines the extension to a forward-looking operation. The enlarged definition governs convictions entered after enactment, and the prohibition at section 922(g)(9), as it applies through the dating partner category, attaches to persons by reason of those later convictions. Second, it preserves the legal treatment that earlier convictions received under the definition as it stood when they were entered. A dating partner conviction entered before June 25, 2022 was not a qualifying conviction under the prior definition, and section 12005(b) leaves that position undisturbed rather than recharacterizing the conviction after the fact.

Prospectivity also interacts with the restoration provision described below. Because no conviction entered before June 25, 2022 can rest on the dating partner category, every disqualification that rests on that category arises from a conviction entered on or after that date. The five-year restoration clock in section 921(a)(33)(C) therefore runs, for dating partner cases, from judgments and sentences that are themselves post-enactment events. Entry into the dating partner disability is confined to post-enactment convictions, and exit from that disability, for a person who meets the statutory conditions, is provided by a restoration mechanism written into the same section of the act that created the entry route.

The restoration provision sits at section 921(a)(33)(C) of title 18, added by section 12005(c)(2). Its mechanism is a change in legal treatment rather than a removal of the conviction. A person whose disqualification rests on a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence against a dating partner, and who meets the conditions the provision states, is no longer treated as convicted for purposes of the disability once the statutory period has elapsed. The conviction itself is not described as erased, and the provision does not depend on any action by the jurisdiction that entered the conviction. The federal definition ceases to treat the person as convicted once the period has run and the conditions are satisfied, which removes the foundation on which section 922(g)(9) and section 922(d)(9) operate in that person's case.

The period is five years, and its starting point is fixed by a later-of rule. The five years run from whichever is later, the judgment of conviction or the completion of any custodial or supervisory sentence. The rule ensures that the clock does not run during a period in which the person remains subject to a custodial or supervisory sentence imposed for the conviction, because the starting point is deferred until that sentence is completed where completion falls after judgment. The five years are counted from the later anchor rather than from the offense, the relationship, or the enactment of the act. Only after the full five years have elapsed from that anchor does the change in treatment take effect, and it takes effect by operation of the provision for a person who satisfies its conditions.

The conditions narrow the class that may use this route. The person's disqualification must rest on a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence against a dating partner. The person must have no other such conviction, so the route is confined to a person with a single dating partner conviction of that character. The person must also not be otherwise prohibited, meaning that restoration under this provision addresses the dating partner disqualification and does not displace any independent basis on which the person might be barred under another paragraph of section 922(g) or under section 922(n). A person who carries a separate disqualification gains no relief from that separate ground through this provision, because the change in treatment applies to the conviction within the provision's scope and leaves every other ground of prohibition where it stood. The provision is thus limited in the relief it confers, since it resolves one disqualification and no other.

Against those conditions stand the bars. Restoration is unavailable to a person subsequently convicted of an offense involving the use or attempted use of physical force. Restoration is unavailable to a person subsequently convicted of an offense involving the threatened use of a deadly weapon. Restoration is also unavailable to a person subsequently convicted of any other offense that disqualifies under section 922(g) or section 922(n) of title 18. The bars are framed by reference to a subsequent conviction, so the event that defeats restoration is itself a conviction, and the categories of defeating conviction reach both offenses defined by violent conduct or threatened violent conduct and offenses that carry their own disqualifying effect under the chapter. Five elapsed years are necessary for restoration under the provision, and they are not sufficient where a barring conviction intervenes.

The scope of the restoration route is as deliberate on its exclusion side as on its inclusion side. Restoration under section 921(a)(33)(C) is not extended to persons whose offenses were against spouses, former spouses, cohabitants, or the other categories covered before the act. A person whose qualifying conviction rests on one of the pre-amendment relationships receives no benefit from the five-year mechanism, however much time has passed and whatever the person's later record may show. The route exists only for the category the act added. That asymmetry is a feature of the enacted text, because the provision that creates the route identifies its beneficiaries by the dating partner character of the offense, and the categories covered before the act are outside that identification. The result is that two persons with qualifying misdemeanor convictions may stand in different positions under the definition years later, depending on which relationship category supplied the predicate for the conviction. The person whose predicate was a dating partner offense has a statutory route to ceasing to be treated as convicted after five years on the stated conditions. The person whose predicate was a spousal, former spousal, or cohabitant offense has no corresponding route in this provision.

Two further elements complete the restoration design. The act requires that records accessible to the background check system be updated to reflect a restoration. That requirement connects the legal change in treatment to the records through which eligibility is ordinarily determined, so that a person who has ceased to be treated as convicted is not left carrying the disqualification in the records consulted when a transfer is proposed. A change in legal status that the checking records did not reflect would leave the practical burden of the disability in place after its legal foundation had ended, and the updating duty addresses that gap within the act's own design. Alongside that duty, the ordinary relief applies. The treatment of convictions that have been expunged or set aside, of convictions for which the person has been pardoned, and of convictions where civil rights have been restored, continues under the existing framework of the definition. The five-year provision is an additional route layered into that framework for the dating partner category. It does not replace the older forms of relief, and it does not restrict them.

Taken as a whole, section 12005 is a study in amendment by definition. The prohibition and the transfer bar were left in place. The category of misdemeanor crime of domestic violence, in place since 1996, was enlarged at the relationship element by a single inserted clause. A new definition at section 921(a)(37) gave that clause its content, through a threshold description of a continuing serious relationship of a romantic or intimate nature and through three considerations that govern case-by-case qualification. A prospectivity clause confined the enlargement to convictions entered on or after June 25, 2022. A restoration clause then provided the one category added by the act, and that category alone, with a route out of the disability after five years measured from the later of judgment or completion of sentence, subject to conditions and bars that the same clause specifies, with records to be updated when restoration occurs and with the older forms of relief preserved beside it.

## Enhanced Checks for Young Buyers and the Dealing Definition

Two provisions of Title II of the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act changed who the federal firearms system examines, and for how long, before a transfer may proceed, and changed the terms on which a person who sells firearms repeatedly must hold a federal license. Section 12001, titled Juvenile Records, altered the background check for one defined class of buyer by directing the checking system toward records that the ordinary check did not routinely reach. Section 12002 altered the statutory definition of a dealer by replacing one qualifying phrase with another and by supplying a definition for the substituted phrase. The two provisions operate at different points in the same statutory scheme in chapter 44 of title 18. The first governs the interval between a prospective transfer by a licensee and a determination of the buyer's eligibility. The second governs the threshold question of whether a seller belongs inside the licensed system at all, with the recordkeeping and checking duties that follow from licensed status. Each provision is narrow in the class it addresses, and each works by amending language that predated the act rather than by creating a freestanding regulatory structure.

The starting point for section 12001 is the ordinary background check that applies when a licensee transfers a firearm. Under the ordinary rule in section 922(t) of title 18, a licensee contacts the national instant criminal background check system before completing a covered transfer, and the system makes a determination whether receipt of the firearm by the prospective transferee would violate federal or state law. Where the system has not returned a final determination within three business days, the ordinary rule permits the licensee to proceed with the transfer at its discretion. That permission is a feature of the statutory design rather than an irregularity in it. The three business day period functions as a limit on how long the ordinary process may hold a transfer in suspense without a determination, and once that period runs without a final answer, the choice whether to proceed rests with the licensee. Section 12001 leaves that general structure in place for buyers generally and narrows the default for one class of buyers by adding inquiries that must occur and by allowing a longer period for those inquiries to be completed where further investigation is required.

The class affected by section 12001 is defined by age. The provision addresses a prospective transferee under 21 years of age, and in practice that class consists of buyers aged 18 to 20. The reason the practical class takes that shape lies in the separate age rules that govern licensees. A licensee may not sell a handgun to a person under 21 years of age, and a licensee may not sell a long gun to a person under 18 years of age. A buyer under 18 therefore does not reach a licensee's counter for any firearm within those rules, and a buyer aged 21 or older falls outside the age band that section 12001 names. The enhanced check accordingly operates in the space the age rules leave open, namely transfers of long guns to buyers aged 18, 19, and 20, together with any other transfer to a buyer in that age band that the licensee age rules permit. The provision does not change the age rules themselves. It takes those rules as given and attaches an additional checking procedure to the transfers that remain lawful for buyers in the defined band.

For a buyer in that band, section 12001 requires the national instant criminal background check system to contact three sources immediately, and each contact is made in the jurisdiction where the buyer resides. The first contact is with the pertinent state, tribal, or territorial repositories of criminal history record information and juvenile justice information for that jurisdiction. The second contact is with the state, tribal, or territorial custodians of mental health adjudication records for that jurisdiction. The third contact is with local law enforcement agencies in that jurisdiction. The residence of the buyer fixes the geography of all three inquiries, so the provision directs attention to the records held where the buyer lives rather than to repositories chosen by the location of the sale. The statute assigns the duty to the checking system, and the Attorney General was directed to upgrade the system to make these contacts. The design therefore works through the same system that conducts the ordinary check, expanded for this class of buyer to reach three additional categories of record holder as a required part of the process.

### How long can a check for a young buyer take?

For a buyer aged 18 to 20, the determination may take up to 10 business days where further investigation is required. The ordinary three business day period under section 922(t) of title 18 does not control in that circumstance, because section 12001 extends the time allowed for the enhanced check to be completed.

The three required contacts reflect the kind of information the enhanced check is built to surface. Juvenile records occupy a distinct place in that design because an adjudication entered while a buyer was a juvenile, or a mental health determination recorded in juvenile proceedings, may bear on eligibility in ways that the repositories consulted in an ordinary adult records check would not necessarily reveal. Before enactment, such records were not routinely queried in a federal firearms background check. Section 12001 responds to that gap in the records actually consulted, rather than to the categories of persons prohibited from receiving firearms, by requiring the system to go to the repositories and custodians who hold juvenile justice information and mental health adjudication records. The state, tribal, or territorial repository is the holder of criminal history and juvenile justice information, the custodian is the holder of mental health adjudication records, and the local law enforcement agency is a further source within the buyer's home jurisdiction. The provision treats those three sources as distinct, and the system's duty runs to each of them, so satisfaction of the requirement does not rest on a single inquiry standing in for the others.

The contacts are paired with a substantive bar. The act bars the transfer of a firearm to a person whose juvenile records contain a potentially disqualifying adjudication or mental health determination. The wording of the bar matters. It attaches to what the juvenile records contain, and it is framed around a potentially disqualifying adjudication or determination, which is the category the three contacts are designed to bring to the system's attention. In that sense the contact requirement and the transfer bar are two halves of one mechanism. The contacts supply the information, and the bar gives that information a consequence for the transfer when the records contain material of the specified kind. Without the required contacts, records held only in juvenile repositories might never enter the determination at all, and a bar addressed to their contents would have little practical reach. With the contacts required, the determination for a buyer aged 18 to 20 rests on a wider set of sources than the determination for an older buyer, even though both determinations run through the same national system and both end in the same question whether the transfer may lawfully proceed.

Timing completes the mechanism. Where further investigation is required, the eligibility determination for a buyer in the covered age band may take up to 10 business days. That period displaces, for this class and in that circumstance, the ordinary three business day point at which a licensee could otherwise proceed at its discretion without a final determination. The extension is tied to the need for further investigation, so it is not an open ended postponement of every transfer to a younger buyer. A determination that resolves without further investigation follows the ordinary course, while a determination that requires more inquiry receives the longer statutory allowance. The distance between three business days and 10 business days measures the additional room the statute gives the system to pursue the juvenile and mental health records that the enhanced check adds. The contact requirement itself carries an express limit in time. The requirement that the system contact these authorities expires 10 years after enactment, on September 30, 2032. That sunset is written into the act as statutory text, so the enhanced contact duty has a defined end date on the face of the provision, separate from the rest of the background check structure that section 12001 amended. The provision as enacted is therefore a bounded design in two respects, bounded by the age of the buyer at the front end and by the September 30, 2032 expiry at the back end, and it states a records procedure and a transfer bar without addressing matters outside that enacted design.

Section 12002 turns from the buyer to the seller, and from the conduct of a single check to the definition that decides whether a person selling firearms must be licensed. The relevant definition appears at section 921(a)(21)(C) of title 18, which defines a dealer in firearms. Before the act amended it, that definition described a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business through the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms, qualified by the phrase "with the principal objective of livelihood and profit". Each element of that sentence does work. The person must devote time, attention, and labor to the activity, the activity must amount to a regular course of trade or business, and the means must be the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms. Repetition and regularity therefore stood in the definition from the start, and section 12002 did not introduce them. What the act changed was the qualifying phrase that described the objective with which the repetitive purchase and resale had to be carried on. The act struck the phrase "with the principal objective of livelihood and profit" from section 921(a)(21)(C). In its place the act substituted the phrase "to predominantly earn a profit". Both phrases are the statute's own words, and the difference between them is the substance of the amendment. The struck phrase joined profit to livelihood as the principal objective of the dealing. The substituted phrase speaks only of earning a profit, and qualifies that earning with the word predominantly rather than with a reference to livelihood.

Because the substituted phrase introduced a term that required explanation, the act also supplied a definition for it. New section 921(a)(22) of title 18 defines the phrase by reference to intent. Under that definition, the intent underlying the sale or disposition is predominantly one of obtaining pecuniary gain, as opposed to other intents, such as improving or liquidating a personal firearms collection. The definition therefore directs attention to the purpose behind the repetitive selling rather than to any single sale viewed in isolation. A seller's intent is characterized as predominantly one of obtaining pecuniary gain when gain is the prevailing purpose of the activity, measured against other purposes that might explain the same transactions. The statutory contrast gives that measurement its content. Improving a personal firearms collection is an intent directed at the collection itself, and liquidating a personal firearms collection is an intent directed at ending or reducing a holding assembled as a collection. Neither intent is, in the terms of the definition, predominantly an intent to obtain pecuniary gain, even though a sale made to improve or liquidate a collection may produce money for the seller. Section 921(a)(22) carries that distinction into the licensure structure by excepting transfers made to improve or liquidate a personal collection from the licensure requirement. The exception and the definition state the same boundary from opposite directions. The definition identifies the profit predominant intent that brings a repetitive seller within the dealer definition, and the exception confirms that collection directed transfers stand outside that requirement.

### Who must obtain a dealer license after the act?

A person who buys and resells firearms repetitively and predominantly for profit must hold a federal firearms license. Licensure carries recordkeeping duties and a duty to run background checks on transfers. Occasional sellers and persons improving or liquidating a genuine personal collection remain outside the licensure requirement described in the statute.

The consequence of falling within the amended definition is licensure, and licensure carries defined duties. A person whose repetitive purchase and resale of firearms is carried on to predominantly earn a profit must obtain a federal firearms license, and with that license come the recordkeeping duties that attach to licensees and the duty to run background checks on transfers. Those duties are the practical difference between a seller inside the definition and a seller outside it. A licensee keeps the records the licensed system requires and submits transfers to the same background check process that section 12001 amends for younger buyers, so the two provisions meet at the licensee's counter. The Congressional Research Service described the effect of the amendment in those terms, noting that persons who buy and resell firearms repetitively and predominantly for profit must hold a license with the duties that attach to licensees, and that some intrastate private transfers by such persons are brought inside those duties. That last point follows from the structure of the amendment. A seller who crosses the definitional threshold does not escape licensure by keeping sales within one state, because the obligation attaches to the person's status as a dealer under the definition rather than to the route a particular firearm travels.

The boundary of the amendment is as much a part of its meaning as its reach. Occasional sales remain outside the dealer definition, because the definition requires a regular course of trade or business carried on through repetitive purchase and resale, and an occasional sale displays neither the repetition nor the regularity the definition demands. Genuine collecting also remains outside, because section 921(a)(22) measures intent and places collection directed intent on the far side of the line from a predominant intent to obtain pecuniary gain, and because transfers made to improve or liquidate a personal collection are excepted from the licensure requirement. The amendment also leaves the general structure of private transfers untouched in a specific respect. The act does not require a background check for private transfers between unlicensed persons as such, and the federal check continues to attach to transfers by licensees. Section 12002 reaches private selling only by way of the definition, where a person's repetitive selling predominantly for profit makes that person a dealer who must be licensed. A seller who remains genuinely unlicensed, because the selling is occasional or collection directed, is not converted into a licensee by section 12002, and transfers by such a seller are not subjected to the licensee check merely because a firearm changes hands. The provision thus works entirely through classification. It redraws the line that separates dealing from other selling, and the duties follow the classification rather than attaching to transfers in general.

The distinction between the struck phrase and the substituted phrase also changes how the definition reads as a whole. Under the struck wording, the stated objective joined livelihood to profit, so the definition described the dealing in terms that connected the profit sought to the seller's means of support. The substituted wording contains no reference to livelihood, and asks instead whether the repetitive purchase and resale is carried on to predominantly earn a profit. The word predominantly supplies a comparison among purposes rather than a threshold measured in hours worked or in any share of income stated in the definition, and section 921(a)(22) then gives that comparison its content in terms of intent. Read together, the two paragraphs divide the definitional work between them. Section 921(a)(21)(C) identifies the activity, a regular course of trade or business pursued through repetitive purchase and resale, while section 921(a)(22) characterizes the purpose that must predominate within that activity. Neither paragraph dispenses with repetition or regularity, and neither treats a single profitable sale, standing alone, as the measure of dealing.

Read together, sections 12001 and 12002 adjust the licensed transfer system at its two ends without rebuilding it. Section 12001 accepts the existing check and lengthens and deepens it for buyers aged 18 to 20, adding three required contacts in the buyer's home jurisdiction, barring transfer where juvenile records contain a potentially disqualifying adjudication or mental health determination, allowing up to 10 business days where further investigation is required, and setting the contact requirement to expire on September 30, 2032. Section 12002 accepts the existing dealer definition and rewrites its qualifying phrase, striking "with the principal objective of livelihood and profit" and substituting "to predominantly earn a profit", then defines that phrase in section 921(a)(22) by a predominant intent to obtain pecuniary gain contrasted with the intents behind improving or liquidating a personal collection. In each provision the operative change is expressed in a small number of statutory words, and in each provision those words determine a practical outcome, whether a transfer waits, whether a transfer is barred, or whether a seller must hold a license and perform the duties of a licensee. The precision of the drafting is therefore not incidental to the provisions. The age band, the three named sources, the 10 business day allowance, the date of expiry, the struck phrase, the substituted phrase, and the intent definition are the provisions, stated in the terms the act itself uses.

## The Funding: Crisis Intervention, Mental Health, and Schools

The funding in the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act has a structure distinct from the amendments to title 18 of the United States Code, and that structure determines what the money does. The authorizing provisions in Division A describe programs, duties, guidance, reviews, and standards. The money itself sits principally in Division B, which appropriates through existing grant programs in three departmental titles covering the Department of Justice, the Department of Health and Human Services, and the Department of Education. Division B does not create a single consolidated fund from which all of the activities described in the act are paid. Each appropriation is tied to a stated purpose, a stated administering component, and a stated period of availability, and it moves through a program that existed before the act. The result is a funding architecture built department by department and program by program.

That architecture has several consequences. The recipient of an appropriation matters as much as the dollar figure, because money appropriated to the Office of Justice Programs is not interchangeable with money appropriated to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, and neither is interchangeable with money for grant programs administered through the Department of Education. The period of availability also matters. Many amounts are provided in equal annual amounts for fiscal years 2022 through 2026, spreading the funding across five fiscal years rather than making the full amount available at enactment. Other amounts are available through a stated date, such as September 30, 2025, or September 30, 2026, and one amount for the Federal Bureau of Investigation is to remain available until expended.

Section 12003 supplies the clearest example of the connection between an authorization in Division A and an appropriation in Division B. Section 12003 does not create a freestanding crisis intervention grant program with its own separate statutory home. It adds a ninth authorized purpose to an existing program, the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program, known as the Byrne JAG program and codified at sections 10151 and following of title 34 of the United States Code. The new ninth purpose is state crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs or initiatives. By placing the purpose inside the Byrne JAG program, the act makes crisis intervention an activity that the existing justice assistance grant structure may support, and Division B then provides money for that purpose as one component of the Office of Justice Programs appropriation. The authorization identifies what may be funded, the appropriation provides the amount, and the existing program provides the channel through which the funding moves to states.

The act describes the included proceedings and programs by their statutory terms, without characterization. The ninth purpose may include drug courts, mental health courts, veterans treatment courts, and extreme risk protection order programs. Those four examples are not interchangeable terms for the same proceeding. They are distinct examples within a broader authorized purpose, listed together because each falls within the category of court proceedings and related programs or initiatives that the new purpose covers. The act does not convert the examples into a requirement that a state operate any one of them.

For extreme risk protection order programs, section 12003 does more than name a category that may receive funding. The act sets statutory standards that a program of that kind must meet to be funded, framed principally in terms of due process rights, adjudicative fairness, representation, evidentiary standards, and penalties for abuse. The due process component includes, at a minimum, the right to an in-person hearing, the right to an unbiased adjudicator, the right to know opposing evidence, the right to present evidence, and the right to confront adverse witnesses. Each element addresses a feature of the proceeding itself. The hearing right specifies an in-person hearing. The adjudicator right specifies that the decision maker be unbiased. The evidence rights operate in both directions, covering knowledge of opposing evidence, the ability to present evidence, and the ability to confront adverse witnesses. Together, those provisions define procedural conditions that attach to funding, stated in the act as minimums that a funded program must satisfy.

Three further standards complete the framework. The act provides for the right to be represented by counsel. It requires evidentiary standards at least equal to those applying to a similarly situated litigant in federal or state court. It also provides for penalties for abuse of the programs. The counsel provision addresses representation of a person subject to the proceeding. The evidentiary provision sets a comparative floor, measured against the standards that would apply to a similarly situated litigant in federal or state court, rather than setting a separate evidentiary code inside section 12003 itself. The abuse provision addresses misuse of the program through penalties. Read together with the due process provisions, the standards form the conditions that distinguish a program eligible for funding under the act from a program bearing the same general description but not meeting the conditions the act attaches to its money. The standards are funding standards. They define what the federal grant may support when a state chooses to operate such a program.

### Did the act require states to set up extreme risk protection order programs?

No. The act offered federal grant funding to states that operated qualifying crisis intervention programs, including extreme risk protection order programs meeting statutory standards. It added that purpose to an existing grant program and appropriated money for it. No provision required any state to create such a program or court proceeding.

The distinction between funding a program and requiring a program is structural. The act funds state programs through grants, and the crisis intervention purpose is written as an authorized purpose of a grant program. Nothing in section 12003 directs a state to establish a crisis intervention court proceeding, a drug court, a mental health court, a veterans treatment court, or an extreme risk protection order program. The standards govern programs funded under the act. A state that operates a qualifying program may receive support through the grant structure. A state that does not operate such a program is not ordered by the act to create one, and the appropriation does not itself create a state proceeding in any jurisdiction.

The Department of Justice package in title I of Division B is the part of the funding most directly connected to section 12003, to the background check provisions, and to school security grants administered through justice programs. Its largest component is an appropriation of 1.4 billion dollars to the Office of Justice Programs, provided in equal amounts of 280 million dollars for each of fiscal years 2022 through 2026. The appropriation has four components, each with its own purpose and annual amount. The first and largest component is 750 million dollars for crisis intervention programs under the Byrne program, provided at 150 million dollars per year and allocated on a modified version of the Byrne formula. That component is the Division B counterpart to section 12003. The authorization adds crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs or initiatives as the ninth Byrne JAG purpose, and the appropriation supplies 750 million dollars for crisis intervention programs under that program across the five fiscal years, using a modified form of the formula by which Byrne funding is allocated.

The remaining three components of the Office of Justice Programs appropriation address subjects that sit alongside crisis intervention within the justice funding package. The second component is 250 million dollars for a community violence intervention and prevention initiative, provided at 50 million dollars per year for fiscal years 2022 through 2026. The third component is 200 million dollars for grants under the STOP School Violence Act of 2018, provided at 40 million dollars per year. The fourth component is 200 million dollars for grants to upgrade criminal and mental health records for the background check system, also provided at 40 million dollars per year. The four components sum to the 1.4 billion dollar total, and the annual amounts sum to the 280 million dollars provided for each fiscal year. The records component has a direct relationship to the background check provisions elsewhere in the act, because the act pairs its changes to check procedures with grant funding directed at upgrading criminal and mental health records for the background check system.

Two further Department of Justice amounts sit outside the Office of Justice Programs appropriation but inside the departmental package. A further 100 million dollars went to school security grants administered by the Community Oriented Policing Services office, provided at 20 million dollars per year for fiscal years 2022 through 2026. That amount is separate from the 200 million dollars for grants under the STOP School Violence Act of 2018 within the Office of Justice Programs appropriation, so school related funding appears in the Justice title through two distinct channels. A further 100 million dollars went to the Federal Bureau of Investigation, to remain available until expended, for the resource needs of the background check system. The availability language for the Federal Bureau of Investigation amount differs from the equal annual installment pattern used for the other Justice amounts, because money that remains available until expended is not divided by the act into stated annual installments. The Congressional Research Service describes the act's Department of Justice grant funding as 1.5 billion dollars in total, a description that captures the grant funding across the departmental package, including the Office of Justice Programs appropriation and the Community Oriented Policing Services school security grants.

The Department of Health and Human Services package in title II of Division B funds the mental health and related health services side of the act, and its principal appropriation is directed to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration. The act provides 800 million dollars to that administration, available through September 30, 2025, with five stated components. The largest component is 250 million dollars for the Community Mental Health Services Block Grant. The second component is 240 million dollars for Project AWARE, identified in the act with school mental health awareness and connection to services. The third component is 150 million dollars for the National Suicide Prevention Lifeline for fiscal year 2022. The fourth component is 120 million dollars for mental health awareness training. The fifth component is 40 million dollars for the National Child Traumatic Stress Initiative. The five components sum to the 800 million dollar total, distributing it across a block grant, a school focused program, a lifeline appropriation for a single fiscal year, training, and an initiative addressing traumatic stress in children.

A further Health and Human Services amount of 190 million dollars, available through September 30, 2026, sits alongside the 800 million dollars for the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration and has three components. The first component is 80 million dollars for pediatric mental health care access. The second component is 60 million dollars for primary care training and enhancement. The third component is 50 million dollars for grants to states supporting school-based health services under section 11003, for fiscal year 2022. The third component creates a direct financial link between Division B and Division A, because section 11003 directs updated guidance on school-based services under Medicaid, establishes a technical assistance center, and authorizes the 50 million dollars in state grants that the Health and Human Services title then provides. The pediatric mental health care access component likewise corresponds to a subject addressed on the authorizing side, where section 11005 adds requirements to the pediatric mental health care access grant program administered by the Health Resources and Services Administration.

The Department of Education package completes the three departmental titles of Division B, and it has two parts, one directed at mental health professionals in schools and one described as school improvement funding. The first part provides 1 billion dollars at 200 million dollars per year for fiscal years 2022 through 2026, divided equally between two grant programs. School-Based Mental Health Services Grants receive 500 million dollars, and Mental Health Services Professional Demonstration Grants receive 500 million dollars. Both programs are directed at increasing the number of mental health professionals providing services in schools with demonstrated need. The equal division is exact, with each program receiving half of the 1 billion dollar total. The stated objective is framed in terms of the number of professionals providing services, and the stated targeting criterion is schools with demonstrated need. The Education title does not create a general education fund for any school purpose a recipient might choose. It appropriates amounts for named grant programs with a stated direction, and the money moves through those programs.

The second part of the Education package is 1.05 billion dollars in school improvement funding, available through September 30, 2025. It has two components. The first is 1 billion dollars for Student Support and Academic Enrichment Grants. The second is 50 million dollars for the 21st Century Community Learning Centers program. The availability period for this part differs from the annual installment structure of the school mental health grant funding in the same package, because the 1.05 billion dollars is stated as an amount available through September 30, 2025, rather than as equal amounts for each of fiscal years 2022 through 2026.

Behind the Division B appropriations sits the authorizing side in Title I of Division A, titled Children and Family Mental Health Services and comprising sections 11001 to 11005. Those five sections do not themselves carry the large appropriations described in Division B, but they state the program provisions to which several of the appropriations correspond, and they add guidance, review, and program requirements that operate independently of a particular grant amount. Section 11001 addresses the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic demonstration under the Protecting Access to Medicare Act of 2014. It extends the demonstration for the 10 participating states and expands the demonstration to additional states. The provision therefore works in two directions, continuing a demonstration in the states already participating in it and opening the demonstration beyond that initial group.

Section 11002 requires guidance and technical assistance to states on delivering telehealth services under Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program. Its instruments are guidance and technical assistance rather than a grant amount stated in the section itself. Section 11003 directs updated guidance on school-based services under Medicaid, establishes a technical assistance center, and authorizes the 50 million dollars in state grants noted in the Health and Human Services funding. Section 11004 requires a review of state implementation of the Medicaid Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment requirements, a review function directed at how states have implemented an existing set of requirements under Medicaid. Section 11005 adds requirements to the pediatric mental health care access grant program administered by the Health Resources and Services Administration.

Title III of Division A, titled Other Matters, contains the provisions that supply the act's principal budgetary offset, together with a Medicare fund amount and the school safety clearinghouse provisions. Section 13101 extends the moratorium on implementation of the Medicare Part D prescription drug rebate rule to January 1, 2027. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the delay would save 20.9 billion dollars from fiscal years 2022 to 2027, making it the act's principal budgetary offset. Section 13201 sets the amount available in the Medicare Improvement Fund at 7.5 billion dollars for services furnished during and after fiscal year 2022.

Sections 13301 to 13305 contain the federal school safety clearinghouse provisions, in a subtitle cited in the act as the Luke and Alex School Safety Act of 2022. Those sections require the Secretary of Homeland Security to establish a Federal Clearinghouse on School Safety Evidence-Based Practices, published through SchoolSafety.gov. The clearinghouse provisions also carry notification duties, a requirement for a review of federal grant programs within a year of enactment, and rules of construction preserving federal civil rights requirements and the existing bar on federal direction of curriculum and instruction. The clearinghouse is an information and coordination mechanism rather than a grant program.

The funding, considered as a whole, moves by grant, formula and competitive, to states and localities that choose to operate qualifying programs. Some amounts move by formula, as the crisis intervention component does through allocation on a modified version of the Byrne formula, and other amounts move through the grant structures of the named programs in each departmental title. The recipients are principally states and localities, reached through the existing programs that Division B uses as its channels, including the Byrne JAG program, the Community Mental Health Services Block Grant, the school mental health grant programs, and the other named grant programs in the Justice, Health and Human Services, and Education titles. The act does not use the appropriations to run the funded activities as direct federal operations in place of state and local programs, and no state is required to establish a crisis intervention program. The money is offered through grants, on the terms each program and each funding standard states, to jurisdictions that operate programs meeting those terms. That grant based structure is the common feature beneath the departmental totals, the annual installments, the availability dates, and the program provisions in Title I of Division A.

## What the Act Did Not Do, and the Dispute Over Its Significance

A statute profile records omissions with the same care that it records operative provisions, because the boundary of a text is part of the text. Public Law 117-159 changed defined portions of chapter 44 of title 18 and funded defined programs through defined grant authorities, and the provisions it did not contain mark the limits of every change it made. A reader who attends only to the sections that were enacted has an incomplete account of the enactment, and a reader who treats an omission as an implied provision has an inaccurate one. The omissions are therefore stated exactly, as textual facts drawn from the act itself, and each is developed in prose before any dispute about significance is considered. What that absence signifies is a separate question, disputed during the passage period by the legislators who voted on the measure and by commentators who measured the measure against different baselines.

The proposals debated during the passage of the act included measures on each of the subjects treated below, stated here in general attributed terms. A category regulation, a limit on the capacity of ammunition feeding devices, a general background check requirement for private transfers, a federal licensing or registration system for owners, and a mandate that states adopt crisis intervention programs all figured in the debates of the passage period as proposals advanced by legislators who sought them. None of those proposals appears in the enacted text, which instead contains the narrower provisions treated earlier in the profile. The distance between a proposal debated and a provision enacted is the distance the dispute over significance would later measure, and stating the omissions first, in the plain language of textual absence, keeps that distance visible without adopting the vocabulary of either side in the debate that produced it.

### Did the act ban any category of firearm?

No. The act contains no provision regulating any category of firearm, no definition or prohibition directed at semiautomatic rifles or any other class, and no revival of the 1994 federal assault weapons ban, which expired in 2004 after its enactment in the preceding decade.

The absence is categorical rather than partial. A prohibition ordinarily requires a definition that identifies the regulated object, and the act supplies no such definition for any category, whether a class is identified by its characteristics, its configuration, or its place in a statutory list. The provisions of Title II of Division A operate through other mechanisms entirely. They amend the definition of a dating relationship and the prohibition that follows from a qualifying conviction, they require additional contacts in the background check conducted for a prospective transferee under 21 years of age, they revise the definition of a person engaged in the business of dealing in firearms, they create offenses directed at straw purchasing and trafficking in sections 932 and 933 of title 18, and they authorize a grant purpose for state crisis intervention programs. Each of those provisions regulates conduct, status, or procedure. None regulates a category of firearm as such, and the difference between regulating the conduct surrounding a transfer and regulating the object transferred is a difference in the structure of the law that the text maintains throughout.

The treatment of the expired ban follows the same pattern. The 1994 federal assault weapons ban expired in 2004, and the act contains no provision reviving it, reenacting its terms, extending its period, or replacing it with a successor prohibition drawn on similar lines. A reader searching the act for a schedule of prohibited firearms, a list of characteristics that would bring a firearm within a prohibition, or a clause restoring the expired ban to force will find none of those elements, because the act was written without them. The omission is a complete absence on a subject the passage debates addressed directly, and it is recorded here in those terms and in no stronger terms.

A second omission concerns the capacity of ammunition feeding devices. The act contains no limit on that capacity, expressed in the fact of the text as the absence of any magazine limit. No section fixes a maximum number of rounds for a device, defines a device by reference to a capacity threshold, prohibits the manufacture or transfer of a device above a stated capacity, or attaches a penalty to possession of such a device. The proposals debated during the passage of the act included limits of that kind, proposed by legislators who sought a federal capacity limit, and the enacted text contains no corresponding provision. The absence extends across the whole statute, through the firearms title and through the appropriations in Division B alike, because neither an operative prohibition nor a funded program in the act is organized around the capacity of an ammunition feeding device. The act regulates identified conduct by identified persons under identified conditions, and the capacity of a feeding device is not one of the conditions it identifies.

The third omission concerns background checks for private transfers, and it requires the most precise statement, because the act did change the law governing who must hold a license, and a change in the licensing definition changes the set of transfers to which a background check attaches without creating a general check requirement for private transfers as such. The act does not require a background check for private transfers between unlicensed persons as such. The federal check continues to attach to transfers by licensees, and a transfer between two persons neither of whom holds a federal firearms license, where neither person is required to hold one, carries no federal background check requirement under the act merely by reason of the transfer itself. An account that describes the act as requiring checks on private transfers generally has overstated the text, just as an account that describes the licensing amendment as having no effect on private transfers at all has understated it.

The precise boundary is drawn by section 12002 and the amendment to the definition of a dealer in firearms at section 921(a)(21)(C) of title 18. Before the amendment, that definition reached a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business through the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms, with the principal objective of livelihood and profit. The act struck the qualifying phrase concerning livelihood and profit and substituted a standard expressed as engaging in that activity to predominantly earn a profit. Section 921(a)(22) of title 18 then defines the substituted term by the intent underlying the sale or disposition, which must be predominantly one of obtaining pecuniary gain, as opposed to other intents such as improving or liquidating a personal firearms collection, and transfers made to improve or liquidate a personal collection are excepted from the licensure requirement. The effect, stated at Congressional Research Service precision, is that persons who buy and resell firearms repetitively and predominantly for profit must hold a federal firearms license, with the recordkeeping and background check duties that attach to licensees, and some intrastate private transfers by such persons are brought inside those duties. Occasional sales and genuine collecting remain outside the requirement.

The distinction the amendment draws is therefore a distinction about the seller, rather than a distinction about the transfer in the abstract. A person whose repetitive selling is predominantly for profit crosses the licensing line and, as a licensee, conducts transfers under the duties that attach to licensees, including the background check duty. A person whose sales are occasional, or whose dispositions improve or liquidate a personal collection, remains an unlicensed person, and transfers by that person to another unlicensed person are not subjected by the act to a check requirement as such. The proposals debated during the passage of the act included a general requirement that private transfers between unlicensed persons pass through a background check, advanced by legislators who sought that requirement in general form, and the enacted text adopted the narrower route of redefining the class of persons who must be licensed. A general requirement attaches the check to the transaction regardless of the character of the seller, while the enacted amendment attaches licensee duties to a defined class of sellers and leaves transfers outside that class under the prior rule.

The fourth omission concerns the owners of firearms rather than their transfer. The act creates no federal licensing system for firearm owners and no federal registration system for firearm owners, and it creates no federal registry of firearms or of owners. No section requires an owner to obtain a federal license as a condition of ownership, no section requires an owner to register a firearm with a federal authority, and no provision establishes a federal record system organized around owners or their firearms of the kind a registry would require. The licensing provisions the act does amend concern dealers, the persons engaged in the business of dealing in firearms under section 921(a)(21)(C) of title 18, and that dealer licensing framework predates the act and serves the recordkeeping and background check duties that attach to licensed transfers. Extending a licensing concept from dealers to owners, or building a registry from the records licensees keep, were measures included among the proposals debated during the passage of the act by legislators who sought them, and the enacted text contains neither measure. A system that licenses the commercial seller is a different legal arrangement from a system that licenses or registers the owner, and the act contains the first arrangement in amended form and the second arrangement not at all.

The fifth omission concerns the states and the crisis intervention programs that section 12003 addresses through funding rather than through command. The act does not require any state to adopt a crisis intervention program, and it does not require any state to establish an extreme risk protection order program. Section 12003 instead adds a ninth authorized purpose to the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program, at sections 10151 and following of title 34, covering state crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs or initiatives, which may include drug courts, mental health courts, veterans treatment courts, and extreme risk protection order programs. Division B then funds that purpose, with 750 million dollars for crisis intervention programs under the Byrne program as part of the Department of Justice funding, allocated across fiscal years 2022 through 2026. The mechanism is a grant offered to states that operate qualifying programs, and a state that operates no such program is not directed by the act to create one, penalized by the act for its absence, or subjected by the act to a federal program administered in its place.

The terms attached to funded programs confirm the character of the mechanism. For extreme risk protection order programs funded under the act, the statutory standards include due process rights with, at a minimum, the right to an in-person hearing, the right to an unbiased adjudicator, the right to know opposing evidence, the right to present evidence, and the right to confront adverse witnesses, together with the right to be represented by counsel, evidentiary standards at least equal to those applying to a similarly situated litigant in federal or state court, and penalties for abuse of the programs. Those standards govern programs that receive the funding. They are conditions on a grant, written into the statute as standards for the funded activity, and a condition on funding that a state may accept or decline differs in law from a mandate that a state must obey. The proposals debated during the passage of the act included measures that would have pressed states toward adoption in stronger forms, and the enacted text chose the grant mechanism with the standards that accompany it. The omission of a mandate is the other half of that textual choice, funding without compulsion.

Taken together, the five omissions define the outer boundary of the enactment with a precision the operative provisions alone cannot supply. The act contains no category regulation and no revival of the expired ban, no limit on the capacity of ammunition feeding devices, no general background check requirement for private transfers between unlicensed persons as such, no federal licensing or registration of owners and no registry, and no mandate that any state adopt a crisis intervention program. Each of those subjects was present in the proposals debated during the passage period, so each absence marks a point at which the enacted text stopped short of a measure placed before the Congress that enacted it. The omissions are the subjects on which the difference between proposal and enactment was widest.

The dispute over the significance of the act begins at exactly that point, and it is carried here with both baselines stated and with no verdict given to either. The first baseline measures the act against the preceding period. Against nearly three decades without a comparable enactment, the statute is substantial, because the comparison is with an interval in which no comparable measure passed both chambers. The act is the first federal statute making significant changes to firearms law since the 1990s, following the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act of 1993 and the 1994 federal assault weapons ban as the preceding comparable enactments. New offenses in sections 932 to 934 of title 18, a new category within the domestic violence prohibition, an enhanced check for buyers under 21 years of age with a stated expiry on September 30, 2032, a revised dealer definition, and funded programs across three departments are, against that baseline, the first significant change of their kind in a generation of federal lawmaking. Supporters in Congress, including its Senate negotiators, described and defended the act on that footing, as a meaningful and overdue step, and voted for it on that basis.

The second baseline measures the same text against the proposals debated during its passage, and against that measure the statute is modest. The proposals included the category regulation, the magazine limits, and the general private transfer check requirements the enacted text omits, together with the licensing and mandate measures treated above, and a text that omits each of the principal measures debated will appear limited when the debated measures supply the standard. Legislators and commentators who had sought the omitted measures said so during the passage period, describing the act as insufficient against the aims they had stated. The two descriptions do not contradict each other on any question of textual fact. Both could point to the same sections, the same omissions, and the same votes.

A third position stood apart from both baselines, because it opposed the act itself rather than its size relative to a comparison. Opponents of the act in the roll calls argued against it on constitutional and federalism grounds, among others, contending in general terms that its provisions exceeded the proper federal role or burdened rights the Constitution protects, and that the funding mechanism for state programs drew the federal government into matters the states should govern. Those arguments were directed at what the act contains rather than at what it omits.

The analytical conclusion the record supports is a conclusion about baselines. The assessment changes with the baseline, and stating the baseline is more useful than a verdict. Against the preceding period, the act registers as the first significant change to federal firearms law since the 1990s, and its supporters defended it in those terms. Against the proposals debated during its passage, the same act registers as a measure that omitted the principal proposals placed before that Congress, and those who had sought those proposals assessed it in those terms. Neither assessment alters a section of the text, and neither is refuted by the other, because each answers the question it set for itself. A reader given both baselines, stated plainly and attributed to their holders, has the material from which an assessment can be formed.

A short word on method closes the section. The operative text, rather than the label attached to an enactment, is where the effect of a statute lives, and a profile statute rewards the reader who weighs its technical provisions with the same attention given to its headline provisions. The headline provisions are readily summarized, an extension of a prohibition to a new class of relationship, an enhanced check for a defined age group, funding for state programs and for school and community mental health services. The technical provisions carry as much of the law. A substituted phrase in a dealer definition determines which sellers must be licensed and therefore which transfers pass through a check. A definition of a dating relationship, built from the length of the relationship, the nature of the relationship, and the frequency and type of interaction between the individuals, determines whether a conviction disqualifies. New offenses in sections 932 and 933 of title 18, with their knowledge standards, their penalty tiers, and the forfeiture provision in section 934, determine what conduct federal law punishes and on what proof. A reader who weighs those provisions against the omissions and the baselines stated above has weighed the statute itself.

Measured against the preceding period, the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act registers as the first federal statute making significant changes to firearms law since the 1990s. That baseline compares the enactment with an interval of nearly three decades in which no comparable measure passed both chambers, following the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act of 1993 and the 1994 federal assault weapons ban as the preceding comparable enactments. Against that comparison, the new offenses in sections 932 to 934 of title 18, the dating partner category added to the domestic violence definition, the enhanced check for buyers under 21 with an expiry on September 30, 2032, the revised dealer definition, and the funding across three departments represent a change in federal law after a long interval without a comparable statute. Supporters in Congress, including its Senate negotiators, described the act as a meaningful and overdue step and voted for it on that basis.

Measured against the proposals debated during its passage, the same text registers differently. That second baseline compares the enactment with the category regulation, magazine limits, and general private transfer check requirements that featured in the debate, together with proposals for owner licensing or registration and for measures pressing states toward adoption of crisis intervention programs. Against that comparison, a text containing none of those provisions presents a narrower measure than the proposals supplied as the standard. Legislators and commentators who had sought the omitted measures described the act as insufficient against the aims they had stated. Opponents of the act in the roll calls argued against it on constitutional and federalism grounds, among others, directed at provisions the act contains. Each position rests on a stated comparison or a stated ground, and the two baselines answer different questions, so neither description alters the sections, omissions, or votes that compose the record.

The provisions themselves support a reading centered on mechanics rather than on scale. A purchase made for another person under stated knowledge conditions became a defined offense graded at 15 years, and at 25 years where knowledge of intended felony, terrorism, or drug trafficking use was present. A transfer or receipt tied to felonious possession became a defined offense at 15 years, with forfeiture and a fine measured by gross profits after conviction. A dating relationship, assessed through length, nature, and the frequency and type of interaction, became a qualifying relationship for a prohibition that had rested on spousal, parental, cohabitant, and similar categories since 1996, confined to convictions entered on or after June 25, 2022 and paired with a five year restoration route confined to the new category. A check for a defined age group gained three required contacts and a 10 business day allowance. A dealer definition turned on a substituted phrase concerning profit. Funding moved by grant through programs in three departments, with standards attached to funded extreme risk protection order programs and with no mandate addressed to any state.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act actually change?

The act created sections 932, 933, and 934 of title 18, establishing straw purchasing and trafficking offenses with forfeiture and enhanced fines. Section 12005 extended the misdemeanor crime of domestic violence definition behind section 922(g)(9) to dating partners for convictions entered on or after June 25, 2022, with restoration after five years for a single qualifying conviction. Section 12001 required enhanced contacts for buyers aged 18 to 20 and allowed up to 10 business days where further investigation was required. Section 12002 replaced the livelihood and profit test with a predominantly earn a profit standard for dealer licensure. Section 12003 and Division B funded crisis intervention, records, mental health, and school programs through grants.

### Q: What is the boyfriend loophole the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act closed?

The label in this question is the shorthand used in public debate for the omission of dating partners from the prior definition. Before the act, section 921(a)(33) reached offenses against a spouse or former spouse, a person sharing a child with the victim, a cohabitant or former cohabitant, a parent or guardian, or a person similarly situated, and dating partners as such stood outside the definition. Section 12005 inserted offenses by a person in a dating relationship with the victim into that definition, for convictions entered on or after June 25, 2022. The extension therefore brought qualifying misdemeanor convictions against dating partners within section 922(g)(9) and section 922(d)(9).

### Q: What checks apply to buyers under 21 in the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

For a prospective transferee under 21 years of age, in practice a buyer aged 18 to 20, section 12001 requires the national instant criminal background check system to contact immediately three sources in the jurisdiction where the buyer resides. Those sources are the pertinent state, tribal, or territorial repositories of criminal history record information and juvenile justice information, the state, tribal, or territorial custodians of mental health adjudication records, and local law enforcement agencies. The act bars a transfer where juvenile records contain a potentially disqualifying adjudication or mental health determination. Where further investigation is required, the determination may take up to 10 business days. The contact requirement expires on September 30, 2032.

### Q: Did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act create a trafficking crime?

Section 12004 added section 933 of title 18, creating a freestanding trafficking offense. The provision makes it unlawful to ship, transport, transfer, cause to be transported, or otherwise dispose of a firearm to another person in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the use, carrying, or possession by the recipient would constitute a felony. It also makes it unlawful to receive a firearm knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the receipt would constitute a felony, and it reaches attempt and conspiracy. The penalty is a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years, with forfeiture and enhanced fines under section 934 after conviction.

### Q: What does the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act say about engaged in the business?

Section 12002 amended section 921(a)(21)(C) of title 18, which defines a dealer in firearms as a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to dealing in firearms as a regular course of trade or business through the repetitive purchase and resale of firearms. The act struck the phrase "with the principal objective of livelihood and profit" and substituted "to predominantly earn a profit". New section 921(a)(22) defines that phrase by intent, predominantly one of obtaining pecuniary gain, as opposed to other intents such as improving or liquidating a personal firearms collection. Transfers made to improve or liquidate a personal collection are excepted, and occasional sales remain outside the definition.

### Q: Did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act fund red flag laws?

The act funded state crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs through the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program, adding that purpose in section 12003 and appropriating 750 million dollars in Division B across fiscal years 2022 through 2026. States may use that funding for extreme risk protection order programs meeting the standards in the act, including due process rights, representation by counsel, evidentiary standards, and penalties for abuse. The label "red flag" is the label in common use for such programs, attributed to users of that label. The act offered the funding by grant and required no state to establish a crisis intervention or extreme risk protection order program.

### Q: What did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act not include?

The enacted text contains no provision regulating any category of firearm, no definition or prohibition directed at semiautomatic rifles or any other class, and no revival of the 1994 federal assault weapons ban that expired in 2004. The act contains no limit on the capacity of ammunition feeding devices. The act does not require a background check for private transfers between unlicensed persons as such, because the federal check attaches to transfers by licensees and the dealer definition reaches only repetitive sellers acting predominantly for profit. The act creates no federal licensing or registration system for firearm owners and no federal registry of firearms or owners, and it requires no state to adopt a crisis intervention program.

### Q: How did Congress vote on the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

The Senate acted on June 23, 2022, and the House acted on June 24, 2022. Senate cloture on the motion to concur was agreed to by 65 to 34, with one senator not voting. Senate passage, recorded as concurrence in the House amendment with an amendment, was 65 to 33, with 50 members of the Democratic caucus and 15 Republicans voting in favor and 33 Republicans voting against. House passage was 234 to 193, with all 220 Democrats and 14 Republicans voting in favor and 193 Republicans voting against, and no Democrat voting against the measure in either chamber. President Joseph R. Biden signed the measure on June 25, 2022.

### Q: What is the public law number and Statutes at Large citation of the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

The act is Public Law 117-159, cited at 136 Stat. 1313. It was enacted by the 117th Congress as S.2938, a bill introduced to designate a federal courthouse in Tallahassee, Florida, with the substantive text offered as Senate Amendment 5099 to the House amendment to that bill and released on June 21, 2022. Sections 1 and 2 of the enacted act retain those designation provisions, including the post office designation in Petaluma, California. President Joseph R. Biden signed the measure on June 25, 2022. Division A contains the authorizing and amending provisions in sections 11001 to 13401, and Division B contains appropriations for three departments.

### Q: What are the penalties for straw purchasing under the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

Section 932(c) of title 18 provides a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years for a straw purchasing violation. Where the violation is committed knowing or with reasonable cause to believe that a firearm involved will be used to commit a felony, a federal crime of terrorism, or a drug trafficking crime, imprisonment may extend to not more than 25 years. A person convicted under section 932 is also subject under section 934 to forfeiture of property constituting or derived from the proceeds of the offense and of property used or intended to be used in the offense, and to a fine of up to twice the gross profits or other proceeds of the offense.

### Q: What conduct counts as firearms trafficking under the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

Section 933 of title 18 covers three forms of conduct. A person commits the offense by shipping, transporting, transferring, causing to be transported, or otherwise disposing of a firearm to another person in or otherwise affecting interstate or foreign commerce, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the use, carrying, or possession by the recipient would constitute a felony. A person also commits the offense by receiving a firearm from another person, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the receipt would constitute a felony. Attempt and conspiracy to do either act are also covered. The penalty is a fine and imprisonment for not more than 15 years.

### Q: What is a dating relationship under the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

Section 921(a)(37) of title 18, added by section 12005(a)(2), defines a dating relationship as a relationship between individuals who have or have recently had a continuing serious relationship of a romantic or intimate nature. Whether a relationship qualifies is determined by considering three matters: the length of the relationship, the nature of the relationship, and the frequency and type of interaction between the individuals. The definition sets no fixed period and makes no single consideration dispositive. Qualification therefore depends on an assessment of the relationship against the statutory description, using duration, character, and the regularity and form of contact between the individuals as the stated considerations in each case.

### Q: When does a dating partner conviction stop disqualifying a person under the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

Under section 921(a)(33)(C) of title 18, a person whose disqualification rests on a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence against a dating partner stops being treated as convicted once five years have elapsed after whichever is later, the judgment of conviction or the completion of any custodial or supervisory sentence. The route applies only where the person has no other such conviction and is not otherwise prohibited. Restoration is unavailable to a person subsequently convicted of an offense involving the use or attempted use of physical force, the threatened use of a deadly weapon, or any other offense that disqualifies under section 922(g) or section 922(n). Records accessible to the background check system must be updated to reflect a restoration.

### Q: How long can the enhanced background check delay a transfer under the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act?

Section 12001 allows the eligibility determination for a buyer under 21 years of age to take up to 10 business days where further investigation is required. That allowance applies to the enhanced check, under which the national system contacts juvenile justice repositories, mental health adjudication custodians, and local law enforcement agencies in the jurisdiction where the buyer resides. The ordinary rule under section 922(t) of title 18 permits a licensee to proceed with a transfer at discretion after three business days without a final determination, and the 10 business day allowance displaces that point for this class of buyer in the circumstance the provision states. The contact requirement expires on September 30, 2032.

### Q: How much did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act appropriate for state crisis intervention programs?

Division B appropriated 750 million dollars for crisis intervention programs under the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program, as part of 1.4 billion dollars provided to the Office of Justice Programs across fiscal years 2022 through 2026. The crisis intervention amount was provided at 150 million dollars per year and allocated on a modified version of the Byrne formula. Section 12003 supplied the authorization by adding state crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs or initiatives as a ninth authorized purpose of the Byrne program, a purpose that may include drug courts, mental health courts, veterans treatment courts, and extreme risk protection order programs meeting the statutory standards.

### Q: What mental health programs did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act fund?

Division B provided 800 million dollars to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, available through September 30, 2025. That amount comprised 250 million dollars for the Community Mental Health Services Block Grant, 240 million dollars for Project AWARE, 150 million dollars for the National Suicide Prevention Lifeline for fiscal year 2022, 120 million dollars for mental health awareness training, and 40 million dollars for the National Child Traumatic Stress Initiative. A further 190 million dollars supported pediatric mental health care access, primary care training and enhancement, and grants to states supporting school-based health services under section 11003. Division A Title I also extended the Certified Community Behavioral Health Clinic demonstration and addressed telehealth, Medicaid, and pediatric access programs.

### Q: Who negotiated the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act in the Senate?

Four senators led the negotiations in the Senate. Chris Murphy of Connecticut and John Cornyn of Texas stood at the head of the group, with Kyrsten Sinema of Arizona and Thom Tillis of North Carolina completing the group of four lead negotiators. Two of the lead negotiators were Democrats and two were Republicans. The framework the negotiations produced was announced on June 12, 2022 by a group of 20 senators, divided evenly with 10 Democrats and 10 Republicans. Legislative text followed on June 21, 2022 as Senate Amendment 5099 to the House amendment to S.2938, and the Senate and House votes followed on June 23 and June 24, 2022.

### Q: What did the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act provide for school safety?

The act provided 200 million dollars for grants under the STOP School Violence Act of 2018 and 100 million dollars for school security grants administered by the Community Oriented Policing Services office, each across fiscal years 2022 through 2026. Sections 13301 to 13305 required the Secretary of Homeland Security to establish a Federal Clearinghouse on School Safety Evidence-Based Practices, published through SchoolSafety.gov, with notification duties and a review of federal grant programs within a year of enactment. Section 13401 prohibited use of Elementary and Secondary Education Act funds to provide a dangerous weapon or training in the use of a dangerous weapon. School improvement funding in Division B added 1 billion dollars for Student Support and Academic Enrichment Grants.

### Q: Does the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act require states to adopt crisis intervention programs?

No provision requires a state to adopt a crisis intervention program or an extreme risk protection order program. Section 12003 added state crisis intervention court proceedings and related programs as an authorized purpose of the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant program, and Division B funded that purpose with 750 million dollars across fiscal years 2022 through 2026. The mechanism is a grant offered to states that operate qualifying programs. Funded extreme risk protection order programs must meet statutory standards concerning due process, counsel, evidentiary standards, and penalties for abuse. Those standards govern funded programs, and a state operating no such program is not directed by the act to create one.

### Q: What should a student know about the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act for an exam?

A student should know the citation Public Law 117-159, 136 Stat. 1313, signed on June 25, 2022, and the structure of two divisions, with firearms provisions in sections 12001 to 12005. The principal provisions are the straw purchasing offense in section 932 of title 18, the trafficking offense in section 933, forfeiture in section 934, the dating partner extension in section 12005 for convictions entered on or after June 25, 2022, the enhanced check for buyers aged 18 to 20 expiring on September 30, 2032, and the predominantly earn a profit dealer standard in section 12002. A student should also know the appropriations for crisis intervention, mental health, and schools, and the omissions concerning category regulation, magazine limits, and general private transfer checks.
