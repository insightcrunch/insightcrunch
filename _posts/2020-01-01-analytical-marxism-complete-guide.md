Analytical Marxism is a name that many readers first meet as an accusation, flung at a group of scholars who were said to have kept the vocabulary of Marxism while surrendering its substance to the methods of the seminar room and the economics department. The accusation gets the founding intention exactly backwards. The philosophers, economists, political scientists and sociologists who began meeting in 1979 were defenders of Marxism, several of them committed socialists, who believed that Marxist claims deserved the compliment of being tested by the same standards of clarity and evidence that governed any other serious inquiry. They held that a claim which could not survive plain statement had not been protected by its obscurity but exposed by it. What gives the school its enduring fascination is the sequel: within roughly fifteen years, most of those defenders had publicly abandoned central Marxist theses, one by one, in print, with reasons given at every step. A school that set out to rescue a doctrine and ended by dismantling much of it poses a question that no summary can evade, and this guide is organised around that question rather than around the safer ground of chronology alone.

![Analytical Marxism seminar table with the trajectory table and the founding texts of the September Group, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-108.webp)

The guide proceeds by separating questions that hostile and admiring accounts habitually fuse. One question concerns the method: what the members committed themselves to when they denied that any distinctive Marxist logic exists and insisted that every valid argument be statable in ordinary terms. A second question concerns the contributions: the defence of historical materialism, the reconstruction of Marx's social theory, the formalisation of exploitation, and the empirical study of class, each of which can be weighed on its merits independently of the school's later history. A third question concerns the trajectory itself, the sequence of abandonments between 1979 and the middle of the following decade, and what that sequence proves. On the third question this guide takes a position that both camps in the literature resist: the trajectory is undecidable, because a history produced by honest inquiry following arguments wherever they lead and a history produced by a method quietly rigged against its object would look identical from outside. Stating that undecidability plainly, and stating what evidence would break it, is more informative than either of the confident readings on offer.

Readers arriving from neighbouring subjects will find the school's fingerprints in unexpected places, since its ideas travelled further outside Marxism than inside it. The demand that functional claims be backed by mechanisms reshaped debates in the philosophy of social science. A class schema built for Marxist purposes became a routine instrument of comparative stratification research. A definition of exploitation framed through property relations entered economic theory on its own merits. Those uptakes matter for assessment as well as for intellectual history, because they show that the school's standards could generate durable work even where its founding doctrine was being surrendered. The sections that follow take the founding and the method first, then the contributions one by one, then the trajectory and its two readings, then the hostile reception at full strength, and finally the survivals and the reading route, so that a reader who finishes the guide holds the whole arc and can judge each segment separately.

## Analytical Marxism and the founding moment

A reader encountering the label for the first time often meets it attached to a complaint, as if clarity itself were a betrayal of a tradition that preferred prophetic sweep to careful statement. That first impression reverses the order in which events unfolded. The scholars who formed the current in 1979 did not gather to bury a doctrine from outside, nor to mock commitments they had never held. They gathered as defenders, convinced that claims associated with Marx deserved the same treatment accorded to any serious position in a university seminar room, a treatment involving definition, distinction, counterexample, and a willingness to follow an inference wherever it led. Their loyalty took an unusual form. Instead of protecting favoured conclusions behind a specialised vocabulary, they proposed to expose those conclusions to ordinary tests of coherence and evidence, and to keep only what survived.

Such a proposal carried risk precisely because the defenders meant it. A test that cannot be failed protects nothing worth calling a belief, and a method that guarantees agreement in advance merely rehearses allegiance. Participants understood that distinction from the outset. If historical materialism were true, its truth ought to be visible when its central propositions were separated, stated without ornament, and compared against rival explanations of social change. If parts failed, intellectual honesty required saying which parts, and how badly, and what repair remained possible. Defence and criticism therefore belonged to a single activity rather than to opposed camps. One defended a tradition by refusing to exempt it from scrutiny, and one criticised it most usefully by first reconstructing its strongest available version.

### What is analytical Marxism?

a current formed in 1979 which applied the standards of analytical philosophy and mainstream social science to Marxist claims, holding that there is no distinctive Marxist method and that valid arguments must be statable clearly. Its members began as defenders and most later abandoned central Marxist theses.

Behind that compact definition lies a quarrel about intellectual manners that had been building through the nineteen seventies. Much writing in the Marxist tradition of that period relied on dialectical formulations whose logical shape shifted under pressure, so that an objection aimed at one reading could be deflected by sliding toward another. Continental influences then fashionable in parts of the left rewarded suggestive juxtaposition over explicit inference, and a paragraph could acquire reputation for depth while leaving a patient reader unable to say what would count against it. Against that background, insistence on plain statement was not pedantry. It was a demand that an author accept responsibility for a determinate claim, with determinate implications, open to determinate refutation.

Philosophers trained in the analytical tradition brought habits formed by close reading of arguments in ethics, metaphysics, and philosophy of science. Economists brought models in which assumptions had to be declared before results could be assessed. Political scientists and sociologists brought comparative evidence, institutional detail, and a suspicion of explanations that invoked vast historical forces without specifying mechanisms connecting cause to effect. None of these habits belonged exclusively to any political camp. Their combination in one discussion circle mattered because each discipline supplied a different way of embarrassing a vague thesis. A philosopher might ask what a key term meant. An economist might ask whether rational agents placed as described would behave as predicted. A sociologist might ask whether the pattern alleged actually appeared across cases.

The founding moment therefore deserves description as a convergence rather than as a conversion. Separate scholars, working in separate fields, discovered that they shared dissatisfaction with a protective style of theorising and shared admiration for a single demanding example of how Marxist theory might be written differently. Meetings gave that private agreement a durable social form. A group that returns to unfinished arguments year after year develops memory, and memory raises standards, because a participant cannot quietly abandon a concession made during a previous session or revive a distinction already shown to collapse. Continuity turned individual scruples into collective discipline.

Defence, in this setting, acquired a meaning sharply different from apologetics. Apologetics begins with a verdict and searches for premises able to support it. The analytical defenders began instead with texts and problems: the account of historical development in the writings of Marx, the explanation of class power, the relation between economic structure and political form, the justification of egalitarian norms. Each problem invited reconstruction before judgement. Reconstruction itself was an act of respect, since it assumed that earlier writers had been attempting to answer genuine questions and that their answers deserved better than either reverent repetition or dismissive parody. Yet respect stopped short of immunity. A reconstructed argument stood or fell by reasons available to any competent reader, not by lineage.

That stance explains why later departures from central theses should not be read backward as evidence of bad faith at the start. Trajectories diverged because inquiry was permitted to have consequences. Some participants revised a thesis while retaining neighbouring commitments. Others concluded that a favoured explanation lacked support, or that a moral ideal required foundations different from those they had expected to find. A school that begins by subordinating loyalty to argument must expect its members to end in different places, and the spread of outcomes can testify to seriousness rather than to incoherence. Unity lay in procedure and in a shared set of questions, not in a creed recited at the door.

Geography reinforced procedure in quiet ways. Members worked in Britain, Norway, and the United States, inside institutions with distinct academic cultures and distinct political surroundings. A claim that sounded persuasive within one national conversation had to travel, stripped of local allusion, into rooms where its presuppositions were less familiar. Travel favoured explicitness. So did disciplinary difference, since a term carrying settled meaning for economists might carry none for philosophers, and a distinction obvious to a moral philosopher might appear arbitrary to a historian of institutions. Misunderstanding, when surfaced early, became a diagnostic tool. It showed where an argument depended on tacit background that its author had mistaken for logic.

There was also a generational element to the founding, though participants differed in age and seniority. Postwar expansion of universities had produced scholars for whom Marxist ideas formed part of serious academic debate rather than merely party instruction or street polemic. Journals, conferences, and graduate seminars provided settings where a thesis could be examined apart from immediate organisational allegiance. The group exploited those settings while also creating a more intimate one of its own. In a small recurring meeting, a speaker could not hide behind the scale of a conference or the politeness of strangers. Interlocutors returned, remembered, and pressed.

Opponents sometimes treated clarity as a kind of reduction, fearing that a theory translated into numbered premises would lose historical richness, dialectical movement, or critical intent. Members answered that translation was a test, not a replacement for every other form of writing. A rich narrative might generate insight, but insight claiming truth had to survive restatement in terms that exposed its commitments. If restatement proved impossible, suspicion fell on the alleged insight rather than on the demand for clarity. If restatement succeeded, the theory gained something valuable: opponents could finally locate disagreement, supporters could identify which premise carried the weight, and further research could target the vulnerable joint instead of circling the whole edifice.

Founding in 1979 thus names more than a date on a calendar. It names a decision about how a tradition would be inherited. Inheritance could mean custody, guarding texts against contamination, or it could mean use, putting inherited claims to work under conditions their originators never faced. The September circle chose use. That choice made the inheritance harder to preserve intact, and deliberately so, because a claim put to work can break. It also made the inheritance intellectually alive. Questions about exploitation, class, history, and justice remained urgent even when inherited answers weakened, and the discipline of the group consisted in refusing to confuse urgency of a question with reliability of a received reply.

Another feature of the founding deserves emphasis, because it separated the circle from study groups organised around a charismatic teacher. Authority inside discussion rested on the quality of an objection or a distinction, not on seniority, publication record, or closeness to any founder. A younger participant who spotted an equivocation could halt a line of reasoning advanced by an established figure, and the halt counted as contribution rather than impertinence. That egalitarian discipline mirrored the egalitarian themes many members investigated in their substantive work, although nobody confused procedural fairness in a seminar with a theory of justice for a whole society. The resemblance nonetheless helped sustain morale. People returned to a room where status had to be earned afresh through argument each September.

Education in the relevant skills was unevenly distributed at the start, and the group functioned partly as a school for its own participants. Philosophers learned to respect data about institutions they had treated as illustrations. Social scientists learned to notice when a model smuggled normative premises into apparently neutral assumptions about preference or rationality. Everyone learned to write for readers who would not supply charitable interpretations on demand. Over several cycles of draft, comment, and redraft, a house style emerged without ever being codified in a manual: define terms at first use, flag assumptions before deriving results, separate textual interpretation from independent justification, and acknowledge the strongest opposing case before answering it. Students of the participants absorbed similar habits, carrying the approach into departments where the original meetings were only a distant rumour, and that diffusion extended influence beyond the membership list itself.

## The founding book and the defence of historical materialism

Every intellectual circle benefits from a text that gives strangers a common object to argue about, page by page and premise by premise. For this circle that object arrived a year before the first meeting, in a study of Marx published in 1978 by G. A. Cohen, a philosopher then teaching at University College London. The volume took the theory of history attributed to Marx and treated it neither as scripture nor as museum piece, but as a set of claims about how societies develop, claims strong enough to be worth defending and precise enough to be worth attacking. Readers across several disciplines recognised a rare combination: sympathy for the theory joined to an almost legal insistence on saying exactly what the theory asserted, what evidence would support it, and where its difficulties lay.

### Which book gathered the school around a single argument?
Karl Marx Theory of History A Defence by G A Cohen published in 1978 gathered readers because it restated historical materialism as a sequence of explicit premises about productive development and social relations that critics and supporters alike could test separately

Historical materialism, in the version Cohen reconstructed, begins with a distinction between the productive forces available to a society and the relations of production within which people use them. Productive forces include tools, machinery, technical knowledge, skills embodied in workers, and raw materials made accessible through technique. Relations of production concern who controls those forces, who directs labour, who appropriates surplus, and under what rights and obligations production proceeds. A factory can be described in either vocabulary. Described through its forces, it is an arrangement of equipment, energy, and competence. Described through its relations, it is a structure of ownership, command, and dependence. Confusing the two descriptions produces muddle, while keeping them apart allows a theory of their interaction.

From that distinction Cohen extracted two central propositions that together formed the spine of his defence. The first, often called the development thesis, holds that productive forces tend to grow over time, because human beings facing scarcity have reason to preserve useful knowledge and to improve means of satisfying needs. The second, the primacy thesis, holds that the character of production relations is explained by the level of development reached by the forces: relations rise, persist, or decay according to their suitability for employing and advancing productive capacity. Stated baldly, both propositions invite immediate questions, and that invitation was the point. A reader could ask whether tendency means inevitability, how long a lag between force and relation may last, or what mechanism selects suitable relations from among possible alternatives.

Cohen welcomed such questions by supplying a style of explanation able to answer some of them. He defended functional explanation in social science, arguing that a pattern can be explained by its beneficial consequences for a system provided a feedback mechanism sustains the pattern because of those consequences. Legal and political structures, on his reading, could be understood partly through their tendency to stabilise prevailing production relations, and those relations partly through their tendency to permit or promote growth in productive power. Functional language in biology rests on natural selection. In society, candidate mechanisms include deliberate legislation, competitive pressure between states or firms, imitation of successful neighbours, and the collapse of arrangements that obstruct production too severely. Naming candidates mattered more than settling on one, since a functional claim without any plausible mechanism remains a promissory note.

Premise by premise examination changed the temperature of debate around Marx. Earlier disputes often moved directly from textual loyalty to historical verdict, asking whether Marx had been vindicated by events in Russia or refuted by prosperity in Western Europe. Cohen slowed the sequence down. First, what did the theory claim. Second, what kind of claim was it: empirical generalisation, conceptual truth, or normative judgement disguised as description. Third, what observations bore on it, and with what weight. Fourth, could a modified version retain explanatory force after a damaging concession. That orderly sequence frustrated partisans on both sides, because it denied anticommunists an easy caricature and denied orthodox defenders an easy retreat into ambiguity about what had been predicted.

Consider the primacy thesis under that treatment. It does not say that technology mechanically stamps out identical institutions wherever it appears, a reading Cohen rejected through careful qualification. Similar levels of productive development can coexist with varied legal forms, cultural traditions, and political regimes. The thesis claims instead that limits exist: relations incompatible with effective use of available forces generate strain, waste, and pressure for change, while relations that harness those forces enjoy an advantage in stability and expansion. Whether pressure issues in transformation depends on agents, organisations, accidents, and resistance, none of which the bare thesis enumerates. Clarifying scope in that manner simultaneously strengthened and narrowed the claim. It became more defensible because it asserted less than caricature suggested, and more vulnerable because its remaining content could be tested against awkward cases.

Awkward cases were not treated as embarrassments to be hidden. A society might retain stagnant relations for centuries despite available technique. A conqueror might impose relations from outside, interrupting any neat correspondence between internal development and institutional form. Knowledge might be lost, through war or institutional collapse, reversing growth that the development thesis portrayed as a tendency. Cohen handled such material by distinguishing a tendency from an exceptionless law, by separating explanation of a structure from prediction of its lifespan, and by asking whether counterexamples defeated the general pattern or merely marked its boundary conditions. Readers could disagree with each move, but disagreement had an address. It attached to a named premise or a named distinction, and could be pursued without denouncing the entire enterprise.

The book also reshaped how participants thought about the superstructure, the legal, political, and ideological forms that orthodox accounts sometimes treated as mere reflections of an economic base. Reflection language suggests passivity, as if law and belief were shadows cast by machinery. Cohen offered a more active picture in which superstructural arrangements endure partly because they perform work: securing property rights, enforcing contracts, legitimating authority, coordinating expectations. Such work explains persistence without reducing content to a simple copy of economic interest, and it leaves room to study how a legal doctrine or moral idea develops internal complexity while still serving stabilising functions. Sociologists and political scientists found here a bridge between grand theory and institutional analysis, since the bridge consisted of questions they already knew how to investigate.

Equally important was the manner in which Cohen handled the texts of Marx himself. Quotations were not assembled as charms. Passages, notably the famous summary in the Preface of 1859, were parsed sentence by sentence, alternative readings were compared, and textual evidence was weighed against systematic plausibility. A reading that made Marx coherent but trivial lost to a reading that made him substantial but exposed, where the evidence allowed that choice. That hierarchy of virtues announced a programme others could imitate with different conclusions. One could apply the same parsing to theories of class, theories of the state, or accounts of ideology, and arrive at rejection rather than defence, while still working inside the intellectual style the book exemplified.

Reception across disciplines explains why a work of philosophy became a gathering point for economists and social scientists. Philosophers admired its argumentative architecture. Economists recognised a theory making claims about incentives, scarcity, and institutional selection, claims they could reformulate, qualify, or contest with models. Historians and sociologists saw a framework definite enough to organise comparative inquiry instead of merely decorating its results afterward. Each constituency found something to dispute, which proved more valuable than unanimous praise. A book everyone admires for its tone generates compliments. A book that gives different experts different premises to attack generates a research community.

Defence in the title therefore carried a conditional sense that later readers sometimes miss. Cohen defended historical materialism by showing what a defensible version would have to look like, and the showing exposed costs. Functional explanation had to be legitimised against critics who regarded it as teleology. The development thesis had to survive evidence of stagnation and regression. Primacy had to be formulated without collapsing into technological determinism. Each rescue narrowed room for manoeuvre elsewhere. That cumulative narrowing is precisely what made the volume generative for a school: it converted loyalty into a list of open problems, and open problems, unlike slogans, can organise years of collective work.

A further consequence followed for readers outside the circle who encountered the volume without attending any meeting. Because its claims arrived in separable form, a reader could accept the account of productive development while rejecting functional explanation, or admire the textual exegesis while doubting that relations of production enjoy explanatory priority over political organisation. Partial agreement became a respectable outcome, replacing the older pattern in which a scholar signalled allegiance by endorsing a system entire or signalled independence by rejecting it entire. Anthologies and syllabuses could assign a single chapter as a self contained exercise in argument, and seminars in unrelated fields could borrow its distinctions for fresh material. That portability multiplied the audience for a demanding monograph, and it modelled a way for Marxist theory to enter general academic conversation without asking for special exemption at the door.

## The September Group and its annual meetings

A shared book can start a conversation, but only a recurring institution can keep one honest across decades of changing minds. The institution here was deliberately modest: a gathering held each year in September, small enough for sustained exchange, regular enough for unfinished disputes to resume rather than restart. Participants arrived from British universities, from Norway, and from campuses across the Atlantic, bringing manuscripts at various stages of completion and a shared expectation that politeness would not be allowed to smother disagreement. Over time the calendar fact hardened into an identity. Outsiders and later historians referred to the circle as the September Group, a plain label derived from the rhythm of its meetings rather than from any doctrine its members professed in common.

### Why did the group take a month for its name?
The group took September for its name because its members assembled each year in that month for several days of discussion built around papers circulated in advance and arguments pursued until disagreements became precise enough to identify and assess clearly together

Inside the circle, however, a blunter label circulated in correspondence and conversation. Members called their enterprise the non-bullshit Marxism group, and Cohen used that self description publicly as well. Precision about the label matters. It was never a sneer coined by hostile critics to mock pretensions from outside, nor a nickname imposed by journalists summarising a movement they disliked. It was the group describing itself to itself, with deliberate irreverence, as a reminder of the vice it intended to avoid. The roughness of the phrase performed useful work. It lowered ceremony, punctured grandiosity, and warned each new draft that ornamental difficulty would receive no respect merely for sounding profound.

What counted as the forbidden vice can be specified without reproducing the joke. It included sentences whose grammar suggested an argument while supplying only atmosphere, technical terms introduced to signal allegiance rather than to mark distinctions, and appeals to dialectical complexity whenever a straightforward contradiction appeared. It also included a subtler scholarly sin: shifting the meaning of a central term between premises, so that a conclusion seemed to follow while in fact equivocation had carried the reader across a gap. Calling such habits by a rude name made them easier to confront in a friend. A participant could be told, in effect, that a paragraph had failed the house standard, and the shared joke softened the blow without softening the judgement.

Meetings were organised around papers circulated before arrival, a practice that transformed the quality of discussion. Reading in advance meant that sessions could begin where a printed argument became difficult, instead of spending scarce hours on summary for the unprepared. Authors listened while colleagues reconstructed their reasoning, sometimes discovering that the argument on the page differed from the argument intended at the desk. Respondents could quote passages back, isolate transitions, and propose counterexamples prepared with care rather than improvised from first impressions. Circulation also equalised standing to a degree. A junior scholar armed with a genuine objection to a circulated text held firmer ground than a junior scholar interrupting a lecture, because the objection could point to lines everyone present had already read.

Sustained argument distinguished these gatherings from the ordinary conference circuit. Conferences reward breadth: many panels, brief questions, rapid movement toward the next speaker. The September meetings rewarded depth, returning to a single distinction across a morning if the distinction bore weight elsewhere in a theory. Such patience could appear indulgent from outside, yet it reflected a conviction that large conclusions in social theory usually rest on small conceptual joints, and that a joint either holds under pressure or it does not. Testing joints one by one was slow. It was also cumulative, since a clarification won in discussion of class might discipline discussion of exploitation later in the same meeting, and participants carried the clarified usage into subsequent drafts.

Disciplinary mixture gave the pressure its particular character. A philosopher interrogating a claim about rationality might be answered by an economist offering a formal model, who in turn faced a sociologist asking whether the model omitted the very institutions that made the behaviour intelligible. Political scientists introduced comparison across regimes, reminding theorists that a mechanism plausible in one constitutional setting might fail where parties, unions, or courts were organised differently. Norwegian participants brought familiarity with Scandinavian welfare arrangements and with a social democratic tradition that complicated simple contrasts between capitalism and socialism. British members wrote against a background of sharp class politics and a strong tradition in moral philosophy. American members worked within large research universities where game theory and rational choice methods carried growing influence. No single background set the agenda for long.

Membership, it should be emphasised, never resembled enrolment in a party with a line to enforce. Attendance shifted, interests wandered, and allegiances to particular theses weakened or dissolved at different speeds. Some central figures moved away from Marxist conclusions altogether while continuing to value the forum, because the forum had been built to examine conclusions rather than to guard them. Others retained more of the original framework while revising its foundations. The group could contain both movements because its bond was a standard of discussion applied to shared problems, not agreement on answers. Indeed, the liveliest sessions often occurred when a former defender presented reasons for abandoning a position that colleagues still hoped to repair, since both sides knew the position intimately and neither could rely on caricature.

Hospitality and rigour coexisted in a balance that small recurring groups either achieve or perish lacking. People who meet annually, eat together, and follow the development of each others projects over many years acquire reasons to remain civil that a single encounter cannot supply. Civility in this case did not mean softness. It meant confidence that a severe objection aimed at an argument, not at a career or a character, and that an author conceding a point would lose no standing. Concession could even raise standing, by demonstrating that the participant valued getting a matter right above winning a session. A culture that honours concession learns faster than a culture that treats every retreat as defeat, and the September circle prized learning speed.

The annual rhythm also shaped the writing members produced between meetings. A draft destined for September faced a foreseeable audience of exacting readers who knew the literature, remembered earlier versions, and disliked repetition of errors already exposed. Anticipation of that audience disciplined composition months in advance. Sentences were shortened or clarified, assumptions migrated from footnotes into the main line of argument where they belonged, and empirical claims acquired qualifications before a sceptical colleague could demand them. Influence therefore flowed through expectation as much as through comment. Even work never presented at a meeting could bear the imprint of the group if its author wrote with those readers in mind.

Historians of ideas sometimes search for a manifesto that fixes what such a group believed, a single document against which fidelity can be measured. The September circle frustrates that search, and the frustration is instructive. Its unity was practical: meet, circulate, argue, revise, return. Beliefs changed under that practice, in some cases beyond recognition, while the practice itself remained recognisable across the years. To understand the school, a reader must therefore attend to habits of work as closely as to doctrines defended or discarded. The doctrines belong to particular books and articles. The habits belong to the group, and they explain how one circle of defenders produced, over time, some of the most searching criticism its own starting points ever received.


Consider what the meetings did for topics that no single discipline owned. Exploitation sat between moral philosophy, where it named a wrong, and economics, where it named a transfer measurable under specified assumptions. Class sat between sociology, where it described stratification and identity, and political theory, where it described collective agents capable of transforming institutions. A September session could move across those registers in a single afternoon, forcing each speaker to declare which sense was in play before evidence or evaluation proceeded. Confusion between registers had generated many sterile exchanges in wider debate, with one side proving a descriptive claim while the other denounced a moral one, both believing they had joined issue. Patient separation of registers rarely produced agreement, but it produced disagreement of a higher grade, in which each side could state the exact proposition it denied and the exact reason for denial.

Even the social texture of the gatherings contributed to that achievement. Returning guests developed a repertoire of shared examples, cautionary tales about arguments that had collapsed under inspection, and shorthand references to distinctions hammered out in earlier years. Shorthand can exclude newcomers, and any durable circle risks turning inward, yet in moderation it accelerates work by sparing veterans the need to rebuild foundations at every session. Newcomers, for their part, supplied a valuable friction. Unfamiliar with house shorthand, they demanded expansions that occasionally revealed a foundation less secure than veterans assumed. The healthiest sessions combined both virtues: memory enough to make progress cumulative, and fresh questioning enough to prevent memory from hardening into dogma. That balance, renewed annually against the twin temptations of amnesia and complacency, may be the most transferable lesson the group offered to academic life beyond its own substantive concerns.

## A commitment rather than a technique: the method stated plainly

Analytical Marxism began less as a shared doctrine than as a shared impatience. Scholars trained in philosophy, economics, history and political theory converged during the late 1970s and 1980s around a suspicion that much Marxist writing protected itself from refutation by refusing to say exactly what it claimed. Grand terms circulated with an air of profundity while their logical relations remained unspecified, and criticism could be deflected by suggesting that the critic had failed to grasp a deeper mode of thought. Against that habit the September Group and associated writers proposed a discipline that sounded modest yet proved demanding in practice. A thesis should be divided into premises that could be examined one by one. Concepts should be defined before they were asked to carry explanatory weight. Inferences should be displayed rather than insinuated, and evidence should be distinguished from illustration, analogy and rhetorical emphasis. Clarity, on this view, was not a stylistic preference added after serious thought had finished. Clarity was part of the thought itself, a test that an idea had been formed sharply enough to be assessed at all.

Moral seriousness lay beneath the methodological demand, and participants often treated obscurity as a failure of respect toward readers and opponents alike. Writing that cannot be pinned down transfers labour unfairly, because the audience must reconstruct an argument that the author declined to supply, and then risks blame for reconstructing it incorrectly. Protective vagueness also confers an unearned advantage in debate, since a position that shifts whenever pressure arrives can survive any encounter without ever winning one honestly. Plain statement reversed those incentives. An author who separated the development thesis from the primacy thesis, or exploitation from domination, or a causal claim from a moral condemnation, exposed each element to a different kind of objection and accepted that some elements might fall while others stood. Such exposure felt risky because it surrendered the comfort of a total system in which every part borrowed strength from the aura of the whole. Yet the risk was the point. Intellectual honesty required that strength be earned locally, claim by claim, instead of borrowed from vocabulary, tradition or political loyalty.

Discipline of that sort also changed how disagreement could proceed inside the school and beyond it. When a writer stated a proposition in a form an opponent could quote, formalise or test against a case, dispute acquired a location. One scholar might accept a definition of productive forces while denying that their growth explained a transition between modes of production. Another might grant a mechanism linking ownership to political power while contesting the scope assigned to that mechanism across different societies. Progress became possible because agreement and disagreement no longer had to be global. Partial convergence, often invisible inside holistic frameworks, could be recorded and built upon, while residual conflict could be narrowed to a premise, a scope condition or an interpretation of evidence. Even failure gained value under such rules, since a refuted formulation taught more than an elastic formulation that survived by changing shape. The school treated a clear mistake as a contribution of a kind, because it marked a path that later inquiry could avoid or repair with full awareness of why repair was needed.

### What did the school mean by clarity as a standard?

It meant that every claim had to survive translation into premises, definitions and inferences an opponent could inspect, reject or repair, without shelter from jargon, metaphor or authority, and that failure to achieve such statement counted against the claim itself rather than against the reader alone.

Translation into explicit form, however, was never confused with reduction to a single favoured vocabulary. Members drew upon conceptual analysis from analytical philosophy, models from formal economics, game theory, theories of rational action and careful historical narrative, selecting instruments according to the question under examination. A problem about why peasants might support or resist collectivisation invited different tools from a problem about whether a concept of exploitation required a labour theory of value. Plurality of technique followed naturally once clarity was treated as the commitment and particular formalisms were treated as servants rather than masters. Some arguments benefited from equations and equilibrium reasoning, because those devices forced assumptions about information, incentives and constraints into the open. Other arguments benefited from close distinctions among senses of a term, because equivocation had allowed incompatible theses to appear mutually supporting. Still other inquiries required archival patience more than formal display. Across those differences the governing question stayed constant: could a competent critic identify what would have to be true for the conclusion to follow, and what observation or counterexample would damage it.

Resistance to the standard came from several directions, and a fair account should grant force to the strongest objections before reaching any verdict. Critics argued that premature formalisation could flatten historical processes whose meaning emerged through context, narrative sequence and contradictory pressures resistant to neat decomposition. Others warned that a demand for definitions might privilege whatever could be modelled with available mathematics, quietly demoting questions about culture, ideology or lived experience that resisted such treatment. A further objection held that ordinary language in political theory carries productive ambiguity, allowing concepts such as freedom or alienation to gather experiences that a stipulative definition would artificially separate. Each warning identified a real danger. Formal clarity can become a filter that admits only tractable problems, and stipulation can disguise a substantive decision as a merely verbal one. Analytical Marxists at their best acknowledged those dangers by treating models as instruments with declared limits, by revisiting definitions when cases strained them, and by allowing that some explanations would remain incomplete after honest effort.

A verdict in favour of the clarity commitment therefore needs a stated condition for reversal, and such a condition is not hard to formulate. If sustained comparison showed that explicitly formulated Marxist arguments systematically generated less historical understanding than the holistic approaches they displaced, or that formalisation repeatedly concealed more substantive choice than it revealed, the commitment would deserve demotion from a standard to one optional style among others. Evidence of occasional misuse would not suffice, because every method can be practised badly, and the school never claimed that clarity guaranteed truth. What clarity guaranteed was accountability. A transparent argument could be wrong in instructive ways, whereas an opaque argument could be neither confirmed nor corrected without extensive charitable reconstruction by others. That asymmetry justified treating plain statement as a default obligation, defeasible in particular cases perhaps, but never as an ornament to be discarded when conclusions became uncomfortable or when a favoured author preferred suggestive depth to demonstrable connection.

Habits formed under that obligation left marks on exposition itself. Writers learned to announce scope before advancing a thesis, to separate a claim about tendencies from a claim about outcomes in every instance, and to mark the difference between a definition adopted for argument and a discovery reported from inquiry. Counterexamples were welcomed early rather than relegated to defensive footnotes, because a case that broke a generalisation often revealed which scope condition had been silently assumed. Thought experiments were used with similar discipline: an imagined society could test whether a concept depended upon contingent institutions, provided the imagined case was described fully enough for readers to judge its relevance. Even the celebrated willingness to borrow from non Marxist scholarship followed from the same ethic. If a tool from mainstream economics or philosophy clarified a Marxist proposition, refusing the tool on grounds of lineage would protect identity at the expense of understanding, and identity protection was precisely what the method had been designed to refuse.

Generational context helps explain why the demand carried such emotional charge for its early advocates. Many had encountered Marxism first as a comprehensive worldview delivered through canonical texts, party education or densely theoretical journals, settings in which mastery of idiom could substitute for independent assessment of evidence. Graduate training in postwar Anglo American philosophy and social science offered a contrasting experience, one in which a seminar could pause for an hour over a single inference and treat that pause as serious work rather than pedantry. Bringing the second experience to bear upon the first produced friction, loyalty conflicts and, at times, polemical excess. Some manifestos overstated the novelty of the approach, as if careful argument had been absent from earlier Marxist scholarship, when in fact historians and economists inside the tradition had long practised forms of precision suited to their materials. A balanced history should therefore credit the school with codifying and publicising a standard, not with inventing careful thought from nothing. Codification still mattered, because a standard shared across a group can discipline newcomers, editors and critics in ways that private virtue cannot, and can turn isolated good practice into an expectation that weak work must answer.

Pedagogy followed from codification in ways that extended influence beyond any single book or debate. Students encountering the literature through analytical reconstructions met Marxist theory as a set of problems that could be entered at many points, rather than as a doctrine requiring prior initiation into a complete vocabulary. A reader could begin with a question about whether exploitation presupposed a particular account of value, follow the distinctions introduced to handle that question, and only later decide how the answer affected wider claims about capitalism or socialism. Accessibility of that kind did not simplify the subject, since the problems themselves remained difficult and the formal treatments could be forbidding in a different register. Accessibility meant something narrower and more valuable: the location of difficulty was visible. Struggle could be directed toward a definition that seemed too broad, a premise that seemed false, or a model whose assumptions seemed unrealistic, instead of toward a fog in which difficulty and profundity were indistinguishable. Teachers, reviewers and rivals could all point to the same place and disagree productively about what should happen there.

## No distinctive Marxist method: the founding refusal

Refusal of a separate Marxist logic formed the negative counterpart to the positive demand for clarity, and for many participants it was the more provocative act. Dialectical reasoning, in some presentations inherited by the school, had been treated as a mode of inference that superseded ordinary logic when dealing with contradiction, totality and historical change. Analytical writers replied that contradiction could name several different phenomena, each requiring separate handling: a logical inconsistency between propositions, a conflict between social forces, a tension between institutional imperatives, or a process in which a development generated obstacles to its own continuation. Only the first kind threatened validity directly, and none of the others licensed an inference that would be invalid under familiar standards. Collapsing those senses into a single dialectical movement allowed dramatic conclusions to appear necessary when, upon inspection, each step depended upon empirical premises that had never been defended. By insisting that such premises be supplied and assessed normally, the school denied that allegiance to Marxism conferred any exemption from the rules governing argument in history, economics or philosophy.

What the refusal bought can be seen in the new conversations it made possible across intellectual boundaries. A Marxist claim about class formation, stated with ordinary causal vocabulary and explicit evidence, could be engaged by a sociologist who rejected Marxism entirely, without either party first settling the legitimacy of a special method. A philosopher puzzled by the structure of functional claims could examine Cohen alongside debates in biology and social science, treating the Marxist case as an instance of a general explanatory problem. Economists could test reconstructions of exploitation or crisis theory against familiar criteria concerning assumptions, equilibrium and comparative statics, even when they disputed the political conclusions drawn from a model. Engagement of that kind carried a price, because arguments that survived translation sometimes looked less distinctive than their originals, and ideas that failed translation could not be rescued by appeals to a deeper logic available only to initiates. Distinctiveness, where it remained, had to reside in substantive theses about production, class, power and historical direction, rather than in a protected manner of reasoning.

Costs extended beyond distinctiveness into questions of identity and intellectual inheritance. Marx had written in a Hegelian idiom at important moments, and generations of interpreters had treated dialectics as central to his achievement, so abandoning a special dialectical logic could look like abandoning Marx himself rather than reconstructing him. Some critics charged that analytical reconstructions preserved conclusions while discarding the form of thought that gave those conclusions meaning, producing a Marxism domesticated by the very academic disciplines it had once challenged. Others argued that attention to totality, mediation and internal relations captured features of social reality that piecemeal analysis tended to miss, especially the way institutions acquire significance through their place within a wider configuration. Those objections deserve a strong hearing. Social phenomena do exhibit interdependence, feedback and emergent patterns, and an analysis that isolates variables carelessly can misdescribe the object before explanation even begins. Analytical Marxists could accept every part of that warning, however, while denying that interdependence required a separate logic. Complexity called for richer models, longer causal chains and sensitivity to context, not for permission to affirm both a proposition and its negation or to treat metaphorical movement as proof.

### Why did the refusal of a separate method matter so much?

It mattered because surrendering a separate logic removed immunity from ordinary refutation, forced Marxist claims to compete on shared evidential ground, exposed hidden premises to opponents, and made agreement possible without conversion, while also making disagreement precise enough to be genuinely instructive for both sides.

Shared evidential ground did not mean neutral ground in every respect, and the school was more divided on underlying philosophy of science than slogans about analytical method sometimes suggested. Some members leaned toward methodological individualism, holding that social explanations should ultimately be grounded in facts about individuals, their beliefs, desires, opportunities and interactions. Others accepted that requirement only as a heuristic, worried that an insistence upon individual level foundations might obscure structures whose causal influence operated through positions, rules and inherited constraints not reducible in any simple fashion to the intentions of occupants. A further strand explored how macro level regularities might supervene upon individual action without being explainable, in practice, at that level alone. Disagreement on that point matters for any accurate portrait, because it shows that the founding refusal did not deliver a single replacement technique. Unity lay in a negative commitment and a standard of statement, while positive explanatory strategy remained contested territory where rational choice modelling, comparative history and conceptual analysis competed and combined in different proportions across authors and topics.

Jon Elster pressed the individualist demand with particular force, arguing that appeals to class interest or systemic need explained little until someone specified through which beliefs and choices those forces operated upon events. John Roemer approached related problems through general equilibrium and game theoretic constructions, showing how exploitation like outcomes might arise from unequal asset distribution even where labour markets cleared and no surplus transfer in a simple sense could be identified. Erik Olin Wright, by contrast, built class analysis that retained structural locations and organisational capacities as indispensable categories, while still accepting obligations of measurement, comparison and explicit causal reasoning. G. A. Cohen defended large scale historical claims whose explanatory form sat uneasily beside any strict individualist programme. Placing those figures inside one school therefore requires care. A common refusal united them more reliably than a common ontology, and later fragmentation surprised only those who had mistaken a shared standard of argument for agreement about what the world ultimately contained.

Historical change posed the hardest test for the refusal, because change in social formations involves thresholds, unintended consequences and interactions among processes unfolding at different speeds. A dialectical vocabulary had often been used to gesture at that complexity in a single phrase, suggesting that quantity passing into quality or negation succeeding negation supplied an explanatory pattern. Analytical treatment slowed the gesture down. A threshold claim had to identify the variable accumulating, the point at which effects altered character, and the reason a new regime of behaviour followed rather than gradual adjustment continuing. A claim about internal contradiction had to specify which institutional requirements conflicted, for whom the conflict created incentives to act, and through what organisations action might transform or stabilise the arrangement. Slowing down cost rhetorical sweep, and some narratives lost the sense of a whole society moving through a comprehensible drama. Gains in return were substantial, because each element could be compared across cases: one transition might share an accumulating variable with another while differing in threshold mechanism, and comparison could then explain divergent outcomes instead of assigning each outcome to a unique spirit of an epoch.

Verdicts on the refusal should again be paired with conditions that would overturn them, since a methodological stance earns authority only by remaining answerable to results. If research programmes built upon dialectical logic, understood as a genuinely distinct inferential practice, produced explanations that were repeatedly confirmed, that resisted translation into ordinary causal and logical terms without loss, and that generated predictions unavailable to rival approaches, then the founding refusal would look like a costly narrowing rather than a liberation. Mere demonstrations that holistic language inspired insight would not meet that bar, because inspiration and justification perform different work, and a suggestive metaphor can guide discovery while still requiring ordinary validation before its conclusions enter an argument. Conversely, the analytical position grows stronger each time a dialectical passage, once unpacked, turns out to contain either a familiar causal hypothesis that benefits from explicit statement or an equivocation that dissolves under distinction. Much of the school literature can be read as an extended attempt to perform that unpacking patiently, text by text and thesis by thesis, while conceding that unpacking sometimes revealed genuine complexity rather than confusion.

Political implications followed, although never in a single direction. Removing methodological immunity made Marxist theory more vulnerable in seminar rooms and journals, yet vulnerability also made persuasion beyond the converted conceivable, because a sceptical reader could follow an argument without first accepting a worldview. Activists suspicious of academic abstraction sometimes heard the refusal as deradicalisation, a trimming of revolutionary theory into puzzles acceptable to liberal universities. Defenders answered that a politics built upon claims that cannot survive scrutiny carries its own danger, since movements guided by protected illusions may misjudge opponents, allies and opportunities with severe practical consequences. Between those poles lay a quieter possibility that many members came to inhabit: radical conclusions remained available, including far reaching judgements about exploitation, domination and the feasibility of egalitarian alternatives, provided each conclusion was reached through premises a critic could examine. Radicalism of conclusion and conservatism of inference proved compatible, and that combination, paradoxical only at first glance, became one of the most recognisable signatures of the enterprise during its formative decades.

Seminars and journals beyond Marxist circles noticed the stance for a further reason, namely its usefulness as a teaching device. A student trained to ask whether a passage asserted a logical relation, a causal hypothesis or a moral judgement acquired a portable skill, applicable to liberal, conservative and socialist arguments alike. Portability confirmed the founding claim in practice, because a method that only vindicated one tradition would have looked suspiciously like the separate logic under rejection, whereas a standard that embarrassed allies as readily as opponents demonstrated genuine impartiality. Embarrassment, distributed fairly, became evidence that the rules were real.

## Functional explanation and its defence

Functional explanation supplied the school with its most discussed test case, because G. A. Cohen used that form of reasoning to defend historical materialism at precisely the moment when allies were demanding mechanisms and critics were questioning whether consequence based explanation belonged in social science at all. Cohen distinguished two central claims. The development thesis held that the productive forces tend, over time, to grow, driven by human rationality confronting scarcity and by the cumulative character of technical knowledge, which is easier to preserve and extend than to lose entirely. The primacy thesis held that production relations in a society take their character from the level of development of its productive forces, in the sense that a given level selects relations suited to further use and expansion of those forces. Together the theses offered a materialist ordering of history in which technology, broadly understood to include knowledge, tools and labour power, set limits and exerted pressure upon economic structure, while economic structure in turn shaped legal and political forms. Cohen insisted that such ordering could be explanatory even before every intervening process had been mapped in detail.

Explanation by consequence was the controversial step. To say that production relations of a certain type exist because they promote development of the forces is to cite an effect of those relations as part of the reason for their presence or persistence. Similarly, to say that a legal or political superstructure endures because it stabilises prevailing relations is to explain an institution through a service it performs for something else. Cohen argued that social science already relied upon patterns of that kind whenever it explained a practice through the needs it met for a system, and he drew an analogy with biology, where a trait can be explained through a beneficial consequence provided a Darwinian history connects consequence to retention. Elaborations, his term for accounts that fill in how a functional connection operates, could take several forms: purposive action by agents who perceive a benefit and act to secure it, selection among competing institutional variants, reinforcement through repeated advantage, or unintended processes in which beneficial arrangements survive while harmful ones collapse. A reader seeking the clearest reconstruction of that position will find it in [Cohen's functional explanation](/2020/01/15/ga-cohen-functional-explanation/), where the distinction between a functional claim and its supporting elaboration receives sustained attention.

### What work did functional explanation do for the school?

Functional explanation connected levels of the theory, showing how relations and institutions could be understood through consequences that sustained development or stability, while preserving causal questions about mechanisms, agents and feedback processes that any complete defence of historical materialism would eventually need to answer satisfactorily.

Connection across levels gave the device its attraction for a theory that aspired to explain large configurations rather than isolated events. Without some such device, primacy risked becoming a bare correlation between technology and economic structure, impressive in sweeping surveys yet silent about why the correspondence should recur across very different cultures and political orders. Functional reasoning proposed an answer: relations that obstructed development tended to generate stagnation, conflict or competitive disadvantage, whereas relations that facilitated development tended to consolidate, spread through imitation or conquest, and shape law and ideology in protective directions. Superstructural explanation extended the pattern one level upward, treating property law, state forms and dominant ideas as intelligible partly through their contribution to the stability of an economic base. Even a reader unpersuaded by the full theory could recognise the explanatory ambition involved, since the pattern promised to link facts that purely intentional history left scattered, such as why similar legal innovations appeared in societies whose rulers shared neither language nor direct contact, at moments when comparable changes in production were placing inherited arrangements under strain.

Objections arrived quickly and, in their strongest form, targeted the gap between citing a beneficial consequence and explaining an existence. Elster argued that a consequence can explain its cause only when a feedback loop connects the consequence back to the maintenance of the cause, and that Marxist functional claims often asserted such loops without demonstrating them. Appeals to the needs of capital or the requirements of a mode of production, on that critique, risked treating society as an organism with purposes of its own, while the actual agents pursued narrower aims whose aggregation might or might not produce the alleged function. A ruling class might desire stability yet lack coordination to achieve it. Subordinate groups might resist arrangements that benefited development in aggregate while imposing concentrated losses upon them. Competing states might select for military effectiveness rather than productive efficiency, preserving relations that a purely economic logic would predict to disappear. Each scenario showed why a functional pattern, however suggestive, remained incomplete until someone identified the actors, incentives, information and selection pressures through which an outcome was reproduced over time.

Cohen replied by separating the truth of a functional explanation from knowledge of its elaboration, and the distinction deserves careful statement before any assessment. A person can have good reason to believe that a feature persists because of what it does, he suggested, while remaining ignorant about the detailed pathway, just as natural historians could reasonably offer functional accounts of organs before genetics supplied underlying mechanisms. Evidence for the functional claim itself could come from robust patterns: the repeated emergence of similar relations at comparable levels of development, the disappearance of relations after they turned into fetters, and the comparative failure of alternatives that lacked the relevant effect. Demanding a completed mechanism before granting any explanatory standing, Cohen warned, would impose a standard that much successful science would fail during its growth, and might discard a well supported pattern for the sake of a philosophical preference. At the same time he welcomed elaborations as genuine advances, treated purposive and selection based accounts as strengthening a defence, and conceded that a functional claim unsupported by any plausible route from consequence to persistence stood on weaker ground than one whose route had been traced.

Assessment therefore turns upon what standard a theory must meet at a given stage, and both sides of the dispute can be steelmanned without caricature. The mechanism demand protects social explanation from teleology, from the slide in which whatever survives is declared functional for survival and every institution receives a flattering rationale after the fact. It also directs research toward politics in the concrete sense: who benefits, who notices, who can organise, which alternatives were available, and what prevented a superior arrangement from being adopted by those with power to choose. The Cohen defence protects a different insight, namely that large scale patterns can be real before their microfoundations are settled, and that premature insistence upon a single favoured level of explanation may blind inquiry to selection effects operating across generations. A judicious verdict favours Cohen on legitimacy and his critics on completeness: functional explanation can explain, yet a defence resting permanently at that level invites the suspicion that the pattern has been named rather than understood. That verdict would reverse if comparative research showed that elaborated mechanisms, once supplied, systematically contradicted the functional patterns they were meant to support, or if the patterns themselves dissolved under closer measurement of development and correspondence. Either result would demote functional claims from explanations awaiting completion to heuristics that had misled a generation of inquiry.

Historical illustrations sharpened both attraction and unease throughout the debate. The transition from feudal to capitalist relations could be narrated functionally as the replacement of arrangements that constrained growing forces by arrangements that released them, yet regional variation complicated any single storyline, since similar pressures produced different settlements depending upon peasant organisation, state structure and access to trade. Enclosure, commutation of dues and changes in tenancy each involved identifiable agents pursuing rents, security or advantage, and those local stories sometimes supported the larger pattern while sometimes revealing coercion that development language alone sanitised. Superstructural cases raised parallel questions. A legal regime protecting contract and alienable property might stabilise capitalist relations, but jurists developed doctrines through professional reasoning with its own momentum, and rulers adopted reforms for fiscal or military reasons that only incidentally served economic stability. Functional explanation handled the convergence among such motives elegantly, by locating significance at the level of consequence, while critics rightly asked whether elegance had been purchased by demoting the very processes through which history actually moved from one state to another.

Influence of the controversy extended well beyond exegesis of Marx, because it forced analytical Marxists to clarify what kind of theory historical materialism aspired to be. If primacy and development were laws, functional explanation had to bear a heavy burden, supporting counterfactual claims about what would happen under blockage across long spans of time. If those theses were instead tendencies, scope conditions became essential, and exceptions involving war, ecology, demography or state autonomy could be admitted without surrendering the core pattern. Cohen later refined and qualified elements of his position, while other members moved toward mechanism centred explanation, historical institutional comparison or normative theory where functional claims played little role. Divergence should not be mistaken for simple collapse. Debate had performed the service the founding method promised: a central thesis was stated sharply enough for weaknesses to be located, for replies to be distinguished from concessions, and for successors to inherit a map showing exactly which premises remained defensible, which required evidence still outstanding, and which had been abandoned under pressure. Functional explanation thus occupies a double place in the history of the school, as both a substantive defence of a materialist philosophy of history and a demonstration, in public, of clarity doing its intended work upon a cherished claim.

## Elster and the reconstruction that became an assessment

Jon Elster approached Marx as a social theorist whose arguments deserved the same disciplined scrutiny applied to any other ambitious account of society, neither reverence nor dismissal in advance. Making Sense of Marx, published in 1985, proceeded passage by passage and theme by theme, restating claims about history, class, ideology, alienation, revolution, and the state in forms precise enough to be tested. Reconstruction in this sense meant charity with teeth. A vague formulation would be given its strongest plausible reading, ambiguous terms would be separated into distinct senses, and hidden premises would be brought into the open where they could be examined. Only after that labour of clarification did judgement begin, and judgement often proved severe. Elster retained admiration for particular insights, especially concerning unintended consequences, self deception, and the ways collective action can fail even when grievances are widely shared. Admiration, however, did not protect a doctrine from rejection when its logical structure collapsed or its empirical support dissolved under questioning. Readers expecting a sympathetic summary encountered instead an audit, patient in method and unsparing in outcome, conducted by a scholar who believed respect for a thinker required taking arguments seriously enough to discard those that failed.

Methodological individualism supplied the central standard against which explanations were measured throughout the book. For Elster, large scale patterns such as class formation, capitalist development, or ideological dominance could not explain themselves by invoking collective entities as if classes, nations, or modes of production possessed intentions of their own. A satisfactory account had to descend to individuals situated in specific circumstances, holding beliefs, facing constraints, pursuing goals, and interacting in ways that generated aggregate outcomes, sometimes intended and sometimes sharply at odds with what anyone wanted. The demand for mechanisms followed directly. Between a structural condition and a social result there had to be a credible chain of action and reaction, not merely a correlation, a functional fit, or a suggestive analogy drawn from biology or engineering. Functional explanation attracted particular suspicion. To show that an institution benefited capital, a ruling group, or system stability did not by itself explain why that institution arose or persisted, unless a feedback process could be specified through which beneficial consequences sustained the practice without conscious design. Elster allowed that such feedback loops exist in limited settings, yet insisted they be demonstrated case by case rather than assumed as a general licence for teleological reasoning about history.

Game theory and rational choice theory gave Elster tools for modelling the interactions that methodological individualism required. Prisoners dilemmas illuminated why workers sharing an interest in collective organisation might nevertheless defect, free ride, or wait for others to bear the costs of strikes and political mobilisation. Bargaining models clarified how concessions emerge when parties hold unequal resources and different tolerances for breakdown. Theories of preference formation raised harder questions still, because people adapt desires to circumstances, sour grapes being his memorable label for the process by which unattainable goods are declared worthless, a mechanism with obvious relevance for understanding resignation under hierarchy. Rational choice in his hands was never a claim that human beings calculate perfectly or pursue only selfish ends. Beliefs could be mistaken, preferences could be altruistic, and norms could bind conduct from within. What mattered was explanatory discipline: an account should state who acts, what those actors want and believe, which options they perceive, and how separate choices combine into collective results. Where Marxian writing moved directly from class position to class action, Elster inserted the missing middle, and the middle frequently turned out to be crowded with obstacles, perverse incentives, and unintended effects.

### How did a reconstruction turn into a largely destructive assessment?

Elster began by restating each Marxian claim in its strongest defensible form, then tested that form against evidence, logic, and explicit causal mechanisms. Claims lacking mechanisms or surviving counterexamples were set aside, so charitable reconstruction gradually produced a negative balance sheet rather than a rescued system of social theory.

Severity accumulated because failure in one area weakened confidence in neighbouring parts of the edifice. Dialectical reasoning, treated by sympathetic readers as a distinctive logic suited to contradictory social processes, appeared to Elster as a source of equivocation, permitting writers to assert opposites without specifying conditions under which either side held. Historical materialism fared unevenly depending on formulation. A modest version, holding that productive capacities shape institutions and ideas in broad and mediated ways, could be retained as a research orientation, though stripped of deterministic confidence. Stronger versions, predicting necessary transitions between modes of production or deriving political forms directly from economic bases, encountered counterexamples from divergent societies sharing similar technologies and from political arrangements that reshaped economic life rather than merely reflecting it. The labour theory of value received especially damaging treatment. Elster argued that exploitation could be discussed without that theory, that price formation did not require it, and that attempts to defend it often traded on shifting definitions of value. What survived was a moral and sociological concern with domination and unequal exchange, detached from the economic machinery originally meant to ground it. A verdict reversing this judgement would require a formulation of value theory that resolves the transformation difficulties, yields distinctive testable implications, and outperforms rival accounts of price and distribution on shared evidence.

Alienation and ideology illustrate the mixed pattern of salvage and rejection that gives the book its peculiar tone, appreciative at the level of observation and destructive at the level of system. Observations about work stripped of autonomy, about products confronting producers as alien powers, and about the psychological costs of repetitive labour retained force as descriptions of experience under particular organisational regimes. Elster resisted converting those observations into a metaphysics of human essence or a necessary stage theory of emancipation, since the normative standard then did work that empirical argument had not earned. Ideology received similar treatment. Belief distortion motivated by interest, wishful thinking, and adaptive preference formation could be modelled with psychological mechanisms and studied empirically. Grand claims that consciousness mechanically mirrors class position could not survive the diversity of beliefs found within classes or the capacity of ideas to motivate action against material advantage. Class itself remained usable as a descriptive category tied to market position and workplace relations, while class consciousness became a contingent achievement requiring organisation, communication, leadership, and perceived chances of success. Readers who consult [Elster's Marxism](/2020/02/01/jon-elster-marxism/) alongside the larger reconstruction can see how admiration and demolition coexist on the same page, separated by whether a claim can specify actors, incentives, and observable consequences.

Political theory within Marxism drew criticism for a different reason, namely a gap between diagnosis and institutional design. Elster found extensive analysis of capitalist crisis, commodification, and bourgeois legality, paired with thin treatment of constitutional safeguards, pluralism, incentive problems under planning, and the protection of minorities against majorities claiming revolutionary authority. Abundance assumptions carried heavy burdens in accounts of a future society beyond scarcity and conflict, yet scarcity of time, attention, status, and desirable locations would persist even under transformed ownership, generating allocation problems that goodwill alone could not solve. His discussion of revolution questioned theories that treated radical change as historically guaranteed or morally self certifying, directing attention instead to commitment problems, information failures, and the strategic dilemmas facing insurgents and incumbents alike. On exploitation, self ownership, and distributive justice, Elster engaged normative argument directly, finding tensions between condemning capitalist appropriation and endorsing principles that might justify some inequalities arising from differential effort or risk. Such tensions did not settle debates about justice. They demonstrated, for Elster, that Marxian critique needed explicit moral premises rather than reliance on historical inevitability as a substitute for justification, and that institutional alternatives had to be compared by feasible performance rather than by contrast with an idealised endpoint.

Steelmanning Elster requires acknowledging the power of his standard before registering its costs, because mechanism based explanation has exposed genuine weaknesses in social theories far beyond Marxism. Demanding a feedback loop for functional claims, a transmission path for structural influence, and an account of individual action beneath aggregate regularities disciplines speculation and reveals where rhetoric has replaced explanation. Critics responded that the standard, applied relentlessly, can fragment social reality into micro episodes and miss emergent properties, durable structures, and historically specific configurations that constrain action before any individual choice occurs. Others argued that rational choice models import assumptions about preference stability and strategic calculation that fit some settings poorly, especially where identities and commitments form through the very conflicts being modelled. Elster himself recognised limits to rational explanation, writing extensively on emotions, norms, and irrationality, which complicates any portrait of him as a narrow reductionist. Even so, Making Sense of Marx shifted burdens of proof inside Marxian scholarship. After its publication, appeals to dialectics, functionality, or historical necessity faced a standing challenge to produce mechanisms, evidence, and precise scope conditions, and analytical Marxism as a school partly defined itself through acceptance of that challenge, even among scholars who rejected many of his conclusions.

What survived examination, gathered together, amounts to a set of portable insights rather than a preserved doctrine. Attention to unintended consequences, to the gap between individual rationality and collective outcomes, to adaptive preferences under deprivation, to ideology as motivated belief, and to exploitation as a moral problem concerning power and distribution all remained available for further work, often in dialogue with economics, psychology, and political science. What was judged dead included dialectics as a separate logic, strong historical determinism, the labour theory of value as explanatory foundation, and functional explanation without demonstrated feedback. Between those poles lay a large territory of claims demoted from laws to hypotheses, plausible in specified contexts and false in others. That demotion may be the deepest sense in which reconstruction became assessment. Marx emerged neither as prophet nor as fraud, but as a brilliant, uneven theorist whose best observations survived separation from his system, while the system itself failed tests its reconstructor believed any social theory should face. Reversal of the overall verdict remains conceivable in principle: a future defence would need to supply the missing mechanisms, reconcile the value theory with price evidence, and show that structural concepts explain variation that individual level accounts systematically miss.

## Roemer and the formalisation of exploitation

John Roemer entered debates about exploitation from mathematical economics, bringing general equilibrium modelling, game theory, and a willingness to rebuild Marxian concepts from explicit assumptions rather than inherited definitions. A General Theory of Exploitation and Class, published in 1982, asked a deceptively simple question: what makes a distribution of benefits and burdens exploitative, and must that judgement depend on labour values? His answer relocated the centre of gravity. Exploitation, on the account developed across a sequence of models, arises from unequal ownership of productive assets, meaning land, capital, and other resources that determine bargaining power and income before any particular working day begins. Workers lacking assets must gain access to means of production owned by others, and the terms of that access allow owners to appropriate part of the social product without equivalent contribution. The moral sting traditionally attached to exploitation therefore survives translation into a property based language, while the explanatory machinery changes completely. Instead of tracing embodied labour through commodities, analysis examines endowments, feasible allocations, market or nonmarket rules, and counterfactual distributions. Roemer treated this relocation as clarification rather than betrayal, arguing that the ethical core of Marxian critique concerned unjust inequality in productive resources, a concern obscured rather than protected by attachment to a contested theory of value.

The property relations definition gave that intuition a precise test. A group could be described as exploited within a model if it would improve its position by withdrawing from the arrangement under specified rules, taking its per capita share of alienable productive assets, while the complementary group would become worse off under the same withdrawal. Variations on the test distinguished feudal, capitalist, and socialist forms by altering what withdraws, which assets count, and whether skills or status privileges enter the calculation. Feudal exploitation involved direct extra economic compulsion tying producers to lords. Capitalist exploitation operated through formally free exchange between asset owners and those compelled by assetlessness to sell labour power. A further category addressed unequal returns to skills, raising the uncomfortable possibility that differential rewards for scarce talents might also count as exploitative under a consistently asset based standard, a result Roemer explored rather than evaded. Precision here had a cost and a benefit. Moral argument could no longer hide inside descriptive vocabulary, because each verdict depended on openly stated withdrawal rules and distributive baselines. At the same time, critics could target exact assumptions, propose rival baselines, and show how conclusions shifted when inalienable capacities, public goods, or ecological constraints entered the model.

Why did Roemer judge the labour theory of value dispensable, and why did that judgement come from inside Marxian theory rather than from hostile dismissal? His models demonstrated that exploitation in the sense of unequal appropriation grounded in asset inequality could arise in economies described entirely through prices, preferences, technologies, and endowments, with labour values computed afterwards, if at all, as derived quantities carrying no independent explanatory load. Surplus could be identified in corn, steel, or any basic commodity under suitable assumptions, a point formalised in results sometimes summarised through the idea that exploitation is not unique to labour as a source. If every productive input can generate an analogous surplus measure, then singling out labour requires a moral or sociological argument rather than a purely accounting one. Roemer supplied that argument at the level of assets and coercion in a broad sense: people separated from productive resources face constrained choices even under legally voluntary contracts. Defenders of value theory replied that labour occupies a unique place because labour power is inseparable from the person, produced outside capitalist production, and central to class identity and workplace conflict. That reply retains considerable force as social theory. Reversal of the dispensability verdict would require showing that labour values explain prices, profits, or crisis tendencies in ways asset based models cannot reproduce, rather than merely restating their importance for lived experience.

### What changed when exploitation was stated through asset ownership?

Roemer shifted the definition from labour transferred in production to assets held before production began. Exploitation became a judgement about distributive inequality under specified property rules, testable through withdrawal counterfactuals, separable from prices and values, and linked to class through positions generated by unequal endowments in model economies.

Class entered the argument through a correspondence that Roemer derived rather than assumed. In model economies with competitive markets, optimising agents, and differential asset holdings, individuals sort themselves into positions that reproduce recognisable class structure: some hire labour, some work for themselves with their own assets, and some sell labour to others. The correspondence principle held that class position, defined by hiring and selling behaviour, aligns systematically with exploitation status defined through the property test, so wealthy asset holders tend to be exploiters and asset poor agents tend to be exploited, with intermediate strata occupying mixed positions. Establishing that alignment mattered because Marxian theory had long asserted a connection between class location and exploitation without always specifying conditions under which the connection holds. Formalisation exposed those conditions. Change assumptions about credit markets, risk, supervision costs, or access to education, and the neat mapping can loosen, generating hybrid positions, indebted owners, or highly paid sellers of scarce skills whose classification resists simple moral sorting. Far from weakening the enterprise, such complications showed what the models were for: they functioned as laboratories where intuitions about class could be stressed, qualified, and occasionally overturned. A reader following [Roemer's theory of exploitation](/2020/03/01/roemer-theory-of-exploitation/) can trace how each modelling choice alters who counts as exploited, which is precisely the transparency Roemer valued over doctrinal certainty.

Historical and institutional breadth formed another ambition of the general theory, since a definition tied exclusively to capitalist wage labour could not easily compare exploitation across feudal dues, collective farms, market socialism, or mixed economies. By varying property rules and withdrawal conditions, Roemer constructed a family of concepts suited to different social formations while preserving a common analytical grammar. Status exploitation addressed hierarchies where rank itself confers claims on surplus, as in bureaucratic systems where office rather than private ownership organises appropriation. Socialist exploitation, in his provocative usage, flagged inequalities arising from differential skills under public ownership, forcing egalitarian theory to decide whether talents should be treated as personal endowments beyond redistribution or as socially produced capacities subject to distributive claims. These extensions unsettled inherited vocabularies. Socialism could no longer be certified nonexploitative by ownership form alone, and capitalism could no longer be condemned through a single mechanism without examining asset distributions, welfare provisions, and exit options that modify dependence. Critics worried that conceptual expansion diluted the specificity of capitalist exploitation, especially the workplace domination and insecurity that asset measures capture only indirectly. Roemer could concede the descriptive point while maintaining the analytical one: a general theory maps a genus, and historical study must still specify species, mechanisms, and lived textures that formal models deliberately simplify.

Objections from several directions clarified both strength and limits of the formalisation programme. Philosophers questioned whether withdrawal counterfactuals track moral wrongness, since a group might benefit from secession arithmetic without suffering domination, deception, or rights violations in the actual arrangement, and since baselines for equal asset shares embed contested principles of justice. Economists questioned the distance between model economies with perfect information and economies marked by unemployment, monopoly, racial segmentation, gendered labour, and state violence, all of which shape asset accumulation and bargaining power in practice. Marxian critics argued that production relations involve authority, resistance, and extraction inside the labour process, dimensions flattened when exploitation becomes a property of distributions evaluated outside production. Roemer responded in later work by engaging distributive justice directly, treating exploitation as one historically important species of injustice rather than the master concept for all social criticism, and by exploring equality of opportunity as a framework for assigning responsibility without ignoring circumstance. Steelmanned, his project never claimed that equations exhaust social reality. It claimed that vague concepts cannot guide comparison or reform, and that any defence of exploitation language should survive translation into assumptions explicit enough for opponents to test, amend, or reject for stated reasons.

Assessment therefore turns on what formalisation is expected to deliver. As a replacement for historical sociology, the models are deliberately thin, populated by agents whose preferences and technologies are stipulated so that logical relationships can be isolated. As a clarification of conceptual commitments, the achievement remains substantial: exploitation separated from labour values, class linked to endowments through derivable correspondence, alternative social systems evaluated under shared distributive standards, and moral premises exposed where rhetoric once concealed them. The verdict that value theory is dispensable for defining exploitation has largely held within analytical Marxism, while debate continues over whether something essential, especially domination at work and the social centrality of labour, escapes the property framework. Evidence capable of reversing the broader assessment would include models where labour value categories generate predictions about distribution or conflict that asset based approaches miss, together with historical cases where withdrawal tests classify plainly coercive arrangements as nonexploitative. Until such demonstrations accumulate, Roemer stands as the school member who most radically preserved a Marxian conclusion by abandoning a Marxian premise, showing that unequal ownership, rather than embodied labour accounting, could carry the critical weight that exploitation was meant to bear.

## Wright and the empirical programme of class analysis

Erik Olin Wright took analytical Marxism in an empirical direction that neither textual reconstruction nor pure modelling could supply, asking whether class concepts could organise systematic evidence about real populations. Where Elster interrogated arguments and Roemer built model economies, Wright designed questionnaires, coded occupations, compared national datasets, and tested whether class locations predicted income, attitudes, and political allegiances in patterned ways. Classes, published in 1985, set out the theoretical architecture for that programme, refining a schema capable of handling managers, supervisors, experts, small employers, and self employed producers who fit awkwardly into a simple two class picture. The ambition was cumulative rather than rhetorical. Concepts would earn their place by generating classifications that could be applied consistently across surveys, countries, and periods, then judged by explanatory performance against rival schemas drawn from stratification research and occupational sociology. Wright treated Marxian class theory as a living research programme whose abstractions had to survive contact with messy labour markets, credentialed professions, public sector employment, and households where members occupied different positions. Failure would also be informative, revealing where concepts required revision, where mechanisms linking structure to consciousness were weak, and where alternative variables such as education, sector, or nationality carried greater explanatory weight than class in its Marxian sense.

Contradictory class locations supplied the conceptual key for mapping intermediate positions without abandoning relational class theory. Managers and supervisors sell labour power to employers, which places them alongside workers in one respect, yet they exercise authority derived from organisational assets, directing hiring, discipline, pace, and evaluation, which aligns their interests partly with owners. Experts and professionals possess skill assets, scarce knowledge certified through credentials, granting bargaining power, autonomy, and income advantages unavailable to routinised employees, while still lacking ownership of the enterprises where they work. Small employers own productive property and may hire a handful of employees, combining ownership with personal labour in proportions unlike either large capitalists or wage workers. Wright described such positions as contradictory because opposing class interests meet within a single job, producing ambivalent political tendencies that cannot be read off from income alone. A highly paid manager might defend corporate hierarchy while resenting shareholder control. A credentialed specialist might support egalitarian public services while protecting professional closure against uncredentialed competitors. The schema therefore explained instability and cross pressure as structural features rather than anomalies, giving survey analysis hypotheses richer than a claim that every nonowner shares identical interests or that gradations of status dissolve class altogether.

Assets formed the organising vocabulary of the mature schema, distinguishing what different groups control and what each form of control yields. Ownership of means of production defined the classical capitalist axis, separating employers from employees and the self employed from both. Organisational assets captured coordination and command within complex production systems, where authority over budgets, appointments, and work processes confers power even without legal title to property. Skill assets captured certified expertise that employers need and cannot easily monitor or replace, allowing experts to extract rents in the form of salaries, autonomy, and career security. Crossing these dimensions generated a typology of class locations within capitalism, twelve positions in the best known version, ranging from large employers through contradictory managerial and expert locations to proletarian positions lacking all three asset types. Each cell carried expectations about material interests and likely alliances, which surveys could examine rather than merely illustrate. Critics asked whether assets language stretched Marxian exploitation beyond recognition, importing Weberian concerns with market capacity and organisational power. Wright answered that exploitation itself could be reconceived around multiple asset bases, with organisational and skill exploitation supplementing property exploitation, a move that preserved relational antagonism while acknowledging how modern economies distribute advantage through bureaucracies and credential systems as well as ownership.

### How was class analysis turned into survey research?

Wright converted abstract class relations into survey categories by asking respondents about ownership, authority over others, control over work, credentials, and employment status. Coding rules placed each respondent within a class map, allowing statistical comparison of structure, attitudes, and consciousness across workplaces, industries, and national institutional settings over time.

Comparative survey projects turned the schema from a diagram into an international research infrastructure. The Class Structure and Class Consciousness programme fielded coordinated questionnaires in the United States and Sweden initially, then extended collaboration to additional countries including Canada, Norway, Britain, Japan, Australia, and others, with instruments adapted to national occupational systems while preserving comparable class coding. Coordination mattered theoretically. If class locations carried similar implications under different welfare states, union densities, and party systems, the case for class as a general structural concept strengthened. If national institutions overwhelmed location effects, Marxian theory would need to specify scope conditions and mediating organisations far more carefully. Wright and collaborators examined class distributions themselves as findings, showing that societies differed substantially in the size of managerial strata, self employment, expert employment, and working class positions, differences reflecting industrial composition, state employment, and historical paths of development. Such descriptive mapping had independent value, correcting impressions formed from national stereotypes or aggregate occupational statistics. It also disciplined debate about convergence under capitalism, since similar technologies coexisted with divergent class structures shaped by policy and collective organisation. Data archives, codebooks, and replication by other scholars extended the programme beyond a single author, embodying the analytical commitment to claims that independent researchers could inspect, contest, and refine.

Class consciousness formed the second major testing ground, and Wright approached it as a multidimensional phenomenon rather than a switch that populations either possess or lack. Questionnaires probed perceptions of class identity, beliefs about whether owners and employees share interests, support for unions and strikes, attitudes toward workplace control, and preferences concerning redistribution and public ownership. Statistical analysis then asked whether location within the class map predicted response patterns after accounting for income, education, age, gender, race, sector, and union membership, variables that rival explanations emphasise. Results typically supported a meaningful but incomplete class effect. Owners and managers leaned toward pro business positions, workers toward egalitarian and pro union positions, and contradictory locations fell between or divided internally, much as theory anticipated. Yet effects varied by country, question wording, and political period, and many respondents held inconsistent combinations of views that no single dimension could summarise. Wright reported such complications openly, treating consciousness as mediated by parties, unions, media, workplace experience, and national political discourse rather than secreted automatically by structure. That openness strengthened credibility. A programme willing to publish weak coefficients, measurement disputes, and revisions to its own typology demonstrated empirical seriousness, distinguishing analytical Marxism from traditions where unfavourable evidence could always be absorbed through reinterpretation.

Methodological debate accompanied every stage, because translating relational concepts into questionnaire items involves contestable decisions at each step. Authority could be measured by formal supervisory status, by actual influence over decisions, or by power to impose sanctions, and each measure classified some respondents differently. Household class posed persistent difficulties where partners occupied divergent locations, raising questions about individual coding, shared material interests, and the gendered assumptions embedded in older breadwinner models. Wright engaged feminist criticism on this terrain, acknowledging that class analysis centred on paid employment can marginalise unpaid domestic labour and dependence within households, and exploring supplementary approaches rather than dismissing the objection. Temporal change created further strain as service employment expanded, platforms reorganised control without conventional supervisors, and credential inflation altered what skill assets signified in particular labour markets. None of these difficulties was unique to Marxian research, yet each tested whether the schema described enduring relations or a historically bounded industrial order. Wright treated revision as normal science within a Marxian framework: concepts could be altered, dimensions added, and hypotheses abandoned while retaining core commitments to exploitation, material interests, and the causal significance of production relations. Reversal of his generally favourable assessment of class effects would follow if well measured locations repeatedly failed to predict outcomes beyond standard stratification variables across diverse settings.

Steelmanning rival approaches clarifies what the programme claimed and what it never attempted. Gradational schemas based on income or occupational prestige often predict attitudes smoothly and parsimoniously, and Wright conceded their utility for many questions, arguing instead that relational class captures mechanisms, antagonisms, and potential collective actors that gradients obscure. Weberian market situation concepts overlap substantially with skill and organisational assets, a convergence Wright regarded as productive synthesis rather than defeat, provided exploitation and production relations remained visible. Ethnographic research reveals meanings, informal resistance, and shop floor cultures that surveys flatten into response categories, so the strongest reading of the programme treats quantitative mapping and qualitative study as complements operating at different resolutions. Within analytical Marxism, Wright supplied the empirical wing that Elster demanded and Roemer bracketed, testing whether concepts clarified on paper corresponded to structured variation in lived societies. His verdict on the tradition he inherited was constructive but conditional: class analysis deserved continuation because class locations demonstrably organised life chances and attitudes, while particular Marxian claims about polarisation, consciousness, and historical transformation required qualification under evidence. That conditional defence, revisable through specified findings, may be the clearest expression of the analytical ethos applied to empirical sociology, ambitious in scope and accountable in detail.

## The trajectory table: the school in four columns

A collective intellectual movement rarely leaves a record tidy enough to summarise in a grid, and the September Group, later called Analytical Marxism, left a particularly untidy one, composed of monographs that revised their predecessors, seminars that sharpened disagreements, and prefaces that quietly withdrew commitments once defended with vigour. A table imposes order on that untidiness by a deliberate act of compression. Four columns of substance, preceded by a name, fix two moments and place a book between them, then add a retrospective gloss in the voice of the person whose movement is being charted. Compression of that kind does not explain anything by itself. What it does is make a pattern visible at a glance that would otherwise emerge only after long sequential reading, and it makes the pattern available for dispute on equal terms, because every reader confronts identical rows and can point to a specific cell when contesting an interpretation.

Choice of the opening date carries an argument within it. The year 1979 marks the first meeting of the group and a moment when defence remained a plausible description of a shared project, even though the participants differed sharply in temperament, training, and political allegiance. G. A. Cohen had published a systematic defence of historical materialism a year earlier. Elster's approach to Marx treated the arguments as deserving reconstruction with tools drawn from rational choice theory and the philosophy of science. John Roemer sought a formal restatement of exploitation and class that could survive contact with general equilibrium modelling. Erik Olin Wright pursued empirical investigation of class structure within advanced capitalist societies, treating concepts as instruments for measurement rather than slogans. Placing those four starting points side by side prevents a common distortion, namely the retrospective projection of later divergence onto an imagined initial unanimity. Divergence was present from the beginning, contained within a willingness to treat Marxian questions as answerable by argument.

Selection of the closing moment matters in a parallel way. The middle of the 1990s does not mark a formal dissolution, since no charter existed to dissolve, and it does not mark a simultaneous conversion, since each member moved at a different pace and to a different destination. Rather, that interval captures a point by which published statements of reorientation had accumulated to a degree that could no longer be described as local revision. Cohen had redirected attention toward egalitarian political philosophy and the justification of distributive principles. Elster had declared central Marxist theses dead and denied the existence of a distinctively Marxist social science. Roemer had urged abandonment of Marx's theory of value while retaining exploitation as a property based concept and turning toward institutional design for market socialism. Wright had shifted emphasis from mapping class structures toward designing alternatives under the heading of real utopias. A reader who grasps why the closing column gathers statements from a span of years, rather than a single calendar year, grasps something essential about how intellectual change actually proceeds, unevenly and through publication lags.

Between the two dated columns stands the principal work, and its placement performs a specific function within the logic of the display. A principal work anchors a trajectory to a text that can be checked. Claims about a starting position might otherwise float free, sustained by reputation or by selective memory, whereas a named book with a publication date invites verification and resists exaggeration in either direction. Cohen in 1978 offered functional explanation of the relation between productive forces and production relations, defended with analytical care. Elster in 1985 subjected a vast range of Marxian claims to piecemeal assessment, accepting some mechanisms and rejecting others. Roemer in 1982 rebuilt exploitation theory on foundations that did not depend on labour embodied accounting. Wright in 1985 attempted to render class locations empirically tractable across differing national contexts. Each volume represented, at the time of appearance, the strongest available statement of a research programme, which is precisely why later movement away from positions stated there carries evidential weight.

Final column material introduces a different kind of evidence altogether, since self description is neither neutral report nor mere rationalisation, but a hybrid that the historian must handle with care. When a scholar states that questions of justice displaced a philosophy of history, or that a salvage operation shrank under examination, or that a foundation failed while a concept survived, or that mapping structures revealed a need to design alternatives, the statement records both a fact about intellectual biography and an interpretation offered by the person best placed to know the sequence of his own reading and doubt, yet also most invested in presenting that sequence as coherent. Tables in reference works often suppress that tension by paraphrasing the final column into editorial summary. Retaining the substance of what each member said, in concise form, preserves the tension and reminds the reader that trajectories are narrated by their subjects as well as documented by their publications.

| Member | Position held in 1979 | Principal work | Position held by the mid-1990s | What they said about the change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G. A. Cohen | Defender of historical materialism | Karl Marx's Theory of History, 1978 | Turned to egalitarian political philosophy; a later preface stated he was no longer confident historical materialism is true | The questions of justice had displaced the philosophy of history |

Reading across a single row yields a miniature biography of an argument. Cohen begins with confidence in a theory of history driven by development of productive forces and ends by relocating intellectual energy to normative questions about equality, luck, and responsibility, a relocation that leaves the earlier theory neither refuted in a single demonstration nor retained as a framework. Elster begins with sympathy disciplined by methodological individualism and ends with a verdict of extensive failure, yet the verdict arrives thesis by thesis rather than as wholesale denunciation, which complicates any attempt to portray the endpoint as repudiation pure and simple. Roemer begins by formalising and ends by amputating a component long regarded as load bearing, while insisting that the structure can stand on different foundations, a combination of radical surgery and continuity of purpose. Wright begins with taxonomy and measurement and ends with design, retaining an empirical sensibility while altering the question from what class structure exists to what institutional arrangements could realise egalitarian values.

Reading down a column yields a different insight, comparative rather than biographical. The opening column shows four distinct relations to a common inheritance: defence, reconstruction, formalisation, and empirical operationalisation are not interchangeable activities, and they expose their practitioners to different kinds of disappointment. Defence risks discovering that the defended theory lacks support. Reconstruction risks finding that the reconstructed remainder no longer resembles the original. Formalisation risks proving that a cherished result depends on assumptions that can be replaced by more general ones with different implications. Empirical operationalisation risks generating findings that redirect attention from structure to agency and design. Viewed in that light, divergence in the closing column ceases to look like a puzzle requiring a single cause. Varied starting relations to Marxian theory generated varied points of strain, and the closing positions register those strains faithfully.

Attention to what the grid omits protects against overreading. Between principal work and closing position lie intervening books, articles, replies to critics, and political events of considerable magnitude, including the collapse of communist regimes in Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union between 1989 and 1991. A compressed display cannot allocate causal weight among internal argumentative pressure, external political shock, generational change, and the ordinary evolution of research interests across a career. Nor can a grid register intensity: two members might occupy verbally similar closing positions while differing greatly in regret, relief, or residual loyalty. Nor can it register influence running in the opposite direction, since each member continued to shape debates within political philosophy, social theory, and sociology long after ceasing to identify with a Marxist programme. Acknowledging those omissions is not a concession that the display fails. Rather, acknowledgement defines the legitimate use of the device, as a map of stated positions at chosen moments, valuable precisely because its limits are visible.

### How should the trajectory table be read without forcing it?

The table should be read as a record of stated positions at two dated moments, with the principal work as an anchor between them, and the final column as self description rather than verdict, so comparison reveals movement without imposing a single cause upon four distinct intellectual paths taken separately.

Careful reading therefore proceeds slowly, returning from closing position to opening commitment and asking what specific argument, finding, or reconsideration intervened, instead of invoking temperament or fashion as all purpose solvents. Cohen invites attention to a distinction between explaining history and justifying distribution, and to the possibility that excellence in the second enterprise does not require commitment to the first. Elster invites attention to standards of explanation, especially suspicion of functional accounts unsupported by mechanisms, and to the cumulative effect of applying those standards without exemption. Roemer invites attention to the separability of normative concern about exploitation from a particular theory of value, and to the constructive ambitions that separability released. Wright invites attention to the gap between diagnosis and prescription, and to a conviction that rigorous description of class relations, pursued honestly, exposes how little description alone contributes to transformation.


## The undecidable trajectory: the claim at the centre

At the centre of any serious assessment stands a claim that can be named, stated in full, and contested on its merits, rather than a mood of suspicion toward either the school or its critics. The claim is the undecidable trajectory: the school began as a defence and ended in abandonment, and honest inquiry following the arguments and a method biased against its object would produce exactly the same trajectory, so the history of the school cannot settle whether its method was appropriate. Every element of that formulation does work. Defence names the initial orientation without exaggerating initial agreement. Abandonment names the closing orientation without implying that every concept was discarded or that every member travelled the full distance. Honest inquiry names one candidate explanation, in which arguments were weighed on their merits and found wanting. The rival candidate, by contrast, locates bias in the method itself, holding that standards of clarity, formalisation, and mechanism based explanation, applied to a tradition built around dialectical and holistic forms of reasoning, predetermined a negative outcome. Undecidability names the relation between the evidence and the explanations: the observed history fits both, and fits both equally well at the level of outward sequence.

Understanding why the outward sequence fits both explanations requires attention to the granularity of prediction. A defender of the honesty reading predicts that close examination of historical materialism would reveal unsupported functional claims, that reconstruction of exploitation would reveal dependence on a dispensable theory of value, that empirical class analysis would reveal complexity resistant to classical categories, and that the cumulative result would be departure. A defender of the bias reading predicts precisely the same sequence, step for step, while offering a different account of why each step occurred: functional claims failed because the standards admitted only mechanism based explanations, exploitation survived in altered form because formalisation translated a relational critique into a distributive one, and classical categories fractured because analytical decomposition dissolved the totalities those categories were designed to capture. When two explanations generate identical predictions about the sequence of publications, prefaces, and programmatic statements, that sequence cannot discriminate between them, however vividly it illustrates the outcome that both predicted.

Discrimination would require evidence of a different kind, located not in the direction of travel but in the relation between method and result within particular episodes. Did a member ever reach a conclusion that favoured Marxian claims despite methodological pressure in the opposite direction, and did the method accommodate that conclusion without strain? Did a member ever abandon a claim for reasons that the method compelled, as distinct from reasons that the method merely allowed a person already losing confidence to articulate? Did critics employing different methods, examining the same texts and evidence during the same period, converge on similar verdicts, or did verdict track method with suspicious regularity across independent cases? Questions of that form point toward evidence that could separate the explanations. The trajectory table, the publication chronology, and the retrospective prefaces, taken together, do not supply answers to them, because each document in that record is compatible with both stories about its own production.

Consider the strongest exhibit usually offered for the honesty reading, namely the gradual and reluctant character of several departures. Reluctance feels like evidence of good faith overcoming attachment, and gradualism feels like evidence of piecemeal assessment rather than wholesale prejudice. Yet a biased method would also produce gradual and apparently reluctant departure in practitioners who began as sympathisers, because bias in a method operates through successive local judgements, each defensible in isolation, rather than through a single dramatic repudiation. A scholar does not experience methodological predisposition as prejudice from within. The experience is of problems accumulating, of distinctions multiplying, of charitable readings becoming harder to sustain, of neighbouring research programmes offering cleaner problems and publishable results. That phenomenology is shared between the scholar who discovers genuine error and the scholar whose tools systematically misrepresent the object under study. Appeals to sincerity therefore cannot break the tie, since sincerity is common ground.

Consider next the exhibit most often advanced on behalf of the bias reading, namely the selectivity of survival. What survived analytical treatment tended to be what could be restated as claims about individuals, incentives, property distributions, and equilibrium outcomes, whereas what perished tended to involve historical tendencies, structural contradictions, and forms of explanation that treated social wholes as more than aggregates. That pattern looks like a fingerprint of the method. Yet the honesty reading accommodates the pattern with equal ease, because a tradition might genuinely contain a mixture of defensible claims that happen to be individualistic in form and indefensible claims that happen to be holistic in form. Survival sorted by translatability would then reflect the actual distribution of truth within the inheritance rather than distortion introduced by translation. Pattern in what survived, like pattern in the order of departure, underdetermines its own explanation.

The defeater for the judgement of undecidability must therefore be stated explicitly, as a condition whose satisfaction would reverse the judgement. The judgement would be defeated by a demonstration that a specific abandonment followed from a step the method required rather than merely permitted, or by a case where a member reached a conclusion the method disfavoured, either of which would dissolve the equivalence of the rival accounts. Required rather than permitted is the pivotal distinction. A method permits many moves that a practitioner might make for independent reasons, including loss of political hope after 1989 or attraction to a flourishing neighbouring field. A method requires a move when refusal would violate its own standards as the practitioner understood and applied them elsewhere. Showing requirement in a particular episode would connect outcome to method with a tightness that the honesty reading could not easily reproduce. Conversely, a conclusion reached against the grain of methodological preference would show the method failing to predispose at the very point where predisposition should have operated, weakening the bias reading in a way that mere professions of open mindedness cannot.

### Why can the trajectory not decide between its two readings?

Both explanations predict abandonment after close examination, one because examination exposed error, the other because examination applied standards alien to the object, so the shared outward sequence of defence, reconstruction, and departure cannot by itself show which mechanism operated in any particular case or across the school as a whole.

Elaboration of that answer clarifies what kind of historical claim undecidability is, and what kind it is not. Undecidability here is not a claim that evidence is scarce, although evidence about private reasoning is always incomplete, nor a claim that participants deceived themselves or others, nor a counsel of despair about intellectual history in general. The assertion concerns a specific body of evidence, the public trajectory from defence to abandonment, and a specific pair of explanations that were constructed, in effect, to account for that trajectory. Because each explanation was tailored to the same outcome, citing the outcome as support for either begs the question. Fresh evidence could alter the situation, as could a more refined version of either explanation that generated divergent predictions about details not yet examined, such as the treatment of a particular argument in seminar discussion or the handling of a counterexample in draft. Undecidability is thus a provisional verdict about a defined evidential base, not a metaphysical doctrine about the impossibility of knowledge.

Refinement also exposes a temptation to dissolve the problem by splitting the school into cases. Perhaps Cohen illustrates honest inquiry while Elster illustrates predisposition, or Roemer illustrates creative salvage while Wright illustrates political redirection, so that no single verdict fits all four rows. Splitting may well be correct as biography. Yet splitting does not rescue the trajectory as evidence, because assignment of individual cases to explanations would still require episode level evidence of the kind described in the defeater, rather than inference from endpoints. A mixed verdict reached by examining arguments in detail would be a triumph of detailed scholarship. A mixed verdict reached by distributing labels across rows according to prior sympathy would merely conceal undecidability beneath variety. The distinction matters for how research should proceed: toward archives, drafts, correspondence, and close reconstruction of particular inferences, and away from further contemplation of the summary pattern that prompted the inquiry.

Another temptation involves treating later fruitfulness as a retrospective test. Analytical Marxism generated distinguished work in egalitarian theory, social choice, and institutional design, and that fruitfulness is sometimes invoked as vindication of the path taken, on the ground that good trees are known by their fruit. Fruitfulness, however, is predicted by both explanations as well. Honest inquiry that abandons error should liberate energy for better questions. A biased method that dissolves its original object should also liberate energy, channelling talented scholars toward questions their tools handle well. Conversely, the loss of a distinctive Marxist social science might count as cost under one reading and as overdue recognition under the other. Evaluation of consequences therefore inherits the same symmetry as evaluation of origins, and cannot be used to settle the question from which the evaluation proceeds.

Stating the claim with precision thus performs a disciplinary function for the remainder of the discussion. Any argument that purports to favour one reading must identify evidence that the rival reading predicts less well, and must show the differential prediction explicitly rather than relying on narrative plausibility. Plausibility is cheap when a skilled narrator controls selection and emphasis. A narrative of courageous truth telling and a narrative of methodological colonisation can both be rendered plausible from the same bibliography. The standard set by the defeater is higher: find compulsion where only permission was assumed, or find resistance where predisposition should have prevailed. Until evidence meeting that standard appears, the honest position is suspension of judgement about appropriateness of method, combined with full acknowledgement of the historical fact that the trajectory occurred and demands to be recorded accurately.


## Two readings of one history: honesty and predisposition

Presented at full strength, the first reading portrays a group of scholars who took Marx seriously enough to test Marxian claims by the best standards of argument they knew, and who reported the results without flinching when results turned negative. On that reading, clarity was a form of respect. Vague formulations that had sheltered weak arguments behind rhetorical grandeur were restated precisely, and precise restatement revealed that some claims were false, others were true but trivial, and others depended on functional explanations that lacked mechanisms capable of bearing explanatory weight. Historical materialism, in the strong form defended in 1978, required confidence that productive forces tend to develop and that production relations are explained by their tendency to promote such development. Sustained scrutiny exposed gaps between tendency claims and evidence, between explanatory sketches and confirmed mechanisms, and between the theory as stated and the theory as charitably repaired. Departure followed discovery. Political philosophy benefited because questions about equality and exploitation, liberated from a doubtful philosophy of history, could be addressed directly with tools adequate to normative argument.

Generosity toward opponents strengthens that reading further. Members of the group did not begin as hostile auditors seeking a pretext for dismissal. Several began with political commitment and with admiration for the explanatory ambition of Marxian theory, and several paid a professional and personal price for association with a tradition that carried stigma in their disciplines. A predisposed debunker would have chosen a cheaper target and a faster route. Instead, years were spent constructing the strongest versions of claims that were eventually relinquished, a pattern visible in the sheer constructive labour of the principal works. Roemer did not dismiss exploitation; a new foundation for the concept was built. Wright did not dismiss class; categories were refined and tested against survey data across multiple countries. Elster did not dismiss Marx as a thinker; hundreds of pages were devoted to separating living insights from dead doctrine. Cohen did not dismiss equality; an entire later career elaborated egalitarian commitments with greater rigour. Continuity of concern beneath discontinuity of doctrine suggests inquiry governed by problems rather than by animus.

External corroboration can also be marshalled. Scholars outside the group, working with different methods and different political sympathies, raised overlapping difficulties during the same decades: historians questioned sweeping tendency laws, economists questioned labour embodied value accounting, sociologists questioned simple mappings from class position to consciousness and action. Convergence among independent investigators ordinarily counts as evidence that an object, rather than an instrument, is responsible for a result. Political events supplied a further independent check, since regimes claiming Marxist legitimacy exhibited economic stagnation, political repression, and eventual collapse on a scale that any empirically responsive theory needed to absorb. On the honesty reading, Analytical Marxists absorbed those lessons earlier and more candidly than defenders who insulated doctrine by reinterpretation. Abandonment appears, in that light, as the predictable fate of a research programme whose core claims failed tests that the programme itself, at its best, had reason to accept.

Presented at full strength, the second reading portrays a method carrying substantive commitments that were never neutral, applied to an object constituted by contrary commitments, and producing the result toward which the method predisposed from the outset. Analytical philosophy of the relevant period prized clarity understood as definitional precision, argument reconstructed as premises and conclusions, and suspicion toward concepts that resisted decomposition. Rational choice modelling prized methodological individualism, strategic interaction, and equilibrium analysis. Philosophy of science influenced by those currents prized mechanisms operating at the level of individual action and treated functional explanation as a promissory note rarely redeemed. None of those commitments is a mere technique. Together they encode a picture of social reality as an aggregate of individuals, preferences, endowments, and rules, a picture within which claims about totalities, contradictions, and historically specific modes of production appear suspect before examination begins. A tradition whose central insights concern the shaping of preferences by social relations, the appearance of systemic outcomes unintended by any agent, and the transformation of wholes through internal tension was therefore being judged by standards that defined its characteristic moves as errors.

Selectivity of attention deepens that reading. Questions that analytical tools handle elegantly, such as the logical structure of exploitation claims or the design of allocation mechanisms, received sustained development, whereas questions that tools handle awkwardly, such as ideology as lived misrecognition or crisis as systemic disequilibrium, faded from the agenda without ever receiving a decisive refutation. Disappearance by neglect leaves a different trace from disappearance by disproof, yet the trajectory table records both identically as movement away from classical positions. Translation effects compound the problem. When exploitation becomes a claim about unequal distribution of alienable assets, something recognisable survives, but the specifically Marxian emphasis on production, domination in the workplace, and extraction of surplus labour recedes. When class becomes a location within a distribution of assets and credentials, empirical precision increases while the relational and antagonistic core of the concept thins. A critic can argue that the school did not discover that Marxian concepts were empty; rather, successive acts of translation replaced those concepts with neighbours that analytical methods could process, and the neighbours then failed or succeeded on terms foreign to the originals.

Institutional context adds force without reducing the argument to sociology of knowledge. Prestige within Anglophone philosophy and economics during the 1980s rewarded formalisation, individualism, and proximity to mainstream research programmes, while penalising association with traditions regarded as scientifically disreputable. Careers are built inside such reward structures, and even scholars of integrity absorb signals about which problems are promising and which vocabularies sound dated. Political defeat on a world scale reinforced those signals: after 1989, defence of Marxian categories carried costs that had little to do with validity and much to do with association. On the predisposition reading, the trajectory records adaptation to an intellectual environment as much as discovery about an object, and the retrospective language of theses dying or salvage shrinking naturalises decisions that were also, in part, accommodations. None of that requires bad faith. Predisposition operates most effectively through sincere judgement, by shaping what strikes a competent practitioner as a serious problem, a plausible mechanism, or a worthwhile next project.

Set side by side at full strength, the two readings reveal why the article refuses to choose between them on the evidence of the trajectory alone. Each reading explains the defence with which the story opens, the rigour of the principal works, the gradual character of departure, the selectivity of survival, and the fruitfulness of later careers. Each reading can absorb apparent counterevidence by redescribing it: reluctance becomes either honesty overcoming attachment or bias working through successive local judgements; convergence with outsiders becomes either independent corroboration or diffusion of a dominant methodological culture; translation becomes either charitable reconstruction or quiet replacement of the object. A contest in which every exhibit can be claimed by both sides, with comparable ingenuity and comparable fidelity to the record, is not settled by accumulating further exhibits of the same type. Settlement requires an exhibit of a different type, one whose probability differs sharply depending on which reading holds.

### What would break the symmetry between the two readings?

Asymmetry would arise if one departure were shown to depend on a methodological step that could not be refused without inconsistency, or if one participant defended a Marxian conclusion that analytical preferences should have discouraged, since either finding would separate compulsion and resistance from mere compatibility with hindsight.

Restating the condition in different words sharpens its practical meaning for future research. Investigators need a documented episode in which analytical standards, consistently applied, left a practitioner no honourable alternative to surrendering a Marxian commitment, where surrender can be traced to an inference rather than to mood, fashion, or political despair. A contrasting discovery would be an episode in which a practitioner, following the same standards with the same consistency, arrived at a robustly Marxian conclusion that those standards should have made unattractive, and retained that conclusion despite its methodological inconvenience. Either discovery would introduce asymmetry into a record that otherwise balances. The first would incriminate method as an active cause rather than a permissive backdrop. The second would exonerate method from the charge of guaranteeing negative outcomes, by showing outcomes escaping predisposition under controlled conditions.

Absence of such episodes in the familiar record should not be confused with evidence that no such episodes exist. Familiar records are built from published books and retrospective prefaces, genres that smooth inference into narrative and rarely preserve the moment at which an alternative conclusion was genuinely available and foreclosed by a methodological rule. Drafts, correspondence, seminar records, and detailed comparison with contemporaries who shared the object but not the method offer richer terrain. Comparison across methods is especially promising, because a Marxian claim that survived analytical treatment while failing under a rival approach, or failed only where a distinctive analytical requirement was invoked, would localise causation in a way that a single tradition of inquiry cannot. Until research of that granularity is carried out and survives criticism, responsible historiography holds both readings open, teaches the trajectory as a fact, and teaches the explanation of the trajectory as an unresolved problem in the philosophy of social science.

Refusal to choose is therefore not evasion, and it is not a disguised verdict in favour of whichever reading a particular audience prefers. Suspension has content: a denial that direction of travel validates method, a refusal to treat that same movement as vindication of doctrine, and an insistence that intellectual history distinguish between recording change and explaining change. The four scholars in the table changed their minds, or changed their questions, or changed the foundations beneath retained concerns, in ways that reshaped several disciplines. Whether those changes constitute a model of inquiry working as inquiry should, or a cautionary tale about tools remaking their object in their own image, remains a question that the changes themselves cannot answer. Holding that question open, with the defeater specified and the evidence described honestly, is the most faithful conclusion that the record assembled here will support.

## The abandonments in sequence: what was given up, and in which order

Understanding the later history of analytical Marxism requires resisting a familiar temptation, namely the temptation to compress a long divergence into a single dramatic break. No collective renunciation took place, no joint statement closed the project, and no meeting ended with participants agreeing that a shared enterprise had failed. Instead, particular claims were examined, found wanting under standards the participants themselves had championed, and then set aside in books and articles spread across roughly fifteen years. Each retreat arrived with reasons attached. Readers could inspect the reasoning, dispute premises, propose repairs, or follow the author toward a different problem. That manner of departure matters as much as the destination, because a tradition that prizes clarity about inference leaves a distinctive kind of record when confidence weakens: the record consists of arguments, not gestures.

Chronology also resists simplification because different members surrendered different commitments at different speeds. Jon Elster pressed hardest against functional explanation deployed without an account of the mechanisms that might sustain it. John Roemer rebuilt the theory of exploitation while detaching that theory from dependence on embodied labour as the source of value. G. A. Cohen defended historical materialism with unusual ingenuity before conceding, in a later preface, a loss of confidence in its truth. Erik Olin Wright moved class analysis toward institutional comparison and design while retaining more of the original sociological ambition than several colleagues retained. Treating these trajectories as identical would erase precisely the variation that explains why the group could continue meeting while agreement thinned.

Consider first the methodological surrender that gathered force during the 1980s. Cohen had given functional explanation a rigorous philosophical defence, arguing that facts about consequences can explain the persistence of institutions when an appropriate feedback loop obtains. Elster accepted that consequence explanations have a legitimate place in biology, where natural selection supplies a known filtering process, yet he demanded an equivalent process in social inquiry. Where no selector, reinforcement pattern, or intentional agent could be identified, talk of institutions existing because they served capital struck him as an explanatory promissory note. Critics sympathetic to Cohen replied that a pattern can be explanatory before its mechanism is fully known, pointing to episodes in science where robust regularities preceded causal accounts. Elster granted the general point but denied that it rescued the Marxist usages at issue, since the alleged regularities themselves were often underspecified and the feedback stories were rarely tested.

That dispute cut deep because it concerned not a peripheral slogan but a style of reasoning embedded in classical statements of historical materialism. To say that legal forms, state structures, or ideologies persist through their contribution to reproducing relations of production invites a functional reading. Once the demand for mechanisms is accepted, each such claim must be translated into statements about actors, incentives, sanctions, learning, and unintended aggregation. Some translations proved possible. Others dissolved into vague attribution of purpose to a system. The strongest version of the critique therefore did not ban functional language outright; it demoted functional language to a heuristic that earns explanatory standing only after a causal pathway has been supplied. A verdict in favour of that demotion would be reversed if defenders produced well confirmed feedback mechanisms covering the central cases, rather than isolated illustrations assembled after the fact.

Retreat from Marx's theory of value followed a partly separate path, driven by problems internal to economics rather than by philosophy of science alone. Roemer explored models in which exploitation could be defined through unequal access to productive assets, using general equilibrium tools and counterfactual tests about withdrawal from an economy with a specified endowment. Within such models, labour transfer appeared as one route to unequal advantage among others, and the moral charge against capitalist distribution no longer depended on proving that labour alone creates value. Sraffian critics, transformation problem debates, and the difficulty of reducing heterogeneous skilled labour to a common unit had already placed heavy pressure on the older doctrine. Roemer drew a practical conclusion from that pressure: egalitarian criticism becomes stronger, not weaker, when it stops resting on a contested theory of price formation.

Elster reached a broader destination from converging lines of attack. Surveying theories of productive forces, class formation, ideology, and revolution, he concluded that several of the tradition's central theses are dead and that no distinctively Marxist social science survives once acceptable methods are imposed. Sympathetic readers should steelman that judgement before resisting it. Elster was not claiming that Marx had asked no fruitful questions, nor that exploitation, domination, and class power are illusions. He was claiming that fruitful questions receive answers from game theory, rational choice modelling, causal inference, and ordinary historiography, none of which belongs uniquely to a Marxist research programme. On that reading, what remains valuable can be absorbed into social science generally, while what remains distinctive fails evidential or logical tests. A reversal of that verdict would require a body of explanations that is both recognisably Marxist in content and superior to rival accounts on shared criteria of confirmation.

Historical materialism as a confident theory of history held longer than either functional explanation in its unmechanised form or Marx's theory of value. Several features protected it. Its central claims could be stated at varying levels of strength, from a bold thesis about the primacy of productive forces to a modest insistence that economic organisation constrains political and legal possibilities. Defenders could abandon technological determinism while retaining an explanatory priority for production, or abandon laws of development while retaining an account of why class conflict recurs. Flexibility of that kind is not automatically evasive; mature theories often survive by distinguishing core commitments from auxiliary assumptions. Yet flexibility also delays falsification, because every disappointment can be absorbed by weakening a formulation whose earlier strength had supplied much of the appeal.

### Which central claims were abandoned first, and which held longest?

Functional explanation without specified mechanisms fell earliest under sustained criticism, followed by Marx's theory of value as an account of price and profit. Confidence in historical materialism as a comprehensive theory of history eroded more gradually, because its claims were broader, looser, and easier to reformulate defensively.

Gradual erosion should not be mistaken for mere stubbornness. Cohen in particular had invested philosophical labour in making historical materialism precise enough to assess, distinguishing the development thesis carefully from the primacy thesis and clarifying what functional claims commit a theorist to accept. Precision made retreat more intelligible when it came, since readers could identify which proposition had lost support. Less careful defences often leave posterity guessing whether a theory was refuted, reinterpreted, or simply abandoned through fashion. Here the sequence is unusually legible. An explanatory form was challenged, a value theory was detached from a theory of exploitation, a philosophy of history was weakened through successive qualifications, and a founder eventually recorded diminished confidence in language that left little room for misunderstanding.

Equally instructive is what did not happen during the staggered withdrawals. Participants continued to cite one another, attend the September meetings, and argue across widening differences without treating disagreement as betrayal. Analytical habits outlived doctrinal convergence. Commitment to explicit premises, counterfactual discipline, and models whose assumptions can be inspected remained shared even as conclusions separated. That persistence explains why descriptions of collapse fit the evidence poorly. A school can lose a common creed while retaining a common intellectual temperament, and analytical Marxism did exactly that through the period ending in the mid 1990s. By then Elster had moved toward a general theory of social mechanisms and rationality, Roemer toward institutional proposals for market socialism, Cohen toward egalitarian moral philosophy, and Wright toward comparative class analysis joined to democratic design. Divergence was the outcome, argued step by step in print.

Whether the abandonments amount to a refutation of Marxism as a whole depends on how widely that label is drawn, a question the participants themselves answered differently. If Marxism requires Marx's theory of value, functional explanation of superstructures, and a law governed philosophy of history held together as one system, then the cumulative retreat is devastating. If Marxism names a looser family of concerns about class power, unequal assets, ideology, and emancipation, then much survived under other descriptions. A balanced verdict favours the narrower reading for theory and the wider reading for influence: the integrated system lost its defenders, while several problems it foregrounded migrated into mainstream inquiry. That verdict would change if a reconstructed system reconnected those problems through confirmed mechanisms and outperformed piecemeal rivals across a broad range of historical cases.

Sequence also shaped reception among younger scholars entering the field during those years. A student encountering Elster first might treat analytical Marxism as a demolition exercise whose positive residue lay in methods borrowed from economics and philosophy. A student encountering Wright first might see a research programme still confident about mapping class structures empirically. A student encountering Roemer first might regard formal modelling as a route toward a renovated theory of exploitation. All three impressions captured part of the truth, and their coexistence made the label increasingly difficult to police. Editors and reviewers continued using the label because a recognisable network of people, meetings, and debates existed, even while the doctrinal centre that had once justified a shared name kept thinning under scrutiny from within.

Another feature of the staggered pattern deserves emphasis: reversals were rarely total even for the figure who changed position most dramatically. Elster discarded grand claims about history while preserving admiration for particular Marxian observations about ideology, self deception, and the unintended consequences of collective action. Roemer discarded a theory of value while preserving a conviction that capitalist ownership generates morally suspect advantage. Cohen set aside his earlier confidence about the theory of history while preserving egalitarian convictions with greater intensity. Preservation alongside surrender complicates any simple narrative of apostasy. What changed was the warrant offered for beliefs, the scope claimed for explanations, and the willingness to let a conclusion fall when supporting arguments failed.

## The founder and egalitarian political philosophy

Among the individual trajectories, the path taken by G. A. Cohen carries singular significance because Cohen had founded the group and supplied its most celebrated defence of historical materialism. His book on the theory of history, published in 1978, treated Marx as a philosopher whose claims deserved reconstruction with the tools of analytic philosophy rather than reverence or dismissal. Cohen clarified concepts, separated theses that commentators had run together, and defended functional explanation against charges of illegitimacy. When a scholar of that standing revises a position, the revision cannot be waved away as misunderstanding. Cohen knew the strongest version of the doctrine, had built much of that strength himself, and therefore spoke from inside the argument when confidence later faltered.

The turn toward egalitarian political philosophy grew from questions that historical materialism had left underdeveloped. Classical Marxism often treated distributive justice with suspicion, presenting principles of distribution as reflections of a mode of production and predicting that scarcity would recede under communism. Cohen pressed on an ambiguity within that inheritance. Capitalism could be condemned as exploitative, yet exploitation claims seemed to rely on a background judgement about what workers are owed and why appropriation by owners is wrongful. Making that judgement explicit required engagement with moral philosophy on its own terms. Rather than treating normative argument as decoration on a scientific theory, Cohen began to ask which principles of equality and freedom could withstand scrutiny when stated plainly and tested against hard cases.

Engagement with John Rawls supplied both a foil and a resource. Rawls had placed distributive institutions at the centre of political philosophy, defending inequalities only when they benefit the least advantaged under fair conditions. Cohen admired the ambition while challenging the way incentives are treated within the difference principle. If talented people demand large rewards as a condition of deploying their abilities productively, a society may satisfy a Rawlsian rule at the level of basic structure while tolerating an ethos of self seeking that Cohen regarded as incompatible with genuine equality. His critique distinguished principles that govern institutional design from principles that should guide personal choice within institutions. That distinction shifted debate away from a simple contest between market and plan, toward the moral culture that any distributive scheme presupposes.

Ronald Dworkin offered a different challenge through an attempt to make equality sensitive to responsibility. On a luck egalitarian view, disadvantages arising from brute bad luck call for compensation, whereas disadvantages traceable to choices for which agents can fairly be held responsible stand on different footing. Cohen found the ambition attractive because it promises to honour equality without ignoring agency, yet he exposed severe difficulties in drawing the relevant line. A person whose expensive tastes were cultivated through upbringing, social expectation, or misfortune may not deserve the full penalty that a crude choice based account imposes. Conversely, insulating every outcome from responsibility threatens to dissolve incentives and personal accountability altogether. Cohen explored these tensions with characteristic patience, often granting an opponent the most favourable formulation before identifying the point at which intuition rebels.

Luck egalitarian debates also revealed a harsher implication that Cohen helped bring into focus. If aid depends on proving that suffering resulted from misfortune rather than imprudence, institutions may treat the imprudent with a coldness that many egalitarians find repugnant. The so called harshness objection asks whether a theory can remain humane when its distinctions license abandonment of people in desperate need. Defenders replied that responsibility sensitivity can be combined with a guaranteed minimum, or that principles of equality operate alongside independent duties of rescue. Cohen took such replies seriously while insisting that patches alter the character of a theory and must be acknowledged as additional commitments. His method in normative philosophy mirrored his earlier method in philosophy of history: isolate the claim, test it against a case designed to reveal its implications, and refuse refuge in slogans.

### How did the founder restate his own position in a later preface?

In a later preface Cohen recorded his lost confidence in historical materialism, separating that admission from his continuing egalitarian commitments and presenting the change as a reasoned revision rather than a repudiation of every argument developed across his earlier philosophical work.

Placement of that admission deserves attention. A preface to a reissued work addresses readers who arrive with the original argument in hand, often expecting reaffirmation from its author. Cohen used that threshold to warn against reading the book as a statement of settled belief. The confession was brief, but brevity should not be confused with casualness. Scholars rarely record loss of confidence in a signature thesis without weighing the effect on reputation, students, and allies. For a founder whose authority rested on having made historical materialism philosophically respectable, the sentence carried a cost that a peripheral commentator would not have paid. It signalled that standards of evidence, applied consistently, can turn against the position those standards were first used to defend.

Weight also derives from the asymmetry between construction and recantation. Many thinkers drift away from earlier views through silence, allowing citations to continue while private conviction fades. Cohen chose explicitness. Explicitness gives historians a dated marker, gives critics a fair target, and gives sympathetic readers permission to separate admiration for an argumentative achievement from assent to its conclusion. One can still learn from the reconstruction of the primacy thesis while doubting that productive forces explain history in the manner proposed. One can also accept that Cohen identified genuine explanatory patterns in some transitions without accepting a general theory covering all social change. The preface therefore performs an intellectual service beyond autobiography: it models how to keep what remains instructive in a work whose central claim its author could no longer endorse with confidence.

Steelmanning the older Cohen position clarifies why the loss of confidence was not a surrender to fashion. Historical materialism, carefully formulated, explained why legal and political arrangements often track requirements of an economic structure, why technological change destabilises inherited institutions, and why ruling groups defend property forms that favour their reproduction. Those insights retain force in particular episodes. The difficulty lies in elevating them into a comprehensive account that assigns explanatory primacy across radically different societies while handling counterexamples without ad hoc rescue. Cohen had narrowed the doctrine to make it defensible; narrowing exposed how much empirical weight the remaining claims still had to bear. A verdict that the confession marks intellectual honesty rather than inconsistency would be reversed only by evidence that Cohen disavowed arguments he still believed sound for reasons external to inquiry, evidence the published record does not supply.

Separation between the fate of historical materialism and the fate of egalitarianism is the deepest consequence of the turn. Earlier Marxist confidence often linked equality to a predicted future: abundance would make distributive conflict less acute, and a transformed productive order would carry new norms in its wake. Cohen detached the moral case from that prediction. Equality had to be defended as a value worth realising under conditions of scarcity, disagreement, and imperfect motivation, or not defended adequately at all. That detachment explains the energy of the later work on incentives, community, and socialist ideals. Socialism appears less as a stage guaranteed by history and more as a moral aspiration whose feasibility remains open to investigation. The founder who began by reconstructing a theory of history ended the period under discussion by insisting that justice cannot wait for history to guarantee it.

The egalitarian writings also reopened an old question about freedom that Marxist traditions had often answered too quickly. If equality requires limiting rewards that talented individuals can command, does respect for liberty permit a society to discourage choices that produce unequal outcomes, or may it only arrange background institutions and leave personal conduct alone? Cohen explored a socialist ethos in which people restrain acquisitive demands on one another, not because law compels restraint, but because shared commitment alters what participants regard as a fair claim. Critics objected that an ethos policed through social pressure can become intrusive, turning private ambition into a matter for communal judgement. Cohen recognised the danger while arguing that every distributive order cultivates some ethos, including orders that celebrate bargaining power as a natural entitlement.

Lectures on incentives, inequality, and community, delivered in the early 1990s, condensed many of these themes into a sustained challenge to liberal egalitarian orthodoxy. Cohen asked whether a just society may rely on talented citizens demanding special rewards for work that benefits everyone, while officially proclaiming commitment to equality of condition. The question exposed a gap between rules a legislature enacts and motives a culture normalises. Defenders of incentive payments answered that productivity gains enlarge the share available to the worst off, making tolerance of unequal rewards a pragmatic necessity rather than a moral endorsement. Cohen pressed the distinction between necessity created by institutions and necessity created by expectations that citizens could, under a different ethos, learn to revise.

Interpreting the turn biographically alone would miss its disciplinary significance. Political philosophy during the 1980s and 1990s was dominated by disputes over distributive principles, responsibility, and the moral limits of markets. Cohen entered those disputes not as a tourist seeking refuge from a failing theory, but as a participant who altered the agenda by forcing Rawlsians and luck egalitarians to confront incentives, ethos, and the harshness of responsibility based exclusion. Influence flowed in both directions. Analytic egalitarianism gave Cohen a new audience and a refined vocabulary, while his Marxist formation kept attention fixed on ownership, class advantage, and the moral status of productive contribution. The founder of analytical Marxism thus became, through the same habits of clarification, a central critic of liberal egalitarian orthodoxy as well.

## Institutional design and the later projects

A constructive impulse replaced much of the energy once devoted to defending inherited doctrine. Roemer and Wright, working in different idioms, asked what institutions could embody egalitarian aims under recognisable human motivations and material constraints. Design replaced prophecy as the characteristic activity. Instead of deriving a future society from tendencies allegedly present within capitalism, the designer specifies rules, examines incentives those rules create, traces distributive consequences, and revises the blueprint when a flaw appears. Feasibility becomes an analytical question rather than a rhetorical assurance. That change in posture may be the most durable legacy of analytical Marxism, because it survives disagreement about Marx himself and can be practised by scholars who share few doctrinal commitments.

Roemer arrived at design through dissatisfaction with both central planning and an egalitarian critique that relied on Marx's theory of value. His models of exploitation had shown that unequal ownership of productive assets can generate unequal advantage even where labour markets clear and no cheating occurs. If asset distribution does the explanatory work, then reforming asset distribution becomes a natural focus for institutional invention. Market socialism, in the form Roemer developed during the early 1990s, attempted to combine decentralised price signals with social ownership of corporate capital. Citizens would hold shares indirectly through coupons that could be used to invest in mutual funds, while firms would compete in product markets and managers would face pressure to perform. Coupons could not be converted into cash for consumption or bequeathed as ordinary wealth, a restriction intended to prevent reconcentration of ownership across generations.

Coupon socialism deserves a fair hearing before criticism begins. The proposal addressed a genuine dilemma. Central planning had struggled with information, innovation, and managerial discipline, while private ownership of capital generated inequalities that egalitarian theories condemn. By separating the income stream associated with capital from direct personal control over firms, Roemer hoped to socialise returns without abolishing markets. Banks and mutual funds would monitor enterprises, citizens would share in profits broadly, and competition would retain a role in revealing costs and preferences. The scheme was deliberately schematic, offered as a model for reasoning about trade offs rather than as legislation ready for enactment. Judged as a thought experiment about which features of capitalism produce which outcomes, it remains instructive even for readers unpersuaded by its details.

Criticism nevertheless exposed formidable problems. Monitoring managers through layers of funds and banks may reproduce agency difficulties instead of solving them, since citizens holding diversified coupon portfolios have weak incentives to scrutinise any single enterprise. Restrictions on converting coupons limit wealth concentration but also limit portfolio choice, and political authorities charged with supervising the banking system could acquire dangerous discretion. Soft budget constraints might return if failing firms expect rescue for employment reasons, eroding the discipline that markets were meant to supply. Roemer acknowledged many such difficulties in the spirit of the exercise: a design earns credibility by surviving hostile examination, not by attracting applause from sympathisers. A favourable verdict on coupon socialism would require evidence from comparable arrangements that dispersed social ownership can sustain investment, innovation, and managerial accountability at scale, evidence that pilot institutions and comparative studies could in principle provide.

Wright approached construction from sociology rather than formal modelling. His class analysis had mapped locations within relations of production, distinguishing owners, managers, experts, and workers by authority and control over assets. Dissatisfaction with purely oppositional politics led him toward the Real Utopias project, a programme for examining institutions that expand democratic control and reduce domination while remaining viable within existing societies. Participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre offered a concrete case in which residents deliberated over municipal investment priorities, with rules designed to favour poorer districts and sustain broad involvement. Unconditional basic income offered a different case, loosening dependence on employers by guaranteeing a floor beneath every citizen. Associative democracy offered a third, envisioning organised groups taking formal roles in governance alongside electoral representation.

### What replaced defence once the members turned to design?

Defence gave way to construction: specifying feasible institutions, testing incentive compatibility, modelling ownership arrangements, and comparing distributive outcomes under explicit rules. Attention shifted from vindicating inherited doctrine toward designing arrangements whose operation could be described, criticised, and revised in publicly assessable terms.

Real utopias, as a label, joins two words that pull against each other on purpose. Utopia marks a normative horizon beyond arrangements that merely administer existing inequality more efficiently. Real marks a demand for institutional pathways, organisational carriers, and evidence that a proposal can function outside a seminar room. Wright evaluated candidate institutions by desirability, viability, and achievability, three criteria that force advocates to separate moral appeal from strategic plausibility. A reform may be desirable yet unachievable under foreseeable political coalitions, or achievable yet too fragile to survive opposition. Sorting proposals along those dimensions turns hope into a research agenda. Case studies matter because a single durable example can refute a blanket claim of impossibility, while repeated failures can reveal which design features depend on unusual local conditions.

Participatory budgeting illustrates both promise and limits with useful clarity. Supporters point to redistribution toward neglected neighbourhoods, civic learning among participants, and greater transparency in municipal spending. Sceptics note that budgets subject to participation often cover only a modest share of public expenditure, that organised groups can dominate deliberation, and that success in one city may rest on party commitment or administrative capacity unavailable elsewhere. Wright treated such mixed evidence as material for refinement rather than embarrassment. Rules about delegate selection, district weighting, technical assistance, and publication of outcomes can be altered and compared. The analytical habit is visible in the treatment: specify a mechanism, observe where it bends under pressure, and ask which modification preserves the egalitarian purpose without denying the pressure exists.

Basic income raised a sharper set of feasibility questions. A payment sufficient to free people from compelled labour would be expensive, and financing it through taxation changes incentives across the economy in ways that simple models capture imperfectly. Critics worried about reciprocity, asking why able bodied recipients who choose leisure should be supported by those who work. Defenders answered that unpaid care, education, artistic labour, and voluntary activity contribute socially without passing through employment, and that a floor strengthens bargaining power against degrading jobs. Wright placed the proposal within a family of pathways that reduce dependence on capitalist labour markets gradually, rather than presenting it as a single leap beyond capitalism. That gradualism distinguishes real utopias from revolutionary blueprints while preserving a transformative direction of travel.

Associative democracy completed the trio by addressing power inside governance itself. Elections aggregate preferences at intervals but leave administration to bureaucracies and lobbying to well resourced interests. Giving associations of workers, residents, service users, and producers recognised roles in policy formation could deepen representation and bring local knowledge into decisions. Risks are equally plain: associations may entrench insiders, exclude unorganised citizens, or blur accountability when outcomes disappoint. Design responses include public funding rules, transparency requirements, overlapping membership, and reserved channels for unaffiliated individuals. None of those responses eliminates risk. Each converts a vague hope about participation into a testable arrangement whose failures can be located and debated.

Comparing Roemer with Wright reveals complementary strengths rather than a single successor programme. Roemer began from formal properties of ownership and asked which allocation rules produce egalitarian distributions under market discipline. Wright began from observed experiments and asked which democratic capacities those experiments cultivate or suppress. One method risks abstraction from politics; the other risks generalising from favourable cases. Together they show how analytical Marxism changed its centre of gravity. Explanation of capitalism remained important, but explanation increasingly served evaluation of alternatives that could be described in institutional detail. Feasibility, once invoked by critics as an external objection, became internal to theory construction itself.

A balanced assessment should resist both nostalgia and dismissal. Design work inherited real difficulties from the doctrines it replaced, including uncertainty about motivation, state capacity, and transition politics, and no proposal from the period settled those difficulties. Yet the turn produced questions that remain productive across ideological lines: which ownership forms disperse power, which welfare floors alter bargaining relations, which participatory rules resist capture, and which market mechanisms can be retained without surrendering egalitarian aims. A verdict that the constructive turn represents maturation rather than retreat would be reversed if the designs proved uniformly unworkable under fair trials or if their egalitarian effects vanished whenever institutions scaled beyond small settings. Until such evidence accumulates, the fairest reading treats design as the place where analytical rigour survived the doctrines that had first called it into being.

Transition questions haunted both programmes and received more honest treatment than revolutionary rhetoric usually allowed. Moving from private capital ownership toward coupon based funds would require legislation, administrative competence, and protection against evasion through foreign holdings or informal control. Expanding participatory institutions would require parties willing to surrender discretion, officials willing to share authority, and citizens willing to sustain involvement after initial enthusiasm faded. Neither Roemer nor Wright claimed that a blueprint abolishes politics. Each treated political mobilisation as a variable to be analysed alongside economic mechanism, asking which coalitions benefit from a proposal, which groups bear visible costs, and which intermediate steps create constituencies for further change. That attention to pathways marks a clear break from philosophies of history that expected structural transformation to supply its own agents.

## The hostile reception: the charge of dissolution

Hostility arrived early, and hostility arrived from inside the Marxist tradition rather than from liberal or conservative opponents who might have been expected to dismiss any revival of Marxist argument. Scholars formed by historical materialism, by Hegelian dialectics, by labour history, and by socialist politics looked at work emerging from the September Group and from writers associated with G. A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer, and Erik Olin Wright, and many concluded that something essential had been surrendered. Admiration for lucidity rarely softened that judgement. Praise for precise definitions, for explicit premises, and for arguments that could be followed step by step tended to be followed by a sharper complaint, namely that clarity had been purchased by emptying Marxism of historical movement, collective agency, and revolutionary purpose. Reception therefore split along a revealing line. Sympathetic readers saw overdue discipline imposed upon a tradition prone to slogan and obscurity. Hostile readers saw a solvent applied patiently to concepts until class, exploitation, and historical transformation remained as words without their former explanatory weight.

Ellen Meiksins Wood gave that suspicion its most influential and most durable formulation in The Retreat from Class, published in 1986. Wood wrote as a Marxist historian defending a particular understanding of class, of politics, and of socialist agency against what she regarded as a fashionable accommodation to defeat. Her book treated analytical Marxism alongside related currents that she grouped under the label of "a new true socialism", and her argument refused to separate intellectual method from political consequence. For Wood, a theory that ceased to place the working class at the centre of historical change had not merely revised one proposition among others. Such a theory had altered the identity of Marxism itself. She insisted that class in Marx signified a social relation constituted through production, conflict, and collective formation, not a convenient label for persons sorted by income, occupation, or asset holdings. Once class became a distributional category inside a model, she argued, class struggle became an optional addition rather than the engine through which social relations were made and remade.

### What exactly did the early critics charge the school with?

Critics charged that analytical Marxism abandoned historical materialism, replaced class struggle with individual choice, dissolved exploitation into voluntary exchange, evacuated politics through technical modelling, and presented retreat from collective agency as rigorous reconstruction, thereby preserving Marxist vocabulary while surrendering explanatory claims about capitalism, power, and transformation altogether in practice.

Wood pressed that charge through a reading of intellectual trajectory as much as through refutation of single theses. Analytical writers, in her account, began by asking for rigour and ended by finding classical Marxist claims indefensible, yet the sequence deserved scrutiny because each step had been guided by prior methodological commitments. Functional explanation was discarded, dialectical reasoning was treated as confusion, historical laws were weakened into contingent generalisations, and collective actors were decomposed into individuals whose choices required explanation at a lower level. Every concession could be defended locally as an improvement in precision. Taken together, however, concessions formed a pattern, and Wood named that pattern retreat. Retreat described movement away from class as historical agent, away from production as the privileged site of social power, and away from socialism as a transformation to be achieved by a collective subject formed inside capitalism. Her polemic gained force because she connected seminar room decisions to a wider political climate marked by defeats for organised labour, by ascendant market liberalism, and by socialist intellectuals searching for respectable ground.

Central to Wood's case stood a distinction between reconstruction and dissolution that hostile critics repeated for years afterward. Reconstruction would have meant restating Marxist explanations so that mechanisms became visible, evidence became assessable, and weak formulations could be repaired without losing the theory that gave those formulations meaning. Dissolution meant something different. A concept could be retained while relations that made the concept Marxist were stripped away, leaving an elegant shell compatible with liberal egalitarianism, welfare economics, or pluralist sociology. Exploitation offered critics their clearest illustration. If exploitation survived only as an unfair distribution of assets judged against a counterfactual of equal ownership, then Marx's account of surplus extraction inside production had been replaced rather than clarified. Class offered a parallel illustration. If classes became positions in a schema derived from ownership and authority, useful for survey research yet detached from struggle and historical formation, then class analysis had been converted into stratification research with radical vocabulary attached.

Political evacuation formed another strand in hostile reception, distinct from complaints about terminology. Critics argued that technical modelling displaced questions of organisation, strategy, state power, and ideology into background assumptions, while foreground attention settled upon equilibrium outcomes, incentive compatibility, and the logical coherence of ideal arrangements. A model could show that rational agents under specified constraints would choose one outcome rather than another, yet silence surrounded the historical processes through which agents acquired their beliefs, their capacities, and their willingness to act together. Collective action appeared as an aggregation puzzle. Solidarity appeared, if at all, as a preference to be inserted into a utility function. Party, union, workplace organisation, and political education receded because formal apparatus handled them awkwardly. Hostile readers therefore suspected that method selected subject matter. Whatever resisted formalisation was pronounced vague, and whatever survived formalisation was celebrated as the rational core, so politics thinned into technique by a series of apparently neutral decisions about standards.

Charges concerning historical materialism cut especially deep because Cohen's early defence of Marx had supplied much of the school's founding prestige. His book of 1978 on Marx's theory of history had reconstructed the primacy thesis and the functional reading of production relations with formidable care. Hostile critics nevertheless maintained that subsequent developments undermined that defence from within. Elster's insistence upon microfoundations and his suspicion of functional explanation without mechanisms appeared to remove the very explanatory form Cohen had laboured to vindicate. Roemer's reconstruction of exploitation without reliance upon Marx's theory of value appeared to sever another link to classical political economy. Wright's empirical class project appeared to translate historical materialism into variable based sociology. Whether each appearance was fair mattered less, for reception history, than the cumulative impression they created. A school that began by defending Marx seemed, to alarmed observers, to be dismantling Marx piece by piece while describing every removal as salvage.

Wood also objected to a separation between capitalism and politics that she detected in allied post Marxist and rational choice currents, and she extended suspicion toward analytical treatments by association. In her reading, detaching political struggle from class structure encouraged a vision of socialism as one value among others, to be pursued through discourse, coalition, or institutional design without a privileged agent anchored in production. Analytical Marxists could reply that many among them retained strong commitments to class and to equality, and several did so in print with unmistakable emphasis. Reception, however, often grouped distinct writers into a single tendency and judged the tendency by its most revisionary conclusions. Guilt by proximity played a part. So did genuine overlap in vocabulary, in conference membership, and in shared impatience with dialectical idiom. Hostile critics treated overlap as evidence of a common direction, and direction mattered more to them than disclaimers. A movement intellectually, they feared, ended politically somewhere else, even when individual members protested loyalty to socialist aims.

Resentment about tone amplified substantive disagreement and should not be underestimated as a cause of the quarrel's bitterness. Analytical writers prided themselves upon impatience with obscurity, and that pride could sound like contempt for entire traditions of Marxist scholarship that valued textual exegesis, dialectical presentation, or historical narrative. Critics heard condescension in demands for clarity when those demands implied that predecessors had been confused rather than differently oriented. Historians heard their craft demoted when narrative explanation was treated as failed theory. Philosophers formed by Hegel heard a narrow conception of reason presented as reason itself. Sociologists heard familiar debates about structure and agency recast in unfamiliar notation and claimed as discoveries. Each disciplinary grievance fed a larger suspicion that analytical Marxism sought authority by redefining competence, so that only arguments cast in approved forms counted as serious. Hostile reception thus defended plural intellectual practices alongside Marxist doctrine, and polemic against method became also a defence of scholarly territory.

Strength of that indictment should be felt before any defence is weighed, because weak paraphrase makes later replies look stronger than they are. At full force, the charge ran as follows. Analytical Marxism claimed to save Marxism by submitting Marxist claims to standards drawn from analytic philosophy and mainstream social science. Standards, however, were not neutral instruments. Standards embodied decisions about what explanation is, about what counts as an actor, about which abstractions are legitimate, and about how history may be generalised. Applying those standards transformed the object under examination. Class lost relational and historical depth. Exploitation lost connection to production and to Marx's theory of value. Historical materialism lost lawlike ambition and functional architecture. Socialism lost a bearer. What remained might be interesting, rigorous, and even egalitarian, critics conceded, but calling the remainder Marxism obscured a rupture. Honest naming, on that view, would have announced a departure into liberal socialist theory. Continued use of Marxist language borrowed authority from a tradition whose central claims had been quietly set aside.


## The strongest objection: preferences, exchange, and the modelling choice

Strongest among hostile objections was not an accusation of bad faith, nor a complaint about jargon, nor grief over abandoned slogans. Strongest was a claim about circularity lodged against the modelling strategy itself, developed within the critical tradition that Wood's 1986 book represented and sharpened by later commentators who shared her suspicion of methodological individualism in its rational choice form. Put bluntly, the objection held that analytical Marxists imported a picture of social life in which separate individuals, endowed with given preferences and unequal assets, meet through voluntary exchange under enforceable rules, and then expressed surprise when distinctively Marxist conclusions failed to emerge. Failure, critics argued, had been arranged in advance. A framework built from atomistic choosers could register coercion only as an external constraint upon choice, could register exploitation only as an unfair starting distribution, and could register class only as a pattern in holdings. Relations that Marx treated as constitutive disappeared at the stage of model construction, long before any theorem was proved or any evidence was examined.

Marx's analysis, on the critical reading, contests each element of that picture at its foundation. Preferences in Marx are not sovereign data handed to theory from outside history. Hunger for commodities, desire for status, acceptance of hierarchy, fear of unemployment, and habits of deference are formed inside definite relations of production and reproduction. Workers do not simply prefer leisure less than wages in some timeless calculus. Workers confront a labour market after separation from means of production, under legal and material conditions that make sale of labour power a condition of life. Exchange in Marx likewise carries a double character. Contract between formally free and equal parties coexists with substantive compulsion, since one party owns productive assets and another party owns capacity to work and little else. Voluntariness at the moment of agreement therefore conceals compulsion in the structure that produced the agreeing parties. Critics maintained that models beginning after that structure had done its work mistook a historically produced surface for a natural starting point.

### Which contributions depend on the contested assumption, and which escape it?

Exploitation formalisation and class analysis built upon asset ownership depend directly upon given preferences and voluntary exchange, while demands for clarity, rejection of functional explanation without mechanisms, and careful empirical class research retain force even when those modelling premises are rejected or substantially revised by critics.

Concession must be made plainly before any sorting can be trusted. Roemer's general theory of exploitation, especially in its property relations form, depends upon the contested assumption in a deep and not merely incidental way. Agents in Roemer's constructions hold bundles of alienable and inalienable assets, form coalitions, withdraw with specified shares, and compare outcomes under alternative rules, while preferences and productive knowledge are taken as given for purposes of the exercise. Exploitation is then identified through counterfactual comparison: a group counts as exploited if withdrawal under an egalitarian asset distribution would leave that group better off and its complement worse off. Elegance of that test should not disguise dependence. Remove given preferences, voluntary coalition formation, and exchange among asset holders as organising devices, and the formalisation loses the machinery by which exploitation is defined. A different theory might still condemn domination inside production, or theft of surplus, or unequal control over work, but condemnation would require other foundations. Roemer's result is a result inside a deliberately constructed world of holders and choosers.

Class analysis in its assets based form carries a comparable dependence, though degree varies between Roemer and Wright and between early schemas and later refinements. Wright's class locations were derived from relations to means of production, organisational assets, and skill or credential assets, and contradictory locations captured positions pulled between domination and subordination along those dimensions. Survey respondents could then be placed, counted, and compared across nations and across periods. Critics objected that placement presupposed assets as possessions carried by individuals into social relations, whereas Marxist class in a relational sense arises through antagonistic interdependence between classes themselves. Dependence here is real. Categories gain operational precision by treating assets as measurable holdings attached to jobs and persons. Historical processes through which credentials became property like advantages, through which organisational authority became a resource, and through which ownership separated from control fall partly outside the schema. Conceding that dependence does not erase empirical findings about income, authority, or mobility associated with class locations. Concession does mark a boundary: findings describe patterned inequality within capitalist societies more securely than findings explain class formation, class consciousness, or class struggle as historical processes.

Elster's contributions divide internally, and honest sorting must resist treating rational choice Marxism as a single block. Game theoretic treatments of collective action, of class compromise, and of technological change lean heavily upon agents with ordered preferences choosing strategically under constraints, so dependence there is substantial. Elster's critique of functional explanation, by contrast, reaches beyond any particular model of choice. Demanding a mechanism that connects a beneficial consequence back to the persistence of an institution is a rule of explanatory hygiene. A historian who rejects utility maximisation entirely can still ask how a practice reproduced itself, through whose actions, under which incentives and sanctions, and with what unintended effects. Likewise, Elster's warnings against treating classes as unified persons with purposes of their own retain force for scholars who explain collective outcomes through organisations, networks, and cultural frames rather than through rational choosers. Part of Elster therefore stands inside the contested framework, and part supplies tools usable outside that framework by critics of the framework itself.

Cohen's work divides along a different seam, one that hostile critics sometimes missed because Cohen was associated with defence of functional explanation while Elster attacked careless versions of that explanatory form. Cohen's demand that Marxist claims be stated so that truth or falsity could be assessed does not depend upon voluntary exchange or upon given preferences. Clarity is a discipline of statement, not a theory of action. His insistence that concepts such as productive forces, production relations, and superstructure be distinguished before relations among them are asserted can be accepted by a dialectical historian who rejects every game theoretic model ever constructed. Cohen's substantive defence of historical materialism is another matter. Primacy and development theses advanced claims about history that stand or fall on historical evidence and on the legitimacy of functional explanation, not on exchange among preference bearers. Sorting Cohen under rational choice dependence would therefore be an error. His vulnerability lay elsewhere, in whether consequences can explain persistence without specified feedback mechanisms, a vulnerability internal critics exposed with lasting effect.

Empirical survey craft associated with Wright also escapes dependence in a partial but important sense, and escape should be specified carefully rather than celebrated loosely. Questionnaires that ask about ownership, supervision, hiring authority, credentials, autonomy, and workplace decision making gather evidence about lived positions inside firms and bureaucracies. Comparative design that holds instruments steady across countries permits contrasts that impressionistic writing cannot supply. Attention to sample, to measurement error, and to rival explanations raises standards for class analysis regardless of theoretical allegiance. A scholar committed to relational class theory can use such evidence while interpreting locations as traces of relations rather than as essences. Craft travels even when framework does not. Limits travel as well. Survey items freeze relations at a moment, privilege attributes that can be coded, and struggle to capture struggle itself, since conflict appears indirectly through attitudes or reported authority. Escape from the contested assumption is therefore genuine for technique and incomplete for interpretation, and that mixed verdict is more useful than either wholesale acquittal or wholesale condemnation.

Objection at full strength also raises a question about falsification that deserves direct engagement rather than dismissal as rhetoric. If Marxist conclusions fail inside models built from liberal premises, has Marxism been tested, or has a translation been tested and found unfaithful? Critics compared the procedure to translating a poem into a language without a needed grammatical form and then announcing that the poem lacked subtlety. Defenders could answer that translation is precisely how hidden assumptions become visible, and that a claim unable to survive any precise restatement may have owed its plausibility to vagueness. Both positions capture something real. Restatement can expose equivocation, and restatement can also amputate meaning. Sorting contributions offers a way between blanket acceptance and blanket refusal. Where a contribution is the restatement itself, as with exploitation defined through asset withdrawal, critical objection strikes at the heart. Where a contribution is a standard for argument, as with mechanism demands, objection strikes at a neighbouring position and leaves the standard standing.

Verdict on the strongest objection is therefore partial success, and partial success of a serious kind. Objection succeeds against claims that Roemer's formalisation recovered Marx's theory of exploitation in a more rigorous form, since recovery changed the theory's basis from production and surplus labour to distribution and counterfactual ownership. Objection succeeds against any suggestion that class schemas derived from assets exhausted Marx's relational and historical conception of class. Objection fails as a total refutation of analytical Marxism, because several durable contributions never required the contested picture of preference bearing exchangers, and because even dependent contributions generated precise hypotheses, comparisons, and institutional proposals that can be assessed on evidence. What would reverse that verdict? Demonstration that Roemer's exploitation judgements remain unchanged when preferences are treated as endogenously formed and exchange as structurally compelled would weaken the dependence claim at its centre. Conversely, demonstration that mechanism demands and clarity standards themselves presuppose atomistic choosers in every usable form would extend dependence far beyond the boundary drawn here and would strengthen the critics' case considerably.


## Misreadings to correct: hostility, synonymy, dissolution

Five misreadings recur in summaries of analytical Marxism, in lecture shorthand, in polemical footnotes, and in reference entries compressed until nuance disappears. Each misreading contains a fragment of truth, which explains persistence, and each distorts the historical record in a way that makes later developments unintelligible. Correction requires evidence from the school's formation, from internal disagreements that were visible early, from the September Group's continuing meetings, and from constructive projects that hostile reception sometimes acknowledged only to dismiss. A school can be criticised severely without being caricatured. Indeed, severe criticism gains credibility when caricature is removed, because charges that survive accurate description cannot be waved away as errors of fact. Sections that follow therefore correct the record in a manner that leaves genuine objections intact and, in places, sharper for having been separated from folklore.

First misreading holds that the school was hostile to Marxism at its founding, a Trojan enterprise whose members set out to destroy a tradition from within while wearing its colours. Evidence points in the opposite direction. Cohen's 1978 defence of Marx's theory of history was an act of rescue undertaken against critics who treated historical materialism as crude determinism or as metaphor masquerading as science. Cohen sought to show that Marx had advanced a coherent and powerful theory worthy of the most demanding philosophical scrutiny. Elster approached Marx as a major social theorist whose insights required separation from Hegelian residue and from functionalist shortcuts, a severe operation performed in the belief that a living core justified surgical effort. Roemer asked whether exploitation could be defended after weaknesses in Marx's theory of value were admitted, because exploitation mattered too much to rest upon a vulnerable foundation. Wright set out to give class analysis empirical substance at a moment when Marxist sociology risked confinement to programmatic assertion. Founding motives differed, yet defence, repair, and renewal describe those motives more accurately than hostility.

Self description chosen by participants reinforces that reading and corrects a related error about labels. Participants described their enterprise with the deliberately provocative phrase non-bullshit Marxism, a self description rather than a critics' label, signalling impatience with evasion, with protective jargon, and with arguments that survived by refusing clear statement. Provocation explains why outsiders heard arrogance. Participants heard a pledge: Marxist claims should be stated so plainly that failure, if failure came, could not hide behind eloquence. Erik Olin Wright and others associated with the September Group, named for the month of its annual gatherings, understood meetings as a workshop for claims that members valued enough to test harshly. Internal criticism was fierce precisely because stakes were internal. A hostile infiltrator does not ordinarily spend years reconstructing a theory of history, formalising exploitation, or surveying class structure across nations. Labour of that scale is evidence of attachment, even when attachment eventually produced revisions that other Marxists judged fatal and even when some members travelled away from Marxism over subsequent decades.

Second misreading treats rational choice Marxism and analytical Marxism as exactly synonymous, as if every analytical writer accepted methodological individualism in its strongest form and every argument proceeded from utility maximising agents. Synonymy collapses distinctions that participants themselves debated openly. Analytical Marxism named a commitment to clarity, to explicit argument, and to standards borrowed from analytic philosophy and from rigorous social science. Rational choice theory named a narrower explanatory programme built around strategic action by individuals with preferences and beliefs. Elster championed that programme most consistently and gave it brilliant expression. Roemer employed optimisation and equilibrium methods extensively in economic reconstruction. Cohen, however, defended functional explanation and resisted reduction of historical materialism to individual action, while Wright's sociology drew upon Marxist and Weberian categories in combinations that no pure rational choice framework would generate. Adam Przeworski used game theory for class compromise and democratic transition, yet other associates pursued normative philosophy, historiography, or empirical research with little interest in games. Umbrella and subfamily should not be confused.

### Was the group dissolved, or did it simply change its questions?

The group continued meeting while members diverged sharply over method, politics, and research priorities, so continuity of conversation coexisted with fragmentation of programme, and later gatherings pursued equality, exploitation, and institutional design through increasingly separate projects rather than through any single shared Marxist doctrine or collective manifesto.

Third misreading, addressed directly by the answer above, imagines a formal dissolution, a moment when the September Group disbanded and analytical Marxism ended as an organised conversation. Meetings continued while membership shifted, while agendas broadened, and while common purpose thinned into shared history and overlapping curiosity. Divergence is not disappearance. Cohen moved toward egalitarian political philosophy and sustained engagement with luck, talent, and responsibility. Elster moved toward mechanisms, emotions, constitutions, and explanation in social science more generally. Wright moved toward real utopias and toward institutional alternatives evaluated through democratic and egalitarian criteria. Roemer moved toward political economy of inequality, ethnic division, and democratic design. Trajectories separated, sometimes dramatically, yet separation occurred through continuing argument among persons who had formed habits of criticism together. Annual gatherings persisted as a setting where former allies could disagree without needing agreement as admission price. Historians who require a death certificate for every school will misdate analytical Marxism and misunderstand how intellectual networks decay, which is usually by diffusion into neighbouring fields rather than by ceremonial closure.

Fourth misreading portrays contributions as purely destructive, a long demolition in which functional explanation, Marx's theory of value, dialectics, and historical laws were attacked while nothing durable was built in their place. Demolition certainly occurred, and some demolition was celebrated by its authors. Construction occurred alongside demolition on a scale that hostile summaries rarely inventory fairly. Wright's class schema gave researchers a replicable instrument for comparing class structures, class consciousness, and class formation across advanced capitalist societies, generating a large empirical literature that supporters and critics alike could engage through data. Roemer's exploitation formalisation gave philosophers and economists a precise alternative account linking exploitation to property distribution, stimulating debates about self ownership, about needs, and about the moral basis of socialist criticism. Cohen's reconstruction of historical materialism, whatever its ultimate fate, set terms for discussion of technological development and social form for a generation. Design projects extended construction into institutions: market socialist blueprints, participatory proposals, unconditional income arguments, and Wright's later real utopias programme all asked how egalitarian values might be embodied in arrangements that could plausibly function.

Fifth misreading concerns tone and origin of the school's most quoted self description, already touched upon, yet the point deserves separate emphasis because attribution reversals reveal how polemic rewrites memory. Calling non-bullshit Marxism a critics' label makes the phrase sound like an insult hurled by defenders of orthodoxy at arrogant newcomers. Historical sequence runs the other way. Insiders coined and embraced the phrase as a badge, half joke and half manifesto, aware that bluntness would provoke and willing to accept provocation as the price of signalling a break with scholastic habits. Understanding insider origin changes interpretation of the quarrel. Critics were not inventing arrogance from nothing; participants had advertised impatience in terms designed to sting. At the same time, a badge chosen in a spirit of reform should not be mistaken for a confession of hostility toward Marx. Bluntness aimed at Marxist writing habits, at evasive commentary traditions, and at political rhetoric insulated from evidence. Whether bluntness sometimes became unfair to predecessors is a legitimate question. Origin of the phrase is not.

Further distortion follows from treating later positions as founding intentions, a retrospective fallacy that flattens intellectual biography. Because some analytical Marxists eventually abandoned central Marxist claims, summaries sometimes read abandonment backward into 1978 or into the first September meetings as a concealed plan. Biography resists that compression. Beliefs changed under pressure of argument, under pressure of political events including the Eastern European regime collapses of 1989 to 1991, and under pressure of internal criticism that participants had invited by their own standards. A scholar who demands that claims be revised when arguments fail can hardly be accused of treachery for revising claims in public. Accusation may still be made against conclusions reached, against premises accepted too readily, or against political judgements formed during revision. Chronology, however, must be respected. Founding defence, middle period reconstruction, and later divergence are distinct phases. Collapsing phases into a single intention produces satisfying polemic and poor history, and poor history eventually weakens polemic by exposing advocates to easy factual correction.

Correction of five misreadings therefore leaves a harder and more interesting history in place of a melodrama about infiltrators, synonyms, dissolution, demolition, and insulting labels. A group of scholars attached to Marx set demanding standards for Marxist claims and discovered, unevenly and at different speeds, that some claims failed those standards while others survived in altered form. Colleagues outside the group warned that standards themselves were loaded and that alteration amounted to abandonment, a warning that proved partly justified for exploitation formalisation and for assets based class theory. Meetings continued as questions migrated toward equality, democracy, institutional design, and mechanisms of social explanation. Constructive achievements in class research, in exploitation theory, and in egalitarian philosophy entered wider scholarship even among readers who rejected Marxist identification altogether. Hostility at founding, exact synonymy with rational choice, formal dissolution, purely destructive output, and external coinage of the group's badge all fail as descriptions. Failure of those descriptions does not settle the deeper dispute about dissolution, which remains a judgement about how much alteration a tradition can absorb before continuity of name misleads more than continuity of problems enlightens.

## Uptake outside Marxism: philosophy of social science and analytical sociology

Ideas rarely travel intact. When a line of argument moves from one field into another, parts that depend on local commitments tend to fall away, while parts that solve a shared problem tend to survive and change shape. Analytical Marxism illustrates that pattern with unusual clarity. Within Marxist debate during the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, its arguments often met resistance, suspicion, or polite indifference, because many readers heard a repudiation of familiar language as a repudiation of the tradition itself. Outside that debate, among philosophers of social science and sociologists concerned with explanation as such, the same arguments frequently found a warmer reception. Readers in those neighbouring fields did not need to accept any claim about capitalism, revolution, or class struggle in order to recognise a useful discipline in the demand that large claims be broken into smaller ones and that alleged connections be shown rather than assumed. The result was a curious asymmetry in reception history: a school that struggled to persuade many Marxists reshaped habits of reasoning among scholars who never described themselves in those terms at all.

Functional explanation stood at the centre of the exchange. In much classical writing, institutions and practices were explained by the beneficial consequences they produced for a system, a class, or a mode of production. Slavery persisted because it served accumulation, schooling expanded because it supplied disciplined labour, ideology endured because it stabilised domination. Such reasoning had an intuitive pull, since consequences are visible and purposes are easy to narrate after the event. Jon Elster pressed a simple and damaging question against that habit: beneficial consequences alone cannot explain why a practice arose or why it persisted, unless a feedback process links those consequences back to the conditions that sustain the practice. Without that link, explanation by consequence resembles explanation by intention without an intending agent, and the gap between assertion and demonstration remains hidden by fluent prose. Philosophers who had no stake in Marxist orthodoxy could still recognise the force of that objection, because parallel problems appeared in evolutionary biology, in theories of institutions, and in accounts of norms.

Criticism of that kind travelled because it was portable. A biologist debating adaptation, an economist debating market selection, and a sociologist debating ritual all faced versions of the same temptation, namely to infer a cause from a useful effect. Elster drew on distinctions developed in philosophy of biology, particularly the requirement that a legitimate functional account specify a causal loop through which an effect maintains its own cause. Imported into social theory, that requirement did not dictate any particular substantive conclusion. It functioned instead as a test that any substantive conclusion had to pass. Tests of that sort are easy to carry across disciplinary borders, since they regulate procedure rather than allegiance. A scholar could accept the test, apply it to phenomena far removed from historical materialism, and discard everything else in the analytical programme while still having been changed by the encounter in a lasting and traceable way.

From the negative critique emerged a positive proposal that proved even more influential. If consequences cannot do explanatory work on their own, then an explanation must identify the entities involved, the activities those entities engage in, and the organisation through which those activities produce the phenomenon to be explained. That proposal, developed in different vocabularies by Elster and later by Peter Hedström and others working in analytical sociology, became known as explanation by mechanisms. A mechanism account refuses to rest at the level of correlation between large variables. It asks how a change in one condition alters beliefs, desires, opportunities, or constraints for situated actors, how those actors then behave, and how their separate actions combine, often unintentionally, into an aggregate outcome. Each step is modest in isolation. Together, the steps replace a gesture toward a hidden purpose with a chain that can be inspected, criticised, and revised at particular points.

Consider how differently two explanations of residential segregation proceed under that discipline. A functional account might observe that segregation benefits dominant groups and treat that benefit as sufficient reason for its persistence. A mechanism account proceeds otherwise. It examines preferences among households, thresholds at which composition of a neighbourhood prompts departure, constraints imposed by credit markets and estate agents, and expectations each household forms about choices neighbours will make. Thomas Schelling had shown earlier how mild individual preferences could generate sharp aggregate separation through iterated movement, and that model became a canonical illustration for the mechanisms literature precisely because no actor intended the final pattern and no system required it. Benefit to some group may still feature in a fuller history, but benefit alone carries no explanatory weight until processes of selection, reinforcement, or deliberate enforcement are demonstrated in detail.

### How did the turn to mechanisms travel beyond the school?
The turn travelled because the demand for mechanisms solved a general problem of explanation rather than a partisan one. Scholars in other fields could adopt the standard of showing entities and activities linking cause and effect without adopting Marxist conclusions about capitalism or class.

Several carriers helped that transmission along. Journals in philosophy of social science provided a first audience, because the debate about functional explanation belonged as much to general methodology as to Marxist theory, and contributors could engage it without entering disputes about dialectics or party history. Textbooks and graduate seminars provided a second carrier, since a crisp prohibition together with a constructive alternative teaches well: students can be shown a flawed explanation, asked to locate the missing feedback, and then invited to construct a mechanism in its place. Through such settings, vocabulary detached gradually from origin. Terms such as mechanism, micro foundation, and unintended consequence entered ordinary methodological discussion, often cited to intermediate sources rather than to Cohen or Elster directly, which is itself a sign of absorption rather than mere influence.

Hedström occupies an important place in that story, and his role clarifies what absorption involved. Working within sociology rather than within Marxist theory, he took from Elster a dissatisfaction with explanations that move directly from one macro condition to another macro outcome, leaving the intervening action unexamined. His response, elaborated with collaborators and students, distinguished situational mechanisms, through which social conditions shape individual situations, action formation mechanisms, through which situations generate particular conduct, and transformational mechanisms, through which conduct aggregates into collective results. That three part scheme owed debts to earlier work by James Coleman and to the broader individualist tradition, yet the insistence that each link be specified in terms of desires, beliefs, and opportunities bore the unmistakable imprint of Elster. Crucially, nothing in the scheme required a Marxist theory of history, a labour theory of value, or a commitment to socialism. Sociology could take the explanatory architecture and leave the politics at the door, and in department after department that selective taking is exactly what occurred during the active decades of the school.

Resistance existed outside Marxism as well, and a fair account should give that resistance its strongest form before any verdict is reached. Critics argued that insistence on individual level mechanisms smuggles in a contestable ontology, privileging persons over relations, structures, and meanings that may not decompose neatly into beliefs and desires. Others worried that mechanism sketches can become just so stories, plausible narratives with little evidential discipline, replacing one form of hand waving with another dressed in finer grain. Still others observed that some phenomena, such as demographic regularities or market equilibria under specified conditions, admit useful explanation at aggregate level without a full reconstruction of intervening action. Those objections have genuine force, and defenders of mechanisms did better when they conceded scope rather than claiming universality. A balanced verdict is that the turn permanently raised the burden of proof for functional claims and for macro to macro shortcuts, without establishing that every good explanation must take a single canonical form. That verdict would need revision if careful comparative studies showed that fields adopting mechanism standards produced no gains in predictive accuracy, intervention design, or error detection relative to fields that retained looser explanatory habits.

Philosophy of social science absorbed a related but distinct lesson concerning clarity as a scholarly virtue. G. A. Cohen modelled a style in which central concepts were defined before use, objections were stated in numbered steps even when presented in prose, and concessions were recorded openly when an argument failed. Elster added a taste for paradox, counterexample, and the close dissection of everyday reasoning about rationality and emotion. Even readers who rejected their conclusions could borrow their standards of exposition. Seminars on explanation began to distinguish more carefully between causal, functional, and interpretive claims. Discussions of social science laws shifted toward scope conditions and middle range processes. Debates about reduction became less theatrical, since mechanism language offered a middle position between crude individualism and unexamined holism: social wholes could matter enormously, provided an account explained through which organised activities their influence ran. Influence of that diffuse kind rarely appears in citation counts, yet teachers who reshaped syllabi around such distinctions transmitted the school more effectively than any single monograph could manage.

By the closing years of the twentieth century, therefore, an observer surveying philosophy and sociology would encounter analytical Marxist influence in places where Marxist influence as such was negligible. Few participants in those settings described the lineage as Marxist, and many would have resisted the label, yet intellectual descent does not depend on self description. Descent can be traced through problems inherited, distinctions preserved, and standards internalised. Measured by that trace, the school achieved a form of success its founders could only partly have intended: its methodological conscience outlived the doctrinal debates that produced it, and fields that never adopted its politics nevertheless learned to explain differently because it had existed.


## Uptake in stratification research and economic theory

Stratification research posed a practical problem that theory alone could not solve. Surveys fielded across many countries needed a classification of occupations and employment relations that interviewers could apply consistently, that statisticians could code reliably, and that analysts could use to compare mobility, income, and life chances between societies with different industrial histories. Grand categories inherited from nineteenth century political economy, owners versus workers, captured something essential about property yet left most of the employed population squeezed into residual boxes. Managers who owned nothing directed the labour of others. Supervisors exercised authority without controlling investment. Professionals sold expertise under conditions quite unlike factory employment, while small proprietors mixed ownership and personal labour in proportions that varied by trade. Erik Olin Wright addressed that untidy reality by constructing a class schema grounded in relations of production but elaborated to handle contradictory locations, positions that share features of more than one class in the classic scheme. Whatever fate awaited Marxist theory in the academy, survey researchers confronted the untidiness every time a questionnaire returned from the field.

Wright built the schema through successive refinements rather than through a single act of definition. Early versions distinguished locations by ownership of means of production, control over investment and accumulation, control over the physical apparatus of production, and control over the labour of others. Later versions simplified the dimensions, emphasising ownership together with authority within organisations and possession of scarce, credentialed skills that command rents in labour markets. Each version generated a matrix of positions, typically twelve categories in the most used formulation, ranging from large employers through expert managers and non managerial professionals down to nonskilled workers and the petty bourgeoisie. Because categories were defined by relations rather than by income bands or prestige scores, the schema promised to measure something theoretically specified instead of something merely convenient. Because the categories could be approximated from standard survey items about self employment, number of employees, supervisory duties, and occupation, the promise could actually be tested against data collected in many nations under a coordinated comparative project.

### Why did researchers without Marxist commitments adopt the class schema?
Researchers adopted the schema because it translated a theoretical account of class into categories that surveys could measure and compare across countries. Its usefulness for explaining differences in income, mobility, and attitudes did not depend on sharing the political commitments of its originators.

Evidence of usefulness accumulated through comparative studies during the nineteen eighties and nineteen nineties. Teams in North America, Europe, Australia, and East Asia applied common coding rules to national surveys and examined whether class location, so measured, predicted earnings, unemployment risk, union membership, voting behaviour, and attitudes toward inequality and redistribution. Results varied by country and by outcome, which itself counted as a scientific virtue: a schema that can fail in particular applications can also succeed informatively in others. In several studies the Wright categories performed at least as well as gradational measures based on occupational prestige or socioeconomic indices, and for some outcomes, especially those tied to authority and employment relations, the relational categories added explanatory power that gradational measures missed. Researchers committed to Weberian or purely empirical approaches could report such findings without embarrassment, since the contest concerned measurement performance, not doctrinal loyalty. Instruments earn adoption by surviving hostile or indifferent use, and the class schema survived a great deal of both.

Debate about the schema also drove productive refinement among its critics. Some analysts argued that skill assets smuggled a market capacity notion into a production relations framework, blurring the boundary between Marxist and Weberian class theory. Others questioned whether authority in the workplace reflected class at all, or whether gender and household position cut across individual class location in ways an occupation based scheme could not capture. Feminist critics pressed especially hard on the treatment of married women whose labour market participation was intermittent, forcing clarification about units of analysis that benefited stratification research generally. Each objection prompted either revision, explicit defence, or alternative operationalisation, and the literature grew through that cycle. Even scholars who ultimately preferred rival schemes, such as the Erikson and Goldthorpe classification widely used in European mobility research, defined their preferences partly against the Wright alternative. Presence as the comparison case in a methodological contest is a durable form of influence, distinct from conversion and arguably more important for cumulative science.

Meanwhile a separate line of analytical work entered economic theory through a different door. Roemer posed the question whether exploitation could be defined without reliance on Marx's theory of value, a theory many economists regarded as untenable and many Marxists regarded as indispensable. His answer reconstructed exploitation in terms of property relations. A group counts as exploited, on the proposed definition, if that group would be better off under an alternative arrangement in which assets were distributed differently, specifically if withdrawal with a per capita share of alienable assets would improve its position, while the complementary group would be worse off. General equilibrium models gave the definition precision. Agents with different endowments of assets and different preferences choose between working for themselves, hiring labour, being hired, lending, and borrowing, and the models show how exploitation and class emerge from those choices under specified rules. Class position and exploitation status correlate in the models without either being defined by the other, which allowed old claims to be restated as theorems with explicit assumptions open to inspection.

Economists who would never cite Marx in a seminar on price theory could still engage that reconstruction, because it spoke the professional language of endowments, constraints, equilibrium, and counterfactual comparison. Journals in economic theory published exchanges about the property relations definition, its handling of inalienable assets such as skills, and its extension to cases involving credit markets and incomplete contracts. Some participants treated the exercise as a rescue operation for a concept worth keeping. Others treated it as a demonstration that exploitation talk collapses into talk about unjust initial distribution, a conclusion they welcomed for opposite reasons. Either way, the concept had been moved onto terrain where formal argument decides, and graduate students encountering exploitation in a theory course met a definition they could manipulate, test for edge cases, and compare against rival definitions of coercion and unfairness. A fair verdict is that the formalisation preserved analytical content at the cost of moral resonance: what gained precision lost some connection to workplace experience and to historical narratives of expropriation. That verdict would shift if subsequent theoretical work showed the property relations definition generating novel, empirically confirmed predictions that rival accounts of distributive injustice failed to generate, since fruitfulness of that kind is the usual ground on which formal reconstructions are vindicated.

Institutional design formed the third strand of uptake in this section, quieter than the other two but consequential for policy discussion. Analytical writers asked a constructive question alongside their critical ones: if exploitation and domination arise from particular distributions of assets and authority, which alternative arrangements could reduce them while remaining feasible under real motivational and informational constraints? Roemer explored models of market socialism involving coupon based shareholding designed to disperse capital ownership without abolishing markets. Wright later organised collaborative inquiry into real utopias, examining institutions that embody egalitarian values within existing societies. Philippe Van Parijs, working in close dialogue with analytical Marxism, defended unconditional basic income as a route to real freedom, grounding the case in asset distribution and in the claim that jobs and resources embody rents that justice may tax and share. Debates about participatory governance drew on related ideas: citizens assemblies, participatory budgeting as practised in Porto Alegre and later elsewhere, and workplace democracy proposals all raised design questions about agenda control, information, and accountability that analytical writers had learned to pose with care.

Policy discussion absorbed those contributions selectively. Basic income moved from seminar rooms into pilot schemes, party programmes, and referendum debate in several countries during the decades after the analytical school flourished, and philosophical defences shaped by Van Parijs were cited by advocates who had little interest in Marxist theory as a whole. Participatory budgeting spread as a municipal practice valued by administrators for transparency and by development agencies for inclusion, usually justified in democratic rather than Marxist vocabulary. Proposals for widening asset ownership surfaced in discussions of sovereign wealth funds, baby bonds, and stakeholder grants, sometimes echoing coupon socialism and sometimes developing independently from similar premises about dispersed capital. Tracing exact descent in policy worlds is harder than tracing descent in journals, because politicians borrow without footnotes and movements rename ideas to widen their appeal. Still, the conceptual groundwork is visible: arguments that treat asset distribution as a variable open to design, rather than as a natural fact, entered mainstream policy reasoning partly through channels the analytical school dug.

Three features unite these otherwise separate stories of adoption. First, each contribution arrived in a form outsiders could detach from the whole doctrine: a coding scheme, a definition, a design proposal. Second, each contribution answered a need the receiving field already felt, whether for comparable categories, for conceptual clarity about exploitation, or for institutions that reconcile equality with efficiency. Third, each contribution exposed itself to failure in public, through datasets, counterexamples, or pilot experience, which made adoption a professional judgement rather than an act of faith. Stratification researchers kept the schema where it predicted well and modified it where it did not. Economists kept the definition where it clarified and discarded it where it obscured. Policy thinkers kept design elements that survived contact with administration and finance. Partial, conditional, revisable uptake of that kind lacks the drama of conversion, yet it represents how research programmes most often change the wider intellectual world, one usable piece at a time.


## Comparison with classical Marxism: methods and claims, not careers

Comparisons between intellectual traditions invite a familiar corruption. Instead of weighing arguments, audiences weigh biographies: who remained loyal, who defected, who held office, who suffered exile, who prospered in a university post. Such material fascinates, and movements understandably preserve it as legend or warning, but it settles nothing about whether a method illuminates evidence or whether a claim survives scrutiny. A comparison conducted fairly must therefore announce its basis in advance and hold to that basis when the biographical material beckons. For the two Marxisms considered here, the basis is restricted to stated methods and stated claims: what each tradition, in its canonical and representative texts, says about how inquiry should proceed and what inquiry has established. Adherents may live up to their stated method or fall short of it, and that gap is itself a legitimate finding, yet the finding must be documented from texts and research practice rather than inferred from careers or political fortunes. Readers who want a fuller orientation may consult [the comparison with classical Marxism](/2020/04/01/analytical-vs-classical-marxism/) after absorbing the discipline proposed in this section.

Classical Marxism, as stated in the writings of Marx and Engels and in the major interpreters who followed them, claimed possession of a distinctive method. Dialectics named that distinctiveness. In its various formulations, the dialectical method held that social phenomena must be grasped as moments of a developing whole, that contradictions internal to a form drive transformation of that form, that quantitative change passes into qualitative change, and that categories adequate to one stage of history may mislead when applied unchanged to another. Hegel supplied the logical ancestry, inverted, on the famous image, so that material processes rather than spirit carried development. Alongside the method stood a substantive theory of history: modes of production succeed one another through conflict between developing productive forces and relations of production that fetter them, class struggle provides the motor of political change, and the economic base conditions, in the last instance, legal, political, and ideological forms. Method and theory reinforced each other, since only a dialectical approach, defenders argued, could perceive totalities in motion rather than static slices of social life.

Unity of theory and practice formed the second stated pillar. Classical texts insist that understanding the world and changing it are not separate vocations. Theory arises from practical struggle, returns to guide that struggle, and proves itself partly through transformative success. The claim carried an epistemological edge: a standpoint rooted in the experience of the oppressed class was said to afford cognitive advantages, a clearer view of social relations than the partial perspectives available to dominant groups. It also carried an organisational edge, implying that intellectual work divorced from collective practice risks scholasticism, while practice divorced from theory risks blindness. Later traditions within classical Marxism elaborated the pillar differently, some stressing party mediation, others stressing council or movement forms, but the stated connection between knowing and doing remained a marker distinguishing the tradition from academic social science as usually conceived.

### On what basis should the two Marxisms be compared fairly?
A fair comparison rests on stated methods and stated claims alone. It asks what each tradition says about explanation, evidence, and the relation between theory and practice, and it judges those positions by argument and record rather than by biography or allegiance.

Against those stated positions, the analytical school stated almost point for point contraries, which is why the comparison repays careful handling. Analytical writers denied that Marxism possesses or requires a distinctive method. Cohen, Elster, Roemer, and their colleagues held that the tools of good inquiry are common property: logic, conceptual analysis, model construction, statistical inference, comparative history, and the ordinary canons of evidence used across philosophy and social science. Dialectics, on their reading, either expressed truisms about change and interconnection that no one disputes, or licensed obscure reasoning that evaded falsification, or occasionally gestured at real phenomena, such as feedback and unintended consequences, that clearer vocabularies could capture without remainder. Nothing was lost, they argued, by surrendering a proprietary method, and much was gained, since claims stated in shared terms can be assessed by anyone competent to follow an argument, regardless of prior commitment to the tradition.

Separation of truth from allegiance followed naturally. Analytical writers insisted that whether a proposition about capitalism is true depends on evidence and argument, not on the political identity of the person asserting it or on the consequences of believing it. A claim advanced by an opponent could be correct. A claim cherished by allies could be false. Normative commitments, socialism, equality, freedom from domination, were to be stated openly as values and defended by moral argument, while explanatory claims were to be tested by explanatory standards, and the two enterprises, though both legitimate, were not to be fused into a single test. Practice retained importance as the sphere where values are pursued and institutions tested, but practical success could not certify theoretical truth, and practical failure could not by itself refute a well supported explanation. Where classical writing treated the standpoint of a class as an epistemic resource, analytical writing treated standpoint as, at most, a heuristic that might suggest hypotheses, with justification still owed to public evidence.

Steelmanning each side sharpens the contrast and prevents a rigged verdict. The strongest case for a distinctive dialectical method points to phenomena that piecemeal analysis handles badly: mutually constitutive relations, such as capital and wage labour, each unintelligible without the other; thresholds where gradual accumulation erupts into structural change; and wholes whose properties emerge from organised interaction rather than from summation of parts. A method attentive to totality and contradiction may function as a research sensibility that directs attention toward such phenomena before formal tools exist to model them. The strongest case for the analytical denial replies that sensibility is not method, that every genuine insight attributed to dialectics has been restated, when pressed, in ordinary causal and structural language, and that a vocabulary which can accommodate any outcome explains none. Both cases deserve hearing in full. Adjudication then turns on track record: whether dialectical formulations generated specific, risky, confirmed findings that clearer restatements failed to preserve. If a careful historical audit uncovered such findings in number, the analytical verdict on method would require reversal. If restatement without loss proves possible case after case, the classical claim to distinctiveness weakens to a claim about emphasis and rhetoric.

A similar disciplined symmetry applies to substantive claims. Classical theory stated a directional account of historical development, a labour based account of value, and an expectation that capitalist development would simplify class structure and intensify crisis. Analytical writers examined each claim separately rather than accepting or rejecting the system wholesale. Cohen defended a carefully qualified version of the primacy of productive forces, using functional language that Elster then subjected to damaging criticism from within the school itself, an episode that demonstrates the stated commitment to public evidence operating in practice. Roemer discarded Marx's theory of value while reconstructing exploitation without it. Wright retained class as a central explanatory concept while revising its structure beyond recognition by orthodox standards. The pattern is neither wholesale preservation nor wholesale abandonment. It resembles a workshop in which inherited tools are tested one by one, some sharpened, some discarded, some melted down and recast. Whether the workshop still counts as the same tradition is a question about criteria for identity of traditions, itself a stated philosophical question to be argued rather than settled by loyalty or excommunication.

Disagreement about the state, ideology, and human motivation shows the same methodological divide in miniature. Classical accounts often explained institutions by their contribution to reproduction of the mode of production, and explained belief by position within relations of production. Analytical accounts demanded mechanisms in both domains: which actors, with which resources and beliefs, sustain a given institution against which alternatives, and through what processes of socialisation, selection, or coercion do particular beliefs spread and stabilise. On human nature, classical texts held a historically variable view, emphasising how needs and capacities are formed within modes of production, while analytical writers, especially in game theoretic work, often began from relatively stable motivational assumptions and explained variation through differing constraints and information. Neither starting point is self evidently correct. Variable human nature risks explaining everything by formation and nothing by constraint. Fixed motivational assumptions risk mistaking a historically specific actor for a universal one. Fair comparison notes the risk on both sides and asks which research programme, in its active decades, recognised and corrected its characteristic error more reliably.

What, then, is the verdict when comparison stays on the agreed basis? On stated method, the traditions genuinely conflict, and the conflict is not a misunderstanding that patient exegesis can dissolve: one claims a distinctive dialectical method joined to a unity of theory and practice, the other denies distinctive method and separates justification of claims from politics of claimants. On stated claims, overlap is substantial but selective, covering class, exploitation, and the critique of capitalist distribution, while diverging on value theory, historical direction, and the epistemic status of practice. On argumentative conduct as documented in texts, the analytical school states assumptions more explicitly and exposes claims to refutation more deliberately, which counts as a real advantage by standards both sides profess when doing careful work. That advantage would reverse if scholarship demonstrated that explicitness was purchased by systematically excluding the relational and emergent phenomena classical method was built to capture, and that those exclusions produced repeated predictive or explanatory failures in domains where classical accounts succeeded. Until such a demonstration arrives, the balanced conclusion stands: two research orientations sharing ancestry, vocabulary, and some conclusions, divided by incompatible accounts of what good inquiry is, and best compared, as here, by arguments they actually made rather than by lives their adherents happened to lead.

## What survives of analytical Marxism

Survival, for a school of thought, rarely means that a system passes intact into later work. More often a method outlives the doctrines that first carried it, a question outlives the answer originally given to it, and a tool built for one controversy proves serviceable in another. Analytical Marxism invites assessment in precisely those terms. By the middle of the 1990s much of the founding programme had been abandoned by its own leading figures: functional explanation had been sharply restricted, the strong version of historical materialism had been weakened or set aside, and confidence in a distinctive Marxist economics had thinned considerably. Yet abandonment on that scale does not settle the question of inheritance. A research tradition can lose its central theses and still leave behind standards, instruments, and habits of inquiry that continue to organise work for readers who share none of the original commitments. Weighing what survives therefore requires discrimination. Each candidate survival must be tested for detachability, for whether it can operate without the doctrines that were surrendered, and for whether it still does explanatory work rather than merely commemorating a style once associated with a famous group of scholars.

Foremost among the survivals stands the standard of clarity itself. G. A. Cohen set that standard in the 1978 defence of historical materialism by insisting that claims inherited from Marx be stated in a form precise enough to be assessed, distinguished from neighbouring claims with which they had been confused, and defended by arguments whose steps could be inspected one by one. Vagueness, on that view, was not depth. A term that could mean several things in successive paragraphs was not dialectical richness but a failure of exposition, and a thesis that shifted its meaning under pressure had not been defended at all. That discipline sounds modest, almost procedural, yet its effects were considerable. It forced distinctions that earlier commentary had blurred, separating base and superstructure treated as metaphors from the same pair treated as an explanatory claim, and separating a theory of history from a philosophy of history content to gesture at direction. The clarity standard survives because it never depended on any one of those distinctions being correct. A reader can reject the primacy thesis entirely and still hold that whoever advances a successor thesis owes the same precision. Seminars far removed from Marxist politics absorbed the expectation that a large claim should be broken into premises, that counterexamples should be met rather than absorbed, and that an author should say plainly which proposition a given argument supports. Where that expectation persists, the founding discipline persists with it, detached from the doctrine it first served.

Closely related, though distinct in function, stands the demand for mechanisms in social explanation. Jon Elster pressed that demand hardest, arguing that to explain an institution through the benefits it confers on a class or a system is incomplete until a feedback process is specified through which those consequences sustain the institution. Without such a process, functional talk borrows the appearance of explanation from biology while omitting the selection story that gives biological functionalism its warrant. The demand generalises well beyond its original target. It asks of any large scale explanation a middle level account: which actors, holding which beliefs and facing which constraints, act in ways that aggregate into the pattern to be explained, and through what channels unintended outcomes arise from intended actions. Game theory, models of collective action, and attention to preference formation all entered the school through that opening, because each offered a way of supplying the missing middle level. Detractors worried that the demand shrank explanation to individual action and excluded structural causation by definition, a worry examined at length in [the criticisms of analytical Marxism](/2020/05/01/analytical-marxism-criticisms/), and the worry retains force wherever mechanisms are treated as the only legitimate form of understanding. Weighed more carefully, however, the demand survives as a burden of proof rather than as a metaphysics. It establishes that an explanation which cannot indicate any process linking cause and effect remains unfinished, and that incompleteness should be acknowledged instead of being concealed by confident vocabulary about necessity or function.

A third survival, more concrete than either standard, is the class schema developed by Erik Olin Wright as a research instrument. Earlier Marxist class theory often operated with a polar picture, bourgeoisie confronting proletariat, into which managers, professionals, supervisors, and the self employed fitted awkwardly or not at all. Wright responded by building categories out of relations to productive assets, distinguishing ownership of capital, control over organisational resources, together with scarce skills and the credentials certifying them, and by allowing positions that were contradictory in the sense that they combined elements of domination and subordination at once. The schema was then put to empirical work in comparative survey research, tested against income, authority at work, and political attitudes across several countries. Wright revised the categories repeatedly in response to criticism and anomalous findings, and revision is part of the point. A schema that generates measurable hypotheses, exposes its own misfits, and can be altered without collapsing into ad hoc relabelling behaves like an instrument rather than a creed. It survives in that capacity even for researchers who discard the surrounding theory of history, because stratification research still needs principled ways of connecting position within production to interests, capacities, and likely alliances.

Exploitation, redefined through property relations by John Roemer, constitutes a fourth survival, though of a more contested kind. Roemer proposed that exploitation be understood counterfactually: a group is exploited when it would do better by withdrawing from the existing distribution of productive assets with a per capita share, while the complementary group would do worse. Formulated in general equilibrium terms and extended across feudal, capitalist, and socialist arrangements, the definition shifted attention from Marx's theory of value, which Roemer judged untenable, toward the distribution of assets as the morally and causally significant fact. Critics objected that the counterfactual test captures inequality of assets rather than the process of production itself. Those objections matter, and they limit how far the definition can travel unaccompanied. Yet the proposal remains a live analytical option for a specific reason: it separates the claim that an arrangement is exploitative from any commitment to surplus value as a measurable substance, and it allows exploitation under socialism, exploitation through skills, and exploitation through organisational position to be posed as coherent questions rather than ruled out by definition. A concept that opens new comparisons while forcing its users to state their baseline explicitly has not been refuted merely by being shown to diverge from an older concept.


A fifth survival concerns orientation rather than any single concept: a design orientation toward institutions. Once historical inevitability receded, questions about socialism changed character within the school. Instead of asking what history guaranteed, writers asked what rules, property forms, and incentive structures a more egalitarian order would require, and whether human motivation as plausibly described could sustain those arrangements. Roemer work on market socialism, with its coupon based distribution of corporate ownership, exemplifies the turn, as does later writing on basic income and stakeholder grants. The durable habit is precise: normative argument about a better society should include an institutional sketch detailed enough to be criticised on grounds of feasibility, stability, and perverse incentive, rather than resting on the moral attractiveness of an end state.

Beyond concepts and standards, the history of the school itself survives as a case study in how methods shape conclusions. A group that began by defending historical materialism with analytic tools ended, within two decades, by surrendering many of the claims it had set out to defend, and the trajectory was not accidental. Insistence on explicit mechanisms made functional explanation look evasive. Formal modelling of exploitation made the older value theory look dispensable. Empirical class analysis made the polar class picture look crude. In each instance the method, honestly applied, pressed against the doctrine it was meant to vindicate. That pattern carries a lesson for any tradition that imports a demanding method for apologetic purposes: standards of evidence and clarity have consequences that cannot be confined to the defence of preselected conclusions. The episode also rewards intellectual historians, since participants documented their own changes of mind in prefaces and retrospectives, leaving an unusually legible record of a school revising itself out of its founding positions.

### Which parts of the school remain usable after the abandonments?

The clarity standard, the demand for mechanisms, the class schema as a research instrument, and the property based account of exploitation remain usable, because each can be detached from functional explanation and from historical materialism and put to work on questions the founders never posed.

Usability, it should be stressed, is not the same as truth. A schema can be usable while misdescribing important features of a particular labour market, and a definition of exploitation can organise comparison while failing to capture what many readers regard as the core wrong of the workplace, namely subjection to another will during the working day. The survivals earn their place by a stricter test than familiarity. Each one generates work: distinctions to be drawn more carefully, processes to be specified more fully, categories to be tested against survey evidence, counterfactuals to be stated with an explicit baseline, institutions to be sketched at a level where incentives become visible. By contrast, several celebrated elements of the founding programme fail that test. The development thesis survives mainly as a historical claim about the beliefs of the school rather than as a tool anyone reaches for. The correspondence principle retains interest for specialists reconstructing Cohen, yet few researchers outside that circle employ it to explain a concrete transition. Sorting the inheritance in this way avoids two opposite errors: embalming the whole school as a classic to be revered, and dismissing the whole episode because its largest claims were given up by the very people who had defended them most ably.

Proportion matters in the final assessment. What survives is methodological and instrumental far more than doctrinal, and that imbalance is itself the most honest summary of the episode. A reader seeking a confirmed theory of history will find the cupboard largely bare, because the strongest arguments in the literature point against the strongest versions of that theory. A reader seeking discipline in argument, suspicion of unexplained functional claims, a differentiated map of class positions, a contestable account of exploitation centred on assets, and a willingness to design institutions rather than invoke destiny will find a substantial inheritance still in working order. Whether an inheritance of that kind counts as the survival of Marxism or only as the survival of tools forged inside a Marxist workshop is the naming question reserved for the closing section of this article. Tools can outlive the workshop, and their continued usefulness testifies to the quality of the craftsmanship without proving that the original product line remains in production.

## Where to start reading the school

Reading a school that changed its mind presents a peculiar difficulty, because the order in which books are encountered can easily create a false impression of what the school believed. Begin at the end, with the retrospectives, and the founding arguments appear as naive preliminaries to conclusions their authors later reached. Begin with hostile summaries, and the arguments never receive the hearing that makes later criticism intelligible. The route recommended here therefore follows the internal logic of the episode: founding argument at full strength first, then the most searching internal examination, then the boldest formal reconstruction, then the empirical programme, then the hostile case stated by a critic from a different Marxist lineage, and finally the retrospective statements in which participants redescribed their own trajectories. Each stage teaches something the next stage presupposes. Skipping stages produces characteristic misunderstandings, such as treating Roemer models as if they were illustrations of Cohen, or treating Elster scepticism as if it were an external attack rather than a demand the school addressed to itself.

Cohen from 1978 should come first for a simple reason: no other text states the founding argument with comparable completeness or force. That volume of 1978 reconstructs historical materialism as a set of theses about the development of productive forces, the correspondence between forces and relations of production, and the explanation of legal and political superstructures by reference to the economic structure they stabilise. Cohen distinguishes the primacy thesis from the development thesis, defends functional explanation as a legitimate pattern while acknowledging that elaborations of the underlying mechanisms are desirable, and answers objections drawn from celebrated counterexamples in ancient and feudal history. Reading the book first teaches a newcomer what a rigorous defence of a large theory looks like from inside: charitable reconstruction before criticism, distinctions introduced only where an argument requires them, and a willingness to concede local difficulties while contesting their significance. It also establishes the baseline against which every later departure can be measured. Without that baseline, talk of abandonment has no content, because a reader cannot tell what was abandoned. With the baseline secured, later texts become legible as revisions of identifiable propositions rather than as a general drift toward moderation or respectability.

Elster should follow, and Making Sense of Marx from 1985 supplies the examination at its most searching. Where Cohen reconstructed, Elster interrogated. Concept after concept is taken apart: dialectical reasoning is challenged as a licence for contradiction, functional explanation is pressed for the missing feedback loop, collective actors such as classes are asked to justify their explanatory role in terms of the individuals composing them, and the theory of ideology is tested against standards drawn from the philosophy of social science. The effect on a reader who arrives directly from Cohen is deliberately unsettling, and that unsettlement is pedagogically central to the route. It demonstrates that analytical Marxism contained its own most damaging critic from an early stage, and that the distance between defence and dissolution inside the school was shorter than the distance between the school and many external opponents. Placing Elster second, before any formal reconstruction, ensures that the demand for mechanisms is felt as a standing question addressed to everything read afterwards, rather than as a technical footnote appended after the models have already persuaded by their elegance.


Roemer occupies the third stage, with his treatise on exploitation and class from 1982 as the central text. After Cohen defence and Elster interrogation, a reader is prepared to appreciate both the audacity and the cost of formalisation. Roemer rebuilds the concept of exploitation using general equilibrium theory and a property relations criterion, shows how exploitation can arise through asset inequality without any fraudulent exchange, and extends the analysis across modes of production so that feudal, capitalist, and socialist forms can be compared within a single framework. The stage teaches what formal modelling does to a moralised concept. Ambiguity is expelled, but so is some of the texture that made the older concept resonate: domination at the point of production recedes, distribution of assets advances to the foreground, and Marx's theory of value is quietly retired as unnecessary for the central result. A reader who reaches Roemer before Cohen may mistake the model for the tradition itself. A reader who reaches Roemer third can see the model as a wager, namely that Marxist normative concerns about exploitation survive translation into the language of mainstream economics and emerge stronger for the translation. Whether the wager succeeded remains disputed, and the dispute is more instructive than either verdict taken alone, because it reveals how much of a doctrine can be preserved when its supporting economics is replaced wholesale.

Wright comes fourth, because Classes from 1985 turns the route from argument and model toward evidence. Cohen supplied theses, Elster supplied doubts, Roemer supplied formalism, and Wright supplied a research programme in which class categories are operationalised, measured, and exposed to data that could embarrass them. Contradictory class locations and the comparative survey project spanning several industrial societies show a Marxist concept being treated as a hypothesis about social structure rather than as a premise of political identity. The stage matters for order as well as content. Encountered too early, Wright categories can look like scholastic multiplication of boxes. Encountered after the theoretical stages, the boxes appear as answers to genuine problems: how to classify managers who command labour they do not own, how to place credentialed professionals whose market position differs sharply from that of routine workers, how to explain variation in class consciousness without treating consciousness as a reflex of a two class map. Wright also teaches intellectual honesty of a distinctive kind, since the published revisions record a scholar altering his own schema when the evidence and the criticism required alteration.

The fifth stage reverses the perspective. The Retreat from Class, published by Ellen Meiksins Wood in 1986, presents the hostile case from within a rival Marxist commitment, not from a liberal or conservative standpoint. Wood charged that the broader retreat from class analysis dissolved the centrality of production, class struggle, and historical specificity into abstract models and political accommodations. Her targets extended beyond the analytical school narrowly defined, and a careful reader will notice where the polemic groups together writers who differed sharply among themselves. That breadth is a reason to read the book critically, not a reason to omit it. Placed fifth, after the primary texts, Wood forces a reckoning that internal criticism cannot supply, because Elster and Roemer questioned methods and concepts while still sharing much of the project vocabulary. Wood questions the project direction itself, asking whether clarity purchased at the price of historical specificity leaves anything recognisably Marxist standing. Reading her earlier would prime a newcomer to dismiss arguments before understanding them. Reading her at this point turns polemic into a test: which charges land on the texts as written, and which land on a composite opponent assembled for rhetorical convenience.

Retrospective statements form the final stage, and they should be read as evidence about trajectory rather than as authoritative summaries. Later prefaces by Cohen, in which the defence of historical materialism is qualified and egalitarian normative theory moves to the centre, show a founder relocating the heart of a project. Prefaces and interviews in which Elster distances himself from Marxism altogether show a different terminus reached from a shared starting point. Wright reflections on the class project record continuity under revision, a contrasting pattern. Roemer later writing on equality of opportunity and market socialist design shows concerns persisting after their Marxist presentation had faded. Read together and read last, these documents teach the skill most needed for studying any school: distinguishing what an author claimed at a given date from what the same author later wished had been claimed, and treating self interpretation as one source among others, subject to the same checking as any secondary account. A route that ends here leaves a reader with a history rather than a creed, which is the appropriate outcome for a literature whose own history became its most instructive product. For readers who wish to extend the route into the modelling literature and its critics, [the sources for rational choice Marxism](/2020/05/15/rational-choice-marxism-sources/) gathers the relevant material in one place.

### Which single argument is the most teachable entry point?

The most teachable entry point is the defence of historical materialism in Cohen from 1978, because its distinction between the forces and relations of production can be stated plainly, tested against examples, and then subjected to criticism without first mastering formal modelling or game theory.

Teachability deserves a word of defence, since the most teachable argument is not necessarily the most original or the most nearly correct. A seminar leader needs an argument that fits within a few sessions, that divides cleanly into premises students can dispute separately, and that generates disagreement without requiring agreement on background politics. The Cohen defence meets those conditions unusually well. One group of students can defend the development thesis using the history of agricultural technique, another can attack it using ecological limits or military destruction of productive capacity, and a third can ask whether correspondence explains change or merely redescribes it. Elster critique can then be introduced as a second act for the same seminar, because students who have committed themselves to positions feel the force of the mechanisms objection in a way that passive readers rarely do. Roemer withdrawal test offers an alternative entry point for economics students, and Wright contradictory locations offer another for sociology students, yet both presuppose more technical or empirical background than the Cohen argument requires.

A final caution about the route concerns translation between vocabularies. Terms such as exploitation, class, and ideology carry ordinary language associations that the analytical writers deliberately narrowed, and a newcomer who imports the ordinary associations into the technical texts will manufacture contradictions that careful reading would dissolve. The route mitigates that risk by accumulation: each stage redefines its key terms in proximity to the problems those terms were built to solve, so that by the Wright stage a reader has encountered several disciplined usages and can compare them. Comparing usages, rather than assuming a single Marxist lexicon shared across the tradition, is itself a historical finding about the school.

## Is analytical Marxism still Marxism: the question readers bring

Few questions about the school are asked more often, and few are answered worse, than whether analytical Marxism is still Marxism at all. The question invites definition mongering, in which a checklist of doctrines is drawn up, the school is measured against the list, and a verdict follows mechanically from decisions smuggled into the checklist itself. Define Marxism by commitment to Marx's theory of value, and Roemer fails by construction. Define it by concern with class and exploitation under capitalism, and most of the school passes trivially. Define it by fidelity to a method associated with Marx, and the answer depends on a prior dispute about what that method was, a dispute Marx scholarship has never settled. Honest treatment therefore begins by refusing to let a definition do the work of an argument. Two separate questions must be held apart. One concerns lineage: from what intellectual descent, institutional setting, and self description did the school emerge. Another concerns doctrine: which claims characteristic of classical Marxism were retained, which were revised beyond recognition, and which were surrendered outright as the work developed. A label can be justified on lineage grounds while failing on doctrinal grounds, or the reverse, and much confusion in the literature comes from sliding between the two justifications mid argument, claiming descent when doctrine is challenged and claiming doctrine when descent is questioned.

On lineage, the case is straightforward and strong. The founding figures presented their project as Marxism, understood themselves as rescuing Marxist claims from obscurity rather than replacing them with a different politics, and were received by allies and opponents alike as participants in Marxist debate. Cohen defence of historical materialism was offered as a defence, not as a farewell. The September Group gathered scholars whose shared reference points were Marxist texts and Marxist problems, even where conclusions diverged. Journals, conferences, and critics of the period located the school inside the Marxist tradition, sometimes to welcome it and sometimes to expel it, and expulsion itself presupposes prior membership in the relevant sense. Self description at founding is not decisive for all purposes, since movements can misdescribe themselves, yet combined with descent of problems, texts, and interlocutors, it establishes lineage beyond reasonable dispute. Genealogy also explains features of the later work that a purely doctrinal inventory would miss: why exploitation remained central after its classical foundation was removed, why class retained priority over other axes of inequality in the research programmes, and why egalitarian conclusions were pursued through Marxist questions long after Marxist answers had been relinquished.

On doctrine, the record points the other way, and candour requires stating the extent of the surrender. Classical Marxism, in any historically recognisable form, asserted some combination of the following: a theory of history in which productive development drives large scale social change, an account of capitalism centred on surplus value extracted in production, an expectation that class polarisation and class struggle would transform capitalist societies, and a political orientation toward socialist transition understood as a historical prospect rather than merely a moral aspiration. Within two decades of the founding, leading figures in the school had abandoned or radically weakened every item on that list. Historical materialism in its strong form was defended by almost none of the founders in its original shape. Marx's theory of value had been rejected by Roemer and treated as dispensable by many others. Class analysis survived, but as empirical stratification research detached from predictions of polarisation or revolutionary agency. Socialist commitment survived in several figures, but increasingly as normative egalitarian theory argued on grounds any liberal egalitarian could assess, with Cohen later work on equality and community engaging Rawlsian and luck egalitarian debates more directly than debates internal to Marxism. What remained recognisably continuous was a set of questions, a standard of argument, and a moral seriousness about exploitation and domination, rather than a body of doctrine that a nineteenth century or early twentieth century Marxist would have recognised as the faith.


Self description within the school shifted alongside the doctrinal record, and the shifts are evidence rather than decoration. Early statements speak the language of defence, reconstruction, and renewal, implying a tradition in good enough health to benefit from clearer statement. Middle period statements speak increasingly of testing, revision, and selective retention, implying a tradition whose claims stand or fall one by one. Later statements diverge by figure. Some participants described themselves as former Marxists or as post Marxist egalitarians, some retained the label with qualifications about which Marxism was meant, and some, notably Wright, continued to work within a self described Marxist social science while rebuilding its foundations. A label whose bearers apply it inconsistently across their own careers cannot be settled by appeal to bearer authority alone. The pattern of usage nevertheless tells a story that matches the doctrinal inventory: confidence in the label was highest when doctrinal retention was highest, and qualification and past tense usage spread as retention declined.

Critics from several directions exploited the gap between lineage and doctrine, and their arguments clarify what is at stake. Wood and other defenders of a more classical Marxism treated the surrenders as proof that the analytical turn had been an exit disguised as a renovation. Liberal critics reached a mirror image conclusion, welcoming the school as evidence that rigorous treatment dissolves Marxist claims, and treating the survivors as recruits to ordinary social science or ordinary egalitarian philosophy. Defenders replied that traditions are defined by evolving problems rather than by fixed creeds, and that liberalism and Darwinism both survived transformations as deep as any undergone here. The analogy deserves respect but also scrutiny. Liberalism survived deep change while retaining identifiable commitments to individual rights across most variants, and Darwinism survived while retaining natural selection at its core. The relevant question is therefore comparative and specific: after the transformations, does a core remain that plays the same organising role, or does continuity rest mainly on biography, institutions, and shared questions.

### What kind of answer can the naming question honestly receive?

The naming question can honestly receive only a divided answer, one that separates lineage from doctrine, states which claims were retained and which were surrendered, and specifies what evidence about the doctrinal core would require the verdict itself to be revised or withdrawn.

The verdict offered here follows from that division. The label fits the founding project: a self described Marxist undertaking, descending directly from Marxist texts and problems, aiming to defend historical materialism and to rebuild Marxist concepts with analytic rigour. For later work, the label fits only by descent. Where a scholar in the middle 1990s retained egalitarian commitments, class as a central category, and exploitation as a central wrong, while surrendering the theory of history, the doctrine of surplus value, and the expectation of socialist transition as a historically grounded prospect, calling the result Marxism without qualification misleads a reader about doctrinal content. Calling it unrelated to Marxism misleads in the opposite direction, erasing the genealogy that explains why those particular questions, and not others, organised the research. Divided verdicts of this kind can look evasive to readers who want a single word answer, yet the evasion would lie in the single word, which would conceal exactly the distinction the evidence supports. Precision here is not pedantry. Library classification, syllabus design, and intellectual history all make practical decisions on the basis of such labels, and a divided verdict guides those decisions better than a forced binary: shelve the founding texts with Marxism, teach the later texts at the boundary between Marxist and egalitarian theory, and describe individual figures by the self description appropriate to the period of the work being discussed.

Every responsible verdict should carry its defeater, and the defeater for this one can be stated exactly. The verdict would be reversed by evidence that the retained claims, rather than the abandoned ones, were the doctrinal core all along. Such evidence would need to show, from the classical tradition itself rather than from retrospective convenience, that concern with exploitation understood through asset distribution, differentiated class analysis, and egalitarian institutional design constituted the centre of Marxism across its major historical forms, while the theory of history, surplus value, and revolutionary transition were peripheral accretions that historical Marxists themselves treated as dispensable. If that showing could be made, then analytical Marxism would count as a purification that recovered the core by discarding the husk, and the doctrinal verdict offered here would fall. Attempts in that direction exist, usually by elevating the critique of domination in the early writings of Marx or the normative passages of the mature work into the essence of the tradition. Those attempts have not persuaded most historians of Marxism, because the textual and organisational record of the classical movement places the surrendered claims near the centre of what adherents believed, taught, and acted upon. Stating the defeater nonetheless matters, because it converts a verdict from a terminological preference into a claim that evidence could overturn, which is the standard the school itself taught its readers to demand.

A sober closing observation concerns what the naming dispute reveals about traditions generally. Labels survive for many reasons: institutional inertia, publisher categories, the loyalties of students, the polemical needs of opponents who require a stable target, and the genuine continuity of problems that outlast every proposed solution. Analytical Marxism exhibits all of those reasons in unusually transparent form, because its participants argued in public about their own label while changing the views the label was supposed to summarise. Studying that transparency is worthwhile even for readers who conclude that the label should be retired for the later work. Few schools document their own transformation with comparable honesty, and fewer still leave behind tools that critics of the school can use without embarrassment. The founding ambition was to make Marxism clear enough to be tested. The tests were applied, many claims failed them, and the record of that failure, together with the instruments that survived it, remains the durable contribution. Whether future historians file that contribution under Marxism, under egalitarian theory, or under the history of social science method will depend on decisions about classification that the evidence can inform but cannot make on behalf of those who must take them.

## For students and exam candidates

The examination value of this school lies in a distinction that strong answers make explicit and weak answers blur: the distinction between a method permitting a conclusion and a method requiring it. A candidate who can show that the analytical standards permitted the abandonments, while the further claim that those standards compelled them needs separate evidence, has grasped the undecidable trajectory at the level examiners reward. Build the answer around the trajectory table, naming the member, the claim surrendered, and the reason published for surrendering it, rather than summarising the school as a mood. For the systematic comparison of methods and claims, work through [a systematic comparison of the two methods](/2020/04/01/analytical-vs-classical-marxism/) after this guide. Keep a [VaultBook study card on the trajectory table](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) among your revision materials, with one row per member and a final column reserved for the defeater: the evidence that would settle which reading of the trajectory is correct. Answers that carry that defeater sentence distinguish themselves, because they show judgement operating on the material rather than loyalty or hostility recited at it.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The most teachable single argument in the school is the founding one, and the natural seminar route runs through [the companion study of that founding argument](/2020/01/15/ga-cohen-functional-explanation/); in its pages students can watch a Marxist claim being rebuilt to survive analytical scrutiny and can locate the exact premise where the rebuilding becomes contestable. A productive seminar sequence sets the 1978 defence beside the 1986 charge of dissolution and asks the room to identify which specific claims each text is addressing, since the two sides often pass each other by arguing about different propositions. The trajectory table works well as a distributed exercise: each participant takes one member, reconstructs the position of 1979 and the position of the middle nineties from the published record, and reports what the member personally gave as the reason for moving. Discussion improves when the defeater question is put formally at the end, requiring every reading of the school's history to name the evidence that would overturn it before the session closes.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Researchers will find the school most useful at its points of export, where its instruments entered fields that never shared its politics: the mechanisms programme in analytical sociology, the class schema in comparative stratification, and the property relations definition of exploitation in economic theory. The uptake sections of this guide map those transfers, and the primary materials are gathered through [a gathered bibliography of the wider school](/2020/05/15/rational-choice-marxism-sources/), which should be the first stop for archival and bibliographical work. Historians of ideas should treat the prefaces and retrospective statements of the later years as primary evidence of a kind the school itself prized, since the members recorded their own changes of position with unusual explicitness. Journalists covering disputes about Marxism's intellectual standing should note the discipline this guide has observed throughout: the trajectory is evidence that something happened, and the fight concerns what it means, so any report that presents either reading as settled fact has taken a side in an argument the record alone cannot close.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is analytical Marxism?

Analytical Marxism names the school that took shape in 1979 by submitting Marxist claims to the standards of analytical philosophy, formal economics and rational choice theory. Its members held that no distinctive Marxist method exists, that no separate dialectical logic licenses conclusions unavailable to ordinary argument, and that any valid Marxist claim must be statable in plain terms and tested by evidence. It began as a defence of Marxism conducted by committed scholars, which is what makes its later history so striking: the same standards that were meant to strengthen the doctrine were followed, step by argued step, to the surrender of many of its central theses.

### Q: When was the September Group founded?

The September Group was founded in 1979, when a circle drawn from philosophy, economics, political science and sociology began meeting each September to examine Marxist claims by ordinary academic standards. The gathering formed around the argument of G. A. Cohen's book of 1978 defending Marx's theory of history, which had shown that a central Marxist doctrine could be restated as a clear argument with premises an opponent could inspect. The annual rhythm gave the group its name and its durability: papers circulated in advance, arguments were pressed hard in session, and positions were expected to move when the pressing succeeded, a discipline that shaped everything the school later produced and surrendered.

### Q: Who were the members of the group?

The core of the group consisted of the philosopher G. A. Cohen, the philosopher and social theorist Jon Elster, the economist John Roemer and the sociologist Erik Olin Wright, surrounded by a wider circle that included historians, political scientists and economists working in British, Norwegian and American universities. Membership was defined by participation in the annual meetings and by the shared methodological commitment rather than by any doctrinal test, which is why the circle could contain scholars who disagreed profoundly with one another about which Marxist claims deserved to survive. That breadth across disciplines mattered to the school's character: arguments had to be stated in terms a neighbouring field could examine, so no claim could hide inside the conventions of a single specialism.

### Q: Why did its members abandon Marxist claims?

The members gave published reasons rather than a collective explanation, and the reasons differed by claim. Marx's theory of value was surrendered because exploitation could be defined more cleanly through unequal ownership of productive assets. Functional explanation was qualified because explanations by purpose were judged incomplete without mechanisms beneath them. Historical materialism lost its founder's confidence as the evidence and the arguments were reweighed over fifteen years of scrutiny. Whether those reasons record honest inquiry following arguments to unwelcome conclusions, or a method predisposed against its object delivering the verdict built into it, is the undecidable question at the centre of this guide, and the published record supports both readings equally.

### Q: Did they really call it non-bullshit Marxism?

Yes. The label was the group's own, used in its correspondence and repeated publicly by its founder, and it was never a critics' insult pasted onto the school from outside. The phrase announced a standard rather than a joke: discourse in parts of the Marxist tradition was held to trade in obscurity that protected claims from examination, and the group defined itself against that habit. Members intended the bluntness as a credential, evidence that their defence of Marxism would be conducted without protective fog. Critics later quoted the label back at the school as its members abandoned the claims they had gathered to defend, but the label's origin as a self-description is a matter of the group's own record.

### Q: What did the school contribute?

The school's contributions were substantial on both sides of its trajectory. Cohen produced a defence of historical materialism resting on functional explanation that remains the strongest analytical statement of Marx's theory of history. Elster subjected Marx's social theory to a reconstruction so searching that it became a largely destructive assessment, while clarifying which fragments survived. Roemer formalised exploitation through the unequal ownership of assets used in production, freeing the concept from dependence on Marx's theory of value. Wright converted class analysis into an empirical programme of international survey research. Beyond those four achievements stand the standard of clarity itself, the demand that social explanation supply mechanisms, and a later body of work on equality and on the design of alternative institutions.

### Q: What did critics say about the school?

Hostility from other Marxists was immediate and sustained, and its most influential statement was Ellen Meiksins Wood's The Retreat from Class of 1986, which argued that the school had not reconstructed Marxism but dissolved it. On that charge, the method carried its conclusion inside itself: a framework built on individuals with given preferences exchanging voluntarily could never recover the class relations Marx analysed, so the abandonment of the working class as historical agent was implicit from the start. Other critics added that technique had evacuated politics, turning a doctrine of emancipation into a set of puzzles for specialists. The school's defenders answered that clarity cannot dissolve a true claim, and that what dissolved under examination was only what examination showed to be indefensible.

### Q: Is the trajectory evidence of anything?

The trajectory is firm evidence that a sequence of abandonments occurred, that each was argued in print, and that the members themselves supplied reasons connecting their standards to their surrenders. It is not, by itself, evidence that settles why the sequence ran as it did, because the two candidate explanations predict the same outward history. Honest inquiry by committed defenders following arguments honestly would produce exactly this pattern of staggered, reasoned retreat. A method rigged against its object would generate the identical pattern automatically. Evidence that could discriminate would be a case where a surrender was compelled by a step the method left no room to refuse, or a conclusion reached against the method's grain, and the standard literature supplies neither in settled form.

### Q: What survives of analytical Marxism?

Several things survive independently of the doctrine they were built to serve. The standard of clarity, the insistence that a claim which cannot be stated plainly may not be a claim, has passed into the ordinary expectations of political theory and social science. The demand for mechanisms beneath functional explanations transformed debate within the philosophy of social science and within analytical sociology. Wright's class schema remains an instrument of comparative stratification research. Roemer's property relations definition of exploitation remains a live option in economic theory. The design orientation, treating alternative institutions as objects of rigorous construction rather than slogans, also endures. What does not survive is the founding confidence that these standards would vindicate the classical doctrine they were first turned upon.

### Q: Where should you start with the school?

Start with the founding argument at full strength: Cohen's 1978 defence of the Marxist theory of history, where the school's standards are applied in defence and the primacy thesis can be examined premise by premise. Move next to Elster's reconstruction of Marx's social theory for the most searching examination of all, and then to Roemer's general theory for the formalisation of exploitation through asset ownership. Wright's Classes supplies the empirical programme in its mature form. Read Wood's book of hostile criticism from 1986 after those four, so the hostile case meets a reader who already holds the positions it attacks. Finish with the later prefaces and retrospective statements, where the members describe their own changes of mind and the trajectory can be judged from their testimony as well as their arguments.

### Q: How does the undecidable trajectory grade the school's history?

The undecidable trajectory grades the school's history by refusing both available marks. It will not award the history to the defence, as though rigorous standards had vindicated inquiry by destroying error, and it will not award it to the prosecution, as though a rigged method had executed a doctrine by construction. Instead it records the trajectory as a result with two sufficient explanations and no discriminating evidence between them, which is a precise finding rather than an evasion. Grading by undecidability also sets the standard for future work on the school: any account that claims the history proves its preferred reading must produce the case or the required step that breaks the symmetry, and until such evidence appears, the honest grade remains undecided between two fully stated alternatives.

### Q: What should a student separate between a method permitting a conclusion and a method requiring it?

A student should separate the observation that a method allows a conclusion from the much stronger claim that the method forces it. Analytical standards permitted the members of the September Group to abandon Marx's theory of value, because those standards allowed the concept of exploitation to be rebuilt on other foundations. Permitting is not requiring: the same standards also permitted Cohen's defence of historical materialism, which was written under them and reached a Marxist conclusion. To show that a method required an abandonment, an answer must identify a point at which the method allowed no refusal, a premise that could not be declined while keeping the standards intact. Answers that keep permission and requirement apart can weigh the two readings of the trajectory; answers that fuse them merely choose a side.

### Q: Why did the school's members abandon claims they had set out to defend?

Because the examination they had committed themselves to kept returning verdicts they had undertaken in advance to accept. Each member had publicly bound his defence of Marxism to standards that made retreat honourable and evasion shameful: state the claim plainly, expose the premises, follow the argument, publish the result. When Roemer's models showed exploitation standing without Marx's theory of value, the theory lost its defender by the defender's own rule. When Elster's reconstruction found purposes where mechanisms were required, the explanations were surrendered by the scholar who had demanded mechanisms. When Cohen reweighed historical materialism across fifteen years, his later preface recorded the loss of confidence in the same plain style the school had made its signature. Defence conducted under those rules contains its own possible defeat.

### Q: How does the trajectory table order the school's development?

The trajectory table orders the school's development by member rather than by doctrine, because the history happened to persons arguing in sequence and not to a creed changing in the abstract. Each row fixes a member at two dated points, the position held in 1979 and the position held by the middle of the nineteen nineties, with the principal work between them and the member's own account of the change in the final column. Reading down the rows shows the stagger: the abandonments were spread across fifteen years and never coordinated. Reading across a row shows the mechanism of the whole history in miniature, a stated starting position, a major work of examination, a different resting place, and a published reason connecting them that the reader can accept or contest.

### Q: What separates a school refuted by its own standards from a school that changed its mind?

A school that simply changed its mind drifted: its members absorbed new fashions, new evidence or new company, and their positions softened without any single examination doing the work. A school refuted by its own standards was driven: each surrender traces back to an argument conducted under rules the members had set for themselves before the outcome was known, with the reasoning published and the premises exposed to opponents. The September Group fits the second pattern in its outward form, since every abandonment arrived attached to a book or paper arguing it. The undecidable residue is that a method chosen for its substantive leanings would generate the same paper trail, so the distinction in form is clear even where the distinction in ultimate explanation remains open.
