## The Bridge the Story Crossed: Matteo Bandello's Novella and the Road to the Play

Matteo Bandello's novella of Romeo and Juliet, printed at Lucca in 1554 as the ninth tale of the second part of his *Novelle*, is the most consequential retelling in the story's long migration from Italy to the London stage. The verdict argued across this study can be stated at once. Luigi da Porto invented the Verona lovers: he fixed their names, their houses, their friar, their tomb. Bandello, however, built the version of their story that Europe actually inherited. Working a generation after da Porto, the Mantuan courtier took a compact Italian *historia* and expanded it into a full prose novella, deepening incident, enlarging motive, promoting the nurse from a function to a character, slowing the poison purchase in Mantua into a scene of poverty and temptation, and staging the tomb sequence at a ceremonial length no earlier telling attempts. Every one of those enlargements then travelled. Pierre Boaistuau translated Bandello into French in 1559, keeping the sequence of incident almost entire. Arthur Brooke versified Boaistuau in 1562, and William Painter Englished the same French text in prose in 1566. Shakespeare, in the middle 1590s, worked from Brooke, with Painter's parallel prose within reach. The chain therefore has a load-bearing middle, and the middle is Bandello.

![Matteo Bandello's novella and the Verona source chain - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-60.webp)

One circumstance of the printing deserves emphasis at the outset, because it explains the speed of everything that followed. Bandello published into a Europe newly organized for transmission: Italian was the prestige vernacular of courts, French translators stood ready to convert Italian successes for northern readers, and the tragic tale was about to become a continental fashion. A story printed at Lucca in 1554 could be French by 1559 and English by 1562, not because anyone conspired to promote it, but because the machinery of the European book trade and the appetite of the Valois and Tudor courts for Italian narrative were already built and waiting. Bandello's novella was the right cargo at the right port, and its expansions, its scenes, speeches and ceremonies, were exactly the qualities that survived conversion, since structure and pathos translate where verbal wit dies. The chain this study follows is therefore not a curiosity of bibliography. It is the normal circulatory system of Renaissance narrative, caught in the act by an unusually well documented case.

The habit of speech that skips that middle is the cliché this series exists to correct. Accounts of the play's origins tend to leap from a vague gesture at "the Italian sources" straight to Brooke's poem, as though the poem were a spring rather than a conduit. Restoring Bandello changes where the credit sits, and it changes what the play can be seen doing. A companion study in this series sets out [the sources of Romeo and Juliet as a family tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), from Masuccio's Sienese pattern to the play itself, and the present study occupies the thickest branch of that tree. Details that look like Shakespearean inventions when Brooke alone is consulted, the nurse's command of the middle acts, the apothecary's poverty as the hinge of the poison scene, the crowded and witnessed tomb, resolve into inheritances once the chain is laid out whole. Bandello is not the play's source in the strict sense; not one word of his Italian reached the playwright's desk. He is something more interesting: the architect of the inherited story, the teller whose expansions decided what the story contained by the time England received it. This study examines the man and his book, separates his additions from da Porto's design with the care the scholarship demands, follows the novella through France into the two English versions, traces his fingerprints inside the play, and adjudicates the standing scholarly dispute about how much of Bandello survives the crossings.

## Who Was Matteo Bandello, and Why Does His Book Matter?

Bandello was born about 1485 at Castelnuovo Scrivia, in the contested border country between Milan and Piedmont, into a family with scholarly connections at the Milanese court. His uncle Vincenzo Bandello, a leading Dominican and eventually master general of the order, was prior of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, and the young Matteo spent formative years in that orbit, wearing the Dominican habit for a time before the cloister lost its hold on him. The Italian Wars made and unmade his career, as they made and unmade every courtier of his generation. He served a succession of masters in the shifting alliances of the peninsula, attached himself for years to Cesare Fregoso, the Genoese condottiere in French service, travelled with the French interest, survived Fregoso's assassination in 1541, and ended in the protection of the French crown, pensioned by Henry II and raised in 1550 to the bishopric of Agen, a dignity that sat lightly on a man whose life's work was a book of tales. He died in 1561.

The book is the *Novelle*: two hundred and fourteen tales, issued in three parts at Lucca by the printer Vincenzo Busdraghi in 1554, with a fourth part appearing after his death at Lyon in 1573. The *Novelle* belong to the great Italian tradition descending from Boccaccio, but Bandello loosened the old frame. Where the *Decameron* locks its tellers into a single company and a single retreat, Bandello prefaces each novella with a dedicatory letter to a named patron, patroness, captain or courtier, and presents the tale as one recounted in a specific gathering, at Milan, at Mantua, at Verona, in the salons and camps of the wars, often by a named speaker whose company Bandello claims to have shared. The device is a claim about kind: these are not fables but reports, things said aloud among soldiers and courtiers and then written down by the man who heard them. Bandello was, by profession, a teller of stories in great houses, and the *Novelle* are that performance fixed in print, the largest single collection of tales the Italian sixteenth century produced.

The Romeo and Juliet novella stands as the ninth story of the second part. Its heading runs, in the old spelling, "La sfortunata morte di dui infelicissimi amanti, l'uno di veleno e l'altro di dolore": the unfortunate death of two most unhappy lovers, one by poison and the other by grief. The heading already does Bandello's characteristic work. It announces an ending, as da Porto's telling had done and as the play's Prologue will do again in the words René Weis prints at the head of the Arden Third Series text, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue.1), and it distributes the deaths between an instrument and an emotion, poison for him, grief for her, a small essay in causation before the tale begins. Bandello presents the matter as history: events of Verona under Bartolomeo della Scala, two centuries and more before his own day, involving houses whose very names carried a warrant of fact, since Dante had coupled Montecchi and Cappelletti among the feuding families of a divided Italy in the sixth canto of the *Purgatorio*. Whether any pair of lovers ever lived and died as the tale describes is a question the evidence cannot support, and modern scholarship treats the story as legend anchored to real names. Bandello's telling neither knows nor cares: it proceeds with the circumstantial confidence of a chronicler, and that confidence, the texture of a world fully furnished, is precisely what he added to the tale he received.

## The World That Told the Tale: Courts, Wars, and the Novella as Performance

To understand what Bandello's expansion is, the conditions of its making matter. The Italy of the *Novelle* is a peninsula at war with itself and with the great powers: French, Spanish and Imperial armies crossing and recrossing Lombardy and the Veneto, courts dissolving and reforming, soldiers and secretaries thrown together in garrisons, lodgings and great houses with time on their hands and catastrophe at the door. Bandello's dedicatory letters keep returning to those rooms. A tale, in his presentation, is something performed before it is printed: recounted after supper in a Milanese palace, offered by a captain to pass a night in camp, exchanged among exiles and hosts as a currency of civility. The named tellers and described companies are partly courtesy and partly claim, the claim being that these stories carry the authority of the world they describe because they were first currency inside it.

Bandello himself was perfectly placed to collect that currency. A secretary and companion to commanders, a familiar of the Gonzaga and Fregoso circles, a man whose livelihood depended on being welcome in rooms where stories were wealth, he accumulated tales the way other secretaries accumulated dispatches. His prose keeps the marks of the spoken occasion. He repeatedly disclaims polish, presenting the *Novelle* as speech written down rather than literature composed, and the disclaimer, whether feigned or honest, describes the method accurately: the tales move at the pace of a voice filling an evening, digressing for a portrait, pausing for a maxim, swelling at the climaxes where a listening room leans in. The Romeo novella's famous amplitude is therefore not an accident of one retelling. It is the house style of a professional teller whose material lengthened in the mouth, performance by performance, before it ever reached Busdraghi's press.

The dedicatory economy deserves a word, since it governs the frame discussed later in this study. Each novella is a gift, inscribed to a patron whose protection the teller needs or whose favour he honours, and a gift must present its credentials: hence the moralizing wrappers, the assurances of truth, the exemplary intentions solemnly declared. Bandello's Verona tale arrives inside that economy like every other, a present for a great household, warranted useful. What distinguishes it is not its wrapper but its afterlife: of the two hundred and fourteen gifts, this is the one the courts of France and the bookstalls of London unwrapped and kept. The tale that travelled farthest from the rooms where such stories were currency was the one whose teller had invested most heavily in the universal instruments, in deliberation, spectacle and pathos rather than in local allusion. Expansion was export strategy before it was anything else, whether or not Bandello ever thought of it in those terms.

## The Story Before Bandello: Siena, Dante, and da Porto's Design

The pattern of the story is older than Verona. In 1476 Masuccio Salernitano printed at Naples his *Novellino*, whose thirty-third tale tells of Mariotto Mignanelli and Gianozza Saraceni of Siena: a secret marriage arranged by a friar, a killing, exile, a sleeping potion supplied to the bride to escape a forced second match, a message that fails, a tomb, and a heroine who survives her lover only to die in religion. The skeleton of the later tragedy is present in Masuccio, but the names, the city, the families, the feud and the double death in the tomb are not. The tale's grandparent is Sienese; its parent is Veronese, and the parent is Luigi da Porto.

Da Porto, a Vicentine gentleman and soldier crippled by wounds received in the Friuli fighting of 1511, composed his tale in the years after his injury and cast it as a story told by an archer of his company, one Peregrino, on the road. Printed at Venice in 1530, after da Porto's death, under the name *La Giulietta*, the *Istoria novellamente ritrovata di due nobili amanti* performs the act of invention in its full sense. Da Porto took the feuding surnames from Dante and built Verona around them. He named the lovers Romeo Montecchi and Giulietta Cappelletti. He gave them their supporting world entire: Tebaldo, the hot kinsman; Marcuccio, the jesting friend whose unnaturally cold hand becomes a byword at the feast; Fra Lorenzo, the Franciscan who marries the pair and devises the potion; the Count of Lodrone, called Paris, the bridegroom chosen by her father; the old servant who carries messages between the lovers. And he fixed the plot in the shape every later version keeps: the feast where the lovers meet, the window colloquy, the secret marriage, the street fight and the exile, the forced match, the potion and the trance, the letter that never arrives, the poison bought in Mantua, the tomb where she wakes in time for a last exchange, the double death, and the reconciliation of the houses over the bodies. The invention itself, and the Vicentine captain who performed it, are treated in full in a companion study of [Luigi da Porto and the Birth of 'Giulietta'](/2025/10/03/luigi-da-porto-giulietta-origin/); the present study takes up the tale at the moment it passes into other hands.

Masuccio's tale deserves its paragraph of respect, because it proves how much of the tragedy's machinery predates Verona and how little of the tragedy does. Mariotto and Gianozza marry in secret with a friar's help; Mariotto, exiled for a killing, crosses the sea to Alexandria; Gianozza, pressed toward a second marriage, takes from the same friar a potion that counterfeits death for three days, wakes in the family tomb, escapes, and follows her husband across the Mediterranean in disguise, arriving in time for his execution and returning to die in a Sienese convent. Potion, tomb, miscarried message and separated lovers are all present in 1476. What is absent is everything that makes the later story ache: the feud as the lovers' native air, the families named and opposed, the double death in the one tomb, the city reconciled over the bodies. Masuccio wrote an adventure of constancy. The Veronese invention turned adventure into tragedy by giving the lovers enemies at home and no Alexandria to escape to.

Da Porto's own frame advertises his method. He presents the tale as one he heard from Peregrino, an archer of his company, during a march in the Friuli wars, a soldier's story told on the road by a man who claimed the events for his native Verona. The wounded officer who had lost his own active life to the wars thus received the story as oral report and fixed it in writing, an act of preservation that mirrors Bandello's later practice and reminds the source hunter that every link in this chain before print was a voice in a room. Da Porto anchored his report in a Verona of two centuries past, under Bartolomeo della Scala, the lord Dante had praised, and borrowed the feuding surnames from the *Purgatorio*'s catalogue of Italy's disorders, where Montecchi and Cappelletti stand as types of civic madness. The borrowing gave the tale its double citizenship, in literature and in seeming chronicle, and Bandello, inheriting it, never questioned the passport.

### Why is da Porto, not Bandello, called the inventor of Romeo and Juliet?

Da Porto holds the title because priority in story belongs to the fixed design, and the design is his: the names, the houses, the feud, the friar, the sequence of feast, marriage, exile, potion, tomb and double death all stand complete in his tale before Bandello touches the material.

Bandello's greatness lies elsewhere, in amplification rather than invention, and the distinction matters for everything that follows. A design can be summarized in a paragraph; da Porto's tale, for all its completeness, remains close to that scale, a *historia* of modest length that moves by summary as often as by scene. What it does not yet possess is duration: the long colloquies in which motive is argued, the secondary figures promoted to speakers, the ceremonial fullness of the closing movement, the sense of a city watching. Those are the gifts of the retelling. The line of descent from Siena to Verona is a line from pattern to design; the line from Verona to London passes through a third term, in which design becomes world. That third term is Bandello's novella, and measuring it requires first an honest account of what he received, because Bandello follows da Porto so closely that the separation of their shares is the standing difficulty of the scholarship.

## Among the Novellieri: Bandello's Place in the Tradition

Bandello wrote in the shadow of a giant, and the shadow shaped his method. Boccaccio's *Decameron* had fixed the novella's prestige form two centuries earlier: a framed company, tales told across days, an art of compression in which a story's point lands like a blade. The tradition that followed divided between Boccaccio's heirs, who prized the pointed tale, and the chroniclers, who prized the documented one. Bandello's *Novelle* declare for a third allegiance: the tale as an evening's entertainment in a great house, where compression is a vice and amplitude a compliment, since the teller's task is to fill the hours of powerful listeners with a world dense enough to hold them. His stories are correspondingly long, circumstantial and speech-laden, and his contemporaries understood the difference: where Boccaccio's novelle glitter, Bandello's accumulate.

The accumulation was a deliberate poetics, and Bandello states its terms in his own disclaimers. Again and again the dedicatory letters protest that the tales are written as they were told, without the flowers of rhetoric, in the plain manner of speech among soldiers and courtiers. The protest is partly a courtier's modesty and partly a manifesto: truth of circumstance is offered as a substitute for beauty of surface, and the reader is asked to value the tale as a document of its world. Posterity has sometimes taken the disclaimer at face value and ranked Bandello as a careless stylist whose matter outran his art. The Romeo novella suggests a fairer verdict. Its plainness is the plainness of a deposition, and its expansions fall exactly where a court performer would place them, in the speeches a room can savour, the negotiations a room can judge, the ceremonies a room can see. Judged as literature for the eye, the novella is uneven; judged as a score for the voice, it is the work of the most successful professional teller of the Italian century, and its conquest of Europe is the judgment its first audiences, in three languages, already delivered.

The comparison with da Porto clarifies the same point from the other side. Da Porto wrote as an amateur in the honourable sense, a gentleman preserving a story he had heard, once, completely, with the economy of a man writing for the record. Bandello wrote as a professional retelling for the hundredth time, with the instincts of a man whose stories had been tested against yawning captains and distracted duchesses and had learned, in the testing, where to swell. Source study usually rewards the amateur, whose text is the origin. The history of the Romeo story rewards the professional, whose text is the vehicle. Both rewards are just, and the map in this study exists to keep them separate.

## The Feast in Detail: Masks, Hands, and the Cold Hand

Bandello's feast repays slow attention, because it is the novella's method in miniature: every element da Porto sketched is present, and every element is given time to signify. Romeo comes masked with his companions, as carnival custom allowed, a young man performing recovery from the lady who scorns him; the company is received in the Cappelletti palace with the ceremonies of a great house at festival; and the first sight of Giulietta is staged not as a thunderclap but as a process, the eye travelling, the inquiry beginning, the dance arranging what the eye desires. In the closing figures of the dancing the company takes hands in sequence, and Romeo finds himself placed between Giulietta, whose hand answers his with warmth and a tremor he can read, and Marcuccio, whose hand is cold as a stone in winter, a jest the teller pauses to enjoy: the lover's hand burns, the jester's hand freezes, and Giulietta's smile at the contrast is the first communication she sends him. It is a tiny machine for showing love as temperature, and it survives, transformed, in the play's instinct for the same physics, the hands of pilgrims and saints in the shared sonnet, "For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch" (1.5.99).

The inquiries after names close the movement with the inherited irony intact: each lover learns the other's house when learning can no longer protect either. Bandello then does what da Porto's summary could not, he stays. Romeo's nights beneath the window become a siege conducted in whispers, the household's dogs and servants and routines mapped, Giulietta's appearances at the window negotiated between her inclination and her terror of discovery, until the colloquy ripens into the constitutional scene described earlier, her terms, marriage or nothing, and the machinery of the nurse and the friar engaged. The feast in Bandello is thus not an episode but a first act, complete with exposition, inciting incident and decision, and its proportions taught the tradition, Brooke and the play after him, that this story is won or lost in the pacing of its opening: hurry the feast and the lovers are puppets; dwell in it, and they are persons choosing at the speed of thought.

## The Letter That Failed: The Novella as a Machine for Miscarriage

Count the communications in Bandello's novella and a design emerges that no synopsis reveals. Romeo writes to Giulietta; the nurse negotiates orally, carrying terms in her memory where no interception can read them; Lorenzo writes to Mantua; Romeo writes to his father; servants carry sealed orders; the friar's narrative at the end is itself a final message, delivered to the Signore, that at last reaches its addressee complete. The story is a network of messages, almost all of which arrive. Exactly one fails, and the one is the one on which two lives depend. Bandello, following da Porto, makes the failure mechanical rather than malicious: the brother of Lorenzo's order who bears the letter toward Mantua is shut into a house under suspicion of plague, quarantined by officers doing their duty, and the errand dies of public health. No villain intercepts it, no rival suppresses it; the city's immune system, functioning correctly, kills the lovers as surely as the feud does.

The retelling's expansions sharpen the irony by multiplying the successes around the failure. Because Bandello gives the nurse's oral traffic such length and competence, the audience of the novella learns to trust channels; because Romeo's farewell letter to his father is written with such care and preserved so faithfully, the audience learns that paper in this world generally works. The single sealed door in Mantua therefore lands with the force of a verdict on systems: a civilization that can move gold, gossip, sacraments and corpses on schedule cannot move one letter past a plague cordon. Shakespeare needed to change nothing in the mechanism, and changed nothing, keeping the searchers, the sealed house and the returned letter for Friar John's report in Act 5 Scene 2. Among all the fingerprints catalogued in this study, the failed letter is the one that proves descent most coldly, because it is not an image or a line but a piece of engineering, and engineering is inherited, not reinvented. Bandello did not design the machine; da Porto did. But Bandello oiled it, displayed it, and surrounded it with so many working parts that its single stoppage became the loudest sound in the story.

## What Bandello Received: The Skeleton, and the Problem of Shares

No honest study of Bandello can begin by awarding him the plot. Scene by scene, the novella walks in da Porto's footprints. The masked entry to the Cappelletti feast, the dance in which the lovers' hands meet and Marcuccio's icy hand parts them with a jest, the exchange of vows beneath Giulietta's window, the marriage in Fra Lorenzo's cell, the encounter in the street where Tebaldo falls and Romeo is banished, the father's rage and the imposed match with the Count of Lodrone, the potion that simulates death for a span of days, the friar sent with the letter who cannot complete his errand, the poison purchased at Mantua, the opened sepulchre, the waking, the deaths, the inquiry, the reconciliation: every hinge is da Porto's. Bandello even keeps da Porto's strangest and most beautiful inheritance, the waking of Giulietta while Romeo still breathes, so that the lovers speak together in the tomb before the poison finishes its work, and her death, when it comes, is a willed extinction, the breath held until the body follows the will. Shakespeare's Juliet, waking to find Romeo already dead and taking his dagger with the words "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath" (5.3.169-170), is Shakespeare's departure from the whole Italian line, and the departure is visible only when the line is known.

The complication the brief for this study names, distinguishing Bandello's additions from da Porto's, is therefore real, and it dictates method. Because the later teller follows the earlier so faithfully, credit cannot be assigned by plot summary, which will always read da Porto's name. It must be assigned by collation: by setting the two Italian texts side by side and marking where Bandello's page carries a speech, a motive, a stage of business, a named pressure of circumstance that da Porto's page does not. The scholars who have done that work, above all Olin H. Moore in his collation of the Italian versions and Geoffrey Bullough in the parallel summaries printed in the first volume of *Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare*, agree on the shape of the result even where they differ on its transmission. Bandello's contribution is not structural but textural, and it is enormous. He multiplies length severalfold. He converts summary into scene. He gives the figures tongues and lets them argue their choices. He furnishes the city. When Boaistuau translated the story for France, it was Bandello's text he translated; when the story crossed into English, it crossed in Bandello's proportions. The sections that follow take the additions in turn: the method of expansion itself, the nurse, the apothecary, the tomb, and the frame that holds them.

## Expansion as Method: How Bandello Lengthens a Tale

Bandello's first addition is time. Da Porto's *historia* is a brief thing, a tale that can be read at a sitting and that hurries its persons from hinge to hinge. Bandello retards every approach to catastrophe. The feast acquires duration: the entry of the masked Montecchi party, Romeo's languishing for the lady who scorns him, counselled by his companions to look about him at the feast and cure one love with the sight of others, the dance, the joined hands, the jest of Marcuccio's cold hand, the first words, the inquiry after names. The window scene becomes a colloquy in earnest, with Giulietta reasoning her position, weighing the danger, proposing the terms on which a further meeting can be honourable, and sending her messenger by daylight to settle the marriage. The friar's consent is argued, not assumed: Lorenzo hears the confession of the love, reflects that a marriage between the houses might compose the feud that has drenched Verona in blood, and accepts the office for reasons the tale states in his own voice. At every joint where da Porto reports, Bandello deliberates.

The deliberation is the point. A story told in summary belongs to Fortune; a story told in speeches belongs to its speakers, who can be watched choosing. Bandello's lovers deliberate more, and therefore err more visibly, than da Porto's. Romeo's opening melancholy is given a history and a diagnosis. Giulietta, confronted with the potion, rehearses her fears in sequence: that the friar, to hide his part in the first marriage, might give true poison; that she might wake before Romeo comes and suffocate among the bones of her ancestors; that the horrors of the vault might drive her mad. The play's audience hears those same fears in Juliet's great soliloquy, where Weis prints her imagining the vault's terrors and asking, "What if it be a poison which the Friar / Subtly hath ministered to have me dead" (4.3.24-25). The enumeration is not Shakespeare's free invention; it is the Italian deliberative habit, da Porto's seed worked up by Bandello into full rhetorical form and carried north by the translators. Expansion, in Bandello's hands, is a theory of character: persons are what their speeches deliberate.

The same method furnishes the city. Bandello's Verona has institutions with procedures: a Signore who holds inquiry, a watch that can be raised, a church of San Francesco where the Cappelletti keep their sepulchre, friars who can be examined, fathers who act through marriage contracts and household discipline. Da Porto names these things; Bandello staffs them. The result is a story that no longer reads as a wonder tale about two lovers but as a civic event, a catastrophe that happens to a commonwealth and must be processed by its officers. The play inherits exactly that doubleness, a private passion processed in public forms, and it inherits it through the proportions Bandello set.

The same deliberative method governs the novella's ugliest room. When the match with the Count of Lodrone is imposed, Bandello stages the confrontation inside the Cappelletti palace as a contest of wills argued in speeches: the father descending from command through contempt to threats of force and disowning, the mother performing the household's softer mediation, Giulietta answering with the only weapons registered to her, tears, delay, and the appearance of submission, while her true resolution hardens offstage in the friar's cell. Da Porto had the confrontation; Bandello gives it duration enough to become the story's second hinge, the point at which the audience understands that the potion is not an adventure but an escape from a house that has become a prison with better furniture. The play's Act 3 Scene 5, with Capulet's curses filling the stage and Juliet kneeling in the wreckage of the interview, draws its proportions from this room.

## Bandello's Giulietta: The Heroine Rebuilt from Inside

If expansion has a centre in the novella, it is Giulietta. Da Porto's heroine is brave and constant, but she is often the object of the plot's verbs: she is seen, married, threatened, drugged, entombed. Bandello's Giulietta increasingly holds the verbs herself. At the window she does not merely consent to be loved; she sets the constitution under which love may proceed, refusing any commerce that is not marriage and dictating the procedure, the messenger, the hour, by which her honour and her desire can both survive. Her reasoning is social and exact: she knows what a discovered intrigue costs a daughter of the Cappelletti, knows the worth of the sacrament as a shield, and wagers that a wife cannot be given away a second time. The play's Juliet, who in the orchard scene moves from ecstasy to the practical demand for marriage within a single speech, is this woman's direct descendant, and the descent runs through Bandello's lengthening of her deliberations.

The pattern holds through the crisis. Confronted with the imposed match, Bandello's Giulietta argues, temporizes, weeps in the right rooms and resolves in the wronged privacy of her own counsel; carried to Lorenzo, she lays her case with a desperation that has a weapon behind it, the threat that the friar's refusal will leave her no instrument but her own hand. Shakespeare's Juliet enters the cell in Act 4 Scene 1 with the same ultimatum in verse: "Give me some present counsel, or, behold, / 'Twixt my extremes and me this bloody knife / Shall play the umpire" (4.1.61-63). Before the potion, Bandello gives her the enumerated fears that the play's soliloquy inherits, the poison that might be real, the waking that might come too soon, the charnel horrors that might unseat her reason, each fear named and answered before she drinks. And in the tomb he gives her the longest share of the last dialogue, her refusal of survival argued point by point against Romeo's pleading, so that her death by held breath reads not as collapse but as the final exercise of the will that has governed her throughout. A heroine who reasons is a heroine who can be wrong in interesting ways, and Bandello's retelling is where Giulietta becomes one. Later ages that found in Juliet a miracle of Shakespearean girlhood were looking at the end of a process whose decisive stage was Lucca, 1554.

One measure of the distance she travels downstream can be taken in years. The Italian tellings make Giulietta eighteen, a marriageable daughter at the full age her society expected for a bride, and Bandello keeps her there, a young woman whose reasoning the tale treats as adult. Brooke's poem lowers the age to sixteen, and the play lowers it again to not quite fourteen, a change that belongs to the northern tradition's heightening of pathos and that no reader of the sources can mistake for an Italian inheritance. The Giulietta of Lucca is not a child destroyed by adults' machinery; she is a contemporary of that machinery, fluent in its rules, beaten by its operators. Much of the novella's distinctive chill, its refusal of the merely pathetic, flows from that single datum, and the play's very different temperature on the same events is a northern invention laid over Bandello's adult tragedy.

## The Nurse Promoted: From Errand to Engine

The clearest single promotion in the novella is the nurse. In da Porto, Giulietta's old servant is a convenience of the plot, a trusted channel through whom messages pass. Bandello takes the channel and makes it a person: the balia who has nursed Giulietta from childhood, who knows her charge's body and humours, who receives the confession of the love, who carries the letter and the answer, who negotiates with Romeo in the street with a servant's freedom of speech, who is present at the marriage, who keeps the secret through the crisis of the forced match, and who stands inside the household as the one figure whose loyalty belongs to the girl rather than to the house. Bandello gives her errands the length of scenes. Her interviews are dialogues, with terms proposed, risks weighed, instructions repeated. The go-between stops being a wire and becomes a counsellor.

The promotion propagates. Through Boaistuau the enlarged nurse passes into Brooke's poem and Painter's prose, and from them into the play, where the Nurse commands the middle acts: the embassy of 2.4, the breathless report of 2.5, the wedding-night preparations, and the catastrophic counsel of 3.5, when the same voice that engineered the marriage advises its abandonment. None of that centrality is da Porto's. It is the unfolding of Bandello's decision that the old woman should speak at length and be believed. Critics of the play sometimes treat the Nurse's bulk as Shakespearean comic inflation, a garrulous part written up for a clown. The line of descent gives a colder explanation: the part was already large in the story as England received it, because Bandello had discovered that a go-between with a voice turns a plot of messages into a drama of confidences, betrayals and divided loyalties.

### Which of Bandello's additions does the play depend on most?

The nurse's promotion carries the greatest weight, because a speaking go-between converts the plot's traffic in messages into drama: confidences, negotiations and betrayals that the middle acts of the play are built from, all descending from Bandello's decision to give the balia scenes rather than errands.

Consider what the promotion makes possible downstream. A silent channel can only deliver or fail; a speaking confidante can advise, can hesitate, can be bribed by circumstance, can desert. Bandello's nurse, enlarged, contains in embryo both the loyal engineer of the marriage and the worldly counsellor who will later urge the second match as the sensible course, since the first husband is as good as dead in exile. The play's Nurse speaks the betrayal in the words Weis prints, advising Juliet that Romeo is banished and Paris is the better bargain. The moral shock of that counsel depends on the audience having trusted the voice, and the trust was built by Bandello's method: give the servant length, familiarity, a history with the heroine's childhood, and the desertion, when it comes, wounds like a family's. Da Porto's servant cannot betray anyone; she has not been given enough self to sell. Bandello's can, and the tragedy is larger for it.

## Bandello's Romeo, and the Friend Who Does Not Die

Romeo receives the same treatment at lower intensity. Bandello opens him as a case: a young gentleman of the Montecchi consumed by an unreturned passion, his melancholy diagnosed by his companions and prescribed the standard cure of the feast, new faces against an old fixation. The prescription scene matters beyond its pathos, because it establishes the male world of counsel and company from which the play's opening acts draw their texture, the friends who tease, advise and accompany, among them Marcuccio, the jester of the company, whose cold hand at the feast supplies the tale's one immortal joke. Bandello keeps Marcuccio at da Porto's scale, a flavour of the feast rather than a pillar of the action, and the source hunter should mark the proportion, since the play's Mercutio, built from this pebble, is the largest single act of magnification in the whole transmission.

A sterner fact belongs here, because synopses persistently blur it. In the Italian tellings, no friend dies. Marcuccio jests at the feast and vanishes from the story's casualties; the street catastrophe that ruins Romeo is the killing of Tebaldo in open fighting between the young men of the houses, after which the Signore's banishment falls on Romeo alone. The dying friend whose curse could darken the play's centre enters the story only in England, in Brooke's poem, where Mercutio is slain under Romeo's arm and the tragedy's emotional geometry changes forever. Bandello's structure is therefore colder than the play's: his Romeo is ruined not by the loss of the beloved friend but by the feud operating directly, house against house, with no buffer of wit to mourn. Restoring the middle link restores that coldness to view, and with it the measure of what the English tradition, first in the poem and then overwhelmingly in the play, added to the Italian design: a third death, placed at the hinge, to make the feud's cost personal before it becomes total.

## The Apothecary's Poverty: The Poison Purchase at Mantua

Da Porto despatched the poison purchase in a breath: Romeo, in Mantua, obtained poison from a poor man and returned to Verona. Bandello slows the errand into a scene, and the scene's hinge is poverty. His Mantuan speziale is a man of small trade and smaller means; the sale of poison is forbidden and dangerous; the apothecary hesitates, and what overcomes his hesitation is not argument but gold, offered by a gentleman whose desperation is itself a kind of pressure. Bandello dwells on the transaction because it concentrates the novella's moral physics: every catastrophe in the tale is a door opened by the wrong key, by a bribe, a secret, a dispensation, a drug. The apothecary's poverty is the lock; Romeo's gold is the key; the law against the sale is the door that should have stayed shut.

The scene's descent into the play is the most quoted of all Bandello's fingerprints. Shakespeare's Romeo finds the Mantuan shop by its look of destitution and reads the man's body as a text of want: "Meagre were his looks, / Sharp misery had worn him to the bones" (5.1.40-41). The negotiation that follows is Bandello's, transposed into verse and sharpened: the apothecary names the penalty, Romeo names the counter-argument that want itself absolves, and the sale closes on the line Weis prints as "My poverty, but not my will, consents" (5.1.75), answered by Romeo's terrible absolution, "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1.80-82). Scholars who set the versions in parallel, Bullough first among them, note that the English tellings carry the scene's shape from Boaistuau, and Boaistuau carries it from Bandello. The poverty, the reluctance, the gold, the moralizing exchange about which of the two poisons is worse: the architecture is the novella's. Shakespeare's contribution is compression and verse, the conversion of a narrated temptation into forty lines of stichomythic pressure. The matter tempted is Bandello's.

The episode also displays the reteller's civic imagination at its sharpest. Bandello's Verona and Mantua are places where prohibitions exist and are priced. The edict against duelling hangs over the street fight; the law against poison hangs over the shop; the discipline of the household hangs over Giulietta. Catastrophe, in his handling, is never the failure of rules to exist. It is the success of money, love and secrecy in routing around rules that everyone can recite. That is a darker and more modern moral than the exemplum his frame advertises, and the play inherits the darkness with the detail.

## The Moral Economy: A Novella That Runs on Prices

Running beneath Bandello's deliberations is a colder system, and the retelling strengthens it at every joint: the story's world is priced. Marriage, in the Verona of the novella, is a transaction between houses, negotiated in portions and prospects, and the Count of Lodrone is not a villain but a bid, the best settlement the Cappelletti can obtain for a daughter whose value the market measures in alliance. The friar's secret marriage is, among other things, an attempt to settle the feud's account by merging the debtors. The nurse's errands travel on the small change of service, rewarded, tipped and remembered. The apothecary's vial has its price in gold, named against the price of his life if the law discovers the sale. Even the ending settles accounts in kind: a monument, jointly raised, is the feud's final payment, the houses discharging in marble what they would not discharge in mercy.

Bandello did not invent this economy; da Porto's Verona already ran on it. What the retelling does is audit it, scene by scene, until the reader can watch every catastrophe arrive as a transaction completed. The father's rage at the refused match is a creditor's rage, an asset misbehaving. Giulietta's insistence on the sacrament is a demand that her transfer be registered in the one ledger that cannot be rewritten. Romeo's gold at Mantua is the economy's reductio: the richest instrument in the tale buys the means of the poorest act, and the apothecary's line about poverty and will, which the play inherits, is the economy confessing itself. Critics of the play have long noted its saturation in the language of price, debt, purchase and wealth; the saturation begins in the Italian prose, and Bandello's expansions, by slowing the transactions until their terms can be heard, are where the play's Verona learned to keep its books aloud.

## The Potion and the Three Days: Bandello's Management of the Trance

Between the apothecary's poison and the tomb's, the novella keeps a third pharmacology, and Bandello's handling of it shows the reteller thinking like an engineer of suspense. Lorenzo's preparation, in the Italian tellings, casts Giulietta into a sleep indistinguishable from death for the span of three days, time enough for the funeral to be performed, the body to be laid in the Cappelletti sepulchre at San Francesco, and word to be sent to Mantua for Romeo to attend the waking. Bandello enlarges every stage of the mechanism's operation. The interview in the cell becomes a negotiation in which the terms are stated like a contract: the draught, the duration, the burial, the letter, the rescue. The household's discovery of the seeming corpse becomes a scene of lamentation at full length, parents and nurse and the cheated bridegroom's party grieving in chorus over the bier, the direct ancestor of the play's Act 4 Scene 5, where mourning is given a whole scene's ceremony before the musicians deflate it. And the interval itself, the three days in which Verona believes its daughter dead, is allowed its weight: a city performs a funeral for a living girl while the one man who must know the truth rides toward a false report.

The play compresses the interval to the two-and-forty hours its Friar names, tightening Bandello's three days into a span that makes the letter's failure a matter of hours rather than of journeys, and the change is characteristic of the last teller's hand, acceleration everywhere. But the engineering tolerances are the novella's: the trance must outlast the funeral and not outlast the rescue, the letter must be timed against the waking, and the whole apparatus depends on a friar's pharmacology being exact and a city's messengers being swift. Bandello, by expanding the funeral and the grief, also raises the cost of the machine's failure: the more completely Verona mourns, the more unforgivable the waking's miscarriage becomes, since a second, real funeral will have to repeat the first one's ceremonies over a body that this time will not rise. Suspense of that quality is not decoration on the plot. It is the plot, understood as a timed mechanism, and the retelling that teaches the tradition to feel the timing is Bandello's.

## The Tomb at Full Length: Bandello's Closing Movement

The measure of Bandello's ambition is the closing movement, which occupies a share of his novella out of all proportion to da Porto's ending and establishes the pattern of witnessed, processed catastrophe that the play's final scene reproduces. Romeo rides from Mantua with his servant, having written to his father a letter that confesses the marriage and begs pardon, a document da Porto had supplied and Bandello retains with emphasis: the hero enters the tomb already explained, his papers in order, so that the city's inquiry after his death will have his own voice to work from. He comes to the church of San Francesco with tools, dismisses the servant under a sealed instruction, opens the Cappelletti sepulchre, descends, speaks to the body at length, drinks, and falls. Then the novella performs its most famous retardation: Giulietta wakes while the poison's work is unfinished, and the two hold in the tomb the conversation that the whole machinery of the plot has been conspiring to prevent, a marriage's worth of speech compressed into the minutes of a death. She refuses rescue, refuses the life he urges on her with his last strength, and when he is dead she performs the Italian ending: the breath retained, the body stilled by an act of will beside his.

What follows is as distinctive as the deaths. Noise brings the watch; the servant is taken; the friar is taken; the Signore holds his inquiry; Lorenzo, examined, tells the whole story from the marriage forward; the fathers arrive at knowledge over the bodies of their children; enmity is formally renounced; and the city buries the lovers together in one monument, converting a scandal into a civic possession. Bandello lingers over each procedural stage because his subject, by the end, is no longer only the lovers but the commonwealth digesting them. The play's Act 5 Scene 3 is unthinkable without that template: the tomb crowded with principals arriving in sequence, the watch, the Chief Watchman's orders, the Prince presiding over an inquiry conducted in speeches, Friar Laurence delivering the long narrative that in Bandello belongs to the examination, and the settlement pronounced over the bodies, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate" (5.3.293). Even the play's machinery of failure carries the Italian watermark. Friar John's explanation of the undelivered letter, that the searchers, "Suspecting that we both were in a house / Where the infectious pestilence did reign, / Sealed up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2.9-11), descends from the Italian tellings, in which the friar entrusted with the letter is shut into a house under plague suspicion and the errand dies of quarantine. The detail is too particular to be coincidence and too functional to be ornament: it is a fingerprint, and it is on Bandello's page before it is on Shakespeare's.

Romeo's own valediction marks the distance the play travelled from its middle link, and the distance is instructive. Bandello's Romeo dies talking with his wife, in dialogue, as the novella's deliberative method requires. Shakespeare, having killed Giulietta's waking minutes by keeping her asleep until Romeo is gone, gives the dialogue to a man alone with a body he believes dead: "Eyes, look your last! / Arms, take your last embrace!" (5.3.112-113). The solitude is Shakespeare's; the ceremonial frame that makes the solitude legible, the opened tomb in a watched city, the approaching officers, the inquiry already forming outside the door, is the inheritance, and the inheritance runs through Bandello.

The tomb's afterlife in Verona itself testifies to the novella's cultural work. An empty sarcophagus preserved at the former Franciscan convent of San Francesco al Corso, the very church the Italian tellings name for the Cappelletti sepulchre, was shown for centuries as Giulietta's tomb, and travellers of the Romantic age, Byron and Dickens among them, made the pilgrimage and recorded their mingled scepticism and surrender. A fiction's furniture had become a city's relic. That transmutation, legend hardened into monument, is the civic ending of Bandello's novella performed by history itself: his Verona processed two invented corpses into a public possession, and the real Verona, in time, did the same.

## The Held Breath: What the Italian Ending Means

The detail on which the Italian tradition and the play most visibly divide deserves a section of its own, because Bandello's handling of it concentrates his whole art. Giulietta's death, in da Porto and in Bandello after him, is accomplished by an act of pure will: having spoken her last words beside Romeo's body, she retains her breath until the body obeys the refusal, and dies without wound, weapon or poison, by the same instrument that had carried her speeches. The English versions keep the held breath, as Bullough's parallel summaries show in sequence; the dagger with which Shakespeare's Juliet dies is the play's own substitution. Critics sometimes treat the Italian ending as a quaint physiology, a Renaissance fancy about the breath as the soul's vehicle. Within the novella's economy it is something stricter: the final proof of the deliberative heroine Bandello built. Every earlier crisis found Giulietta reasoning her way to a decision and executing it against the house's opposition; the tomb asks for one decision more, and she executes it with the only property left entirely in her ownership, her own breathing. Death by held breath is suicide as self-possession, the will closing its own account.

Bandello's expansions prepare the gesture so carefully that its meaning cannot be missed. His Giulietta has been defined throughout by the government of her own voice: setting terms at the window, arguing in the cell, enumerating fears before the draught, pleading in the tomb for the terms on which she will not survive. A death by breath is a death in her medium, speech's element withdrawn by the speaker. The play's dagger makes a different woman at the last: quicker, bloodier, borrowing the man's weapon and the man's manner, and the difference measures the distance between the novella's Rome and the stage's. Neither ending is a mistake. But the source hunter should register that the tradition's most famous stage property, the happy dagger, enters the story at its final telling, while the ending it replaced, quieter and in its way more terrible, is the one Bandello's Europe read, wept over, translated and carried across three languages for forty years. Recovering the held breath is recovering the emotional signature of the story as the sixteenth century knew it, and that signature is Bandello's bequest as surely as any speech or scene.

## The Courtly Frame: Dedication, Exemplum, and the Sympathy That Escapes It

Every Bandello novella arrives wearing its frame: a letter to a patron, a company described, a teller named, a moral offered. The Romeo tale is no exception, and the frame matters because it is the one part of the novella that the translators handled roughly. Boaistuau rewrote the framing for French readers, Brooke replaced it with his own moralizing address to the reader, and Painter trimmed toward the story. What the frame declares, in Bandello's handling, is an exemplum: the tale is offered as a warning, a mirror of what befalls young people who marry in secret, disobey parents, and trust friars and potions, and the narrator's voice returns at intervals to judge, to pity, and to generalize. Bandello the courtier was selling moral utility; the dedicatory machinery of the *Novelle* required each tale to present its credentials as instruction for the great household it honoured.

Yet the telling escapes the frame, and the escape is the novella's signature achievement. Inside the tale, sympathy distributes itself by scene rather than by doctrine. Giulietta's deliberations are rendered from within, at a length that makes her the story's reasoning centre; Romeo's despair is argued, not merely deplored; the father's tyranny in the matter of the forced match is shown in its domestic brutality; the friar's good intention, to reconcile two houses by a marriage, is stated fairly and given its dignity before it fails. A reader who took only the frame would expect a sermon against disobedience. A reader who takes the tale receives a distribution of blame so even that no sermon can collect it: the parents' hardness, the feud's idiocy, the friar's overreach, the lovers' haste, the apothecary's poverty, the letter's quarantine, all are load-bearing. The play's famous refusal to settle its own moral account, its closing distribution of punishment and pardon in which some shall be pardoned and some punished, is the final form of a tension Bandello built into his novella and the translators, to their credit, failed to iron out.

The frame's doubleness can be stated as a division of labour between the two books Bandello was writing at once. As a courtier's gift, the *Novelle* had to be safe: dedicated upward, moralized at the joints, its pleasures taxed by warnings. As a teller's performance, the same book had to be true to the rooms it came from, where everyone knew that marriages were policy, that fathers could be tyrants in broad daylight, that friars schemed like other men, and that the young, cornered, would break the rules and be broken by them. Bandello served both masters without reconciling them, and the refusal to reconcile is precisely the quality that survived translation. Boaistuau taxed the tale toward doctrine; Brooke taxed it further; the play, standing at the end of the line with Bandello's matter in its hands, quietly refunded the tax.

### Does Bandello blame the lovers, their parents, or Fortune?

The frame blames the lovers: the declared moral warns against secret marriage and disobedient haste. The tale refuses to obey its frame, spreading responsibility across the father's hardness, the feud, the friar's scheme and the letter's miscarriage until no single verdict can stand.

That doubleness, doctrine at the door and sympathy in the room, is also historically legible. Bandello had lived his life in courts where marriages were instruments of policy and where the young were counters in games their elders played. His novella knows both truths at once: that the children broke the rules, and that the rules were a machine for breaking children. When Brooke versified the story for English readers, his address to the reader famously hardened the frame into condemnation of the lovers' "unhonest desire" and disobedient lust, a sterner moral than Bandello's telling supports. The play, read against the Italian middle, can be seen declining Brooke's hardening and returning to Bandello's distribution, which is one more reason the middle link matters: skipping it makes the play's sympathy look like a solitary Shakespearean miracle, where the chain shows it to be an inheritance recovered.

## Reading Bandello: Style, Speech, and the Losses of Translation

A candid difficulty attends any English account of Bandello, and this study names it rather than concealing it: the novella is almost never read in its own language by the audiences of the tradition it founded. English readers meet the story in Brooke or in the play; French readers met it in Boaistuau; even most Italian readers of later centuries met Bandello in selections. Judgments of his art are therefore judgments of a man heard through interpreters, and the interpreters were not neutral. Boaistuau smoothed the Lombard plainness of the original toward French periodic elegance, regularizing the syntax, elevating the diction, inserting the connective moral tissue his genre required. Brooke then converted the smoothed prose into jogging verse, trading what remained of the voice's grain for narrative momentum. By the time the matter reached the play, Bandello's sentences had been twice remade, and what survived, structure, incident, distribution of sympathy, is precisely what translation cannot kill. The experiment is nearly controlled: whatever in the story still moves a reader of the play is likely to be architecture, and the architecture is Bandello's.

What the Italian page itself offers, to those who return to it, is a distinctive music that the translations spend. Bandello's prose runs in the cadence of the spoken court narrative, paratactic for pages as errands and incidents accumulate, then swelling into set rhetoric at the great deliberations, where Giulietta argues with the friar or Romeo argues with the corpse. The set speeches belong to the Italian rhetorical inheritance, the lament and the suasoria, formal occasions on which a speaker marshals reasons in ranked order, and Bandello's expansions give that inheritance room to work: his heroine does not merely feel her fears, she itemizes them like an advocate, and his hero does not merely grieve, he pleads. The play's own rhetoric, the ranked fears of the potion soliloquy, the forensic narrative of the Friar's final speech, is the same inheritance arriving by the long road. To call Bandello a mere retailer of da Porto is therefore to miss the specific gravity of the prose: the tale's events are inherited, but its verbal ceremonies, the occasions on which persons justify themselves at length before witnesses, are the professional teller's signature, and the play is inconceivable without the habit of justification those ceremonies taught it.

## The Ferryman: Boaistuau and the French Crossing

In 1559, five years after the Lucca *Novelle*, Pierre Boaistuau published at Paris his *Histoires tragiques*, a small volume of tales rendered from Bandello and advertised on the strength of their Italian original. The Romeo story stands among them, Englished names still in French dress, Rhomeo and Juliette, and the fact that governs everything downstream is the fidelity of the crossing. Boaistuau was not a literal translator; he abridged, heightened and interjected freely elsewhere in his work. With this tale he kept Bandello's sequence of incident almost entire: the feast, the window, the marriage, the nurse's enlarged embassy, the Mantuan poison purchase with its poor speziale and its gold, the tomb with its waking and its speeches, the inquiry and the monument. Whatever else he altered, the architecture that crossed the Alps was Bandello's architecture, and that is why the novella, rather than da Porto's briefer *historia*, stands behind everything England made of the story. The crossing itself, and the French tragic tale it founded, receive separate treatment in [Pierre Boaistuau and the French Tradition](/2025/10/24/pierre-boaistuau-french-tradition/).

Boaistuau's own hand shows in the frame and the temperature. He supplied a moralizing prologue of his own, addressed the reader with sententious commentary at the joints of the action, darkened the friar toward self-interest, and tuned the tale to the French taste for tragic histories whose pleasures are licensed by their warnings. Some of the sternness that Brooke's English preface later displays is therefore French before it is English: the condemnation that frames the story in its northern versions is a Boaistuau varnish over Bandello's ambivalence, not the Italian's own final word. The distinction is a standing lesson in source study. Each crossing taxes the story in the currency of the receiver: France took its tax in moral commentary, England in verse and further moralizing, and the play, last in the line, declined to pay, which is why the play feels morally freer than any of its ancestors while standing on their shoulders.

The timing of the crossing was not accidental either. The France of 1559 was a court saturated in Italian culture under a Medici queen, Catherine, whose household had made Italian letters, Italian music and Italian manners the currency of refinement, and a Parisian publisher offering tragic tales from the Italian was pushing at an opened door. Boaistuau, a Breton man of letters who had travelled in the Levant and attached himself to great patrons, understood his market exactly: the *Histoires tragiques* sold Bandello to France as both fashion and warning, Italian passion exhibited for French moral profit. The Romeo tale, with its Italian city, its friar, its potions and its tomb, was the collection's perfect exhibit, foreign enough to be thrilling, structured enough to be exemplary. England, a decade behind in the same appetite, took the tale from the French shelf rather than the Italian one for the simplest of reasons: French was the language its translators read.

The French life of the tale then outgrew its first ferryman. Boaistuau's little volume founded a genre and a publishing enterprise: François de Belleforest took up the *Histoires tragiques* and continued them through volume after volume in the decades after 1566, mining Bandello above all other Italians, until the French Bandello, in aggregate, dwarfed the Italian original and carried the Mantuan's stories into every library in northern Europe. The Romeo tale rode that flood. When English translators of the next generation went shopping for Italian narrative, they shopped in French, in the great tragic warehouse Boaistuau had opened and Belleforest had stocked, and the tale they found there, dressed for France but built at Lucca, was Bandello's novella still, its proportions intact under two changes of language.

## Two English Spans: Brooke's Poem and Painter's Prose

England received the story twice in six years, both times from Boaistuau's French, both times therefore from Bandello at one remove. Arthur Brooke's *The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet*, printed in 1562, versified the tale at length in poulter's measure, the alternating twelve and fourteen syllable lines whose jog-trot later ages mocked and whose narrative stamina carried the story into the memory of two generations of readers, players among them. Brooke prefaced the poem with an address to the reader that performs the northern frame at its sternest, presenting the lovers as a cautionary pair who pursued dishonest desire, disobeyed their parents, and trusted friars and potions, and the poem interrupts itself with moral asides in the same key. Yet Brooke the versifier was more faithful than Brooke the moralist: the poem keeps the French tale's incident, its enlarged nurse, its Mantuan scene, its tomb, at the length the story required, and it adds English weather of its own, Fortune's wheel turning in its choruses between the acts of the narrative. William Painter's prose version, printed in the second volume of *The Palace of Pleasure* in 1566, follows Boaistuau more closely in wording and gives the story the alternative English dress of the novella, the form Bandello himself had used.

Two biographical facts complete the English picture. Brooke's address to the reader contains, almost as an aside, one of the most tantalizing remarks in the whole transmission: the poet notes that the same argument had lately been set forth on stage, with more commendation than a poem could look for. A stage version of the Verona story therefore existed in England before 1562 and is lost, which means Bandello's matter reached English boards a generation before Shakespeare, through a play no scholar can now read. Brooke himself was dead within a year of his poem's printing, drowned in 1563 on a crossing to France with the expedition sent to hold Le Havre, a translator killed by the same wars of religion whose Italian original had been a by-product of the earlier wars for Italy. Painter, by contrast, lived at the centre of the Elizabethan state's paperwork, a clerk of the ordnance whose *Palace of Pleasure* gathered the novella tradition, Boccaccio and Bandello chief among its quarries, into a storehouse English dramatists would raid for decades. The tale's two English carriers thus embody its two natures: the poem that actors remembered, and the prose treasury that playwrights mined.

### How much of Bandello survives inside Brooke's poem?

The incident survives nearly whole and the words survive not at all: Brooke versified Boaistuau's French, so Bandello's sequence, his enlarged nurse, his apothecary and his tomb crossed intact in structure while every sentence was remade, first into French prose and then into English verse.

For the source hunter, the pair of English versions constitutes a controlled experiment. Brooke's poem, the version that stood closest to the playwright's desk, receives its own full study as [Arthur Brooke's 'Romeus and Juliet' (1562)](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/), the long English poem that carried the French tale into the memory of the playgoing generation. Where Brooke and Painter agree against Boaistuau, the agreement is the French text; where they agree with Boaistuau against any Italian reading, the chain is confirmed; and where the play agrees with Brooke against Painter, Shakespeare's desk is identified. The experiment's result, established by the collators, is that Shakespeare worked chiefly from Brooke's poem, the version in verse, mining its narrative while discarding its moralizing and compressing its months into days. Nothing in that result reaches behind Brooke to an English Bandello, for there was none: no English translation of the *Novelle*'s second part existed for him to use. Bandello reached the play the way a river reaches a mill, through channels dug by other hands, but the water is his.

## What the Play Owes the Middle Link

The debts can be itemized, and itemizing them is the practical use of the chain. First, the nurse: her scale, her scenes, her function as the marriage's engineer and its eventual betrayer descend from Bandello's promotion of the balia, as argued in the section devoted to her. Second, the apothecary: the poverty, the reluctance, the gold, the debate over comparative poisons, Bandello's Mantuan scene versified. Third, the tomb's ceremonial: the opened sepulchre, the valedictory speeches, the waking that Shakespeare redistributed, the watch, the inquiry, the friar's narrative, the settlement, Bandello's closing movement supplying the template of Act 5 Scene 3. Fourth, the deliberative heroine: Giulietta's rehearsed fears before the potion and argued resistance to the forced match stand behind Juliet's soliloquies, behind "I will do so," the play's heroine thinking in sequences the Italian tale first gave her. Fifth, the plague-stopped letter, the small mechanical fingerprint that certifies the line. Sixth, the cast in its proportions: Mercutio, magnified by Shakespeare beyond recognition, enters the line as da Porto's Marcuccio and passes through Bandello's feast with his cold-hand jest intact, the pebble from which the avalanche grew.

Against these debts stand the play's own sovereign acts, which the chain also clarifies by contrast. Shakespeare compressed the tale's months into a handful of days, where the Italian tellings and Brooke allow the marriage a longer life and the exile a slower ripening; the compression belongs to the play, not the sources, and the siblings in this series devoted to Brooke treat it in its place. Shakespeare kept Juliet asleep through Romeo's death and gave her the dagger, cancelling the Italian waking exchange for a crueller arithmetic. Shakespeare invented Mercutio's stature, the Queen Mab speech, the sonnet the lovers share at their meeting, the whole verbal surface. A source study that credits Bandello with the architecture does not diminish those acts; it locates them. Genius, in a chain of transmission, is what the last teller does to a story whose possibilities the middle tellers had already enlarged.

The civic frame deserves its own line in the ledger of debts. The Italian tellings set the street killing inside a city already legislated against brawling, where the Signore's authority stands behind proclaimed penalties and banishment is a sentence argued within a system, merciful against the letter of the edicts and ruinous in its effect. Bandello, whose Verona is staffed with officers, keeps that frame taut: Romeo is not merely exiled by a plot requirement but processed by a state, and the processing is what makes the Mantuan scenes, the purchase, the ride, the tomb in a watched churchyard, cohere. The play reproduces the frame exactly, opening with a Prince who prices street brawls at the brawlers' lives and closing with the same Prince presiding over the inquiry his edict failed to prevent. That architecture of proclamation and failure, law announced in the first scene and law confessing its limits in the last, is the Italian novella's civic imagination at work, and Bandello's expansions of inquiry, watch and judgment are where the play learned to build it.

A smaller inheritance makes the same point in miniature. The Italian lovers meet and speak at a window, the *finestra* of a palace overlooking a garden or a street, and the window colloquy in Bandello is staged with architectural precision: she above, within, guarded by the house; he below, without, exposed to the street and the watch. The play's orchard scene keeps the geometry entire while later stage tradition roofed the window into the balcony of popular imagination, a piece of scenery no early text requires. The geometry is the meaning: a threshold neither inside nor outside, where a daughter can be at once obedient and rebellious, audible and unseen. Bandello did not invent the window, which stands in da Porto, but his long colloquy is where the threshold learned to sustain a scene's full weight, and every balcony since has been built on his sill.

### Did Shakespeare ever read Bandello in Italian?

No evidence places Bandello's Italian on Shakespeare's desk, and none is needed: every Bandello-derived element in the play stands already in Brooke's poem, with Painter's prose as a parallel channel, so the descent is fully explained by the English versions Shakespeare is known to have used.

The contrary suggestion, that the playwright glanced at Boaistuau's French or even at the Italian, has been made by serious scholars and is treated in the next section. Its persistence testifies to something real: the play sometimes feels closer to the novella's spirit, its distributed sympathy, its deliberative heroine, than to Brooke's moralizing poem, and critics have reached for a direct channel to explain the affinity. The economical explanation is colder and better. Brooke's poem, beneath its preface and asides, transmits Bandello's matter with a versifier's completeness, and a dramatist reading for matter rather than morals would receive the whole inheritance through the one book. The affinity is real; the extra channel is an unnecessary hypothesis.

## The Scholars Argue: Moore, Bullough, and the Editors

The modern study of the chain begins with collation, and its two monuments disagree in a way this study must adjudicate. Olin H. Moore, in *The Legend of Romeo and Juliet* (1950) and in the articles that preceded it, set the Italian versions side by side with a patience no predecessor matched, established da Porto's priority and Bandello's dependence, and then pressed a further claim: that the play contains touches closer to Boaistuau's French than to anything in Brooke, suggesting that Shakespeare supplemented the poem with the French prose, and at moments seems to breathe the Italian air itself. Geoffrey Bullough, printing the relevant texts and summaries in parallel in *Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare* (1957), judged the claim unproven: Brooke, in his assessment, is the working source, Painter a possible companion, and every alleged French or Italian touch in the play can be matched within the English versions or explained as a dramatist's natural heightening. Kenneth Muir's *Shakespeare's Sources* concurred in substance. The editors have inherited Bullough's settlement: René Weis, in the introduction to the Arden Third Series edition from which this study quotes, treats Brooke's poem as the principal source and the Italian tellings as the poem's ancestry, the deep background against which the play's compressions and inventions become measurable.

The adjudication offered here divides the honours by question. On the narrow question of the playwright's desk, Bullough wins: collation is a discipline of necessities, and no detail yet produced compels a second book beside Brooke. On the larger question of where the play's substance originates, Moore's instinct was the sounder one, and his collation of the Italian texts remains the indispensable map. The story Shakespeare received was not Brooke's invention with Italian roots; it was Bandello's novella in English dress, its proportions, personnel and pressure points set in Lucca in 1554 and taxed, but never redesigned, by every crossing after. To name Brooke the source is correct bibliography and misleading history. The middle link is where the story became the thing the play could be made from, and the credit for that becoming belongs to Bandello.

The dispute is worth one refinement of vocabulary, since the vocabulary is where the public misunderstanding lives. Source study distinguishes the proximate source, the text a writer demonstrably worked from, from the ultimate sources behind it. Brooke is the play's proximate source, and no argument in this study disturbs that settlement. But proximate is a bibliographer's term, not a measure of substance: a proximate source can be a transparent conduit, as Brooke largely is, and an ultimate source can be the true origin of everything that matters in the matter transmitted, as Bandello is. Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition, the other standard modern text, presents the same double aspect, Brooke at the desk and the Italian line behind the poem, and the convergence of the editors on that doubleness is the closest thing the field has to a consensus. What Moore saw, and what the consensus now quietly incorporates, is that naming the desk is the beginning of a source study and not its end. The end is the map: the whole road, with the tolls and the cargoes itemized, and on that map the largest single estate between invention and play belongs to the bishop of Agen who wrote tales for courts.

Bullough's method deserves its sentence in any adjudication, because the settlement this study follows is a product of it. By printing the candidate sources and their summaries in parallel, Bullough converted an argument about influence into an inspection of documents: the reader can watch the nurse grow from da Porto's servant to Bandello's confidante, watch the Mantuan purchase acquire its poverty, watch the tomb acquire its inquiry, and then watch Brooke transmit the grown versions entire. Arguments from parallel text are the nearest thing source study has to proof, and their cumulative effect is to relocate the creative centre of the tradition. Shakespeare's originality, inspected in Bullough's columns, does not shrink; it changes address, residing less in the story's furniture, which the columns show arriving prefabricated, and more in the language, the compression and the redistribution of sympathy among persons the furniture surrounds. That relocation is the intellectual foundation on which the present study stands, and it is why the adjudication can be confident: the columns, not the rhetoric, award Bandello his estate.

## The Verdict on Bandello

The verdict, then, is threefold and can be carried away in one breath. Da Porto invented the Verona lovers and deserves the inventor's fame. Bandello, following him scene by scene and adding no hinge of plot, nonetheless transformed the tale in scale, speech, sympathy and civic substance, and it is Bandello's transformed version, through Boaistuau's faithful French and the twin English versions of Brooke and Painter, that constitutes the story of Romeo and Juliet as Europe and Shakespeare received it. The cliché that leaps from Italy to Brooke skips the bridge; restored, the bridge turns out to be where most of the travelling was done. A retelling, the case proves, can matter as much as an invention when the retelling decides what the invention contains.

There is a methodological moral for readers of the play, and it is the note on which this study closes its argument. Every act of reading Romeo and Juliet is an act of reading a chain: the words are Shakespeare's, the verse is Shakespeare's, the compression and the daring are Shakespeare's, and beneath them runs a story that had already been told for seventy years, by a Sienese pattern-maker, a wounded Vicentine captain, a Mantuan courtier who filled evenings with it, a French moralist, and two Englishmen, one in verse and one in prose. To restore the middle link is not to diminish the last one. It is to see the last one's labour accurately, as the transformation of a great inherited machine rather than a creation from nothing, and to give the machine's builder his name at last: Matteo Bandello, whose novella of 1554 is the bridge the story crossed into immortality. The sections that follow the verdict trace the consequences: how the middle link came to be forgotten, what the map of his additions looks like when drawn whole, and the questions readers most often ask about the man, his book and the road between Lucca and London.

For readers returning to the play with the map in hand, the practical difference is a change of attention. Scenes that a source-blind reading credits to sudden invention, the Nurse's long embassy and longer report, the apothecary's haggling over his soul's price, the tomb's crowded procedural close, resolve into inheritances executed at genius level, and admiration moves, correctly, from the matter to the handling: the verse that compresses a novella's deliberation into forty lines, the soliloquy that concentrates a heroine's itemized fears into a single speech's crescendo, the dagger that replaces a held breath with a stroke. Other scenes gain the opposite lustre by standing clear of the map entirely, Mercutio's wit, the shared sonnet, the dawn parting's lyric duel, Shakespeare's own estate, fenced and gleaming. A chain honestly surveyed thus flatters nobody and instructs everybody: it shows a great story being built by many hands across seventy years, and it shows exactly where the greatest pair of hands took hold.

## Why the Middle Link Was Forgotten

If Bandello's contribution is as large as this study argues, its neglect requires explanation, and the explanation is itself a lesson in how literary fame is allocated. Invention is memorable and amplification is not: da Porto's claim fits a sentence, the coining of the lovers, while Bandello's claim requires a collation, and posterity prefers its credits in sentences. The *Novelle* are also, frankly, forbidding in bulk, two hundred and fourteen tales in an Italian that later centuries read less and less, so that Bandello survived in general memory as a name in footnotes while his matter circulated anonymously inside other men's books. English scholarship compounded the amnesia by studying the play's sources from the play backward, stopping at the first English text it met, as though a river's source were the first bridge. Brooke's poem, being English, verse, and copious, made a satisfying terminus; the French and Italian reaches beyond it were left to specialists, and the specialists' findings, Moore's above all, took decades to seep into the common account.

The proof that the seepage was deserved stands in the other plays the same channels fed. Painter's *Palace of Pleasure*, the prose treasury that Englished Boaistuau's Bandello, supplied the English stage with far more than one tragedy: Webster's *The Duchess of Malfi*, to name the most famous case, is built on Bandello's tale of the Duchess of Amalfi as Painter Englished it, so that the Mantuan's narrative instincts shaped the Jacobean stage's darkest masterpiece as surely as they shaped the Elizabethan stage's most beloved one. Bandello was, for a century of English drama, a silent partner in the repertory, his stories naturalized past recognition. The Romeo novella is merely the largest instance of a general condition, which is why restoring it matters beyond a single play's pedigree: the middle of the chain is where early modern Europe actually did its storytelling business, and Bandello was one of its great houses.

The map is designed to be read in both directions. Read downward, from da Porto to the play, and it tells the story of accumulation: a design acquiring a nurse, a rhetoric, a priced world, a ceremonial tomb and a civic judgment as it travels, each teller depositing the layer his medium and moment required. Read upward, from the play to da Porto, and it performs the source hunter's discipline in reverse, stripping the last teller's additions away until the bare design stands, so that Shakespeare's own contribution can be measured by subtraction and Bandello's by the thickness of the middle column. Either direction yields the study's verdict without further argument: the column that changes most between the first telling and the last is the middle one, and the hand that fills it is the hand of the Mantuan courtier who never invented a single hinge of the plot and rebuilt everything the hinges carried.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Bandello map, and how does it trace the tale from Siena to the London stage?

The InsightCrunch Bandello map is the table in this study that sets the story's descent element by element: the frame, the nurse, the speeches, the apothecary, the letter, the tomb and the ending, each traced from da Porto's version through Bandello's treatment to the form that reached the play. It charts a double truth. Structurally, almost nothing in the story after 1530 is new; texturally, almost everything that makes the story actable is Bandello's work. The map exists because plot summaries cannot show that distinction, since a summary of Bandello and a summary of da Porto read nearly alike, while their pages, and their descendants, differ in scale, sympathy and stageworthiness at every point.

### Q: Who was Matteo Bandello, and where does the Romeo and Juliet story sit inside his Novelle?

Matteo Bandello (about 1485 to 1561) was an Italian courtier, former Dominican novice, soldier's companion and pensioner of the French crown who ended as bishop of Agen, and whose life's work was the *Novelle*, the largest tale collection of the Italian sixteenth century. The Romeo and Juliet story stands as the ninth novella of the second part, under a heading that announces the unfortunate death of two most unhappy lovers, one by poison and the other by grief. Within the collection it is one tale among two hundred and fourteen, framed like the rest by a dedicatory letter and presented as a story recounted in company and written down from the telling.

### Q: When and where were Bandello's Novelle first published?

The first three parts of the *Novelle* were printed at Lucca by Vincenzo Busdraghi in 1554, eleven years after the last of the events the collection describes and seven years before Bandello's death. A fourth part appeared posthumously at Lyon in 1573. The Lucca edition is the text that matters for the Romeo and Juliet descent, since the lovers' tale stands in the second part, and since Pierre Boaistuau's French *Histoires tragiques* of 1559, the version through which the story reached England, was rendered from Bandello's printed Italian. The five years between Lucca and Paris measure how fast a successful Italian book could become European property in the age of the hand press and the court translator.

### Q: How closely does Bandello follow da Porto's plot?

Bandello follows da Porto's plot almost without deviation: the feast, the window colloquy, the secret marriage, Tebaldo's death, the exile, the forced match with the Count of Lodrone, the potion, the failed letter, the Mantuan poison, the tomb, the waking, the double death and the reconciliation all stand in da Porto's order and with da Porto's machinery. The fidelity is the standing difficulty of the scholarship, because it means Bandello's contribution cannot be shown from a synopsis. It appears only in collation, where his page is found carrying longer speeches, fuller motives, an enlarged nurse, a slower apothecary scene and a ceremonial ending that da Porto's briefer *historia* does not attempt.

### Q: What did Bandello change about the nurse's part in the story?

Bandello changed the nurse from a channel into a character. Da Porto's old servant carries messages; Bandello's balia, who has nursed Giulietta from childhood, receives her confession, negotiates with Romeo at length, helps arrange the marriage, attends it, keeps the secret through the crisis and speaks in scenes rather than errands. The enlargement propagates through Boaistuau into Brooke and Painter and so into the play, whose Nurse engineers the marriage in the middle acts and counsels its abandonment in Act 3 Scene 5. Her scale in Shakespeare is therefore not a dramatist's comic inflation but an inheritance: Bandello discovered that a go-between with a voice turns a plot of messages into a drama of loyalty and betrayal.

### Q: What does Bandello add to the poison purchase in Mantua?

Bandello adds poverty, reluctance and price. Da Porto's purchase is brisk; Bandello's Mantuan speziale is a poor man who knows the sale is forbidden, hesitates, and is overcome by Romeo's gold, so that the transaction becomes a small tragedy of want in which the law is not absent but unaffordable. The scene is the direct ancestor, through Boaistuau and Brooke, of the play's Act 5 Scene 1, where the apothecary's consent is given in the words "My poverty, but not my will, consents" and Romeo answers that gold is the worse poison of the two. The moral architecture, poverty as the lock and gold as the key, is the novella's construction.

### Q: What happens in Bandello's tomb sequence that earlier tellings kept shorter?

Bandello expands the tomb into a full closing movement. Romeo arrives with a confession letter for his father already written, dismisses his servant under sealed orders, opens the Cappelletti sepulchre with tools, speaks to the body at length and drinks. Giulietta then wakes while he still breathes, and the two hold the conversation the plot has conspired to prevent, after which she refuses rescue and dies by an act of will, holding her breath beside him. Noise brings the watch, the servant and the friar are examined, and the sequence ends in inquiry and judgment. Earlier tellings contain the events; Bandello gives them ceremony, duration and a city in attendance.

### Q: Does Bandello present the Verona story as history or as invention?

Bandello presents the story as history. The tale is set in the Verona of Bartolomeo della Scala, more than two centuries before the telling, and involves houses whose names Dante had already coupled as feuding families, a warrant Bandello's audience would recognise. His frame claims the status of report: a thing recounted in company, involving real names, civic officers and a named church, the Cappelletti sepulchre at San Francesco. Modern scholarship treats the lovers themselves as legend anchored to genuine names, since no evidence supports their existence, but the distinction would not have governed Bandello's page, which proceeds with a chronicler's circumstantial confidence throughout.

### Q: By what route did Bandello's version travel from Lucca to the London stage?

The route has three crossings. Bandello's Italian *Novelle* were printed at Lucca in 1554; Pierre Boaistuau rendered the tale into French in his *Histoires tragiques* at Paris in 1559, keeping its incident almost entire; Arthur Brooke versified Boaistuau in English in 1562, and William Painter Englished the same French text in prose in 1566. Shakespeare, writing in the middle 1590s, worked chiefly from Brooke's poem, with Painter's prose as a parallel channel. No English Bandello existed for him to consult, and none was needed: the novella reached the stage as a river reaches a mill, through channels dug by translators, but the water, in proportion and substance, remained Bandello's.

### Q: What did Boaistuau take from Bandello, and what did he add of his own?

Boaistuau took the story's architecture entire: the feast, the window, the marriage, the enlarged nurse, the Mantuan poison purchase, the tomb with its waking and speeches, the inquiry and the shared monument all pass from the *Novelle* into the *Histoires tragiques* of 1559 in Bandello's order. His own additions are chiefly tonal and framing: a moralizing prologue, sententious commentary at the joints of the action, and a darker colouring of the friar toward self-interest. The sternness that later frames the story in English, including the condemnation in Brooke's address to the reader, is therefore substantially French varnish on Italian ambivalence, a distinction source study ignores at its peril.

### Q: How many tales do Bandello's Novelle contain, and how was the collection organized?

The *Novelle* contain two hundred and fourteen tales, issued in three parts at Lucca in 1554 with a fourth part printed after Bandello's death at Lyon in 1573. The organization loosens the Boccaccian frame: instead of one company telling tales in one retreat, each novella carries a dedicatory letter to a named patron, patroness or captain, and is presented as a story recounted in a specific gathering at Milan, Mantua, Verona or in the camps of the Italian Wars, often by a named speaker. The device advertises the tales as reports from the courts Bandello served, and the Romeo and Juliet novella, ninth of the second part, wears the same credentials as the rest.

### Q: How does Bandello's narrator intervene inside the tale as it runs?

Bandello's narrator is a presence, not a pane of glass. Between scenes he steps forward to judge an action, pity a sufferer, generalize a motive into a maxim about youth, parents or Fortune, and remind the company of the tale's exemplary purpose. The interventions belong to the courtly frame, in which a story must present its credentials as instruction. Their interest lies in their failure to govern the telling: the scenes themselves distribute sympathy more evenly than the maxims allow, giving Giulietta the reasoning centre of the tale and her father the brutality of the forced match, so that the narrator's doctrine and the story's evidence stand in a tension the translators never quite ironed out.

### Q: Which details in Shakespeare's play are traced back through the chain to Bandello?

The traced details form a consistent set: the nurse's scale and her role as the marriage's engineer; the Mantuan apothecary's poverty, reluctance and purchase by gold in Act 5 Scene 1; the plague suspicion that seals the letter-bearing friar into a house in Act 5 Scene 2; the tomb's ceremonial of opened sepulchre, valedictory speech, arriving watch and public inquiry in Act 5 Scene 3; and the heroine's rehearsed fears before the potion, enumerated in Act 4 Scene 3. Each element stands in Bandello, passes through Boaistuau into Brooke and Painter, and reaches the play in the proportions the novella set, as the parallel summaries in Bullough's *Narrative and Dramatic Sources* display.

### Q: Why does Bandello's Friar Lorenzo agree to marry the lovers?

Bandello's Lorenzo agrees from reasons of state as much as from pastoral sympathy. Hearing Romeo's confession of the love, the friar reflects that a marriage between the Montecchi and the Cappelletti might compose the feud that has repeatedly drenched Verona in blood, converting a private passion into a public peace. The motive dignifies the friar and darkens the catastrophe, since the instrument of reconciliation becomes the instrument of ruin: the secret marriage he performs supplies the potion, the letter and the tomb with their premises. Da Porto had given the friar the same hope in brief; Bandello gives it argument and voice, making Lorenzo a counsellor whose reasoning the audience can follow and whose failure is therefore a judgment on reasoning itself.

### Q: How does the length of Bandello's novella compare with da Porto's tale?

Bandello's novella is several times the length of da Porto's *historia*, and the difference is the substance of his contribution. Da Porto's tale is a brief, swift narrative that reports much of its action in summary; Bandello retards every approach to catastrophe, converting summary into scene, report into dialogue and motive into argued speech. The feast gains duration, the window exchange becomes a colloquy, the friar's consent is debated, the potion fears are enumerated, the tomb sequence becomes a closing movement with an inquiry attached. Collators from Moore to Bullough treat the multiplication of length as the measurable form of the retelling's work: nothing structural is added, and everything human is enlarged.

### Q: What did Painter's prose version take from the same French source?

William Painter's version, printed in the second volume of *The Palace of Pleasure* in 1566, took from Boaistuau's French what Brooke's poem took, the whole Bandello architecture, and rendered it in English prose closer to the French wording than Brooke's verse could be. Its value to the descent is as a parallel channel and a control: where Painter and Brooke agree, the agreement certifies Boaistuau, and where the play agrees with Brooke against Painter, the playwright's desk is identified. Painter also returned the story to Bandello's own medium, the prose novella, so that English readers possessed the tale in both its northern dresses, poem and prose, within four years.

### Q: What happens to Friar Lorenzo at the end of Bandello's novella?

At the end of Bandello's novella Lorenzo is arrested, examined and made to tell the whole story, from the secret marriage through the potion and the failed letter, before the Signore of Verona. His narrative is the city's official account of the catastrophe, and the novella gives it the weight of a deposition: the friar is neither villain nor scapegoat but a witness whose good intention is on the record beside its consequences. Judgment and punishment follow within the civic settlement that closes the tale, after which the reconciled fathers bury the lovers together. The play preserves the pattern in Friar Laurence's long narrative before the Prince in Act 5 Scene 3.

### Q: Did Bandello invent the feud between the Montecchi and the Cappelletti?

Bandello did not invent the feud or the surnames. Dante, in the sixth canto of the *Purgatorio*, had coupled Montecchi and Cappelletti among the emblematic divided families of Italy more than two centuries earlier, and Luigi da Porto took the names from Dante when he built his Verona tale, casting the Montecchi and Cappelletti as the city's rival houses. Bandello inherited the feud from da Porto's design entire and contributed not its existence but its civic texture: the edicts it provokes, the marriage market it distorts, the street discipline it defeats and the reconciliation, over the lovers' bodies, that finally suspends it.

### Q: How does Bandello handle the lovers' first meeting at the Cappelletti feast?

Bandello gives the meeting duration and choreography. Romeo enters masked with his companions, languishing for a lady who scorns him and counselled to cure one love by looking at others; the dance joins his hand with Giulietta's, and the jest of Marcuccio, whose hand is unnaturally cold, parts and remarks their joined hands before the company. Words follow, then inquiries after names, so that each learns the other's house only when the attachment is formed. Da Porto had designed the sequence; Bandello slows it into a scene of glances, touch and dawning knowledge, the template from which the play's meeting, compressed into a shared sonnet, departs and against which it is measured.

### Q: How should an essay use the Bandello map to argue that Shakespeare's source was a chain and not a book?

An essay should use the map's rows as its evidence and its columns as its argument. Take one element, the apothecary, the nurse or the tomb, and show it changing state at each crossing: da Porto's summary, Bandello's scene, Boaistuau's faithful French, Brooke's verse or Painter's prose, and the play's final form. The pattern that emerges, structure inherited and texture transformed at every stage, proves that "source" names a process rather than a volume. The essay's strongest sentence is the map's own verdict: Shakespeare's working book was Brooke's poem, but the story inside the poem had been built at Lucca, and its builder was Bandello.
