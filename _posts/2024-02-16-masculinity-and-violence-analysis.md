Masculinity in Romeo and Juliet is not a quality a man possesses in private. It is a performance staged before witnesses, marked by examiners, and paid for in blood when the performance falters. Verona's young men do not simply happen to fight. They fight because the city's definition of manhood leaves them no other legible way to prove that they are men, and the tragedy is the audit of that definition carried out to its final balance. The verdict of this reading is plain: the play condemns the code it depicts. It condemns the code not by sermon but by consequence, arranging its scenes so that every success the code awards is a step toward a corpse, and every refusal the code punishes is the one gesture that might have saved the city. Romeo's story, read through this lens, is the story of a young man who twice declines the examination, once in the square and once at the tomb, and who is destroyed both times by the pressure to sit it after all.

![Masculinity and violence in Romeo and Juliet, the duel in Verona's square - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The performance begins below stairs, among servants who cannot afford the swords they boast about, and it ends in a churchyard where a count's son dies asking to be laid beside a girl he barely knew. Between those poles the same examination is set again and again: a challenge issued in public, a reputation weighed in public, a body carried off in public. What varies is only the candidate. Sampson performs for Gregory. Tybalt performs for the honour of a house whose master has just forbidden the performance. Mercutio performs for the friend whose silence he reads as a failed recitation. Romeo performs, at last, for a dead man's ghost, and passes the examination so completely that the Prince must close the school. The sections that follow trace the performance through its venues: the street, the feast, the square, the cell, and the tomb, asking at each stop who sets the test, who watches it, and what the certificate costs.

## Masculinity as Performance: The Verdict in Brief

The governing claim can be stated as an equation the play itself keeps writing on walls, tables, and tombstones: in Verona, manhood equals readiness for violence, publicly displayed. Every element of that equation matters. Readiness, because the code grades the will before the deed, and a man who must be provoked into competence has already lost marks. Violence, because no other currency is accepted; eloquence, wealth, lineage, and love are all treated as small change until they are converted into a drawn sword. Public display, because a proof nobody witnesses is, within the code's bookkeeping, a proof that never happened. Tybalt's fury at the feast is not diminished by the fact that Romeo has left unharmed. It is inflamed by the fact that the insult was witnessed and the answer deferred, and the deferral itself becomes a debt that gathers interest until it is collected in the square.

### Is Romeo's refusal to fight the play's model of manhood or its failure?

The play presents the refusal as its buried model and the code presents it as a failure, and the distance between those two judgments is the tragedy's engine. The two judgments never meet, because the witnesses who grade the refusal do not know the fact that would acquit it.

Romeo's courtesy to Tybalt is the only conduct in the square that answers to the marriage he has just contracted and to the city the Prince has just threatened, yet every witness on stage reads it as disgrace, and the reading, not the conduct, determines what happens next. The refusal fails socially while succeeding morally, and Verona is a place where social failure is the kind that gets a man killed by proxy, through the friend who cannot bear to watch it.

Set against the code's equation, the play keeps smuggling in a rival measure. Courage in this text is repeatedly located where the code refuses to look: in a girl who drinks a vial alone in a darkened room, in a friar's failed patience, in a peacemaker who parts fighters with his hands and is mocked for arriving with the wrong equipment. The rival measure never gets a manifesto. It gets something better, a demonstration repeated until the pattern is unmistakable: the qualities the code calls unmanly are the qualities that preserve life, and the qualities the code certifies are the qualities that fill the Capulet vault. A reading that treats the duels as decoration on a love story misses the play's own bookkeeping. The love story is interrupted, judged, and finally buried by the examination system, and the examiners are not villains imported from outside the romance. They are the romance's own world, speaking its first language.

The psychology underneath the performance deserves naming at the outset, because it explains why intelligent characters obey a code that visibly ruins them. Manhood performed before witnesses is manhood held in escrow by other people. A Veronese gentleman does not own his reputation; he rents it from the companions who watch him, and the rent is payable on demand, in the street, at whatever hour a challenger chooses. This is why the challenges in the play arrive at such ruinous times, on a wedding afternoon, at a feast, beside a tomb at night. The examiner sets the hour precisely because the candidate does not. To decline is to confess that the rented identity was a fraud, and the confession is priced higher than the risk of death, because death at least pays the rent in full. Mercutio understands the pricing perfectly, which is why his dying curse is aimed at both houses rather than at the man whose sword actually opened him. The houses are the landlords. Tybalt is only their bailiff.

## The Servants Invent the Examination in Act 1 Scene 1

The play opens its account of manhood at the bottom of the household, and the choice of altitude is the first piece of analysis the text performs on its own theme. Sampson and Gregory, Capulet servingmen with no property in the feud beyond their livery, spend the opening exchanges constructing a complete syllabus of violent manhood in miniature. Sampson announces the curriculum in terms of territory and gender at once: "I will push Montague's men from the wall and thrust his maids to the wall" (1.1.10-15, in René Weis's Arden Third Series edition, the text quoted throughout this article). The wall is the dry side of the street, the place of precedence, and to take it is to rank oneself. Gregory's deflating reply, that the weakest goes to the wall, forces Sampson to repair the boast by distributing weakness downward onto women: women, "being the weaker vessels, are ever thrust to the wall" (1.1.13-15). Manhood is thus defined in the play's first minute as a ranking exercise in which women exist as the bottom rank against which male rank is measured.

### Where does the play first equate being a man with being ready to fight?

The equation is completed within the first twenty lines, in Sampson's promise to cut off the maids' heads, corrected under Gregory's questioning into the taking of maidenheads (1.1.20-24). The boast is made to a fellow servant, for a fellow servant's grading, before an enemy is anywhere in sight.

Sexual conquest and swordplay are fused in a single boast before any gentleman has appeared, and the fusion is the point: violence against men and possession of women are presented as two denominations of one currency, each convertible into standing among the companions who hear the boast. The first audience for Veronese manhood is never the enemy. It is the friend standing close enough to grade the performance, and Gregory grades with a teasing cruelty that establishes the examination's tone. Every boast is instantly audited. Every correction must be answered with a larger claim. The street code's first law is that a man is only ever as large as his last unchallenged sentence.

The sexual boast deserves a slower look, because it establishes how thoroughly the code confuses categories that a healthier order would keep apart. Sampson's slide from heads to maidenheads is a pun, and the pun is doing structural work: it announces that in Verona's idiom, the treatment of women and the treatment of rival men are dialects of one language. When Gregory observes that the maids must take the sense that feel it, and Sampson answers that he is "a pretty piece of flesh" (1.1.26-27), the body itself becomes a credential, displayed like a sword. Nothing has happened. No Montague has appeared. Yet the full apparatus of masculine proof is already running at capacity: territory claimed, women demoted to scenery, flesh exhibited, wit exchanged as a fencing warm-up. The play's gentlemen will refine this apparatus with better grammar and costlier clothes. They will not add a single new component to it.

Then the apparatus meets its first reality check, and the check is legal rather than martial. When Montague servants actually appear, the two performers discover an urgent interest in who shall begin, because the law punishes the beginner. Gregory resolves to frown as he passes and let the others take the gesture as they choose; Sampson, emboldened, bites his thumb, then panics into the famous qualification that he bites his thumb, certainly, but not at anyone in particular. The comedy is exact. Performance manhood requires an audience and an enemy, but it requires even more urgently an alibi, and the search for the alibi exposes the performance as performance. These are not warriors. They are actors improvising a heroic scene while glancing sideways at the magistrate. Shakespeare gives the code's purest statement to its most frightened speakers, and the irony is diagnostic rather than merely comic: the examination frightens even its examiners, because everyone in Verona suspects, at some level, that the certificate is printed on nothing.

The servants also establish the examination's most dangerous feature, its upward contagion. Within a hundred lines the street performance has drawn in Benvolio, then Tybalt, then the two old masters themselves, then the citizenry with clubs, until the Prince must threaten death to clear the stage. A boast by two liveried clowns has mobilised a city. The mechanism matters for everything that follows: manhood performed at any altitude licenses performance at every altitude, because the code recognises no ceiling. The masters who should discipline the servants instead audition before them. Old Capulet calls for his long sword; old Montague comes flourishing his blade; and the wives of both houses must physically restrain their husbands, in a tableau the play will keep reusing, in which the women of Verona do the work of the law while the men of Verona compete to break it. The rulebook that governs these exchanges, the formal code of honour with its challenges and satisfactions, is anatomised in the companion account of [honor and the code of the feud](/2024/02/09/honor-code-feud-romeo-and-juliet/); the concern here is narrower and colder. The rulebook would be a dead letter without performers willing to die for an audience, and the play opens by showing where the willingness is manufactured: in banter, below stairs, between friends, before breakfast.

## Old Men and Crutches: The Fathers Perform for the Street

The entrance of the two household heads in the opening scene is staged as a competition in geriatric swordsmanship, and its comedy carries a serious measurement. Capulet, in his gown, calls for his long sword. Montague enters flourishing a blade and must be held. Lady Capulet's response to her husband is the single most efficient piece of criticism in the scene: "A crutch, a crutch! Why call you for a sword?" (1.1.77). The joke measures the distance between the performer's self-image and the audience's reading of the act. Capulet believes he is answering an insult to his house. His wife sees an old man reaching for a prop from a play he is no longer cast in. Both are correct, and that double correctness defines the fathers' place in the system. The code does not retire its performers. It keeps them on stage long after their bodies have resigned, because the feud's senior men are its memory, and a performance tradition survives only while its oldest actors still insist on their lines.

Lady Montague's restraint is physical rather than verbal: "Thou shalt not stir one foot to seek a foe" (1.1.79). Read together, the two wives perform the scene's only effective governance. The Prince will arrive with threats and statutes, and his intervention, though louder, is not obviously more effective, since the brawl he disperses in the first scene reassembles at every level of the play until the vault receives the final cast. The wives' success is temporary too, but it is achieved without a weapon, and the play quietly registers the contrast. In Verona, the work of peace is women's work and servants' work, unpaid and uncredited, while the work of violence is men's work, applauded and inherited. A son growing up in either household learns the curriculum by watching who is praised at dinner. Nobody praises the restraint. The restraint is treated as an interruption of the real business, a commercial break in the performance, and the sons absorb the valuation exactly as delivered.

This is the sense in which the fathers matter to a study of performed manhood out of all proportion to their swordplay. Neither old man lands a blow in the entire play. Their contribution to the examination system is not violence but theatre management. They own the stages, the feasts, the guest lists, the marriage negotiations, on which the young men will be graded. Capulet's feast is the venue of Tybalt's humiliation and therefore of the square's catastrophe. Capulet's household is the venue in which Juliet is threatened with the street when she fails her own examination in obedience. The fathers set the questions even when their hands are too old to hold the swords, and their sons and nephews sit examinations the fathers wrote in their own youth and have never revised. Coppélia Kahn's account of the feud as a rite of masculine identity, examined at length later in this article, finds its strongest evidence in this generational machinery: the rite persists because each cohort of elders certifies the next, and certification is the one paternal duty Verona never neglects.

## The Feast: Tybalt's Deferred Answer and the Debt of Honour

Tybalt's appearance at the Capulet feast is the code's first examination at gentlemen's level, and its result is the tragedy's first loaded weapon. Tybalt recognises Romeo's voice and sends for his rapier with the serene theology of a man for whom killing is liturgy: "Now, by the stock and honour of my kin, / To strike him dead I hold it not a sin" (1.5.55-56). The couplet is worth pausing over, because it shows how completely the performance has been internalised as conscience. Tybalt does not experience the code as pressure. He experiences it as piety. Striking a guest dead at his uncle's table would not, in his accounting, be a breach of the feast's obligations. It would be their fulfilment, the house's honour defended at the house's own board, before the house's own guests. The witnesses are the feast's whole company, and Tybalt's instinct is to convert a social occasion into an examination hall the moment an examiner's opportunity appears.

Capulet's intervention then creates the tragedy's first debt. The old man, playing the master of the house rather than the master of the feud, orders his nephew to endure the intruder, praises Romeo's bearing to his face, and escalates to the question underneath every performance dispute in the play: "Am I the master here, or you?" (1.5.76-78). Tybalt's submission is extracted by a rival performance, the patriarch's, and the extraction is public within the household's hearing. His exit speech is therefore the most consequential dozen lines in the act: "Patience perforce with wilful choler meeting / Makes my flesh tremble in their different greeting. / I will withdraw, but this intrusion shall / Now seeming sweet convert to bitt'rest gall" (1.5.88-91). The imagery is digestive and financial at once. Sweetness will be converted, as currency is converted, at a rate the debtor does not choose. Tybalt leaves the feast with his manhood in arrears, and arrears in Verona are always collected from a third party if the original creditor cannot be reached. The collection will fall on Mercutio.

The feast scene also refines the psychology of the examination in a way the square scene will exploit. Tybalt's humiliation is not that he was prevented from fighting. It is that he was prevented by his own side, before witnesses of his own side, in the role of a boy being managed. Capulet's language sharpens the wound by infantilising the candidate: the nephew is counselled to keep the peace, to make no show, to be content, in the tones reserved for a guest's difficult child. A code that grades men by their readiness to answer insults has just required its most diligent student to swallow one from his own uncle, and the syllabus offers no procedure for that case. There is no form of satisfaction a nephew can demand from the head of his house. The debt is therefore unpayable in kind and must be discharged in substitute coin, against Romeo, at the first public opportunity, with interest set by the trembling of the flesh that Tybalt reports with such unnerving honesty. The play is often described as a tragedy of haste. In the feast scene it is more exactly a tragedy of accounting, in which an emotional ledger is opened that only blood can close.

Set beside this, Romeo's conduct at the same feast is the anti-examination in embryo. He comes masked, in company, for love rather than honour; he sees Juliet; the feud's vocabulary simply falls out of his speech and is replaced by the pilgrim language of the shared sonnet. Nothing in the scene suggests that Romeo has renounced the code on principle. He has not yet been examined since his marriage raised the stakes. But the juxtaposition is the play's own: at one end of the hall, a young man composing a sonnet with a stranger; at the other, a young man composing a death sentence for the composer. Verona contains both scripts at every moment. The tragedy is that only one of them is compulsory.

## Mercutio: Wit as a Rival Style of Manhood

Between Tybalt's formality and Romeo's lyricism the play sets a third style of performed manhood, and gives it the best lines. Mercutio's masculinity is verbal fencing conducted at sword-point's distance from the real thing. His set pieces, the Queen Mab speech, the conjuring of Romeo in the Capulet orchard's street, the great prose portrait of Tybalt in Act 2 Scene 4, are all duels fought with breath instead of steel, and all are staged before the same examining audience of companions. When he conjures his absent friend by the inventory of Rosaline's body, "by her bright eyes, by her high forehead and her scarlet lip, / By her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh" (2.1.17-19), the performance has a disciplinary purpose beneath its obscenity. Lovesickness is being treated as a failed style, a softness to be laughed back into shape. The friend who pines has wandered off the syllabus, and the company's senior wit conducts a public retrieval, grading Romeo's desire downward into appetite so that appetite can be managed with the usual jokes.

His doctrine is stated as a fencing instruction: "If love be rough with you, be rough with love. / Prick love for pricking, and you beat love down" (1.4.27-28). Even tenderness is to be answered as an opponent's touch, with a riposte. The metaphor is not decorative. Mercutio's whole style rests on the premise that every encounter is a bout and every bout is graded, and that the man who treats experience as combat cannot be wounded by it in the places where passive men bleed. The portrait of Tybalt shows the same premise turned outward in parody. Tybalt is "more than Prince of Cats," a duellist of the first house, who fights "as you sing prick-song, keeps time, distance, and proportion" (2.4.19-24). The mockery is expert and affectionate in its expertise. Mercutio can parody the formal style so precisely because he has mastered its rulebook in order to transcend it. His own style advertises spontaneity, improvisation, the bout won by brilliance rather than by the book.

### Which style of manhood does the play kill first, Mercutio's or Tybalt's?

The play kills the improviser first and the formalist minutes later, and the order is an argument. Mercutio's style claims that wit can keep its practitioner above the machinery of the code, fencing with words while lesser men fence with steel.

His death exposes the claim: when the examination turns earnest, the wit is drawn into it as a candidate like any other, and his brilliance purchases him nothing but a more articulate dying speech. Tybalt's formalism is killed second, by the code's newest and most reluctant graduate, and the symmetry is the play's bleak joke. Both styles are certificates from the same school, and the school cremates its honours students in the same afternoon.

The rivalry between the styles is therefore a rivalry inside a single institution, and recognising that fact disciplines a common misreading. Mercutio is often treated as the play's free spirit, the outsider whose death marks the code's victory over spontaneity. The text is less sentimental. Mercutio enforces the code at its most sensitive point. It is Mercutio, not Tybalt, who first grades Romeo's peace-making in the square and returns the failing mark that Romeo cannot endure: the charge of "calm, dishonourable, vile submission" (3.1.69-71) is delivered by the friend, in the idiom of the school, before the enemy has landed a blow. The improviser's freedom turns out to be freedom to examine others. When Tybalt withdraws from Romeo's courtesy, it is Mercutio who supplies the sword the examination requires, insulting Tybalt with the fiddlestick joke until the bout he claims to transcend is arranged around him. The play's psychology here is merciless: the style that advertises itself as an escape from the code is the code's most effective recruiting instrument, because it makes obedience feel like personality.

His death also removes the examination's last humane invigilator. Mercutio grades cruelly, but he grades in wit, and wit leaves its victims alive to appeal the mark with a better performance tomorrow. The examiners who succeed him grade without humour and without appeal: a confessor with a taxonomy of womanishness, a father with a penalty schedule published in advance, a suitor at a tomb with an arrest to make. The play's temperature falls with its jester because the institution he served was never dependent on his mercy. It was dependent on his presence, and the offices he filled, examiner, second, companion, witness, are inherited by men who perform them as duties rather than as art, which is to say by the code itself, operating at last without the distraction of a personality.

## The Square: A Refusal Performed Before the Wrong Audience

Act 3 Scene 1 is the examination scene proper, and its dramatic architecture is a study in audiences. The duel itself, its choreography and its place as the play's hinge, belongs to the scene's own article and is compressed here to what the performance argument needs. What matters for the theme of masculinity is the composition of the crowd. Present are Benvolio, the play's one habitual peacemaker; Mercutio, the examiner in chief; a challenger with a debt to collect; and, arriving late to his own examination, a bridegroom carrying a secret that disqualifies him from the syllabus and that he cannot declare. Benvolio's opening counsel sets the conditions like a proctor announcing the weather: "The day is hot, the Capels are abroad, / And if we meet we shall not scape a brawl, / For now, these hot days, is the mad blood stirring" (3.1.1-4). The examination, in Verona, is a climate. Candidates do not choose whether to sit it. They choose only how to be graded.

Tybalt's approach shows the code's etiquette functioning as a trap with polished manners. He asks for a word with one of the company. Mercutio converts the courtesy into a provocation with an examiner's speed: a word should be coupled with something, made "a word and a blow" (3.1.37-39). When Tybalt, with a patience that deserves more credit than readings usually grant, observes that Mercutio consorts with Romeo, the pun on consort as musical company detonates the scene's verbal duel, and Mercutio flourishes the fiddlestick that shall make his opponent dance. Every stage of this escalation is a performance decision made for the gallery. Tybalt has come for Romeo and says so. The bout with Mercutio exists because Mercutio's style cannot permit a scene in which he is present and not performing, and because Tybalt's debt cannot permit a scene in which an insult from a Montague associate goes uncollected. Two examination systems, the formal and the improvisational, interlock like gears, and the bridegroom walks into the machinery at the moment of maximum engagement.

Romeo's answers to the challenge are, by the code's rubric, catastrophic, and by any other rubric in the play, exemplary. To the formal charge, "thou art a villain" (3.1.56-57), he returns the language of the marriage the square cannot know about: "Tybalt, the reason that I have to love thee / Doth much excuse the appertaining rage / To such a greeting. Villain am I none" (3.1.58-61). He protests that he never injured his new kinsman, loves him better than the kinsman can devise, and begs him, "good Capulet, which name I tender / As dearly as mine own, be satisfied" (3.1.64-68). As conduct, this is the feud's cure arriving in person: a Montague declining satisfaction from a Capulet on grounds of kinship, offering the enemy a name he now claims as his own. As performance, it is illegible. The square has no column in its mark book for a refusal grounded in a secret marriage. It has a column for submission, and Mercutio reads the entry aloud.

### Why does the shame fall on the peaceful man and not on the violent one?

Because the code grades visibility rather than intention, and peace, performed in public, is indistinguishable from fear to any witness who does not hold the script. Romeo's reasons are genuine and are also, by design, unstateable: the one fact that would convert his courtesy from submission into policy is the one fact his marriage requires him to conceal.

Secrecy thus functions within the examination as a forfeited defence. The violent man, by contrast, carries his reasons on his sword point, where everyone can read them at a glance, and the code's preference for legible motives over true ones is the deepest of its corruptions, the point where a system of honour becomes a system of theatre and admits it.

Mercutio's intervention completes the examination's logic. "O calm, dishonourable, vile submission! / Alla stoccata carries it away" (3.1.69-71). The three adjectives are a grade transcript: calm, the temperament the Friar had praised in the abstract that morning; dishonourable, the verdict; vile, the sentence of excommunication from the company of men. The friend then sits the examination on the candidate's behalf, an act of loyalty that the code counts as its highest expression and the play counts as its most expensive. Romeo, still attempting the peacekeeper's part, steps between the fighters with the Prince's edict in his mouth, "Gentlemen, for shame, forbear this outrage" (3.1.80-84), and the intervention places his arm across his friend's sword arm at the moment Tybalt's thrust arrives. The code's bookkeeping will record that Mercutio died of a wound received under Romeo's arm. The play's bookkeeping records something colder: that every attempt to interrupt the performance from inside its own theatre becomes part of the choreography, and that the peacekeeper's body is simply the screen through which the examination delivers its result.

Mercutio's dying performance is the style's last examination answer, and he knows the marking scheme well enough to joke in its margins. The wound is a scratch, not so deep as a well nor so wide as a church door, but enough; ask for him tomorrow and the company shall find him a grave man (3.1.90-96). Then the wit curdles into the audit the whole scene has been avoiding: a braggart, a rogue, a villain "that fights by the book of arithmetic" (3.1.97-99) did this, and the devil brought Romeo between them. The curse that follows, on both houses, is usually read as the play's thesis about the feud. Within the performance argument it is something more specific: a candidate's discovery, at the point of death, that the examination was rigged. Mercutio sat every paper brilliantly. He mocked the formal style, mastered the informal one, defended his friend's honour at the cost of his own safety, and the certificate the school issues him is a grave. His curse names the examiners, not the questions, because he has understood at last that the houses need the examination more than any candidate does. A generation graded is a generation governed.

## Effeminate: Romeo's Self-Diagnosis and the Price of Passing

Romeo's speech over his dying friend is the moment the theme stops being sociology and becomes psychology, because the candidate internalises the examiner. Alone with his guilt, he conducts the grading himself, in the code's own vocabulary, and returns the verdict the square had reached: "O sweet Juliet, / Thy beauty hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valour's steel" (3.1.109-111). The metallurgy of the image deserves the closest attention this article can give it. Steel is tempered by heating and cooling in sequence; its virtue is a hardness that holds an edge without snapping. Romeo imagines love as a botched tempering, a reheating that left the blade soft. Within the metaphor, the self is an instrument manufactured for one purpose, and any experience that unfits the instrument for that purpose registers as damage. That a husband of three hours should describe his marriage as industrial damage to a weapon is the play's most compact statement of what the code does to the interior life. Romeo does not say that his love was wrong. He says that his love has unmade his equipment, and the distinction is the tragedy's, because a man who believes his equipment is damaged will reach for the nearest repair, and the repair shop is the duel.

The self-diagnosis also performs a quiet act of misdirection that the play invites its audience to catch. The charge is laid on Juliet's beauty, as though the softening were a property of the beloved rather than a judgment of the code. Yet nothing in Romeo's conduct in the square was soft in any sense the scene itself validates. He offered kinship to an enemy, cited the law, interposed his body between armed men: a programme of actions that, described neutrally, reads as the bravest sequence in the play, since every other brave act in it is performed with a sword in the hand and companions at the back. The effeminacy is not in the conduct. It is in the caption the code supplies for the conduct, and Romeo's tragedy in this scene is that he believes the caption over the evidence of his own afternoon. Shakespeare gives the audience both texts, the deed and the caption, and lets the gap between them stand as the definition of the code's power. A performance system rules most completely not when it directs the limbs but when it edits the memory.

What follows is the examination retaken and passed, at the price the code always intended. Tybalt returns to the square, and the candidate who had tendered the name of Capulet as dearly as his own now formally withdraws his lenity: "Away to heaven respective lenity, / And fire-eyed fury be my conduct now" (3.1.121-122). The speech that follows is a recitation, point by point, of the syllabus he had declined an hour earlier. The insult is returned with its exact wording, "take the 'villain' back again / That late thou gavest me" (3.1.123-125). The stakes are framed as an escort duty for a dead friend's soul, which "is but a little way above our heads, / Staying for thine to keep him company" (3.1.125-127). Even the killing is announced as companionship, the company's business transacted in the company's idiom. Tybalt's reply demotes the new graduate to the rank from which the examination had threatened to expel him: "Thou, wretched boy, that didst consort him here / Shalt with him hence" (3.1.128-129). Boy is the code's failing grade in a single syllable, the same demotion Tybalt had attempted with villain and Capulet had attempted with his managing tones at the feast. Romeo's answer is the shortest line of his life: "This shall determine that." The sword determines it. Within moments the Prince's officers are asking for the offender, and the graduate is fleeing the square with the code's own bewildered epitaph on his afternoon: "O, I am fortune's fool" (3.1.136).

The play's judgment on the passing of this examination is delivered not in commentary but in sentencing and consequence, which is how the brief's complication, whether the play endorses or condemns the code, is settled by the text itself. Romeo's submission to the code purchases him exactly the goods the code advertises: his standing among men is restored, his friend's death is answered, his name is cleared of the submission charge. The same act purchases him exile, the destruction of his marriage's future, and the chain of accidents that ends in the vault. The goods and the ruin arrive in the same parcel, inseparably, and the play's accounting never permits the audience to enjoy the first delivery without signing for the second. A code that is endorsed by its text is usually allowed some scene in which its values produce an unambiguous good. Verona's code is allowed none. Its feasts produce debts, its squares produce corpses, its satisfactions produce exiles, and its finest product, the duel in the churchyard, produces a double suicide's antechamber. The condemnation is structural, which is why it survives every production that tries to stage the duels as glamour. Glamour is a lighting choice. Structure is a body count.

## The Cell: The Friar's Rebuke and the Code Inside the Church

If the square is the code's examination hall, Friar Laurence's cell in Act 3 Scene 3 is its appeals court, and the appeal is heard in the code's own language. The Friar is the play's appointed alternative to Verona's values: a man of herbs and patience, whose doctrine of measure, "wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast," is quoted wherever the play's wisdom is summarised. Yet when the banished Romeo lies on the cell floor offering to tear his own name out of his body, the instrument the Friar reaches for is not his doctrine. It is the square's shaming vocabulary, sharpened by a churchman's authority. "Hold thy desperate hand. / Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art; / Thy tears are womanish, thy wild acts denote / The unreasonable fury of a beast. / Unseemly woman in a seeming man, / Or ill-beseeming beast in seeming both" (3.3.109-113). The rebuke is a taxonomy of failed manhood: man, woman, beast, arranged as a descending scale, with Romeo's tears filed under the female heading and his violence of grief under the animal one. The speech works. It is meant to work, and its working is the darkest datum in the play's study of the code, because it proves that even the counter-institution cannot counsel a Veronese man except by threatening his certificate.

### Does the Friar condemn the code or speak for it in Act 3 Scene 3?

He speaks for it, instrumentally and perhaps unavoidably, while condemning its conclusions, and the scene's discomfort lies in that division. The Friar wants Romeo alive, patient, and gone from Verona before the watch is set; every goal is the code's opposite.

But the lever he trusts is the code's own: shame at womanishness, pride in form, the promise that endurance will be read as manhood. His counsel succeeds on the code's terms, in the code's currency, and a success so purchased quietly refinances the system it was meant to circumvent, since Romeo leaves the cell having learned that even his confessor grades men by the standard that ruined him in the square.

The Friar's argument continues in the same register, and its most quoted sentence is a piece of metallurgy to set beside Romeo's softened steel: "Thy noble shape is but a form of wax, / Digressing from the valour of a man" (3.3.126-127). Wax is the anti-steel, the substance that takes impressions and holds none of its own. Between the square and the cell, the two men who love Romeo most, his friend and his confessor, describe him in a shared workshop vocabulary of male substance, too soft in one account, too impressionable in the other, and the consistency of the metaphor across the play's supposed opposites is the evidence that the code is not a faction's opinion. It is the city's operating system, installed in the church as firmly as in the street. The Nurse, entering with news of Juliet's grief, completes the cell's unlikely consensus. The remedy the whole room eventually assembles, go to Juliet, rise at morning, flee to Mantua, wait for the reconciliation the marriage may purchase, is prefaced by the Friar's instruction, delivered as Juliet is invoked, to rise and be a man (3.3.87-89). Manhood is the name given to getting up off the floor. The definition is gentler than the square's, and the play will spend its remaining acts discovering whether gentler definitions of the same word can save anyone who still answers to it.

There is a further subtlety in the rebuke that an honest reading must register before the critics are heard. The Friar's shaming is aimed at suicide, not at peace-making. He never tells Romeo that the fault lay in refusing Tybalt. He tells him that the fault lies in despairing of the sentence, in prizing banishment as death, in answering mercy with a dagger. The code's vocabulary is here conscripted against the code's own likely outcome, since a Romeo shamed out of suicide is a Romeo preserved for the marriage the Friar still hopes will end the feud. The scene thus stages a kind of linguistic civil war: the old words fighting for new masters, womanish and beast and man conscripted into a sermon whose conclusions the square would not recognise. Whether vocabulary so loaded can be safely conscripted is the question the ending answers. The words survive their conscription. Romeo, preserved by shame in the cell, will be killed by shame's cousins in the churchyard, where another young man's challenge and another demoting insult accomplish what the dagger in the cell could not. The play does not say the Friar was wrong to save him. It says the instrument was borrowed from the arsenal that was always going to reclaim him.

## The Excluded: Women, Poison, and the Courage the Code Cannot Grade

A performance system is known by its exclusions as surely as by its syllabus, and Verona's examination excludes women from candidacy while taxing them for its results. Lady Capulet and the Nurse are the brief's appointed witnesses, and their testimony runs in opposite directions. The Nurse is the code's servant and occasional casualty. In Act 2 Scene 4 she carries Juliet's errand through a street examination she never enlisted for, enduring Mercutio's elaborate mockery while her escort, Peter, preserves a discretion he afterward converts into a boast: he saw no man use her at his pleasure, he reports, for if he had, his weapon should quickly have been out (2.4.163-165). The boast is the servants' scene in miniature, a sword certified in the conditional tense, performance manhood at its purest because entirely hypothetical. The Nurse's errand succeeds anyway, as the play's errands by women usually do, through endurance rather than display, and the contrast is left for the audience to file: the mission is accomplished by the person the code cannot grade, while the person it can grade composes speeches about the mission.

Lady Capulet is the darker witness, because exclusion does not exempt her from the code's appetites; it only restricts her choice of instruments. Barred from the square, she practises its jurisprudence by petition, demanding of the Prince that Romeo, having slain Tybalt, must not live, and rejecting Montague's plea that the killing merely concluded what the law should have ended. Denied the petition's full satisfaction by the sentence of exile, she transfers the feud to the one channel her exclusion leaves open. In Act 3 Scene 5, comforting a daughter whose tears she misreads, she promises a poisoner in Mantua: one shall be sent to where "that same banished runagate doth live," who "shall give him such an unaccustomed dram / That he shall soon keep Tybalt company" (3.5.88-91). Poison is the code's violence translated for the unarmed, and the play's placing of the plan in a mother's consolation speech is among its coldest effects. The examination's values have so completely colonised the household that grief itself is expressed as a contract killing. Her husband, in the same scene, applies the code downward to the daughter who fails her examination in obedience: "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend. / An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5.192-193). The conditional is the feast speech's master question turned domestic. A daughter is an asset whose performance is graded by compliance, and the penalty schedule is published in advance.

Against this whole apparatus the play sets Juliet, and the setting is deliberate enough to constitute the alternative the brief asks after. Juliet never performs for the code's gallery. Her bravest acts are unwitnessed by any examiner who could certify them: the potion speech is delivered alone, to an empty room, after she has dismissed the two women who constitute her entire society. Her courage is not readiness for violence against a challenger. It is readiness to endure, in solitude and without applause, terrors she enumerates more honestly than any duellist enumerates his. Where the code's candidates require companions, insults, and the stimulus of shame, Juliet requires nothing but a vial and a deadline. The play does not call her conduct manly, because its vocabulary has no honourific that fits her, and the absence is the indictment. A city whose language of praise cannot describe its bravest person has published, in that failure, the most damaging entry in the manhood map that closes this article's argument. The map records the moments where manhood is equated with fighting and the cost each equation exacts. Juliet's row in it is the empty one, the examination never offered, and it is the row the whole table exists to frame.

## The Tomb: Paris, the Last Candidate

The examination's final sitting is held in the churchyard in Act 5 Scene 3, and its candidate list shows how thoroughly the code has consumed the play's alternatives. Romeo comes to the tomb as a suicide, not a duellist. He has dismissed his servant, bought his poison, and prepared a farewell letter; his business is with death, not with honour. He even attempts, one last time, the refusal that failed in the square. Paris, keeping his own vigil among the flowers, moves to arrest the exile as a felon, and Romeo answers with the square speech rewritten by three days of ruin: "Good gentle youth, tempt not a desperate man" (5.3.59). He begs to be left alone, warns the intruder off with a plain account of his own danger, and offers, in effect, the courtesy he once offered Tybalt, now stripped of every hope that courtesy could be understood. Paris reads the warning as the code has taught him to read all such speeches, as a performance requiring an answer: "I do defy thy comminations / And apprehend thee for a felon here" (5.3.68-69).

Romeo's reply completes the play's bleakest rhyme. "Wilt thou provoke me? Then have at thee, boy!" (5.3.70). Boy was Tybalt's demotion of Romeo in the square, the failing grade that the code's candidates spend their lives fleeing. In the churchyard the word has changed hands. The graduate of the square now administers the examination to a younger candidate, in the same idiom, with the same result, and the play permits no character on stage to remark the transfer. Paris dies as he had lived within the code, correctly: he fought a felon, before the witness of his page, in defence of a tomb he regarded as his betrothed's. His dying request is for the only certificate left in circulation, proximity to Juliet's body: "If thou be merciful, / Open the tomb, lay me with Juliet" (5.3.72-73). Romeo grants it. The courtesy he could not make legible in the square he performs flawlessly over a corpse in the dark, where the gallery consists of the dead. The examination system does not merely kill its candidates. It trains its survivors to run it, in the dark, for no audience, at the grave's edge, because by the end there is no other language in which a Veronese gentleman knows how to transact with a stranger.

## The Critics: Rite, Mirror, Moral, and Text

Four accounts of this material command the field, and they do not agree. Coppélia Kahn, in the coming-of-age reading that the brief makes mandatory, treats the feud as a rite of masculine identity: a patriarchal order into which sons are initiated by proving, against the rival house, that they are men and not boys, with violence as the rite's ceremony and women as its tokens rather than its participants. On Kahn's account Romeo's crisis in the square is a crisis of initiation. The secret marriage threatens his standing precisely because love and marriage are coded as the sphere in which a youth is governed, softened, made subject; the killing of Tybalt is his violent re-entry into the male order, purchased at the price of the marriage itself. The reading explains the play's otherwise puzzling emotional sequence, in which Romeo experiences his most husbandly act, defending his new kinship, as emasculation, and his most destructive act, the revenge killing, as recovery. Kahn's rite also explains the fathers. A rite of identity requires officiants, and the old men who call for swords they cannot lift are the priesthood of a ceremony whose meaning is certification, not combat.

René Girard's account of the feud starts from the opposite end of the phenomenon. For Girard, violence between the houses is mimetic: each side imitates the other's desire and the other's violence until the rivals become mirror images, doubles whose reciprocal escalation dissolves every original difference between them. The account is powerful on the play's symmetries, the matched households "both alike in dignity," the servants who are interchangeable, the insults that are always answers, the deaths that arrive in pairs. It explains why no participant can name the feud's cause: a mirror has no cause, only a counterpart. What it does not explain is the gendering. The square's crisis is not triggered by symmetry but by a specific charge, that a man has behaved like a woman, and the charge has a direction. Nobody in Verona calls Tybalt womanish for fighting too much, or the Prince beastly for intervening too late. The insults of failed manhood travel one way, downward onto the peaceful, and a theory of pure reciprocity cannot say why the mirror is hung at that angle.

The older moral reading, represented by Franklin M. Dickey's study of the play as a tragedy of rashness rightly judged, locates the failure in individuals. On this account the play's world contains a sound standard of measure, stated by the Friar and embodied in patience, and Romeo's catastrophe is the wages of departing from it: the killing of Tybalt is a sin of suddenness, the suicide a sin of despair, each intelligible and each condemned. Dickey's reading has the merit of taking the Friar's doctrine seriously as doctrine rather than as decoration. Its difficulty is the cell scene itself. The play's appointed spokesman for measure cannot deliver his sermon without reaching for the shaming vocabulary of the square, "womanish," "beast," the waxen shape digressing from the valour of a man. If the standard of measure were as sound and as separate as the moral reading requires, its spokesman would not need to borrow the disease's language to administer the cure. The text, at the level of its dialogue, keeps contaminating the remedy with the malady, and a reading of the play must decide whether that contamination is an accident of rhetoric or the argument itself.

The editor's evidence, finally, is René Weis's Arden Third Series text, whose lineation the quotations in this article follow and whose notes document how much of the square scene's choreography the early texts leave to inference. The editorial contribution to the debate is sobriety about the performance itself: the stage directions for the fatal interposition are reconstructed, not given, and the scene's meaning in the theatre is therefore partly a decision each production makes about whose arm, whose body, whose shame the audience is watching at the fatal second. That uncertainty is not a defect in the evidence. It is the theme restated in the archive. A scene about a performance misread by its witnesses survives in texts that require every new witness to reconstruct the performance, and to take responsibility for the reconstruction.

### What does the play put in place of the sword as a measure of worth?

Endurance without an audience. The substitution is never announced, which is why it is missed, but it is distributed across the play's whole length: a wife's restraint of a husband in the first scene, a peacemaker's hands in the third act, a girl's solitary vial in the fourth, a bridegroom's courtesy to a corpse in the fifth.

Each instance is denied the code's rewards, unpraised, uncertified, sometimes unnoticed by any character on stage. The play measures worth by what survives the withdrawal of witnesses, and by that measure its violent men are bankrupt in exact proportion to their certificates, while its unexamined women and its one refusing man carry the only credit the ending honours, in gold statues raised too late to spend.

The adjudication among the four accounts can now be stated. Kahn is upheld as the governing explanation, because the rite-of-identity reading is the only one that predicts the play's distinctive details rather than merely accommodating them: the direction of the insults, the exclusion and taxation of women, the fathers' priesthood, the confessor's borrowed vocabulary, the graduate administering the examination in the churchyard. Girard is upheld in second place, as the correct account of the feud's mechanics between the houses, and set aside only where his mirror fails to register the gendered angle of the glass. Dickey is respected for the doctrine and overruled on the diagnosis: the rashness he condemns is real, but it is not a private departure from a public standard. It is the public standard, operating exactly as designed, in the soul of its most gifted student. Weis's textual caution is accepted as the method of the whole inquiry. The verdict that follows from the four, and from the scenes, is the brief's complication answered: the play condemns the code. It condemns it the way an audit condemns a firm, not by denouncing its prospectus but by publishing its accounts, in which every asset the code claims is entered, line by line, as a liability that Verona's children paid.

## Shame: The Enforcement Psychology of the Examination

Every system of control has an enforcement mechanism, and Verona's is shame, administered not by officials but by friends. The insight organises the whole play once it is stated. No character in Romeo and Juliet is ever forced into a duel by a superior's order. The Prince forbids fighting on pain of death. The fathers, when present, usually forbid it too. The law, the church, and the household all formally oppose the examination, and the examination proceeds anyway, invigilated by companions. Its sanctions are not legal but lexical: boy, villain, coward by implication, womanish, vile submission. Its rewards are equally lexical: brave Mercutio, the Prince of Cats, a name cleared. A system whose entire police force is vocabulary can be defeated only by a candidate willing to be misnamed, and the play contains exactly one such candidate, for exactly one afternoon. Romeo's distinction is not that he rejects violence in the abstract; the tomb will disprove that. It is that in the square, for the length of one scene, he accepts the misnaming. He hears himself called villain, answers with love, and lets the record stand. The cost arrives within minutes, invoiced through his friend, and the lesson the city draws from the afternoon is the one the code requires: refusal is survivable only by the man whose friends are willing to die of it.

Shame also explains the examination's strange temporal politics, its preference for immediate settlement. A reputation held in escrow by witnesses decays by the hour, because the gallery disperses and the story hardens in the telling. Tybalt cannot wait for a private occasion to answer the feast, because the feast's witnesses are already narrating his submission in every hall in Verona. Mercutio cannot let Romeo's courtesy stand unexamined until evening, because by evening the unexamined version will be the official one. The code therefore manufactures emergencies as a by-product of its bookkeeping: every deferral is a default, every default a confession. This is the psychology beneath the play's famous haste, and it assigns the haste a specific address. The lovers rush because they are young and watched by a ticking calendar. The men rush because their certificates expire at sundown. When the Friar counsels slowness he is, without knowing it, counselling his parishioners to hold an asset their whole economy treats as perishable, and their refusal to hold it is not folly but arithmetic performed in the only currency their city mints.

The gendered charge is shame's sharpest instrument because it threatens not merely status but category. To call a fighter a boy is to postpone his certificate. To call a peaceful man effeminate is to revoke the document's premise, to allege that the holder was never entitled to sit the examination at all. Romeo's self-diagnosis shows the instrument turned inward, and his grammar is precise about the direction of the threat: beauty has made him effeminate, as though manhood were a substance that female influence dilutes on contact. The play surrounds that grammar with refutations it never states. Juliet, in the same twenty-four hours, will display a fortitude under examination, her father's rage, the Nurse's defection, the vial's catalogue of terrors, that no male character is asked to match, and she will display it while being, in the code's taxonomy, the diluting agent incarnate. The taxonomy cannot survive its own play. What survives is the observation the play keeps staging and its characters keep missing: the qualities filed under womanish, tears, tenderness, reluctance to kill, are distributed in the text without regard to sex, and the catastrophe correlates not with their presence but with their suppression.

## A Generation Trained to Perform

The examination is not improvised by its candidates. It is taught, and the play is unusually explicit about the faculty. The full roster of the young men's generation, its casualties charted as a single class, belongs to the companion study of [Verona's young men as a generation bred to fight](/2023/05/19/verona-young-men-violence-masculinity/), and is compressed here to the training itself. Consider the curriculum a Veronese boy absorbs before he is old enough to be examined. He hears the servants of his house rehearse the syllabus in the street as comedy. He watches his father call for a sword against a neighbour and be restrained, laughing, by a wife, and files the laughter as part of the ceremony rather than as its cancellation. He attends feasts at which challenges are issued across the dinner table and managed, not repudiated, by the master of the house. He learns a foreign fencing literature, its terms of art, its first and second causes, as other boys learn a catechism, until the vocabulary of the duel is simply the vocabulary of educated self-respect. By the time Tybalt sends his written challenge to Romeo's father's house, every participant in the transaction, sender, bearer, recipient, seconds, witnesses, has been rehearsing its steps since childhood. The duel in the square shocks nobody on stage. Its only surprise is its result.

This training analysis settles a question that purely psychological readings leave open: why the code's victims defend it. Romeo does not experience the examination as an alien imposition because it was administered to him as love's other name, the approval of the companions among whom he grew up. Mercutio's esteem is the warmest social fact of Romeo's youth, and Mercutio's esteem is denominated in the code's currency. To refuse the examination is therefore not to defy an institution. It is to disappoint a friend, in public, in the friend's own idiom of care, and the play's cruellest structural decision is to make those two acts the same act. The marriage offers Romeo a competing economy of approval, one witness whose esteem is denominated in vows rather than in violence, and the square scene is the collision of the two economies at the moment of their maximum incompatibility: the wife is absent and cannot witness, the friend is present and cannot understand. A man graded by an absent examiner will always fail before a present one. The play's whole tragedy of masculinity is contained in that scheduling.

Kahn's rite-of-identity reading, given its fullest statement in the study of [Coppélia Kahn's coming of age in Verona](/2026/12/18/coppelia-kahn-coming-of-age-verona/), is the criticism that best names this training, because a rite is precisely a training that certifies: it takes a boy, subjects him to ordeals the community prescribes, and returns him to the community with a new status the community has agreed to recognise. The feud's violence, on the rite account, is not a breakdown of Verona's social order. It is the social order's graduation ceremony, held in the street because the street is where the community can watch. Read so, the ending's statues acquire a final irony the performance argument is bound to record. The fathers, at the tomb, certify their children at last, in gold, at monumental expense, before the whole assembled city. It is the only graduation the play's young people receive, and its condition, as with every certificate the city issues, is that the candidate be past profiting by it.

## Two Models in One Afternoon: The Formalist and the Bridegroom

The contrast between Romeo and Tybalt as rival models of Verona manhood has its own dedicated study, [Romeo against Tybalt as two models of manhood](/2023/03/31/romeo-vs-tybalt-masculinity/), and the present article compresses that contrast to the single axis its argument needs: the two men's opposite relations to performance itself. Tybalt is the code's sincere candidate. He believes the examination measures something real, prepares for it with a technician's devotion, and experiences its obligations as conscience. His tragedy, insofar as the play grants him one, is that sincerity is no protection. The code uses its believer as its instrument and discards him within the hour, mourned by his aunt as a loss to be avenged rather than as a warning to be read. Romeo is the code's reluctant candidate, who believes in nothing the examination measures and is destroyed by the unbelief's incompleteness: he will not perform on schedule, but he cannot yet let the record of non-performance stand. Between sincerity and reluctance the play finds no third position available to a man of their class and city. Benvolio gestures at one, peacekeeping as a practice rather than a performance, and the play's treatment of Benvolio is its quietest verdict. He intervenes in both street fights, tells the truth to the Prince, survives, and vanishes from the text without explanation or farewell. Verona has no further use for him, and neither, the structure admits, has the story. A city that cannot narrate its peacemaker has confessed the limits of its imagination more completely than any curse could confess them.

Juliet's judgment of the two models, delivered in the oxymoron storm of Act 3 Scene 2, is the play's own comparison and the only one conducted by a witness with standing in both economies. Learning that her husband of three hours has killed her cousin, she first reads Romeo through the code's antithetical grammar, beautiful tyrant, fiend angelical, honourable villain, and then corrects the reading by an act of loyalty that is also an act of reasoning: Tybalt would have killed Romeo; the survivor's hand is to be defended; her tears are reassigned from the husband's disgrace to a grief she can govern. The scene matters to the masculinity argument because it shows the examination's transcript being edited by its supposed prize. The woman for whose sake, in the code's mythology, men perform, declines the performance's account of events and substitutes her own. Her household will repay the edit with threats and a forced marriage. The play repays it with the ending's allocation of honour, in which the girl who edited the transcript is raised in gold beside the bridegroom, while the examination's certified men are carried off in the order of their graduation.

## The Alternative and the Final Accounting

What alternative, then, does the play offer? The honest answer begins by refusing exaggeration. No character articulates a rival doctrine of manhood and lives to exemplify it at length. The Friar's measure is real doctrine, and its spokesman cannot administer it without the enemy's vocabulary. Benvolio's practice is real, and the text lets him evaporate. Romeo's refusal is real, and it lasts one scene. Juliet's courage is real, and it is never named as a model for men at all. The alternative exists in the play the way a watermark exists in paper: visible only when the page is held against the light of the ending, and composed of the very substance of the sheet. Held so, the pattern is consistent enough to state. Worth, in this text, is what a person does when the gallery is absent, hostile, or deceived. It is the quality shown by the wife restraining the husband, by the peacemaker parting the fighters, by the bride drinking the vial, by the exile sparing, for one speech, the boy at the tomb. The play's bleakness is that it can imagine this worth only in fragments and in minor keys. Its honesty is that it never pretends the fragments were enough. An alternative that cannot organise a city's afternoons is a criticism, not a programme, and Shakespeare's play, on the evidence of its structure, offers criticism with its whole heart and a programme not at all.

The state's own grading, delivered in the square itself within minutes of the killings, shows how completely the examination has colonised even the law that formally prohibits it. The sentencing of Romeo is conducted as a contest of performances before the Prince: Lady Capulet pleading the code's tariff in the idiom of grief, Romeo must not live; Montague answering with the code's own doctrine of the completed satisfaction, that his son's fault concludes only what the law should have ended in Tybalt. Benvolio's narrative, the one factual account offered, is challenged not on its evidence but on its speaker's house, as though testimony too were a performance to be graded by livery. The Prince's sentence of exile is a compromise between the two performances rather than a judgment on the act, mercy calculated to offend neither gallery, and its inadequacy is structural. A court that grades by audience will always sentence the peacekeeper's afternoon and the avenger's minute as a single offence, because the distinction between them, the distinction on which the whole tragedy turns, is legible only to a bench willing to read intentions. Verona's bench reads standings. The exile that follows is therefore not the law interrupting the examination. It is the examination's result countersigned by the state, and Romeo's flight from the square is a candidate leaving the hall with his grade already posted, to be appealed in a cell, in the code's own language, by a friar who knows the examiners personally.

The final accounting belongs to the Prince, whose closing audit distributes the catastrophe among all the city's systems: heaven has found means to kill the fathers' joys with love, the Prince himself is punished for winking at the discords, and some shall be pardoned and some punished (5.3.291-307). Within that audit the masculinity account is the largest single ledger. The feud was the fathers' rite, administered by friends, examined in the street, certified by shame, and paid for by candidates in an order the play is careful to preserve: the improviser, the formalist, the obedient suitor, the reluctant bridegroom, and, in the vault's far corner, the girl the system never examined, whose death is the one entry in the books that no column of the manhood map can classify. A criticism that reads the play as a romance with unfortunate duels has the accounting backward. The duels are the system working. The romance is the interruption, three days long, in which two young people attempted to hold their worth in a currency Verona did not mint, and the attempt's failure is measured, to the last line, in the examination's own coin: in boys, in villains, in softened steel and waxen shapes, in a boy's demoting word spoken at last by the bridegroom himself, over a body, in the dark, where no gallery remained to applaud.

## Boy, Villain, Womanish: The Demoting Words

The examination's grades are delivered in a small vocabulary of demotion, and tracing that vocabulary across the play shows the system at work in its purest form, as language that wounds before any weapon is drawn. Boy is the elementary grade. Capulet applies it to Tybalt at the feast, in the hearing of the household, at the exact moment he overrules the young man's honour: the nephew who would strike a guest dead for the stock of his kin is addressed as a saucy boy and a princock, and sent to keep the peace like a child dismissed from the table (1.5.83-86). The demotion is the wound the square scene collects. Tybalt's patience speech, with its trembling flesh and its conversion of sweetness into gall, is the record of a candidate resolving that no elder, enemy, or occasion shall place him in the nursery again. When he meets Romeo in the square, his chosen instrument is the second word of the vocabulary. Villain, in the period's street usage, is not primarily a moral verdict. It is a status allegation, the claim that the man addressed is base-born, a serf, a person to whom the courtesies among gentlemen do not extend, and it functions in the challenge as a legal fiction: if the opponent is a villain, the obligation to answer is absolute, because the allegation denies the standing that courtesy protects. Romeo's reply, "Villain am I none," is therefore a precise denial of the status claim, grounded in a kinship he cannot exhibit. The square hears a paradox and grades it as fear. The audience, holding the marriage lines, hears the only accurate status report in the scene.

Womanish is the vocabulary's terminal grade, the demotion that alleges not immaturity or baseness but a wrong substance altogether. Its two great deployments frame the duel. In the square Romeo applies its cognate to himself, effeminate, in the metallurgical speech over Mercutio's body. In the cell the Friar applies the word itself to Romeo's tears, in a rebuke whose taxonomy runs man, woman, beast in descending order. Between the two deployments the word does the same work in opposite directions. In Romeo's mouth it is the code's verdict internalised, the candidate grading his own paper with the examiner's pen. In the Friar's mouth it is the code's verdict conscripted, the examiner's pen borrowed to write a pardon. The play's interest in the word is diagnostic throughout: womanish is never in this text a description of any woman's conduct. The women of Verona restrain, endure, reason, and dare. The word describes a man's departure from a standard that the play's women, by their conduct, expose as a fantasy about both sexes at once, a standard no man meets in the square and no woman is permitted to be measured by in the chamber.

The vocabulary's travels end in the churchyard, where the bridegroom, three days a widower in fact and an outlaw in law, addresses the last challenger with the elementary grade: "have at thee, boy." The line is usually passed over as the conventional heat of a stage fight. In the pattern this article has traced it is the examination's final certificate, issued by its most unwilling candidate to its most obedient one, and it certifies the issuer as completely as the examined. Romeo entered the play as the young man the code could not tempt out of his lovesick retirement, the absentee of the opening brawl. He leaves the churchyard scene as a man who has administered every grade in the demoting vocabulary, villain answered, submission recanted, effeminacy repented in steel, and boy bestowed. The Prince's officers, arriving with their lanterns, find the graduate beside three bodies. No scene in the play states the condemnation more economically, because none is needed. The vocabulary has completed its circuit, and the circuit is the verdict.

## Marriage: The Rival Rite the Square Refuses to Recognise

One further institution competes for the certification of Verona's young men, and the competition explains why the square scene, rather than any other, is where the tragedy turns. Marriage, in the world the play inhabits, is itself a rite of manhood, the recognised passage by which a youth becomes a householder, a governor of dependents, a man whose standing no longer requires proof by arms because it is anchored in establishment. Kahn's framework makes the rivalry exact. Verona offers its sons two graduations. The feud's rite certifies by violence, in the street, before companions. The marriage rite certifies by vow, in the church, before God and a friar, and issues a standing that is supposed to retire the graduate from the street's examinations altogether. Romeo, alone among the play's young men, attempts to transfer from the first rite to the second, and the square scene is the moment the first rite refuses to honour the transfer.

The Friar understands the marriage as a counter-examination with a civic purpose, and states the purpose at the moment of consent: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancour to pure love" (2.3.91-92). The hope is institutional, not sentimental. A Montague certified as a Capulet husband is a man the feud's machinery cannot process: his new kinship rewrites the obligations that the feud's rite exists to enforce. The wedding in the cell is therefore the play's one deliberate attempt to beat the examination system with a rival certificate, and its secrecy, necessary in the short term, is its structural weakness in the long. A rite that nobody witnesses issues a standing that nobody else is bound to recognise. The marriage certificate is genuine and unregistered, valid in the cell and void in the square, and Romeo's tragedy is that he must present it for payment in the one venue where its registration cannot be checked without being destroyed.

His conduct in the square is the conduct of a man acting on the new certificate in good faith. The sentence that carries the claim is easy to pass over, because it is phrased as a remark about recognition: "I see thou know'st me not" (3.1.61). The line asserts an identity change. The man Tybalt has challenged, on the file the feud keeps, no longer exists; a husband stands in his place, related to the challenger, holding obligations the challenger has not been told about. Everything Romeo says in the exchange is consistent with the new standing and inexplicable without it: the tenderness of the enemy's name, the protestation of uninjured love, the request to be satisfied, a term drawn from the duel's own jurisprudence and here offered as its replacement. He is not declining the examination out of fear of its questions. He is pleading a change of jurisdiction, and the court, consisting of one challenger collecting a debt and one friend guarding a reputation, declines to hear the plea because the plea's evidence is sealed.

The collision of the rites is timed with a cruelty that the calendar makes visible. The wedding and the duel fall on the same afternoon, and the proximity is not merely ironic decoration. It is the mechanism by which the feud's rite defends itself against its rival. A marriage given time would gather witnesses, registration, a household, a public standing that even the square would have to process. A marriage given three hours remains a rumour in one man's breast, and the examination can proceed against the bridegroom as though the rival ceremony had never occurred. Verona's haste, so often treated as a property of the lovers, is in this transaction a property of the system that destroys them: the feud examines on the wedding day because the wedding day is the last day on which the examination can still be held. After the marriage is published, Tybalt's challenge becomes an attack on a kinsman, Mercutio's invigilation becomes interference in a settlement, and the whole syllabus loses its candidate. The code is never more efficient than in its defence of its own monopoly, and never more efficient than on the afternoon it kills the alternative before the alternative can be announced.

Juliet is the rival rite's only full witness, and her conduct in Act 3 Scene 2 is the new certificate being honoured by the one person entitled to honour it. Faced with the report that her husband has killed her cousin, she performs the adjudication the square refused to perform: she weighs the husband's act against the cousin's intent, finds that Tybalt would have killed Romeo, and reassigns her loyalty on the evidence rather than on the house's instruction. The scene is the marriage rite's court in session, one judge, no gallery, a verdict reached by reasoning about conduct instead of by grading appearances. Set the two adjudications side by side and the play's argument about manhood completes itself. The masculine court, public, armed, and ancient, grades a refusal it cannot read and produces two corpses and an exile. The marriage's court, private, unarmed, and three hours old, reads a killing correctly and produces the play's only just verdict on the duel. Verona executes the first court's sentence and ignores the second's, and the ending, in which the fathers at last adopt the marriage's account of events over the tombs of both courts' candidates, is the city's confession that it hanged the wrong jurisdiction's man.

The golden statues are the rival rite's certificate issued at last, registered in the most public medium Verona commands, at a price no square performance ever commanded. Registration arrives five days after the examination it should have prevented, and the delay is the whole argument in a single interval. A city that can certify its bridegroom only in gold, only posthumously, only after its other rite has examined him to death, has not reformed its system of manhood. It has merely published, in its costliest material, the result of running both systems to their conclusions and preferring, too late, the one that required no blood for its seal.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch manhood map, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch manhood map is the table in this article that logs each moment where manhood is equated with fighting, the form the equation takes, and the cost the play exacts for it. The pattern the completed rows expose is distributional: the price of each performance is never paid by the institution that set the examination. Servants boast and citizens brawl; nephews open debts of honour and friends settle them in blood; fathers schedule the rite and sons sit it in the square and the churchyard. The map's final row is an absence, Juliet's potion night, where no equation is offered at all. That empty row is the pattern's sharpest entry, since the bravest act in the play is the one its grading language cannot record.

### Q: Why does the play open its account of manhood with servants rather than with gentlemen?

The opening altitude is the argument in miniature. Sampson and Gregory, who own nothing in the feud beyond their livery, rehearse the complete syllabus of violent manhood, territory, women demoted to the bottom rank, flesh exhibited as a credential, before any gentleman appears. Beginning below stairs proves that the examination is not a refinement of the great houses but the city's common property, manufactured in banter between companions. The scene also establishes the system's upward contagion: a servants' boast draws in a peacemaker, a duellist, two masters, the citizenry, and finally the Prince within a single scene. Gentlemen refine the apparatus with better grammar and costlier clothes across the following acts. They add no new component to it, and the play's first scene is the receipt proving the design was complete before they arrived.

### Q: Why must manhood in Verona be proved before witnesses rather than in private?

Because a Veronese reputation is not owned but rented, held in escrow by the companions who watch, and a proof nobody witnesses is, in the code's bookkeeping, a proof that never happened. The gallery converts conduct into standing; without the gallery, the same act earns nothing and answers nothing. Hence the challenges arrive at ruinous public hours, at a feast, in a market square on a wedding afternoon, beside a tomb with a page nearby to carry the report. Privacy is the one condition the examination cannot process, which is why Romeo's secret marriage is so destructive to his standing: his true reason for refusing Tybalt is genuine, honourable, and completely unstateable before the only audience empowered to grade the refusal.

### Q: How do the servants hand their masters a script that the masters then perform in the same scene?

The transfer happens through licensing. Sampson and Gregory open with a rehearsed fiction of themselves as tyrants and men of flesh, and the fiction, once spoken aloud in the street, creates the expectation that the household's men will honour it at every rank. Benvolio enters to part the quarrel and is immediately cast as an opponent; Tybalt enters and promotes the brawl into the idiom of honour; the two masters then arrive calling for swords against each other, performing, in their gowns and their age, the exact scene their servants had sketched in prose minutes earlier. The masters believe the performance originates with them. The scene's structure records the opposite: the script travelled upward, from livery to lordship, because a code recognised at every altitude obliges every altitude to keep it in repertory.

### Q: What does Lady Capulet's crutch joke reveal about the fathers' performance of manhood?

The joke is the scene's most efficient piece of criticism. Capulet calls for his long sword against old Montague and experiences the gesture as a defence of his house. His wife answers, "A crutch, a crutch! Why call you for a sword?" (1.1.77), and measures in one line the distance between the performer's self-image and the audience's reading. The fathers still insist on their lines in a play their bodies have left, because the feud's senior men are its memory, and the rite survives only while its oldest actors keep certifying it. The joke also marks the wives' assigned role in the system: governance without credit, restraint treated as an interruption, while the competition they interrupt is praised as the household's real business and inherited as such by the sons.

### Q: How does the feast leave Tybalt with a debt of honour that the square scene collects?

At the feast Tybalt's humiliation is not prevention from fighting but the manner of it: overruled by his own uncle, before his own household, in tones that address him as a saucy boy, for recognising an enemy the master chose to endure. The code offers no procedure for satisfaction against the head of one's own house, so the debt cannot be paid in kind and must be discharged in substitute coin. Tybalt's exit speech keeps the ledger openly, promising that the intrusion, seeming sweet, shall convert to bitterest gall (1.5.88-91). The square scene is the collection date. Romeo is the nearest Montague asset, Mercutio becomes the interest, and the arrears opened at a dinner table are settled, with the whole city watching, in the only currency the ledger accepts.

### Q: How do Mercutio's wit and Tybalt's formality function as rival styles of manhood?

The two styles are competing certificates from one school. Tybalt's manhood is formal: fencing by the book of arithmetic, time, distance, and proportion kept like music, honour administered through correct procedure. Mercutio's is improvisational: the bout won by brilliance, every encounter treated as verbal fencing in which desire, grief, and danger are all to be answered with a riposte, as in his doctrine that rough love should be answered roughly (1.4.27-28). The rivalry matters because it disguises the institution's unity. Each style advertises itself as the other's opposite, so a candidate can change styles and believe the school has been escaped. The square scene collects both certificates in one afternoon, the improviser first and the formalist minutes later, and the order is the play's comment on the rivalry's real significance.

### Q: Why does Mercutio treat Romeo's courtesy in the square as a performance failure rather than a moral choice?

Because Mercutio grades with the only mark book he owns, in which a refusal performed before witnesses is entered under submission regardless of its motive. His cry of "calm, dishonourable, vile submission" (3.1.69-71) is a transcript, not an insult improvised in anger: calm records the temperament, dishonourable the verdict, vile the sentence. A moral choice would require the examiner to weigh intentions, and intentions are precisely what the examination cannot process, since its whole authority rests on legibility at a glance. Friendship sharpens the failure. Mercutio experiences Romeo's courtesy as a stain on the company's shared credit, a default by the member whose standing the group holds jointly, and his intervention, sitting the paper on his friend's behalf, is the code's ideal of loyalty executing its most expensive sentence.

### Q: Why does the play deny Romeo a witness who can understand his refusal to fight?

The denial is structural, and it is the marriage's price. The one fact that would convert Romeo's courtesy from submission into policy, his kinship with Tybalt through Juliet, is the one fact the secret wedding requires him to conceal. His potential witnesses are therefore all disabled: Mercutio and Benvolio lack the fact, the Friar is absent, Juliet is in her chamber, and Tybalt cannot be told without the marriage being published to the enemy it most concerns. Shakespeare arranges the square so that the truth is present in the scene, carried in one man's breast, and inadmissible as evidence. The refusal must be graded on its appearance alone. That arrangement, not any flaw in Romeo's nerve, is what makes the scene a tragedy of manhood rather than a misunderstanding that better dialogue could have repaired.

### Q: What work does the image of softened steel do in Romeo's account of himself after Mercutio falls?

The image converts a moral event into an industrial defect. Steel holds its virtue by tempering, a controlled sequence of heat and cold that leaves the blade hard enough to keep an edge. Romeo's sentence, that Juliet's beauty "hath made me effeminate / And in my temper softened valour's steel" (3.1.109-111), imagines love as a botched reheating and the self as an instrument manufactured for a single purpose, now unfit for it. The metaphor matters because it locates the damage in equipment rather than in choice, and a man who believes his equipment is damaged reaches for the nearest repair, which Verona stocks in one model only. The play invites the audience to notice the misdirection: the conduct being captioned as softness was kinship offered, law cited, and a body interposed between armed men, the afternoon's bravest sequence by any neutral description.

### Q: How does killing Tybalt restore Romeo's credit among men while destroying everything his marriage built?

The restoration and the ruin are the same entry posted to two ledgers. In the company's ledger, the revenge answers the submission charge in the exact wording the charge was issued, returns villain for villain, and escorts a dead friend's soul with the escort the code prescribes; by sundown Romeo's standing among men is not merely repaired but enhanced. In the marriage's ledger, the identical act is a catastrophe with a schedule: the bride a widow in effect before her wedding night, the groom exiled by the sentence the killing made necessary, the secret that might have reconciled the houses now a liability that must be hidden from a grieving Capulet household demanding the killer's death. The play's accounting refuses to separate the parcels. Credit among men is the invoice; the marriage is the payment, and the exchange rate was fixed by the code long before Romeo drew.

### Q: Why does the Friar use the language of manhood to shame Romeo out of suicide in the cell?

Because it is the only language the patient is guaranteed to obey. The Friar's own doctrine, measure and patience, has no hold on a young man lying on the floor offering to tear his name from his body; what holds is the certificate. So the confessor reaches for the square's vocabulary: "Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art; / Thy tears are womanish" (3.3.109-113), with the waxen shape digressing from the valour of a man to follow (3.3.126-127). The conscription works, and its working is the datum: even Verona's counter-institution counsels in the code's currency when the stakes are a life. The scene's discomfort is deliberate. A remedy administered in the disease's idiom may save the patient and refinance the disease in the same motion, and the play's ending, in which shame's cousins reclaim Romeo at the tomb, is the bill for the loan.

### Q: What alternative measure of a man does the Friar offer, and why can he not sustain it himself?

The offered measure is endurance ordered toward reconciliation: live, visit Juliet, flee to Mantua, wait while the marriage is published and the Prince's pardon sought, a programme in which worth consists of patience under sentence rather than satisfaction in the street. As a standard it is complete, humane, and stated with a planner's specificity. The failure is not in the programme but in its spokesman's instruments and nerve. The Friar argues for the measure in the code's shaming vocabulary, entrusts its crucial message to a single messenger, and, at the tomb, flees the watch and leaves Juliet alone with the consequences. His doctrine describes a man he cannot himself remain under pressure. The play's verdict is not contempt; it is structural. An alternative that its own best spokesman administers in borrowed language and abandons at the crisis remains a criticism of the code, not a replacement for it.

### Q: Why are the labels of the code applied to Romeo's tears but never to Juliet's resolve?

The asymmetry exposes the labels as instruments rather than descriptions. Womanish is applied where the system needs a lever: to a man's grief that threatens his usefulness to the group, in the square by implication and in the cell by name. Juliet's resolve offers the system no purchase for the lever. Her fortitude is exercised in private, in a register the code does not monitor, toward ends the household believes it has authorised, a wedding, and so it passes unlabeled until it is too late to grade. The deeper point is that the labels were never reports on conduct at all. Juliet weeping would not have been called womanish either, since the word's function is not to describe tears but to discipline men. Verona's vocabulary of praise and blame is a tool kit for managing male candidates, and its silence about the play's bravest person is the confession of its purpose.

### Q: Why must Lady Capulet's vengeance travel by poison and petition rather than by the sword?

Exclusion restricts instruments, not appetites. Barred from candidacy in the square, Lady Capulet practises the code's jurisprudence through the channels left to her: first petition, demanding of the Prince that Romeo, having slain Tybalt, must not live; then, when exile disappoints the petition, procurement, promising Juliet that a man in Mantua shall give the banished runagate "such an unaccustomed dram / That he shall soon keep Tybalt company" (3.5.88-91). Poison is the duel translated for the unarmed, private where the duel is public, deniable where the duel is certified. The play's coldest effect is the setting: the contract is offered as a mother's consolation, in a speech meant to dry a daughter's tears. The code has so completely colonised the household that grief itself is expressed as an assassination arranged by post.

### Q: How does Coppélia Kahn's rite of identity redistribute blame for the duel scene?

Kahn's reading moves the blame upward, from candidates to officiants. If the feud is a rite by which sons are certified as men, then the square scene is not an outbreak of individual tempers but a scheduled ceremony going wrong in its usual way. Tybalt is administering an examination his elders designed and his uncle's feast had made unavoidable; Mercutio is invigilating by the standards his whole generation was taught; Romeo is a candidate whose private exemption no officiant had authorised. Blame, redistributed on this account, settles on the rite's priesthood, the fathers who kept the ceremony in the calendar, and on the institution that made certification by violence the price of adult standing. Individual fault does not vanish. Romeo's drawn sword remains his. But the reading insists that a city which trains its sons to kill for their certificates has limited standing to be surprised by the graduation.

### Q: Where does Girard's mirrored rivals account fail to explain the square's gendered insults?

Girard explains the feud's symmetry: matched houses, reciprocal insults, violence imitated until the rivals are doubles and no originating cause survives. The failure appears at the level of direction. The square's decisive charge is not that Romeo has imitated Tybalt but that he has behaved like a woman, and the charge travels one way only, downward onto the peaceful. Nobody calls Tybalt womanish for excess of zeal, or the fathers beastly in the same register, or the Prince's lateness a failure of his sex. A mirror cannot explain its own angle. The insults presuppose a hierarchy the reciprocity model does not contain, in which manhood is a status that peace forfeits and violence confirms, and in which women figure as the negative term against which the status is defined. Kahn's rite supplies the hierarchy; Girard's mirror supplies the mechanics; the scene requires both, in that order.

### Q: Why does Romeo address Paris with the demoting word his own enemy used on him?

The transfer completes the examination's circuit. Boy was the grade Capulet issued to Tybalt at the feast and the status Romeo's whole conduct in the square was organised to escape; in the churchyard, warned, begged, and finally provoked, Romeo issues it himself: "Wilt thou provoke me? Then have at thee, boy!" (5.3.70). The line is short because the training is complete. The graduate now administers the examination in the dark, for no gallery, to a candidate whose only offence was correctness, and the play lets no one on stage remark the inheritance. The word's journey is the theme in miniature. A vocabulary of demotion, learned under shame, is discharged under provocation by its former victim, and the system's reproduction is accomplished not by its believers but by the man who had once, for one afternoon, declined to sit its paper.

### Q: Can a production present Romeo's refusal as heroic without altering the text, and which staging decision carries it?

It can, and the decision is the management of sightlines in the square. The text supplies a refusal grounded in kinship, law, and a tenderness the challenger cannot know about; whether an audience reads that refusal as courage depends on whether the production lets the house hold the script the square lacks. A staging that keeps Juliet present in the audience's mind at the refusal's moment, through the morning's wedding imagery still warm in the design, through Romeo's stillness held a beat longer than the code allows, grades the courtesy before Mercutio can grade it aloud. The counterweight must also be honoured: the same production has to let Mercutio's charge land with its full social truth, or the tragedy dissolves into a compliment. Heroism in this scene is not an acting note. It is an information policy, deciding which witness the production trusts, the gallery on stage or the house that knows about the wedding.

### Q: How can an essay argue that Romeo passes Verona's examination while failing the play's?

The essay's spine is the double ledger this article has used throughout. Begin with the square, where the same conduct earns opposite grades from the two benches: submission from the gallery, and from the audience, holding the marriage lines, the feud's only credible cure. Follow the grades into consequence, showing that every later improvement in Romeo's Verona standing, the revenge that clears his name, the graduation he administers at the tomb, is purchased with a deterioration in the standing the play itself tracks, husband, bridegroom, living man. Close with the sentencing and the statues, where the city's final certificates are issued in gold to the dead. The argumentative gain of the frame is precision: instead of asking whether Romeo is brave, a question the text refuses to stabilise, the essay asks whose examination is being sat in each scene, and lets the body count mark the papers.
