## What the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act Actually Does

The Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act is a short federal statute with a long definitional core. Congress enacted the measure as S. 397 of the 109th Congress, and President George W. Bush signed the bill on October 26, 2005. The enactment became Public Law 109-92 and was codified at sections 7901 to 7903 of title 15 of the United States Code. Section 7901 states the findings and purposes of Congress. Section 7902 states the prohibition and the treatment of actions pending at enactment. Section 7903 supplies the definitions that give the prohibition its reach. The same public law also amended sections 921, 922, and 924 of title 18 and contained the Child Safety Lock Act of 2005.

![Organizing statute notes and case citations for the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-95.webp)

Section 7902(a) provides that a qualified civil liability action may not be brought in any Federal or State court. Section 7903(5)(A) defines that category as a civil action or proceeding or an administrative proceeding brought by any person, including a governmental entity, against a manufacturer or seller of a qualified product, or a trade association, for damages, punitive damages, injunctive or declaratory relief, abatement, restitution, fines, or penalties, or other relief, resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the person or a third party. The category is defined by the plaintiff, the defendant, the product, the relief sought, and the causal source of the harm alleged. Section 7902(b) adds the direction for actions already on file at passage, providing that a qualified civil liability action pending on October 26, 2005 shall be immediately dismissed by the court in which the action was brought or is pending.

In public debate the act is sometimes described as blanket immunity. That description does not match the enacted text. The definition in section 7903(5)(A) states that the defined term does not include six categories of action. The statute expressly preserves an action for death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture under the conditions clause (v) states, an action for breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase of the product under clause (iv), an action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se under clause (ii), and an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a State or Federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm under clause (iii). Clauses (i) and (vi) complete the set, covering a convicted transferor action and Attorney General enforcement of chapter 44 of title 18 or chapter 53 of title 26. Six exceptions, stated inside the definition itself, mark the boundary of the bar.

Section 7901 frames the prohibition through attributed findings and purposes. The findings state that lawsuits had been commenced against manufacturers, distributors, dealers, and importers of firearms that operate as designed and intended, seeking relief for harm caused by the misuse of firearms by third parties, including criminals. The purposes stated in section 7901(b) include prohibiting causes of action for harm solely caused by the criminal or unlawful misuse of products by others when the product functioned as designed and intended, and preserving a citizen's access to a supply of firearms and ammunition for all lawful purposes.

The rule of construction at section 7903(5)(C) provides that the exceptions in clauses (i) through (v) are to be construed so as not to be in conflict, and that nothing in the chapter creates a public or private cause of action or remedy. An excluded category therefore keeps a claim supplied by another body of law available, while the federal statute supplies no claim of its own. A plaintiff must plead the elements of the preserved claim under the governing law and must also bring the action within the words of the relevant clause.

The sections that follow read the statute in the order its litigation unfolded. The definitions come first, because the elements of a qualified civil liability action and the six exclusions decide the threshold question in case after case. The municipal lawsuits come next, followed by the appellate record of the first wave, passage through two Congresses, and a clause by clause account of the six exceptions. The predicate exception receives separate treatment, because the dispute over which statutes count as applicable to the sale or marketing of a product generated the largest body of appellate law under the act. The arguments are stated through the named holders of each position, a comparison places the instrument beside other federal liability limits, and the closing section separates what the text and the decisions through May 15, 2021 settled from what that record left open.

## The Definitions That Decide Every Case

The Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act, Public Law 109-92, was signed on October 26, 2005, by President George W. Bush and was codified at sections 7901 to 7903 of title 15 of the United States Code. Section 7901 states findings and purposes. Section 7902 states the prohibition. Section 7903 supplies definitions. The prohibition itself occupies two short sentences, and the definitions occupy far more text, and that distribution of length reflects the distribution of work in the federal statute. A court asked to apply the act reads the prohibition quickly and then spends the substance of the analysis on the defined terms, because the prohibition has no independent reach beyond the category that the definitions create.

Section 7902(a) provides that a qualified civil liability action may not be brought in any Federal or State court. Section 7902(b) provides that a qualified civil liability action pending on October 26, 2005, shall be immediately dismissed by the court in which the action was brought or is pending. The two provisions address two postures. The first provision bars the filing of a new action that falls within the defined category. The second provision reaches backward to actions already on a docket on the date of enactment and directs dismissal rather than allowing the action to proceed under the law in place at filing. Neither provision names a type of claim, a theory of liability, or a body of State law. Each provision instead incorporates a single defined term, and the scope of both provisions rises and falls with the scope of that term.

For that reason, litigation under the 2005 act has a characteristic shape. A defendant manufacturer, seller, or trade association invokes section 7902 and argues that the complaint describes a qualified civil liability action. A plaintiff responds that an element of the definition is absent, or that the complaint falls within one of the actions the definition expressly excludes. The merits of the underlying claim, whether the defendant acted reasonably, whether a distribution practice was careful, and whether harm was foreseeable, enter the analysis only after the categorical question has been answered, and in many cases the categorical question ends the action before those merits questions are reached. The definition, rather than the ban sentence in section 7902, is where PLCAA does its operative work, and a close reading of the definition element by element explains the structure of nearly every dispute decided under the statute.

### What is a qualified civil liability action?

A qualified civil liability action is a civil or administrative proceeding brought by any person, including a governmental entity, against a manufacturer, seller, or trade association, seeking damages or other listed relief for harm resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the plaintiff or a third party.

The definition appears at section 7903(5)(A), and each phrase in the definition performs a separate screening function. The definition identifies the kind of proceeding covered, the class of plaintiffs whose actions are covered, the class of defendants against whom a covered action is brought, the forms of relief that bring a proceeding within the category, and the causal connection between the relief sought and the misuse of a product. A proceeding that satisfies every element is a qualified civil liability action and is subject to the bar in section 7902(a) and, for actions pending on the enactment date, to the dismissal command in section 7902(b). A proceeding that fails any one element falls outside the category, and section 7902 does not reach the proceeding on that ground, although a separate question remains whether the definition expressly excludes the proceeding even when the elements appear to be satisfied.

The opening words of the definition cover a civil action or proceeding or an administrative proceeding. Coverage therefore extends beyond a complaint filed in a trial court seeking a judgment, and a plaintiff cannot avoid the bar by changing the forum or the procedural label of the proceeding. Section 7902(a) reinforces that breadth by stating that a qualified civil liability action may not be brought in any Federal or State court, language that closes both court systems to a covered filing rather than displacing the action from one system to the other. A State court confronted with a covered action applies the federal definition and the federal dismissal direction.

The plaintiff element is expressed in the broadest available term. A qualified civil liability action is an action brought by any person, and section 7903(3) provides that person includes any governmental entity. A city, a county, a State, or another governmental body suing as a plaintiff is therefore inside the definition on the plaintiff side, in the same position as a private individual or an organization. Governmental plaintiffs had brought actions against manufacturers, distributors, dealers, and trade associations seeking damages and injunctive relief for harms attributed to third party misuse, and under the enacted text the governmental character of a plaintiff supplies no exit from the definition. The analysis turns on the defendant, the relief, and the causal element, not on whether the plaintiff is public or private.

The defendant element has three parts, and each part has a separate definition elsewhere in section 7903. The action must be brought against a manufacturer or seller of a qualified product, or against a trade association. An action against a defendant who is none of those three does not satisfy the definition, however the claim is framed. An action against a defendant who fits one of the three categories satisfies the element even if the theory concerns conduct far removed from a sale to the person who misused the product, such as a theory about distribution or marketing practices at a national level. The definition asks who the defendant is under the statutory definitions, not how close the defendant stood to the eventual misuse.

The relief element is stated as a long list, and the length of the list carries meaning. The definition covers an action for damages and for punitive damages. The definition also covers an action for injunctive or declaratory relief, for abatement, for restitution, for fines, or for penalties, and the definition closes with a catchall covering other relief. A plaintiff therefore cannot place an action outside the category by abandoning a damages demand and asking only for an injunction restructuring distribution, or by framing the recovery as restitution or abatement. Governmental actions seeking the public costs of services and orders changing industry practice, and private actions seeking compensation, are measured against the same relief list.

The causal element completes the definition and supplies the element most closely tied to the findings in section 7901. The action must be one for relief resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the person or a third party. Three features of that phrase deserve attention. The misuse may be committed by the plaintiff or by a third party, so the definition expressly reaches the characteristic case in which the person who misused the product is someone other than the plaintiff and someone other than the defendant. The misuse must be criminal or unlawful, a qualification addressed further in the definition of unlawful misuse. And the relief sought must result from that misuse, which ties the covered category to harm flowing from misuse rather than to harm flowing from the product in ordinary use. An action alleging that a product injured a user because the product malfunctioned presents a different causal picture from an action alleging that a third party used a functioning product to commit a crime, and the definition draws the boundary between those pictures at this element.

Section 7903(9) defines unlawful misuse as conduct that violates a statute, ordinance, or regulation as it relates to the use of a qualified product. The definition is conduct based and law based. Conduct qualifies as unlawful misuse because the conduct violates an identified legal provision governing the use of the product, not because the conduct is considered wrongful in a general sense. Criminal misuse and unlawful misuse overlap in many settings, since criminal conduct ordinarily violates a statute, and the statute lists the two together in the causal element. Read together, the two terms cover misuse that constitutes a criminal offense and misuse that violates a statute, ordinance, or regulation relating to use without necessarily being pleaded as a crime. The definition of unlawful misuse also confines the causal element to misuse connected to law governing use of the product, and disputes over the causal element frequently reduce to whether the conduct alleged is misuse of that character and whether the relief sought results from that conduct within the meaning of section 7903(5)(A).

The product at the center of every element is itself a defined term. Section 7903(4) provides that qualified product means a firearm as defined in section 921(a)(3)(A) or (B) of title 18, including any antique firearm as defined in section 921(a)(16) of title 18, or ammunition as defined in section 921(a)(17)(A) of title 18, or a component part of a firearm or ammunition, that has been shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. The definition incorporates the title 18 definitions by reference rather than restating them, so the meaning of firearm and ammunition under the Gun Control Act framework supplies the meaning under PLCAA. The express inclusion of antique firearms removes any doubt about that subcategory. The express inclusion of component parts extends the product element beyond a complete firearm or a complete round of ammunition to a part of either. The closing clause adds a commerce element: the firearm, ammunition, or component part must have been shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. A product that satisfies the title 18 definition but lacks that shipment element does not satisfy the definition of qualified product, and an action concerning such a product fails the definition on that ground.

The manufacturer definition at section 7903(2) combines an activity element with a licensing element. A manufacturer is a person engaged in the business of manufacturing the product in interstate or foreign commerce and licensed as a manufacturer under chapter 44 of title 18. Both conditions matter. A person who manufactures a qualified product without the required federal license does not fit the definition as written, and a person who holds a license but is not engaged in the business of manufacturing the product in interstate or foreign commerce likewise falls outside the definition. The licensing element ties the protected defendant category to the federal regulatory system for firearms commerce, under which manufacturers operate under federal licenses and under the recordkeeping and transfer rules of chapter 44. The definition therefore protects a defined and federally licensed segment of the industry against the defined category of action, rather than extending the bar to any person who has at any point made a firearm or a component.

The seller definition at section 7903(6) is more elaborate, because sellers participate in firearms commerce in several distinct capacities. The first branch covers a licensed importer engaged in the business as such an importer. The second branch covers a licensed dealer engaged in the business as such a dealer. The third branch covers a person engaged in the business of selling ammunition in interstate or foreign commerce at the wholesale or retail level. The first two branches turn on the federal license held and on engagement in the business in that licensed capacity. The third branch is framed by activity and commerce, and reaches wholesale and retail ammunition selling alike. An occasional or isolated sale by a person not engaged in the business does not, without more, make that person a seller within the meaning of the act.

Section 7903(1) supplies the meaning of the recurring phrase engaged in the business. The phrase has the meaning given in section 921(a)(21) of title 18, with a specified livelihood and profit meaning for ammunition sellers. Incorporation of the title 18 meaning aligns the seller and manufacturer definitions with the established federal vocabulary for distinguishing commercial activity in firearms from occasional activity by private persons. For ammunition sellers, the specified meaning centers on a person who devotes time, attention, and labor to selling ammunition as a regular course of trade or business with the principal objective of livelihood and profit through the sale of ammunition in interstate or foreign commerce. A complaint against a defendant at the margin of commercial activity therefore presents a definitional question that must be resolved before section 7902 can be applied.

The third defendant category, trade association, is defined at section 7903(8). A trade association is an organization described in section 501(c)(6) of title 26, not organized for profit, two or more of whose members are manufacturers or sellers. The definition combines a tax status element, a nonprofit element, and a membership element, and an organization lacking any one element falls outside the definition. Actions against industry organizations had formed part of the litigation that preceded enactment, and where an organization satisfies all three elements, the defendant element is met and the analysis proceeds to relief, causation, and the excluded actions in the same sequence as an action against a manufacturer or a seller.

Reading the elements together shows why the definition decides outcomes. Each element is a gate, and the gates operate in sequence. When every gate is passed, the action is a qualified civil liability action, subject to the bar on bringing the action and, for actions pending on October 26, 2005, subject to immediate dismissal. When any gate is not passed, the action stands outside the defined category, and the plaintiff may pursue the claim under otherwise applicable law, subject to any other defenses that law supplies.

A second layer of the definition then becomes relevant for actions that appear to pass every gate. Section 7903(5)(A) states that the term does not include six categories of action, identified in clauses (i) through (vi). Those excluded categories include an action against a transferor convicted of a knowing transfer to a prohibited person, an action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se, an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a State or Federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm, an action for breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase of the product, an action for death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture under stated conditions, and an action or proceeding commenced by the Attorney General to enforce chapter 44 of title 18 or chapter 53 of title 26. The rule of construction at section 7903(5)(C) adds that the exceptions in clauses (i) through (v) are to be construed so as not to be in conflict, and that nothing in the chapter creates a public or private cause of action or remedy. The excluded categories therefore preserve specified claims rather than granting new claims, and a plaintiff invoking an excluded category must plead and establish a claim that otherwise exists under applicable law.

The placement of the excluded categories inside the definition, rather than in a separate defenses provision, has a procedural consequence that explains the pleading stage character of litigation under the act. Because the exclusions define what a qualified civil liability action is not, a court deciding whether section 7902 applies asks whether the complaint, on the allegations presented, describes an action inside the definition and outside every exclusion, or an action outside the definition or inside an exclusion. That question can ordinarily be answered from the complaint and the statutory text, without testimony, without expert evidence, and without the exchange of documents that discovery entails. A defendant raises the bar by motion addressed to the pleading, the plaintiff responds by identifying a missing element or an applicable exclusion, and the court resolves the categorical question as a matter of statutory interpretation applied to the allegations. If the court concludes that the action is a qualified civil liability action, section 7902 directs dismissal, and the underlying allegations are not tested through discovery or trial.

That sequencing is the practical core of the federal statute. A definition driven dismissal decides the case at the threshold, before the cost and burden of discovery, and the decision rests on the fit between the pleaded claim and the statutory categories rather than on a weighing of evidence. State law supplies the underlying claim, while federal law supplies the categorical screen. An exclusion is not an affirmative excuse for conduct that has been proved; an exclusion is a boundary of the definition itself, and a complaint drafted to allege the elements of an exclusion, such as a knowing violation of a statute applicable to sale or marketing with proximate causation, presents a different categorical case from a complaint alleging only that a manufacturer distributed a lawful product that a third party later misused.

The definitions also explain the limits of the bar in textual terms. PLCAA does not state that manufacturers, sellers, and trade associations are free from liability, and the act does not repeal the State and Federal law governing sales, records, transfers, product defect, contract, or warranty. The act states that one defined category of action may not be brought, defines that category by plaintiff, defendant, product, relief, and causation, and then carves six categories out of the definition. The contests in litigation recur at the same points: whether a defendant is engaged in the business under the incorporated title 18 meaning; whether a component part that moved in interstate commerce is a qualified product; whether relief framed as abatement or restitution falls within the relief list; whether the harm resulted from criminal or unlawful misuse by a third party; and whether a pleaded statutory violation brings the action within the exclusion for knowing violations. Resolution of those contests, under the text Congress enacted in 2005, determines whether an action proceeds or ends at the pleading stage.

## The Municipal Lawsuits That Moved Congress

The governmental litigation that preceded the federal statute began as a municipal campaign. One city filed first, another followed within weeks on a different theory, and other cities and counties then filed actions borrowing arguments from the cases before them. The complaints differed in defendants, doctrines, and relief. The municipal plaintiffs shared one central contention, however. In their account, lawful manufacture and sale could not be separated from the distribution system through which firearms moved, and a system that foreseeably supplied unlawful secondary markets imposed recoverable public costs and justified injunctions against the practices that sustained it.

The first filing came in October 1998, when the City of New Orleans sued manufacturers, distributors, dealers, and trade associations in Louisiana state court in Morial v. Smith & Wesson Corp. The Center to Prevent Handgun Violence served as co-counsel to New Orleans. The lead theory, as the municipal plaintiffs and their counsel framed it, attacked product design. The complaint presented the failure to incorporate safety features, including authorized user technology, as a defect and nuisance theory aimed at changing design through litigation. Authorized user technology meant features intended to limit use of a firearm to a person authorized to use it. The claim did not allege that a particular firearm had malfunctioned in ordinary operation. The plaintiffs argued instead that a design without the identified safety features was itself legally deficient.

Chicago filed in November 1998 and placed public nuisance at the center of its action. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the complaint named 22 manufacturers, 12 gun stores, and 4 distributors. Manufacturers stood at the point of production, and distributors and stores stood at successive points in commerce. The complaint treated the connections among those defendants as part of the condition the city sought to abate. The municipal plaintiffs argued that distribution practices supplied an illegal secondary market. The alleged wrong was not confined to a retail transaction completed in violation of law. The plaintiffs contended that practices higher in the chain made unlawful acquisition foreseeable and recurring, and that the resulting condition burdened the city in its public functions. New Orleans had led with design. Chicago led with nuisance and distribution. Later complaints combined the two templates in varying proportions.

By early 2000 approximately thirty cities and counties had filed actions, on contemporary official surveys, with later counts running above thirty municipalities. A single complaint could be treated as a local experiment in tort doctrine. Thirty complaints in different courts under different bodies of State law presented parallel exposure and a recurring institutional question. The question was whether product and distribution policy for a nationally distributed article would be set through municipal lawsuits or through legislation of general application. In 2000 New York City filed its action in federal court. The State of New York, through its Attorney General, also sued in 2000. An attorney general filing differed from a municipal filing because the plaintiff was the State itself, rather than a city or county acting under municipal authority. That difference mattered when State legislatures later reserved such actions to the State, because a reservation closed the municipal route without eliminating litigation by the State in its own name.

The pleaded theories allocated responsibility differently. Public nuisance treated distribution as a condition interfering with public interests. Negligence treated the same practices as a failure of reasonable care. Negligent entrustment focused on supplying a product to a person likely to use it in a manner involving unreasonable risk. Design defect and failure to warn returned attention to the product and to information supplied with it. Unjust enrichment asked whether defendants had retained a benefit at public expense. Consumer protection counts placed marketing and sales conduct under statutes governing trade practices. Pleaded in combination, the theories meant that rejection of one claim did not necessarily dispose of a complaint.

Public nuisance supplied the broadest frame. The Chicago theory described a condition, rather than a single act, as the source of harm. The municipal plaintiffs identified that condition as a distribution system channeling firearms into an illegal secondary market. They argued that manufacturers and distributors knew or could learn, from firearms traced after criminal use, that particular channels and dealers accounted for a disproportionate movement of firearms into unlawful possession. Continuation of established practices without tighter controls, the plaintiffs contended, maintained the condition. The theory connected distribution decisions to public consequences in policing, emergency response, health services, and courts. Defendants could dispute whether a lawful product sold through licensed channels could be a nuisance when unlawful acts by later possessors intervened, and whether a manufacturer or distributor controlled the condition enough to abate it. The complaints answered the control question with commercial levers in the chain, including dealer selection, dealer monitoring, allocation of supply, and termination of dealer relationships.

Negligence in distribution and marketing gave the complaints their most detailed allegations. The municipal plaintiffs argued that industry members oversupplied markets where regulation was comparatively low, with the foreseeable result that firearms would move into places with stricter regulation and into unlawful hands. Oversupply did not mean merely that sales volume was large. The allegation was that volume continued without regard to signals that a market could not absorb firearms through lawful demand alone at the rate supplied. The plaintiffs also alleged a failure to monitor or discipline dealers whose firearms were traced to crimes. A trace connected a recovered firearm to its manufacturer and to the chain of licensed distribution. Aggregated traces, the plaintiffs argued, identified dealers and patterns warranting inquiry. In their account, manufacturers held information revealing risky patterns, held commercial power to investigate dealers, restrict supply, or end relationships, and failed to use that power with reasonable care. Marketing allegations extended the theory to promotion, on the contention that marketing formed part of the system determining who acquired firearms and through which channels.

Negligent entrustment required adaptation in a distribution chain. In ordinary form, entrustment concerns supplying a product for use by another when the supplier knows, or reasonably should know, that the person is likely to use it in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical injury to the person or others. A retail claim fits that form readily, because a seller deals with an identifiable buyer. The municipal complaints pressed the concept further from the counter. They treated supply through distributors and dealers as a sequence of entrustments, each participant passing firearms toward a foreseeable end user. That adaptation raised questions of knowledge and proximity. A manufacturer generally did not meet the eventual possessor, and licensed intermediaries stood between production and retail sale. The plaintiffs answered with aggregate knowledge, arguing that trace patterns and dealer histories supplied the functional equivalent of notice about a particular buyer.

Design defect and failure to warn formed a separate track, with New Orleans as the leading example. The design allegation challenged the absence of safety features, including authorized user technology, rather than a departure from the manufacturer's own specifications. A product could operate as its maker intended and remain, under the plaintiffs' theory, defectively designed because the design omitted a feasible safeguard. That premise mattered later, because the federal statute preserved a defect channel for design or manufacture when a product was used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner. Within the complaints, an injunction or settlement requiring new features would change products across a market, not merely transfer money after a loss. Failure to warn was related but distinct. The municipal plaintiffs contended that purchasers and users were not adequately warned of risks associated with the absence of safeguards and with unsecured possession. A court could sustain a warning claim without a design claim, and a change in warnings required a different remedy from a change in physical design.

Unjust enrichment and consumer protection counts did not depend entirely on negligence. Unjust enrichment, as the municipal plaintiffs framed it, began with public expenditures for police, emergency, health, and court services. The plaintiffs argued that industry members profited from distribution practices generating part of the need for those services, while the public bore costs that sound practices would have reduced. Retention of profits earned through that system, without contribution to its public costs, was said to be unjust. Consumer protection counts invoked statutes governing unfair or deceptive practices in trade or commerce. Those counts directed attention to representations, omissions, and marketing conduct. They also tied part of a complaint to a statute rather than to common law doctrine alone. That distinction later became significant under the federal exception for knowing violations of statutes applicable to the sale or marketing of a product, although the complaints were drafted before that exception existed.

Relief matched the breadth of the theories. The municipal plaintiffs sought the public costs of police, emergency, health, and court services. Those categories presented municipal losses as institutional and recurring, rather than as damage to a single item of municipal property. Cities could document budgets, personnel, facilities, and caseloads, and the plaintiffs attributed a share of those expenditures to the alleged distribution condition. Such damages required a causal bridge built from foreseeability and pattern evidence, not from one firearm in one transaction for each dollar claimed. The plaintiffs also sought injunctions restructuring distribution. Injunctive relief asked courts to require changes in dealer oversight, supply decisions, product design, and related practices. Compensation alone would leave the alleged condition in place, subject only to deterrence. An injunction converted a judgment into a regulatory instrument, and that conversion was a purpose of the litigation in the plaintiffs' own design.

The working model for that strategy came from State tobacco litigation. The State tobacco cases produced the Master Settlement Agreement of 1998, resolving State claims through payments and changes in industry practice. The municipal plaintiffs and their counsel treated that settlement as evidence that coordinated governmental litigation could achieve industry wide change without a legislature enacting each element of the program. A manufacturer facing claims in many jurisdictions could settle on terms broader than a judgment in one case. A settlement could bind the settling company to design, marketing, and distribution commitments negotiated with government officials. The analogy had a structural limit. Tobacco litigation had been brought principally by States. The firearms wave was led by municipalities whose authority to sue was open to restriction by their own States. That difference invited the State legislative response, and it set up a settlement with one manufacturer as the high water mark of the municipal strategy.

On March 17, 2000, Smith & Wesson entered an agreement with the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development, among other parties. The other parties included the Treasury Department, the Attorneys General of New York and Connecticut, and cities that pledged to dismiss their claims against the company. The settlement group joined federal agencies, two State attorneys general, and municipal plaintiffs holding pending claims. Smith & Wesson was the manufacturer that settled. The agreement divided one major defendant from the rest of the industry rather than resolving the wave as a whole. For the pledging cities, the agreement exchanged claims against Smith & Wesson for commitments by the company. Its terms gave concrete form to the restructuring the complaints had requested in general language.

The safety undertakings came first in substance. Smith & Wesson undertook to fit external locks and to develop internal locks and authorized user technology. External locks addressed storage and access through a device fitted to the firearm. Internal locks carried the same objective into the firearm itself. Development of authorized user technology returned to the subject New Orleans had placed at the head of the campaign in 1998. A theory pleaded as design defect had been translated into a negotiated product commitment. The agreement also required background checks on all sales, including at gun shows. That undertaking treated the setting of a sale as immaterial to the check to be performed. One agreement thus reached the physical product, development of a new feature, and the procedure for transfers.

Distribution and dealer conduct occupied the second part of the agreement. Smith & Wesson accepted a code of conduct for its dealers and distributors, and it accepted an oversight commission. A dealer code converted general allegations about monitoring and discipline into standards dealers and distributors were expected to observe as a condition of their relationship with the manufacturer. The oversight commission supplied a mechanism for commitments extending over time and turning on records, dealer behavior, and product development rather than on a single payment. The company also undertook to change marketing practices. Marketing had appeared in the complaints in the negligence allegations and in consumer protection counts. The settlement treated marketing as a subject for negotiated revision. Product safeguards, transfer checks, dealer discipline, oversight, and marketing changes were all present in one document, obtained from one manufacturer while litigation against others continued.

The reaction then limited the agreement and ended its practical force. The agreement drew an organized consumer boycott directed at Smith & Wesson. A boycott operated outside the courtroom, and it showed that a manufacturer settling governmental claims faced commercial consequences from purchasers opposing the terms. The company was sold in 2001, after the boycott had made it distinct from competitors that had not settled. The succeeding administration declined to enforce the agreement. Enforcement had depended on the federal parties to the settlement, and the decision not to enforce removed the governmental pressure behind the undertakings. The sequence ran in dated steps, from the agreement on March 17, 2000, through the boycott and the sale in 2001, to the declination of enforcement. No judgment in the municipal wave imposed the Smith & Wesson terms on the industry, yet the episode marked the furthest point of the litigation strategy, because a major manufacturer had accepted the kind of commitments the complaints sought.

State legislatures responded on a track that began before the Smith & Wesson agreement. Georgia enacted the first statute of the type in February 1999, reserving municipal firearms actions to the State under Ga. Code Ann. section 16-11-184. Georgia acted four months after the New Orleans filing and three months after the Chicago filing. The statute addressed authority rather than the merits of nuisance or negligence. A reservation to the State withdrew from municipalities the power to initiate actions of the covered kind, leaving the decision to bring such an action with the State. Louisiana followed in 1999 with a statute reserving such actions to the State. That enactment directly affected the first case, because New Orleans was a Louisiana municipality suing in a Louisiana court. A State legislature can alter the authority of its own municipalities by statute, and Louisiana applied that principle at the point of origin of the wave.

The New Orleans action ended under the Louisiana statute on February 28, 2000, when the Louisiana Civil District Court dismissed Morial v. Smith & Wesson Corp. The dismissal closed the case that had opened the wave, on authority grounds rather than through a judgment on the design theory. Timing sharpened the contrast. The dismissal came eighteen days before the Smith & Wesson agreement, in which other cities pledged to dismiss claims against that company as part of a settlement. One route ended a municipal action because the State had reserved the action to itself. Another ended claims against one defendant because the defendant had accepted design, distribution, and marketing commitments. Both routes showed that the fate of the cases depended on State legislatures, settlement negotiations, and federal enforcement choices as well as on trial courts.

Indiana supplied another State example, and the federal committee record supplied the catalogue. Indiana's Immunity Statute took effect in 2001, under Ind. Code sections 34-12-3-1 to 34-12-3-5. The House Judiciary Committee report on the federal bill, H. Rept. 109-124, published in 2005, catalogued the State statutes state by state, with enactments concentrated in 1999 to 2001. A catalogue organized by State showed a national legislative pattern rather than a reaction confined to States where early cases had been filed. By the spring of 2005, twenty seven States had enacted legislation prohibiting municipalities from initiating such lawsuits, a count given in the House committee's proceedings that year. For Congress, the catalogue showed that many States had removed the municipal plaintiff within their own systems. Cases and theories outside the reach of those statutes supplied the remaining ground for federal action.

What the State statutes did is clearest when authority is separated from liability. A reserving statute, such as the Georgia and Louisiana statutes of 1999, decided who could bring the action. A prohibiting statute reached the same result by barring municipal initiation directly. Neither form, as described in the record, decided whether a manufacturer had been negligent, whether distribution was a nuisance, or whether a design was defective. The statutes removed the municipal plaintiff and left the doctrines to a plaintiff with authority to sue. A legislature could act on municipal authority without resolving contested tort questions. The same legislature could not, through a municipal authority statute, dispose of an action brought by the State itself, an action in federal court, or a case brought by a municipality of another State under that State's law. The State statutes narrowed the wave without supplying a national rule for what remained.

The federal campaign developed against that background, and the federal bill complemented the State statutes rather than duplicating them. State enactments closed the municipal route in the enacting States. The federal bill was aimed at the State court and federal court actions that the State statutes did not reach. Those actions included cases in States without a reserving statute, cases by plaintiffs other than municipalities, and federal cases founded on State theories of nuisance, negligence, and design. A federal statute addressed to the category of action, rather than to the authority of a municipal plaintiff, could reach across those variations. The chronology reflects the division of labor. State action concentrated in 1999 to 2001. Congressional action came after the catalogue in H. Rept. 109-124 and after the count reached twenty seven States by the spring of 2005. The question before Congress was narrower than the question of October 1998, because municipal plaintiffs had been removed in many States, but broader in forum, because a federal prohibition could govern qualifying actions in federal and State courts throughout the country.

Three consequences endured from the municipal wave. The complaints treated distribution, trace information, dealer discipline, marketing, warnings, and product design as connected subjects of legal inquiry. The Smith & Wesson agreement showed that settlement could translate those subjects into undertakings, while the boycott, the 2001 sale, and the decision not to enforce the agreement showed the limits of a settlement accepted by one manufacturer. The State statutes, beginning with Georgia in February 1999 and reaching twenty seven States by the spring of 2005, removed municipal plaintiffs by reserving actions to the State or prohibiting municipal initiation. A residue of State court and federal court actions remained outside their reach. The federal statute enacted later in 2005 addressed that residue by category of claim rather than by municipal authority.

## How the First Wave Ended in the Courts

The appellate record that accumulated before October 2005 did not produce a single theory on which a municipal or organizational plaintiff carried a firearms distribution case to a final judgment. It produced instead a sequence of distinct holdings, each turning on a different element of the claims that had been pleaded. One case tested whether manufacturers owed a duty of reasonable care in the marketing and distribution of handguns and whether liability, if it existed, could be divided among defendants according to market share. Another tested proximate cause in negligence and the control element in nuisance. Others tested whether complaints in nuisance and negligence could survive a motion to dismiss at all. One case reached a bench trial and produced findings favorable to the plaintiff on the character of distribution practices, followed by judgment for the defendants on the separate question of injury. Read together, the decisions left the litigation campaign with findings in one courtroom, reinstatement orders in two others, and dismissals elsewhere, and the obstacles identified across the sequence were doctrinal rather than evidentiary. The courts that ended the cases did so, for the most part, by holding that an element required by the governing law had not been established, and that pattern matters because Congress in 2005 did not answer the cases by rewriting the elements of duty, causation, or nuisance. Congress answered by defining a category of action that could not be brought.

The first decision in the sequence began with the strongest trial result any plaintiff obtained in the first wave and ended with that result removed. In Hamilton v. Accu-Tek (E.D.N.Y. 1999), a federal jury in the Eastern District of New York found negligent distribution against several manufacturers and apportioned damages by national market share. The verdict therefore rested on two propositions rather than one. The first proposition was that manufacturers could be liable in negligence for the manner in which handguns were marketed and distributed, even though the harm alleged arose through later misuse by others. The second proposition was a method for dividing responsibility among multiple manufacturers when the proof did not assign the entire loss to a single maker. Apportionment by national market share supplied that method, treating share of the national market as the measure of each liable manufacturer's portion of the damages. The combination was significant for the litigation campaign because negligent distribution and market share apportionment, taken together, offered a route from general proof about distribution practices to a monetary judgment against an industry segment. Without the duty proposition, the negligence claim had no foundation. Without the apportionment proposition, a plaintiff who proved negligence in general terms faced a further problem in allocating damages among defendants. The appeal placed both propositions before a state court through certification, and both were rejected.

Certification was the procedural bridge between the federal verdict and the state holding that displaced it. The Second Circuit certified two questions to the New York Court of Appeals, asking that court, as the authoritative expositor of New York law, to answer the controlling state law questions on which the federal case depended. The answers given in Hamilton v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 96 N.Y.2d 222 (2001), were against the plaintiffs on each question. The New York Court of Appeals held that manufacturers owed no duty of reasonable care in the marketing and distribution of handguns to these plaintiffs on this record. The qualifying language carries weight. The court answered the duty question presented by the record before it in the negative, which removed the legal basis for the negligence verdict, because a negligence claim requires a duty owed by the defendant to the plaintiff. The second answer closed the allocation route that the verdict had used. The court held that liability could not be apportioned on a market share basis. The result was that the verdict did not stand. For the wider campaign, Hamilton removed, under New York law, the two supports on which the only jury verdict for plaintiffs in the sequence had rested, and later plaintiffs who invoked negligent distribution had to contend with a high court holding that addressed the duty element directly and a separate high court holding that refused the market share mechanism for dividing damages among manufacturers.

The county litigation in the Third Circuit encountered a different pair of obstacles, one sounding in causation and one sounding in control. In Camden County Board of Chosen Freeholders v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 273 F.3d 536 (3d Cir. 2001), the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the county's nuisance and negligence claims. The negligence claims failed on proximate cause. That holding located the defect in the negligence case not at the duty stage examined in Hamilton but at the stage that asks whether the connection between the conduct alleged and the harm for which relief was sought satisfied the causal requirement imposed by the governing law. A claim framed in terms of careless distribution nevertheless fails if the causal link, as a matter of law, does not meet the proximate cause standard applied by the court. The nuisance claim failed on a separate ground. The court held the claim deficient because the county had not alleged that the industry exercised control over the condition to be abated. Control in that formulation functioned as a threshold allegation for the nuisance theory as pleaded, and a complaint that did not allege such control did not state the claim. The combined effect of the two holdings was broader than a ruling on a single element. Negligence was closed off on causation and nuisance was closed off on control, so the county's action ended on appeal with both of its principal theories rejected for distinct doctrinal reasons.

The City of Philadelphia's action ended earlier and at the district court level. In City of Philadelphia v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 126 F. Supp. 2d 882 (E.D. Pa. 2000), the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania dismissed the city's action. The decision is placed in the sequence by its posture and its result. A city had brought a municipal action of the kind then being filed across the country, and the district court dismissed the action in 2000, before the New York Court of Appeals answered the certified questions in Hamilton and before the Third Circuit ruled in Camden County. Its place in the balance sheet is as an early district court dismissal of a city case. It contributed no appellate qualification and no reinstatement, and it formed part of the background against which the later appellate decisions were read. When the chronology is assembled in full, Philadelphia stands at one end as a dismissal at the trial court, Cincinnati and Gary stand in the middle as appellate decisions allowing claims to go forward at the pleading stage, and Hamilton, Camden County, and Chicago stand as appellate authority ending claims on duty, causation, control, and state nuisance law.

Ohio supplied one of the exceptions to the prevailing direction of the record. In City of Cincinnati v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 95 Ohio St. 3d 416 (2002), the Supreme Court of Ohio reversed a dismissal and reinstated the city's nuisance and negligence claims at the pleading stage. The posture defines both the strength and the limit of the result. A reversal of a dismissal restores the complaint and permits the action to proceed beyond the threshold test of whether the pleading states a claim on which relief may be granted. It does not enter judgment for the plaintiff, it does not resolve contested questions of proof, and it does not hold that the defendants are liable after a trial on the merits. Within those limits, the Cincinnati decision mattered considerably to the campaign. It was one of the few appellate decisions allowing a municipal action to proceed, and it allowed both the nuisance theory and the negligence theory to proceed together, whereas Camden County had rejected a county's versions of those theories on proximate cause and control grounds and Hamilton had rejected the duty foundation for negligent distribution under New York law on the record presented there. The contrast illustrates why the pre-2005 record cannot be summarized as a uniform judicial rejection of the municipal theory. State law governed significant parts of these claims, and an appellate court applying Ohio law at the pleading stage reached a result opposed in direction to the results reached elsewhere. Cincinnati kept a city case alive. It did not give any city a judgment.

Indiana supplied the other principal appellate decision on which a municipal claim survived during this period. In City of Gary v. Smith & Wesson Corp., 801 N.E.2d 1243 (Ind. 2003), the Indiana Supreme Court held that the city's public nuisance claim could proceed, reversing the dismissal of that claim. The action had been filed in September 1999, so the 2003 decision arrived after more than three years of litigation over the threshold sufficiency of the claim rather than after a trial. The holding, like the holding in Cincinnati, was a pleading stage and threshold holding in the plaintiff's favor. The Indiana court did not enter judgment on the nuisance claim and did not award relief. It held that the claim could proceed, which meant that the dismissal that had removed that claim was reversed and the city retained a public nuisance theory to litigate. For plaintiffs' counsel assessing the national record in 2003 and 2004, Gary and Cincinnati were the two appellate results that could be cited for the proposition that a municipal complaint in nuisance, and in Cincinnati in negligence as well, was capable of surviving dismissal in a state supreme court. For the members of the two Houses of Congress who later weighed federal legislation, the same two decisions showed that state doctrinal litigation had not closed every municipal case and that, in at least two jurisdictions, the path beyond the pleading stage remained open after appellate review.

The most instructive decision in the record came from a trial rather than from a pleading ruling, and its outcome divided sharply between findings and judgment. In NAACP v. AcuSport, Inc., 271 F. Supp. 2d 435 (E.D.N.Y. 2003), the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York conducted a bench trial. A bench trial places the fact finding function in the judge rather than in a jury, and the decision that followed therefore carried a set of judicial findings made after evidence had been presented and tested at trial, a posture materially different from the assumption in a plaintiff's favor that governs the review of a dismissed complaint. After that trial, the court found clear and convincing evidence that some manufacturers' distribution practices were careless and contributed to a public nuisance. The standard named in the finding is a demanding one, and the language of the finding addressed both the quality of the practices and their connection to the nuisance alleged. On the evidentiary questions encompassed by that finding, the plaintiff organization prevailed to a degree that no municipal plaintiff matched elsewhere in the sequence. No city obtained findings of that character from a trial court after a full bench trial, and the Hamilton jury verdict that might have been compared with it had already been displaced by the New York Court of Appeals' answers on duty and apportionment.

Judgment in NAACP v. AcuSport nevertheless went to the defendants, and the reason for that judgment defines the lesson of the case. The court entered judgment for the defendants because the plaintiff organization had not shown an injury distinct from that borne by the public generally, so no injunction issued. Public nuisance law, in the form applied in that courtroom, separated the existence of a nuisance and the contribution of careless practices to it from the standing of a particular plaintiff to obtain relief for it. A private or organizational plaintiff seeking an injunction on a public nuisance theory had to show a distinct injury, different in kind from the injury shared by the public at large, and the plaintiff organization did not make that showing to the court's satisfaction. The consequence was a split outcome that resists any simple classification as a victory for either side in full. The evidentiary record, as the trial court assessed it under a clear and convincing standard, supported findings of careless distribution practices contributing to a public nuisance. The remedial record, assessed under the special injury requirement, supported no relief at all. The defendants left the trial with judgment in their favor and without an injunction restructuring distribution. The plaintiff organization left with findings that could be quoted in legislative debate and in later argument about distribution practices, but with no order that any defendant change a practice and no award that any defendant pay. That division between proof of practices and entitlement to relief became the clearest demonstration in the first wave that success on the factual merits of a distribution critique and success in obtaining a legal remedy were governed by separate elements, and that a plaintiff could satisfy a trial court on the first while failing on the second.

The foundational city action from November 1998 closed the sequence in the Illinois courts in the following year. In City of Chicago v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 213 Ill. 2d 351 (2004), the Illinois Supreme Court held that the city's public nuisance claims failed as a matter of Illinois law and affirmed dismissal. Chicago had been framed from its filing as a public nuisance action arguing that distribution practices supplied an illegal secondary market, and the nuisance theory was the theory on which the action had been built. The Illinois holding therefore went to the center of the case rather than to a peripheral count. The state's highest court concluded that the nuisance claims could not be sustained under Illinois law, and dismissal, rather than reinstatement of the Cincinnati and Gary kind, was the affirmed result. The timing gave the decision a particular place in the legislative chronology. It closed the foundational Chicago action months before Congress completed the federal bill in 2005. Read alongside the Indiana decision in Gary, Chicago also confirmed that state supreme courts divided on the viability of the municipal public nuisance theory. Indiana allowed Gary's nuisance claim to proceed past dismissal in 2003, and Illinois held in 2004 that Chicago's nuisance claims failed as a matter of state law. Neither outcome was a judgment after trial on a developed evidentiary record of the NAACP kind.

The balance sheet that the two Houses of Congress read in 2005 followed directly from those postures and holdings. No city had won a judgment. The municipal plaintiffs who had filed beginning in the autumn of 1998 had obtained, at the highest point of the campaign, appellate reinstatement of claims at the pleading stage in Ohio and Indiana, and no municipal case in the sequence had carried through trial to a judgment in the city's favor against the manufacturers. The strongest result obtained by any plaintiff was a set of findings without relief, the bench trial findings in NAACP v. AcuSport that clear and convincing evidence showed careless distribution practices on the part of some manufacturers contributing to a public nuisance, paired with judgment for the defendants and no injunction because a distinct injury had not been shown. A small number of actions survived at the pleading stage, Gary on its public nuisance claim and Cincinnati on its nuisance and negligence claims, and survival at that stage preserved the possibility of further litigation without resolving liability. Against those limited survivals stood dismissals affirmed or entered in Philadelphia, Camden County, and Chicago, and a jury verdict in Hamilton that did not stand after the New York Court of Appeals answered the certified questions against the plaintiffs.

The doctrinal obstacles named across the record were consistent even though the decisions came from different courts applying different bodies of law. Duty defeated the negligent distribution verdict in Hamilton, where the New York Court of Appeals held that no duty of reasonable care in the marketing and distribution of handguns was owed to those plaintiffs on that record, and the same decision removed market share apportionment as the mechanism for dividing any such liability. Proximate cause defeated the negligence claims in Camden County, and the absence of an allegation that the industry exercised control over the condition to be abated defeated the nuisance claim in the same case. The special injury requirement defeated the organizational plaintiff in NAACP v. AcuSport after the trial court had made findings in its favor on careless practices and contribution to a nuisance. Illinois law, as the Illinois Supreme Court stated it in Chicago, defeated the municipal nuisance claims themselves. Each obstacle operated at a different point in the structure of a claim, at the existence of an obligation, at the causal connection between conduct and harm, at the plaintiff's entitlement to sue over a public condition, and at the scope of the nuisance doctrine under state law. That spread explains why the federal response, when it came in 2005, took the form it did. The federal statute addressed the problem by definition rather than by doctrine. It defined a qualified civil liability action and barred that defined category, instead of legislating a national rule of duty for negligent distribution, a national standard of proximate cause for distribution claims, or a national version of the special injury requirement for public nuisance plaintiffs. The pre-2005 courts had decided the cases element by element under the law each court was bound to apply. Congress displaced the inquiry for the defined category altogether, and the difference between those two methods is the point at which the judicial history of the first wave ends and the statutory history begins.

## Two Congresses, Two Outcomes

The Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act reached enactment through two distinct legislative efforts separated by a change of Congress. In the first effort, during the 108th Congress, the House of Representatives approved a companion bill and the Senate took up S. 1805, amended the measure on the floor, and then rejected the amended measure on the same day the amendments were adopted. In the second effort, during the 109th Congress, a new bill, S. 397, passed the Senate in July 2005, passed the House of Representatives in October 2005, and received the signature of President George W. Bush on October 26, 2005. The 2005 sequence joined adoption of a different set of amendments to passage in both chambers and to enactment on signature, with an express statutory direction for actions pending on the enactment date.

The House of Representatives moved first in the 108th Congress. In April 2003 the House passed a companion bill by a margin of better than two to one. The size of the House margin recorded broad support in that chamber for the proposition that a qualified civil liability action, as the legislation defined that category, should not proceed against a manufacturer or seller for harm resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a product by another person. House passage alone could not enact the proposal. The Constitution requires passage of the same measure by both chambers and presentation to the President, and the Senate remained free to amend its own bill or to reject the bill after amendments had been adopted. Control of the Senate amendment process would therefore decide whether a bill reached a final vote in a form its original coalition could accept.

In the Senate, S. 1805 reached the floor in February and March 2004. Floor consideration exposed the bill to amendments offered and voted on in the full chamber, a setting different in kind from committee markup, where members consider a measure within a smaller body and report a text to the chamber. On the floor, each senator may offer amendments within the governing rules, and the chamber disposes of each amendment before the question of final passage arises. That order of business mattered for S. 1805 because the chamber acted on two amendments with substantial independent content before it acted on the bill itself. Each amendment, once adopted, became part of the text on which the final vote would be taken. A senator could therefore support the underlying prohibition and oppose the amended whole, or support an amendment and support the whole, and the record of March 2, 2004 contains both kinds of action in sequence.

The first of the two amendments adopted on March 2, 2004 extended the 1994 assault weapons ban for ten years. That ban was a separate body of federal law with its own definitions, and a ten year extension would have continued it for a further decade. Attachment of the extension to S. 1805 changed the legislative character of the vehicle. A vote for final passage after adoption was no longer a vote on civil liability rules alone. It was a vote on civil liability rules combined with a decade of continued operation under the ban, and senators who had come to the floor prepared to support the liability measure had to weigh the combined text. After adoption, the liability measure carried a major provision on a different question of firearms regulation, and every subsequent vote on S. 1805 was a vote on the combination.

The second amendment adopted on March 2, 2004 concerned background checks at gun shows. The amendment is identified in the record as the McCain amendment, and its text carried the short title "Gun Show Loophole Closing Act of 2004", a title attributed here to the amendment's own text. Its subject was the background check requirement as applied to sales at gun shows, a setting for transfers that the underlying liability bill did not address. Adoption placed that subject inside S. 1805 alongside the ban extension, so the text awaiting a final vote joined three distinct matters in one measure: a prohibition on a defined category of civil liability action, a ten year extension of a separate federal ban, and a provision on background checks for a defined setting of sale. The final vote would test a different proposition, whether a majority of the Senate would enact all three matters together as a single law.

The Senate then defeated the amended bill on the same day, March 2, 2004. The order of the votes is the central fact of the 108th Congress effort. Adoption of an amendment records majority support for that amendment as an addition to the pending text. Defeat of the amended bill records that the combined text lacked majority support for passage. The defeat ended the effort for that Congress. A bill defeated in one Congress does not carry forward as pending business into the next Congress, and proponents who sought enactment after the 108th Congress adjourned required a new bill, new committee and floor action, and new passage votes in both chambers. The 109th Congress therefore began the question again as a matter of procedure, whatever continuity existed in sponsorship, text, and argument between S. 1805 and the measure that followed.

### How did the bill pass after failing a year earlier?

The bill passed on the second attempt because a new Congress took up a new bill, the Senate adopted child safety lock amendments rather than the amendments adopted in March 2004, and majorities in both chambers then approved the measure by recorded votes before the President signed it in October 2005.

S. 397 was introduced on February 16, 2005 by Senator Larry Craig of Idaho. The bill that Craig introduced proposed the prohibition that became section 7902 of title 15, together with findings and purposes and definitions, and it reached the Senate floor during the first session of the Congress. A bill introduced in February and passed by the first chamber in July left the second chamber an autumn in which to act during the same session, and the House of Representatives took up the Senate bill itself rather than resting on the companion measure the House had passed during the previous Congress. Passage of the Senate bill by the House, without a different House text requiring reconciliation between two versions, allowed the measure to move directly to presentation after House approval.

The Senate passed S. 397 on July 29, 2005 by 65 to 31, Record Vote Number 219. Before passage, the Senate adopted amendments, and the amendments that the chamber adopted included the child safety lock requirements that became the second half of the enacted law. The content of the adopted amendments distinguishes the 2005 floor action from the floor action of March 2, 2004. In 2004 the adopted amendments added the ban extension and the gun show background check provisions, and the amended bill failed. In 2005 the adopted amendments added requirements governing devices that accompany handguns at transfer, provisions directed to the handling and storage of the product after sale rather than to categories of firearms or to the settings of sale, and the amended bill passed.

The House of Representatives passed S. 397 on October 20, 2005 by 283 to 144, Roll Number 534. President George W. Bush signed the measure on October 26, 2005, and the act took effect on enactment. Enactment converted the prohibition from a proposal into a rule addressed to courts. Section 7902(a) of title 15 provides that a qualified civil liability action may not be brought in any Federal or State court, and section 7902(b) supplied the transitional rule for actions already in being. An action of that category pending on October 26, 2005 was to be immediately dismissed by the court in which it was brought or in which it was pending. The reach to pending actions distinguished the statute from a purely prospective bar on new filings. The public law number, Public Law 109-92, and its placement in the Statutes at Large at 119 Stat. 2095, complete the formal record of enactment.

The enacted law contained a second component alongside the prohibition. Its later portion bore the name Child Safety Lock Act of 2005, and it amended chapter 44 of title 18, the chapter of the United States Code that contains the principal federal provisions governing firearms commerce and licensing. The public law also amended sections 921, 922 and 924 of title 18 within that framework. The lock provisions addressed the conduct of licensees at the point of transfer. A licensee transferring a handgun was required to provide a secure gun storage or safety device with the handgun, subject to the exceptions the statute provided. The requirement attached to the transfer itself rather than to later conduct by the purchaser, and it operated through the licensing structure that chapter 44 already maintained for persons engaged in the business of importing, manufacturing or dealing in firearms.

The inclusion of the lock requirements mattered to passage because the Senate adopted those requirements as amendments to S. 397 before the Senate passed the bill, and the House of Representatives passed the Senate bill in the form that contained them. The lock provisions were therefore part of the text that commanded 65 votes in the Senate and 283 votes in the House of Representatives, not a separate measure enacted on a separate record. Their presence changed the content of the enacted statute in a way that the vote record makes concrete: a member voting for final passage in either chamber voted for a law that both barred the defined category of action and required a storage or safety device with each handgun a licensee transferred, subject to statutory exceptions. In 2005 the adopted amendments traveled with the bill through passage, signature and enactment, and the lock requirements entered the Code as part of the same public law that created sections 7901 to 7903 of title 15.

Sections 7901 to 7903 of title 15 divide the enacted text between findings and purposes, the prohibition, and definitions. Section 7901 states findings in subsection (a) and purposes in subsection (b), and the statements in that section are attributed statements of Congress rather than operative commands addressed to courts. The operative command appears in section 7902, and the definitions that give the command its scope appear in section 7903. The findings nevertheless formed part of the enacted text that the President signed, and the findings supplied the stated rationale on which Congress rested the exercise of federal authority, including the rationale concerning interstate and foreign commerce. Litigation under the statute after enactment followed the order that the structure suggests. Courts read the operative text first, construing the prohibition and its exceptions through the definitions, and the findings entered the cases where a party challenged the constitutional basis for the statute and the commerce rationale stated in section 7901 was tested against the powers Congress had invoked.

The findings open with recitals concerning the Second Amendment, placing the constitutional provision at the head of the stated rationale. The findings state that lawsuits had been commenced against manufacturers, distributors, dealers and importers of firearms that operate as designed and intended, seeking money damages and other relief for harm caused by the misuse of firearms by third parties, including criminals. Congress found that the actions within that description sought relief against businesses for harm inflicted by persons other than the businesses themselves, where the product at issue functioned as designed and intended. The recital identifies the defendants by position in the chain of commerce and the alleged wrong by its distance from the defendant's own act: the misuse belonged to a third party, and the claim asked the court to move the cost of that misuse to the business that sold the product.

Congress also found that the manufacture, importation, possession, sale and use of firearms and ammunition are heavily regulated by Federal, State and local law. The findings name the Gun Control Act of 1968, the National Firearms Act and the Arms Export Control Act as components of that regulatory structure. The recital matters to the logic of section 7901 because it describes an industry that Congress understood to operate inside an existing body of statutes, licenses and controls rather than outside public regulation. It frames the civil actions described in the findings as an addition to that statutory scheme rather than as the only form of oversight applied to the industry. The findings move from that description of regulation to a conclusion about liability, stated as a finding about the position the law recognizes and the position Congress enacted the prohibition to secure.

Congress found that businesses engaged in interstate and foreign commerce through the lawful design, manufacture, marketing, distribution, importation or sale of firearms or ammunition products shipped in that commerce "are not, and should not, be liable" for harm caused by those who criminally or unlawfully misuse products that function as designed and intended. The quoted words are the center of the findings. The first clause states a position about liability as Congress understood the law, and the second clause states that position as a rule for the cases the prohibition would govern. The findings then state that the possibility of imposing liability on an entire industry for harm solely caused by others is an abuse of the legal system, erodes public confidence in that system, threatens the diminution of a basic constitutional right and constitutes an unreasonable burden on interstate and foreign commerce. Each element of that sentence carries a distinct claim.

The findings state a further institutional objection. Congress found that actions of the described kind attempt to use the judicial branch to circumvent the legislative branch of government. The findings state that such use of litigation threatens the separation of powers, the principles of federalism and comity among the States. Separation of powers in this recital concerns the allocation of policymaking authority between courts and legislatures, and the finding presents the described actions as an effort to obtain through adjudication a regulatory result that the plaintiffs had not obtained through legislation. Federalism and comity concern the relations between the Federal government and the States and among the States themselves, in a field where State law supplied many of the causes of action that the described lawsuits pleaded and Federal law supplied the licensing framework within which the industry operated.

Section 7901(b) states seven purposes, each attributed to the text of the statute. The first stated purpose is to prohibit causes of action against manufacturers, distributors, dealers, importers and their trade associations for harm solely caused by the criminal or unlawful misuse of products by others when the product functioned as designed and intended. The second stated purpose is to preserve a citizen's access to a supply of firearms and ammunition for all lawful purposes. The third stated purpose is to guarantee rights under the Fourteenth Amendment, in an exercise of congressional power under section 5 of that Amendment. The fourth stated purpose is to prevent the imposition of unreasonable burdens on interstate and foreign commerce. The fifth stated purpose is to protect the First Amendment rights of industry members and trade associations. The sixth stated purpose is to preserve the separation of powers, federalism, State sovereignty and comity. The seventh stated purpose is to exercise congressional power under the Full Faith and Credit Clause. Read with the findings, the purposes map the prohibition onto the constitutional provisions and structural principles that the findings recite. The commerce purpose answers the burden finding, the structural purposes answer the circumvention finding, and the first purpose restates the prohibition itself as the object of the enactment.

The findings performed a defined function in litigation under the statute after enactment, within the limits that the structure of the Code imposes. A finding does not itself dismiss an action, and section 7901 creates no operative prohibition that a court can apply apart from section 7902. Courts therefore read the operative text first, beginning with the bar in section 7902(a), the dismissal direction in section 7902(b) and the definitions in section 7903 that determine whether an action falls inside the barred category or inside an exception to that category. The findings entered at a different point in the analysis. When the statute was challenged on constitutional grounds, the findings supplied the record of the rationale Congress had stated for legislating, and the commerce finding in particular supplied the proposition to be tested: the findings state that the described actions constituted an unreasonable burden on interstate and foreign commerce, and the challenges required courts to measure that stated rationale against the powers Congress had invoked.

## The Six Exceptions in the Text

The six exclusions that define the reach of the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act sit inside the definition itself. Section 7903(5)(A) of title 15 defines a qualified civil liability action as a civil action or proceeding or an administrative proceeding brought by any person against a manufacturer or seller of a qualified product, or a trade association, for damages, punitive damages, injunctive or declaratory relief, abatement, restitution, fines, or penalties, or other relief, resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the person or a third party. The same paragraph then states that the term does not include six kinds of actions, identified by the clauses numbered (i) through (vi). That placement controls the analysis. An action within one of the exclusions is not a qualified civil liability action at all, and the prohibition in section 7902(a), which provides that a qualified civil liability action may not be brought in any Federal or State court, therefore has no defined object to attach to. The court asks the definitional question first, before any question about dismissal, because dismissal under the act presupposes that the action falls within the category Congress defined.

The sequence matters for procedure as well as for substance. A plaintiff whose claim belongs inside an exclusion does not obtain an exemption from a bar that otherwise applies, and a defendant does not receive a pardon for conduct that the definition never covered. The pleading must be matched, element by element, against the words of one of the six clauses. A claim that resembles an excluded action in general purpose but lacks an element the clause requires remains inside the definition and subject to the prohibition. A claim that satisfies every element of a clause falls outside the definition even if the dispute also involves a qualified product, a manufacturer or seller, and harm connected with criminal or unlawful misuse by a third party. The six clauses are therefore not illustrations of a broader judicial discretion to preserve claims that appear meritorious. Each clause is a bounded textual category, with its own defendant, its own required conduct or condition, its own plaintiff in two of the clauses, and its own causal requirement.

Two further provisions in section 7903(5) govern how the six clauses operate together. The rule of construction in section 7903(5)(C) provides that the exceptions in clauses (i) through (v) are to be construed so as not to be in conflict, and that nothing in the chapter creates a public or private cause of action or remedy. The first part instructs a court to read the five clauses as parts of one definition rather than as competing grants, so that the specific conditions of one clause are not displaced by a looser reading of a neighboring clause. The second part fixes the source of any right a plaintiff asserts. An exclusion preserves an action that some other body of law supplies, whether contract law, tort law, a State or Federal statute, or another source, but the exclusion itself supplies no claim, no remedy, and no element. A plaintiff must therefore plead and prove a claim under that other law in addition to showing that the action fits an exclusion. Section 7903(5)(D) adds a provision for a minor child, stating that nothing in the chapter limits the right of a person under 17 years of age to recover damages authorized under Federal or State law in a civil action that meets one of clauses (i) through (v). That provision is not a seventh exclusion. It confirms that a qualifying action brought for a person under 17 retains whatever damages the governing law authorizes, while leaving the requirement of meeting one of the five clauses unchanged.

### The Convicted Transferor Action

Clause (i) excludes an action against a transferor convicted under section 924(h) of title 18, or under a comparable or identical State felony law, brought by a party directly harmed by the conduct underlying the conviction. Section 924(h) addresses the knowing transfer of a firearm to a person prohibited from possessing one. The exclusion has four components, and each one narrows the category. The defendant must be a transferor. A criminal conviction under one of the two specified kinds of law must exist. The plaintiff must be a party directly harmed. The harm must arise from the conduct underlying the conviction, rather than from some other act by the transferor or from a later event connected with the firearm only in a general way.

The conviction requirement gives clause (i) a structure unlike the other seller conduct exceptions. The civil action rests on a completed criminal adjudication against the transferor, and the conduct that the criminal case established forms the basis for identifying the civil claim. A comparable or identical State law must be a felony law, so a lesser State offense does not satisfy the clause on its own terms. The direct harm requirement performs a matching function on the plaintiff side. It ties recovery to the person harmed by the specific transfer conduct proved in the criminal case and prevents the clause from becoming a general vehicle for claims by persons whose losses bear only a remote relation to that conduct.

Precision about clause (i) matters because the enacted text is often described in broader terms than Congress used. The clause is not a general exception for a sale to a prohibited person. No conviction, no clause (i) action, even if a complaint alleges that a seller transferred a firearm to a person who could not lawfully possess it. A transfer to a prohibited person enters the definition through a different door, clause (iii)(II), which covers a manufacturer or seller who aided, abetted, or conspired with another person to sell or otherwise dispose of a qualified product to an actual buyer the participant knew or had reasonable cause to believe was prohibited under section 922(g) or section 922(n) of title 18. That subclause is part of the statutory violation exception, it requires participation in another person's sale rather than a conviction of the defendant, and it supplies its own knowledge standard. Conflating the two provisions would erase the conviction element from clause (i) and the aiding, abetting, or conspiracy element from clause (iii)(II), producing a single sale exception that appears in neither place in the text.

### Negligent Entrustment and Negligence Per Se

Clause (ii) excludes an action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se. The defendant specified by the clause is a seller, and the conduct specified by the clause is the seller's own conduct in supplying the product. Congress supplied a definition of negligent entrustment in section 7903(5)(B). Under that definition, negligent entrustment means the supplying of a qualified product by a seller for use by another person when the seller knows, or reasonably should know, that the person to whom the product is supplied is likely to, and does, use the product in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical injury to the person or others. Every phrase in that definition carries an element. There must be a supplying by a seller, a recipient who will use the product, knowledge or constructive knowledge on the seller's part, a likelihood that the recipient will use the product in the described manner, actual use in that manner, and a use involving unreasonable risk of physical injury to the recipient or to another person.

The knowledge standard in section 7903(5)(B) has both a subjective and an objective limb. Knowledge held by the seller satisfies the first limb. Facts available to the seller that would support the judgment the definition describes satisfy the second limb through the words reasonably should know, even without proof that the seller formed the judgment in those terms. The likelihood element and the actual use element are separate. A recipient who appears likely to use a product in a manner involving unreasonable risk but does not do so has not completed the statutory definition, because the definition uses the paired words likely to, and does. Conversely, a harmful use by a recipient whose likely conduct the seller neither knew nor reasonably should have known also falls short of the definition. The clause therefore turns on the transaction at the point of supply, judged by what the seller knew or reasonably should have known at that point, combined with the use that followed.

Negligence per se appears in clause (ii) as a distinct theory named beside negligent entrustment, although section 7903(5)(B) defines only the entrustment term. The pairing places both theories in the same defendant specific category, an action against a seller, and both concern the seller's failure to meet a governing standard of care in connection with supplying the product. The rule of construction in section 7903(5)(C) remains relevant to both theories, because neither theory draws its elements or its remedy from the 2005 act itself. The governing law outside the chapter supplies the standard, the breach, the causation, and the damages rules that a plaintiff must establish, and the exclusion determines only that an action of that kind against a seller is outside the definition of a qualified civil liability action. The textual boundary is equally clear in the opposite direction. Clause (ii) names a seller rather than the broader set of manufacturers, sellers, and trade associations in the opening language of section 7903(5)(A), and it names two negligence theories rather than negligence in every form in which a complaint might plead it.

### The Statutory Violation Exception

Clause (iii) excludes an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a State or Federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought. The clause has three general elements before its two illustrative subclauses are reached. The defendant must be a manufacturer or seller. The violation must be knowing, a mental state the clause states expressly and that separates the excluded action from an action based on an inadvertent breach of an applicable statute. The statute violated must be applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, a subject matter limit that connects the predicate law to the commercial conduct the 2005 act addresses rather than to any law a manufacturer or seller might violate in another part of its operations. The violation must also be a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought, so a knowing violation without the required causal connection to the pleaded harm does not bring the action within the clause.

Subclause (I) identifies a record keeping and false statement category within clause (iii). It covers any case in which the manufacturer or seller knowingly made a false entry in, or failed to make an appropriate entry in, any record required to be kept under Federal or State law with respect to the qualified product. The same subclause covers a manufacturer or seller who aided, abetted, or conspired with any person in making a false or fictitious statement material to the lawfulness of the sale or other disposition of the product. Record keeping in this field rests principally on the Federal scheme described in [the Gun Control Act of 1968 guide](/2010/09/15/gun-control-act-1968-guide/), and subclause (I) treats the integrity of the records required by that body of law as a condition of lawful sale rather than as a clerical matter detached from the harm analysis. A false entry, a missing entry, and a false or fictitious statement are distinct acts within the subclause, and materiality to the lawfulness of the sale is an express requirement for the statement category. A statement that is false but immaterial to whether the sale could lawfully proceed sits outside the words of the subclause, while the general elements of clause (iii), including a knowing violation of an applicable statute and proximate causation of the harm, frame the inquiry for the action as a whole.

Subclause (II) identifies a prohibited buyer category, and its terms must be stated in full to keep the category distinct from clause (i). It covers any case in which the manufacturer or seller aided, abetted, or conspired with any person to sell or otherwise dispose of a qualified product to an actual buyer, knowing or having reasonable cause to believe that the actual buyer was prohibited from possessing or receiving a firearm or ammunition under section 922(g) or section 922(n) of title 18. The prohibited status therefore comes from those two Federal provisions, and the mental state can be either knowledge or reasonable cause to believe. The defendant's role can be aiding, abetting, or conspiring with another person, so the subclause reaches participation in a sale carried out through another participant as well as participation in a disposition the defendant arranged with others. No conviction of the defendant is required by this subclause, and that absence is the clearest textual contrast with clause (i). At the same time, subclause (II) is not a freestanding prohibition on every sale that reaches a prohibited person. It sits inside clause (iii), so the action must be one in which the manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm.

The two subclauses illustrate different ways in which a sale can depart from the legal conditions governing it. Subclause (I) addresses the paper record and the statements on which the lawfulness of a transaction depends. Subclause (II) addresses participation in placing a product with a buyer whose possession or receipt Federal law forbids. Both categories concern the defendant's own statutory compliance in the transaction, and both leave the proximate cause requirement in place for the particular harm pleaded.

### Breach of Contract and Warranty

Clause (iv) excludes an action for breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase of the product. The clause preserves the ordinary commercial relationship between a buyer and the party that sold the product under agreed terms or under a warranty attached to the transaction. A buyer who alleges that the product delivered did not conform to the contract, that a promised repair or replacement was not provided, that an express warranty was breached, or that a warranty recognized by the governing commercial law was breached brings an action of the kind clause (iv) describes, provided the claim arises in connection with the purchase. The source of the obligation is the bargain or the warranty, and the governing contract and commercial law supplies the elements, the available relief, and any conditions on notice, cure, or limitation that apply to the claim.

The connection with the purchase requirement keeps clause (iv) within the commercial setting its words describe. A warranty claim by a purchaser rests on a different legal foundation from a claim by a person injured through a third party's criminal use of a product, even when the same product is involved in both disputes. The first claim asks whether the seller performed the obligations undertaken in the sale, while the second asks whether responsibility for another person's misuse should be placed on a manufacturer or seller under a tort theory. Congress separated those questions by placing the contract and warranty category inside the list of actions the definition does not include. Ordinary commercial claims between buyer and seller are untouched by the prohibition, and a seller cannot convert a dispute over its own promise into a qualified civil liability action merely because the subject of the promise was a firearm or ammunition.

Clause (iv) also interacts with the rule of construction in a concrete way. The 2005 act creates no contract and no warranty, so a plaintiff who proceeds under clause (iv) must identify an obligation that exists under the applicable contract or warranty law. Conversely, clause (v) may cover a related dispute about the condition of a product when the claim is framed as death, physical injury, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture, because that clause turns on the product and on causation rather than on a promise made at purchase. The two clauses can therefore address neighboring facts through different elements, and section 7903(5)(C) instructs that they be read so as not to be in conflict.

### Defect in Design or Manufacture

Clause (v) excludes an action for death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture of the product, when used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner. The statement the text supports is plain. Product defect litigation survives the statute entirely, because an action meeting clause (v) is excluded from the definition of a qualified civil liability action and therefore lies outside the prohibition from the outset. The clause turns on the condition of the product rather than on the conduct of a third party who misused it. Its elements are a defect in design or manufacture, a qualifying kind of loss in the form of death, physical injuries, or property damage, a direct causal relation expressed by the words resulting directly from, and a use of the product as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner.

The intended and reasonably foreseeable use requirement distinguishes a defect claim from a claim that a product should have been designed to withstand or prevent a use outside the range the clause describes. A design defect allegation under clause (v) concerns the product as designed for its intended or reasonably foreseeable use, and a manufacture defect allegation concerns a product that departed from its design in manufacture and then caused a qualifying loss in such a use. The clause does not require a contract, a warranty, a statutory violation, a conviction, or an entrustment, because none of those elements appears in its text. It also does not extend by its terms to every kind of loss. Economic loss standing alone, without death, physical injuries, or property damage, falls outside the three categories of harm clause (v) names, although a buyer with such a loss may have a contract or warranty action under clause (iv) if the governing commercial law supplies one, including the consumer remedies surveyed in [the consumer protection law history article](/2017/08/01/consumer-protection-law-history/).

One qualification inside clause (v) requires exact statement, because it reverses the causal analysis in a defined setting. The clause provides that where the discharge of the product was caused by a volitional act that constituted a criminal offense, that act is considered the sole proximate cause of any resulting death, personal injuries, or property damage. In that setting, the volitional criminal act that caused the discharge occupies the entire proximate cause position the clause assigns, and the action cannot proceed under clause (v) on the theory that a defect was also a proximate cause of the same loss. The qualification is limited to the setting its words create, a discharge caused by a volitional act that constituted a criminal offense. It does not repeal the defect exclusion for a qualifying loss resulting directly from a defect during intended or reasonably foreseeable use where that setting is absent, and it does not convert a defect allegation into a misuse allegation merely because a product was discharged.

The operation of the defect channel can be documented through a completed class action that proceeded outside the prohibition. In Carter v. Forjas Taurus, S.A., No. 1:13-cv-24583-PAS (S.D. Fla.), a class action alleged drop-fire and false-safety defects in nine pistol models, with an estimated 955,796 pistols in the class. The allegation as pleaded concerned the condition of the pistols in the nine models, and the proceeding illustrates the category clause (v) describes, in which the plaintiffs' theory rested on defect rather than on the criminal or unlawful misuse of a product by a third party. The district court approved a settlement in 2016, and the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit affirmed in 2017, reported at 701 Fed. Appx. 759. Under the settlement, an owner could return a pistol for a payment of up to 200 dollars or obtain inspection and repair under an enhanced lifetime warranty, with safety training offered as part of the arrangement. The manufacturer denied any defect or wrongdoing. The settlement therefore resolved the class claims through a choice between a payment on return and an inspection and repair remedy under the enhanced warranty, without an admission by the manufacturer that the alleged defects existed.

### Enforcement by the Attorney General

Clause (vi) excludes an action or proceeding commenced by the Attorney General to enforce the provisions of chapter 44 of title 18 or chapter 53 of title 26. Chapter 44 of title 18 contains the principal Federal firearms provisions, including the licensing, transfer, record, and prohibited person provisions that also supply terms used elsewhere in section 7903. Chapter 53 of title 26 contains the provisions the clause identifies by citation, and enforcement of those provisions by the Attorney General is likewise placed outside the definition. The statute does not touch the Federal government's own enforcement. An enforcement action or proceeding of the kind clause (vi) describes is not barred, not subject to dismissal under section 7902(b) principles that apply to a qualified civil liability action, and not dependent on clauses (i) through (v) for its authority.

The plaintiff and the purpose together define this exclusion. The action or proceeding must be commenced by the Attorney General, and it must be brought to enforce one of the two cited bodies of law. A damages action by a private plaintiff, a municipal plaintiff, or another governmental entity does not become a clause (vi) action merely because the complaint discusses chapter 44 or chapter 53, and an Attorney General proceeding addressed to another body of law does not meet the clause on its terms. Section 7903(3) provides that person includes any governmental entity for purposes of the definition, which places governmental plaintiffs inside the general category of persons who can bring a qualified civil liability action. Clause (vi) then separates one governmental function, Federal enforcement by the Attorney General of the two cited chapters, from the actions the definition covers.

That separation preserves the enforcement structure on which the rest of the definition depends. The licensing and record provisions of chapter 44 supply part of the legal framework for determining whether a sale complied with Federal law under clause (iii), and the prohibited person provisions in section 922(g) and section 922(n) supply the standard clause (iii)(II) uses for an actual buyer. If an Attorney General enforcement proceeding under those chapters were itself treated as a qualified civil liability action, the statute would obstruct the public enforcement of the same provisions it incorporates by reference. Clause (vi) avoids that result by excluding the enforcement proceeding from the definition altogether.

## The Predicate Exception and the Litigation It Carries

The predicate exception decided more of the litigation under the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act than any other single provision after enactment. The bar in 15 U.S.C. section 7902 applies to a qualified civil liability action, and the definition in section 7903(5)(A) removes several categories from that bar. Clause (iii) removes an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a State or Federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought. Every element generated litigation. Plaintiffs had to identify a statute, not a general duty drawn from the common law. The statute had to be applicable to the sale or marketing of the product. The violation had to be knowing, and the violation had to be a proximate cause of the particular harm pleaded. A complaint that failed on any one element remained inside the bar, however the underlying claim was labeled.

That structure made the interaction between Federal law and State law central. Congress wrote the exception in Federal terms, but the statutes most often invoked to satisfy it were State statutes, supplemented in some cases by Federal provisions governing firearm sales and records. The distinction between a codified State rule and an uncodified State duty, and between a statute that regulates the sale or marketing of firearms and a statute of general application, ran through the decisions in the Second, Ninth, and District of Columbia Circuits, through the Indiana decisions in the Gary litigation, and through the Connecticut decision in Soto. For a broader account of how the two levels of law divide authority over firearms, the [the federal and state gun laws guide](/2019/11/15/federal-vs-state-gun-laws/) collects the governing framework in one place. Within the predicate litigation itself, the question was whether the particular provision pleaded by a particular plaintiff counted as a statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product within clause (iii).

Litigation under the exception also carried constitutional challenges to the federal statute itself, resting on the Commerce Clause, the Tenth Amendment, separation of powers principles, and due process. Appellate courts rejected those challenges in the decisions described in this section. The body of law through May 15, 2021 consisted largely of case by case answers about pleaded statutes, pleaded violations, and the stage at which each action stood.

### What is the predicate exception?

The predicate exception preserves an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a State or Federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought in the case.

Each component of that definition did independent work. The requirement of a statute excluded common law theories, as the Ninth Circuit held in Ileto, because a negligence duty recognized by courts without codification is not itself a statute. The requirement that the statute be applicable to the sale or marketing of the product excluded statutes that regulated other subjects and reached a firearm only through a later chain of events, as the Second Circuit held for the New York criminal nuisance provision. The requirement of a knowing violation excluded theories that treated a lawful transaction as a violation merely because harm followed from a later misuse by another person, a point illustrated by Estate of Charlot v. Bushmaster Firearms, Inc., 628 F. Supp. 2d 174 (D.D.C. 2009), where a lawful sale to a licensed retailer did not violate the District statute invoked as the predicate.

The pleading stage mattered throughout. Several consequential rulings were made on motions to dismiss or to strike, before any trial. At that stage a court asks whether the complaint alleges a knowing violation of a qualifying statute and alleges proximate cause with sufficient specificity to place the action within clause (iii). A decision allowing an action to proceed at that stage, as in the Gary litigation in 2007 and in Williams in 2012, is a decision about the legal sufficiency of the predicate theory, not a finding that the violation occurred.

City of New York v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 524 F.3d 384 (2d Cir. 2008), supplied the fullest appellate exposition of the categories of statute that can qualify. The City had filed its action in 2000, and after enactment of the federal statute the action was dismissed under the statute. On appeal, the Second Circuit considered whether New York Penal Law section 240.45, the provision defining criminal nuisance in the second degree, fell within the predicate exception. The court held that section 240.45 is not within the exception. The provision addresses conduct that creates or maintains a condition endangering the safety or health of a considerable number of persons, in general terms that do not single out firearms, their sale, or their marketing. A statute of that generality did not become a statute applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms merely because the City sought to apply the provision to the distribution of firearms. The Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 9, 2009.

The enduring part of City of New York lies in the three categories the Second Circuit used to describe the statutes the exception encompasses. The first category consists of statutes that expressly regulate firearms, naming firearms or the transactions by which firearms are sold, transferred, or marketed. The second category consists of statutes that courts have applied to the sale and marketing of firearms. A provision in that category may be written in broader language, but a body of judicial application has treated the provision as governing the sale or marketing of firearms. The third category consists of statutes that clearly can be said to implicate the purchase and sale of firearms, reaching a provision whose subject and operation plainly concern purchase and sale even without an express reference and without a prior line of judicial application. The court concluded that section 240.45 fell outside all three categories. The provision did not expressly regulate firearms. Courts had not applied the provision to the sale and marketing of firearms in the manner the second category requires. The provision could not clearly be said to implicate the purchase and sale of firearms, because its prohibition reached a wide range of conditions and conduct without reference to commercial transactions in firearms. A plaintiff could therefore not convert a general nuisance prohibition into a predicate by alleging that distribution practices had created the kind of condition the prohibition describes, and dismissal of the City action followed.

In the same decision, the Second Circuit rejected Commerce Clause, Tenth Amendment, and separation of powers challenges to the federal statute. The Commerce Clause challenge questioned congressional power over the subject the statute regulates. The Tenth Amendment challenge questioned the effect of the federal bar on State authority. The separation of powers challenge questioned whether Congress, by requiring dismissal of a defined category of actions, had intruded on the judicial function. The court rejected each challenge and sustained the statute.

The Ninth Circuit reached the predicate question from a different pleading in Ileto v. Glock, Inc., 565 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2009), for which the Supreme Court denied certiorari on May 24, 2010. The claims were pleaded under California common law negligence and under the codified nuisance provisions of California law, Civil Code sections 3479 and 3480. The Ninth Circuit held the claims barred. The common law negligence theory failed as a predicate because common law theories are not statutes, and clause (iii) requires a violation of a statute. A duty that exists because courts have recognized the duty in negligence law does not satisfy the textual requirement, however closely the duty might concern the manner in which a product was sold. The codified nuisance provisions failed for a separate reason. Although sections 3479 and 3480 are statutes in form, the Ninth Circuit treated the provisions as general nuisance definitions that did not qualify as statutes applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms. Codification alone therefore did not end the inquiry. A plaintiff had to show both a statute and applicability to sale or marketing, and Ileto satisfied neither requirement. The court also recorded that the plaintiffs had not alleged a case within clause (iii)(II), the subparagraph covering a manufacturer or seller who aided, abetted, or conspired with another person to sell or dispose of a qualified product knowing, or having reasonable cause to believe, that the actual buyer was prohibited from possessing or receiving a firearm or ammunition under 18 U.S.C. section 922(g) or (n).

The District of Columbia litigation tested the federal statute from the constitutional side and from the predicate side in two decisions. In District of Columbia v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 940 A.2d 137 (D.C. 2008), the District of Columbia Court of Appeals upheld the federal statute against separation of powers and due process challenges brought in litigation over the District strict liability act. The court rejected both challenges, reinforcing in a second appellate system the conclusion the Second Circuit had reached on overlapping structural grounds.

Estate of Charlot addressed the predicate side of the District scheme. The statute invoked was the District Assault Weapon Manufacturing Strict Liability Act, enacted in 1990. The manufacturer sale at issue was a lawful sale of a rifle to a licensed retailer. The district court held that a lawful sale to a licensed retailer did not violate the 1990 act, so the predicate exception did not apply and the action was barred. The reasoning turns on the violation element rather than on the applicability element. Even a statute directed at the firearms industry cannot supply a predicate unless the defendant violated the statute, and conduct that falls outside the prohibition the statute imposes is not a violation for purposes of clause (iii).

The Indiana decisions in the Gary litigation followed a different course. In Smith & Wesson Corp. v. City of Gary, 875 N.E.2d 422 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), the Indiana Court of Appeals held that the claims of the City of Gary fell within the predicate exception at the pleading stage. The complaint alleged knowing violations of Indiana statutes, described in the decision as the public nuisance statute and regulatory statutes applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms. On those allegations, the federal statute did not bar the action at the pleading stage. Transfer was denied in January 2009. The contrast with City of New York and Ileto is precise. The Indiana court did not hold that common law claims are statutes. The court held that the particular complaint, predicated on Indiana statutes treated as applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms and alleging knowing violations, satisfied the exception for purposes of the motion before the court.

A later Indiana decision addressed a State law defense distinct from the federal predicate question. In City of Gary v. Smith & Wesson Corp., No. 18A-CT-181 (Ind. Ct. App. May 23, 2019), the Indiana Court of Appeals held that the 2015 amendment to the State immunity statute, which made that statute retroactive to August 26, 1999, did not bar all of the City claims. The decision is a ruling on the reach of the retroactive State amendment, delivered in the same litigation that had produced the 2007 predicate ruling.

Williams v. Beemiller, Inc., 100 A.D.3d 143 (N.Y. App. Div. 2012), illustrates the Federal side of the predicate definition. An intermediate New York appellate court sustained claims past a motion to dismiss where the plaintiffs alleged that a manufacturer had sold through a dealer engaged in straw sales, predicating the action on Federal statutes governing firearm sales and records, 18 U.S.C. sections 922 and 924. Williams turned on the allegations connecting the manufacturer to a dealer engaged in straw sales and on the sufficiency of those allegations at the dismissal stage, and the court sustained the claims past dismissal.

The Badger Guns litigation shows the seller conduct exceptions operating at trial. In the Badger Guns case, an action against a licensed dealer proceeded on negligent entrustment and statutory violation theories, the two exceptions addressed to a seller's own conduct at the counter. On October 13, 2015, a Milwaukee County jury returned a verdict for the plaintiffs and awarded just over five million dollars in compensatory and punitive damages. The jury found that the dealer had violated Federal law and had acted negligently in completing what the evidence showed was a straw sale. Negligent entrustment, as defined in section 7903(5)(B), concerns the supplying of a qualified product by a seller for use by another person when the seller knows, or reasonably should know, that the person supplied is likely to, and does, use the product in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical injury to that person or to others. The statutory violation theory concerns a knowing violation of a statute applicable to the sale, with proximate cause.

Estate of Kim v. Coxe, in Alaska, supplies the companion trial outcome on the entrustment side. The Alaska Supreme Court vacated a dismissal that had rested on the federal statute and held that a negligent entrustment claim against a dealer could fall within the exceptions. The case was tried after that ruling, and a jury found for the dealer. The sequence matters more than either step alone. Read beside the Badger Guns case, Kim completes a balanced pair of outcomes through May 15, 2021. In one case a jury found for the plaintiffs on entrustment and statutory violation theories arising from a straw sale. In the other case a jury found for the dealer after an appellate court held that an entrustment claim could proceed.

Soto v. Bushmaster Firearms International, LLC, 331 Conn. 53, 202 A.3d 262 (2019), produced the most divided appellate treatment of the predicate exception in the period. The Connecticut Supreme Court decision was four to three, and the holdings require statement in order. First, the court held that the trial court had properly struck the negligent entrustment and general negligence claims. No legally sufficient entrustment claim had been pleaded, and the court declined to extend the entrustment doctrine to the ordinary commercial sale of a lawful product in the circumstances pleaded. Second, the court held that the first theory under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act was time barred. That theory, that the sale of the rifle class was itself an unfair practice, was subject to the limitations period governing the claim, and the court held that the period had run. A theory that might otherwise have raised a predicate question never reached that question, because limitations disposed of the theory on its own terms.

Third, the Connecticut Supreme Court held that the narrower wrongful marketing theory could proceed within the predicate exception. The statute at issue was the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act, whose operative provision, Conn. Gen. Stat. section 42-110b(a), forbids unfair or deceptive acts or practices in trade or commerce. The court held that the Act is a statute applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms within clause (iii). The Connecticut court treated a trade practices statute of general wording as applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms where the claim concerned the marketing of firearms themselves and alleged unfair or deceptive marketing conduct directed at the product at issue. On that reasoning, applicability did not require that the statute mention firearms by name, and applicability did not require that the statute regulate only firearms. Fourth, the court recognized standing for the plaintiffs to predicate wrongful death claims on the trade practices statute for purposes of the claim that survived. The court then remanded the case for further proceedings on the wrongful marketing claim. The Supreme Court of the United States denied certiorari, sub nom. Remington Arms Co. v. Soto, 140 S. Ct. 513, on November 12, 2019, leaving the Connecticut construction in place without any merits ruling on the predicate question. The posture of Soto at May 15, 2021 requires exact statement. The case had returned to the Connecticut Superior Court and no judgment on the claim had been entered.

The statutes litigated as predicates through May 15, 2021 share a common feature. The statutes were pre existing laws. No State had enacted a purpose built predicate statute by May 15, 2021. The provisions that generated the appellate law in this section were laws of general or regulatory application that existed before the claims were brought. The Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act is a general trade practices statute. New York Penal Law section 240.45 is a general criminal nuisance provision. California Civil Code sections 3479 and 3480 are general codified nuisance provisions. The District of Columbia act of 1990 is an industry directed liability statute that predates the federal act by fifteen years. The Indiana statutes in the Gary litigation are State public nuisance and regulatory provisions assessed as applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms. The Federal provisions in Williams are the longstanding Federal sales and records provisions of chapter 44 of title 18.

One proposal existed at the vantage, and the proposal may be described by its terms only. New York Assembly Bill 6762 was introduced in the New York Assembly on March 29, 2021 by Assemblymember Patricia Fahy. The bill would have amended the General Business Law in relation to the dangers to safety and health and the creation of a public nuisance caused by the sale, manufacturing, distribution, importing and marketing of firearms. By its terms, the bill declared the illegal use of firearms a public nuisance. By its terms, the bill defined duties of reasonable controls and procedures for industry members, including screening against straw purchasers, traffickers, and persons prohibited from possessing firearms, and including security and inventory practices. The bill had been introduced and referred to committee. The bill had not been enacted. The terms summarized here are the terms of an introduced bill, not the terms of a law in force in New York or in any other State at that date.

By May 15, 2021 the predicate exception had produced a body of law with a coherent structure and unresolved edges. The decided cases agree on the questions. A court asks whether the plaintiff has identified a statute, whether that statute is applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, whether the complaint alleges a knowing violation, and whether the violation is alleged to be a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought, with the specified examples in clause (iii)(I) and (II) available where their facts are pleaded. Courts answered the applicability question differently on different statutes. The Second Circuit excluded a general criminal nuisance provision and described three categories for qualifying statutes. The Ninth Circuit excluded common law negligence and general codified nuisance provisions. The Indiana Court of Appeals allowed a complaint predicated on Indiana nuisance and regulatory statutes to proceed at the pleading stage. The Connecticut Supreme Court, by a four to three division, allowed a wrongful marketing theory predicated on a general trade practices act to proceed.

## The Case for the Bar and the Case Against It

The case for the bar begins with the findings Congress wrote into section 7901. The findings state that lawsuits had been commenced against manufacturers, distributors, dealers and importers of firearms that operate as designed and intended, seeking money damages and other relief for harm caused by the misuse of firearms by third parties, including criminals. The findings also state that the manufacture, importation, possession, sale and use of firearms and ammunition are heavily regulated by federal, state and local law, including the Gun Control Act of 1968, the National Firearms Act and the Arms Export Control Act. On that regulatory foundation, the findings declare that businesses engaged in interstate and foreign commerce through the lawful design, manufacture, marketing, distribution, importation or sale of firearms or ammunition products shipped in that commerce are not, and should not be, liable for harm caused by those who criminally or unlawfully misuse products that function as designed and intended. The possibility of imposing liability on an entire industry for harm solely caused by others is described as an abuse of the legal system, as a practice that erodes public confidence, as a threat to the diminution of a basic constitutional right and as an unreasonable burden on interstate and foreign commerce. The findings also state that such actions attempt to use the judicial branch to circumvent the legislative branch, threatening separation of powers, federalism and comity among the states. The purposes in section 7901 carry the same reasoning into operative aims: prohibiting causes of action for harm solely caused by others, preserving access to a supply of firearms and ammunition for all lawful purposes, and preserving separation of powers, federalism, state sovereignty and comity.

The House Judiciary Committee report on the bill, H. Rept. 109-124, presented the federal measure against a record in which states had acted first. The report catalogued state statutes state by state, with enactments concentrated in 1999 to 2001, and the committee proceedings in the spring of 2005 counted twenty-seven states that had enacted legislation prohibiting municipalities from initiating such lawsuits. The defenders' substantive claim, stated in its strongest form, is that the statute codifies the traditional rule that a lawful manufacturer or seller is not liable for a third party's crime committed with a non-defective product, and that the rule follows from where responsibility for the harm sits. A product that functioned as designed and intended did not fail in the manufacturer's or seller's hands. The criminal or unlawful misuse that produced the harm was the act of another person, and tort liability, on this account, attaches to conduct rather than to the lawful presence of a product in commerce. Once a claim depends on treating the lawful sale itself as the wrong, the defenders' argument runs, a court is being asked to convert a regulated commercial transaction into liability for a later crime, and to do by judgment what legislatures, which wrote the regulatory scheme, did not do by statute.

The preservation side of the defenders' case rests on the exceptions written into the definition of a qualified civil liability action. An action against a transferor convicted under 18 U.S.C. section 924(h) for knowingly transferring a firearm to a person prohibited from possessing one may be brought by a party directly harmed by the conduct underlying the conviction. An action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se is outside the bar. An action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a state or federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm, is outside the bar, including cases of false entries in records required by federal or state law and cases of aiding, abetting or conspiring in a sale to a person known or reasonably believed to be prohibited from possessing or receiving a firearm or ammunition. Actions for breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase of the product remain available, and an action for death, physical injuries or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture, when the product was used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner, remains available. Each preserved theory attaches to the defendant's own conduct: a defective product, a broken warranty promise, a sale to a person the seller knew or should have known presented an unreasonable risk, or a knowing violation of a statute governing the sale. On October 20, 2005, at House passage, Doug Painter, president of the National Shooting Sports Foundation, described bipartisan support in both chambers and the end of years of litigation the industry characterized as abuse of the legal system. The National Rifle Association made the bill its leading legislative priority of the period. In the defenders' framing, passage closed a category of claims that reached beyond a defendant's conduct while leaving the conduct based theories of ordinary tort and contract law in place.

The municipal plaintiffs and their counsel presented the opposing case from the litigation record itself. New Orleans filed the first municipal action in October 1998, and the Center to Prevent Handgun Violence served as co-counsel to New Orleans in that effort. By early 2000 approximately thirty cities and counties had filed actions, and the theories across the complaints included public nuisance, negligence in distribution and marketing, negligent entrustment, design defect and failure to warn, unjust enrichment and consumer protection counts. The distribution and marketing claims had a specific content. The complaints alleged oversupplying low regulation markets and failing to monitor or discipline dealers whose guns were traced to crimes, and the relief sought included the public costs of police, emergency, health and court services and injunctions restructuring distribution. The plaintiffs' working model was the state tobacco litigation that produced the 1998 Master Settlement Agreement, used as an example of litigation changing industry practice. In NAACP v. AcuSport, Inc., 271 F. Supp. 2d 435 (E.D.N.Y. 2003), Judge Jack B. Weinstein, after a bench trial, found clear and convincing evidence that some manufacturers' distribution practices were careless and contributed to a public nuisance. Judgment in that case went to the defendants because the plaintiff organization had not shown an injury distinct from that borne by the public generally, and no injunction issued, but the trial findings gave the opponents of the bar an evidentiary foundation: a court that heard the proof had found careless distribution practices in the record, even though the legal claim failed on a separate element. For the municipal plaintiffs, that sequence showed why the courthouse mattered. Litigation could develop facts about how products moved through distribution channels, facts that a legislature considering regulation, or a manufacturer considering a change in practice, would otherwise have had no comparable means of assembling.

Senator Carl Levin's floor opposition in 2003, preserved in the Congressional Record, argued that the bill would shield negligent and reckless dealers from legitimate civil lawsuits of the NAACP type. That argument placed the dealer, rather than the distant manufacturer, at the center of the objection. A dealer whose own practices were negligent or reckless, on Levin's account, stood in a different position from a lawful seller held responsible only because a product later figured in a crime, and a categorical bar risked treating the two positions as one. Legal scholars including Timothy D. Lytton of Georgia State University developed a related institutional argument. Lytton wrote that the municipal litigation generated evidence about distribution practices and that litigation was functioning as a regulatory instrument where legislatures had not acted. The critics' substantive claim, stated in its strongest form, follows from those premises: the statute removes the deterrent and the discovery mechanism that tort litigation supplies in every other industry. Deterrence, in tort law, works before judgment, through the prospect that careless distribution practices may be examined and priced in a civil action. Discovery works during litigation, through compelled production and testimony that bring internal distribution records into the open. A bar on the action removes both functions for the barred category, whether or not an individual claim would have succeeded on duty, causation or injury.

The structure of the exceptions is the second half of the critics' case. The claims most often brought in the municipal wave, negligence in distribution and marketing, fall outside the exceptions unless a statutory violation can be pleaded. Negligent entrustment reaches a seller who supplies a product to a person the seller knows, or reasonably should know, is likely to use the product in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical injury, a definition centered on the individual transaction. Negligence per se and the contract, warranty and defect theories each address a defendant's conduct, but none of them reproduces a distribution negligence claim directed at patterns across many sales, many dealers and many traces to crime. The predicate exception offers the only route for such a claim, and it requires a knowing violation of a state or federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, with the violation as a proximate cause of the harm. The appellate readings of that exception, in the cases decided by May 15, 2021, were narrow in the Second and Ninth Circuits. In City of New York v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 524 F.3d 384 (2d Cir. 2008), the court held that New York Penal Law section 240.45 is not within the predicate exception. In Ileto v. Glock, Inc., 565 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2009), claims pleaded under California common law negligence and the codified nuisance provisions were barred, because common law tort theories are not predicate statutes. Broader readings in Connecticut and Indiana left the scope of the exception contested, but for the critics the demanding pleading burden, joined to the narrow readings, shows a bar whose exceptions preserve the individual transaction claims while excluding the systemic distribution claims that the municipal litigation was built to present.

## How This Statute Compares With Other Federal Liability Limits

### Is this kind of federal liability limit unique to firearms?

No. Congress had limited or restructured civil liability in other industries before 2005, through compensation programs, liability channeling, damages caps and repose periods for nuclear power, vaccines, general aviation, implanted medical devices and anti-terrorism technologies, although no earlier federal measure in the comparison set used the PLCAA's exact combination of a categorical bar and conduct based exceptions.

The Price-Anderson Act of 1957, codified at 42 U.S.C. section 2210, supplies the oldest comparison in the set and the most structurally distant one. Price-Anderson channels and limits liability for nuclear incidents within a federally structured compensation and indemnification regime. Channeling, in that setting, directs claims arising from a nuclear incident into a single defined framework rather than leaving each defendant and each theory to be pursued separately under ordinary tort law, and the limit on liability operates inside a regime that pairs responsibility with indemnification. The PLCAA shares with Price-Anderson a congressional decision to displace part of the ordinary tort landscape for a defined subject, and the resemblance ends at that level of generality. The PLCAA channels nothing. A claim within one of its exceptions proceeds as an ordinary civil action in federal or state court, and a claim outside the exceptions is dismissed rather than redirected into a federal compensation framework. No indemnification arrangement stands behind a firearms manufacturer or seller under the PLCAA, and no aggregate limit governs the liability that the exceptions preserve. Where Price-Anderson altered how liability for nuclear incidents is financed and bounded, the PLCAA altered only whether one defined category of action may be brought at all.

The National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act of 1986, Public Law 99-660, took a different instrument again. The act created the no-fault National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program in the United States Court of Federal Claims. A person claiming a vaccine related injury files in the program first. Compensation for pain and suffering in the program is capped at 250,000 dollars, and a claimant who rejects the program's result may bring a tort action subject to statutory limitations. The sequence is the essential feature of the design. The program does not begin by asking whether a manufacturer was at fault; it provides a no-fault route to compensation first, and it preserves a tort route afterward for a claimant who declines the program's outcome, with limits attached to that later action. The PLCAA contains no sequence of that kind. No federal program receives a claim barred by section 7902, and no claimant election moves a claim from a compensation track into a tort track. The vaccine statute traded part of the tort remedy for a no-fault payment mechanism and capped one element of program compensation. The PLCAA made no such trade within its text. A plaintiff whose claim fell inside the barred category received no substitute forum from the statute, and a plaintiff whose claim fell inside an exception received the ordinary tort forum without a program stage in front of it.

The General Aviation Revitalization Act of 1994, Public Law 103-298, signed on August 17, 1994, limited liability through time rather than through a compensation structure. The act established an eighteen-year statute of repose. After that period, no civil action for damages for death, injury or property damage arising from an accident involving a general aviation aircraft, defined by fewer than twenty seats and no engagement in scheduled service, may be brought against the manufacturer of the aircraft or of a component. The repose period runs from the age of the aircraft or component rather than from the date of an injury, so the act cuts off claims that ordinary limitation periods, which run from accrual, might have allowed. The statute contains exceptions, including knowing misrepresentation to or concealment from the Federal Aviation Administration and claims on written warranties. A repose statute leaves the underlying liability rules intact and closes the courthouse after a defined interval, so claims arising early in a product's life proceed on ordinary terms. The PLCAA has no interval. Its bar applies from enactment to the defined category, reaching even actions pending on October 26, 2005, which section 7902(b) directed courts to dismiss immediately, and the age of a firearm or of ammunition plays no part in the analysis. Both statutes use exceptions tied to a defendant's conduct, concealment from a regulator in one statute and knowing statutory violation in the other, to mark the point at which the federal limit gives way.

The Biomaterials Access Assurance Act of 1998, Public Law 105-230, addressed a supply problem inside another industry's liability system. The act limits the liability of suppliers of raw materials and component parts used in implanted medical devices, absent the supplier's own culpable conduct. The protected position is therefore the upstream position: a supplier whose material or part became one element of a finished implanted device, and whose exposure arose from the finished device rather than from conduct of the supplier itself. That conduct qualification resembles the logic of the PLCAA exceptions, which preserve claims based on a defendant's own defect, warranty breach, negligent entrustment or knowing statutory violation. The scope of the two protections differs. The biomaterials statute protects a defined class of suppliers within the device litigation system, while the finished device and its manufacturer remain within that system on ordinary terms. The PLCAA protects manufacturers, sellers and trade associations against one defined claim category, claims resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the claimant or a third party, while preserving other claim categories against the same defendants. One statute sorts defendants by position in the supply chain. The other sorts claims by the source of the harm.

The SAFETY Act, enacted as part of the Homeland Security Act of 2002, Public Law 107-296, supplies the closest comparison in time and the clearest contrast in mechanism. The SAFETY Act provides liability protections and damages caps for sellers of qualified anti-terrorism technologies designated by the Secretary of Homeland Security. Designation is the gateway: a technology enters the protective framework through an administrative determination, and the protections, including caps on damages, then govern claims connected with the designated technology. The PLCAA has no designation step and no administrative gateway. Its coverage follows from statutory definitions of a qualified product, a manufacturer, a seller and a trade association, and from whether the product was shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. No secretary decides which firearms or which sellers receive the bar, and no damages cap moderates the liability preserved by the exceptions. A defect action or a negligent entrustment action that clears the exceptions proceeds without a statutory ceiling of the SAFETY Act kind, and a barred action is barred in full rather than capped. The two statutes also differ in the event that triggers the federal limit. The SAFETY Act framework addresses technologies deployed against terrorism, while the PLCAA addresses the criminal or unlawful misuse of a defined product, a triggering category drawn from the conduct of the person who used the product.

Read together, the comparison set shows Congress working with a recognizable set of instruments for limiting civil liability: caps on damages, compensation funds and no-fault programs, repose periods that close claims after a defined interval, channeling that gathers claims into a single regime, and protections for defined suppliers or designated technologies. The PLCAA uses none of the first four instruments in its operative text. The act creates no fund, caps no damages and sets no repose period, and it channels no claims into a federal forum. Its instrument is a categorical bar on one defined claim type, a qualified civil liability action, joined to conduct based exceptions that preserve defect, warranty, negligent entrustment, negligence per se and statutory violation claims. For claims resulting from a third party's criminal or unlawful misuse of a non-defective product, the bar is complete, and dismissal rather than compensation is the statutory result. For claims grounded in a defendant's own conduct, the statute leaves the ordinary civil system in place, uncapped, with no federal program behind it.

A separate federal exclusion places firearms outside another regulatory system altogether. Firearms and ammunition are outside the definition of a consumer product in 15 U.S.C. section 2052(a)(5), because that definition excludes articles subject to the manufacturers excise tax imposed by 26 U.S.C. section 4181, so the Consumer Product Safety Commission holds no recall authority over firearms of the kind it exercises over other consumer products. The exclusion predates the PLCAA and operates independently of the liability bar, and the two provisions answer different questions: the Consumer Product Safety Act definition governs whether a federal safety agency may order a recall, while the PLCAA governs whether a defined category of civil action may be brought in court. The excise tax connection behind the exclusion has a long statutory history of its own, traced in the [history of the federal firearm excise tax](/2018/03/01/firearm-excise-tax-history-guide/). The practical consequence is that a defective firearm is addressed through the defect exception in a civil action, through warranty and contract claims, and through any state law mechanism that applies, rather than through a federal recall order of the ordinary kind.

The comparisons therefore return to the text of sections 7901 to 7903 and to the line the text draws between misuse based claims and conduct based claims. Price-Anderson bounded and financed liability. The vaccine act paid claims through a program before preserving a limited tort action. The general aviation act closed claims after eighteen years. The biomaterials act sheltered upstream suppliers absent culpable conduct. The SAFETY Act capped damages for designated technologies. The PLCAA sheltered defined defendants by the distinct method of barring a claim category while enumerating the conduct based claims that survive. Much of the public argument about the statute has proceeded as if a broader description settled the question, a habit examined in the [recurring myths about federal gun law](/2021/02/15/federal-gun-law-myths/). The statutory text supports a narrower and more exact statement: no fund, no cap, no repose period, no channeling, and no designation, but a prohibition on qualified civil liability actions, with six exceptions that keep the defendant's own conduct inside the civil liability system.

## What the Text Settles and What It Leaves Open

The text settles the identity of the category that section 7902 reaches. A qualified civil liability action is a civil or administrative proceeding brought by any person, including a governmental entity, against a manufacturer or seller of a qualified product, or a trade association, for damages or other relief listed in section 7903(5)(A), resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the person or a third party. Each element is definitional, and failure on any element places a proceeding outside the category. The relief element reaches punitive damages, injunctive or declaratory relief, abatement, restitution, fines, penalties, and other relief alongside compensatory damages, and the governmental character of a plaintiff supplies no separate exit, because section 7903(3) provides that person includes any governmental entity.

The text settles the treatment of actions pending at enactment with equal directness. Section 7902(b) provides that a qualified civil liability action pending on October 26, 2005 shall be immediately dismissed by the court in which the action was brought or is pending. The dismissal command reached filings made years before passage, and the City of New York action filed in 2000 was dismissed under the federal statute. The text settles the converse proposition through the six exclusions inside the definition. An action within clause (i), (ii), (iii), (iv), (v), or (vi) is not a qualified civil liability action at all, so the prohibition has no defined object in such a case and the dismissal command has no application. The rule of construction in section 7903(5)(C) settles the source of rights in those cases as well. The exclusions preserve actions supplied by other law and create no cause of action or remedy, so every preserved claim depends on the contract, warranty, tort, or statutory law that supplies its elements.

The preservation of defect and warranty claims is settled on the face of the clauses. Clause (iv) excludes an action for breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase of the product, and clause (v) excludes an action for death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture when the product was used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner. The Carter class action, Carter v. Forjas Taurus, S.A., No. 1:13-cv-24583-PAS (S.D. Fla.), documents the defect channel in operation, with a class settlement approved by the district court in 2016 and affirmed by the Eleventh Circuit in 2017 at 701 Fed. Appx. 759. The settlement gave an owner a choice between return for a payment of up to 200 dollars and inspection and repair under an enhanced lifetime warranty, and the manufacturer denied any defect or wrongdoing. Clause (v) also carries one internal qualification that the text settles expressly. Where the discharge of the product was caused by a volitional act that constituted a criminal offense, that act is considered the sole proximate cause of any resulting death, personal injuries, or property damage for purposes of the clause.

The constitutional challenges decided through May 15, 2021 were rejected by the appellate courts that heard them, and that part of the record is settled for the cases in which the challenges were raised. In City of New York v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., the Second Circuit rejected Commerce Clause, Tenth Amendment, and separation of powers challenges to the federal statute in the same 2008 decision that addressed the predicate question. In District of Columbia v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 940 A.2d 137 (D.C. 2008), the District of Columbia Court of Appeals upheld the federal statute against separation of powers and due process challenges brought in litigation over the District strict liability act. No court in the record through May 15, 2021 held the federal statute unconstitutional.

The predicate exception is where the text leaves the largest question open, because the decided cases answer the applicability element in opposed directions on different statutes. The Second Circuit in City of New York held that New York Penal Law section 240.45, a criminal nuisance provision of general terms, is not within the predicate exception, and the court described the qualifying statutes through three categories covering statutes that expressly regulate firearms, statutes that courts have applied to the sale and marketing of firearms, and statutes that clearly can be said to implicate the purchase and sale of firearms. The Ninth Circuit in Ileto v. Glock, Inc., 565 F.3d 1126 (9th Cir. 2009), held claims pleaded under California common law negligence and the codified nuisance provisions at California Civil Code sections 3479 and 3480 barred, because common law theories are not statutes and the codified provisions did not qualify on the allegations presented. Against those narrow readings stand the broader readings in Connecticut and Indiana. The Connecticut Supreme Court in Soto v. Bushmaster Firearms International, LLC, 331 Conn. 53, 202 A.3d 262 (2019), held by four votes to three that a wrongful marketing theory under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act could proceed within the predicate exception, and the Indiana Court of Appeals in Smith & Wesson Corp. v. City of Gary, 875 N.E.2d 422 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), held that the Gary claims fell within the exception at the pleading stage on allegations of knowing violations of Indiana statutes applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms. No decision in the record through May 15, 2021 reconciles the narrow and broader approaches into a single national reading of clause (iii), and the exception therefore carries different practical scope depending on the statute pleaded and the court construing the applicability element.

A second open point concerns the predicate statutes themselves. The statutes litigated as predicates through May 15, 2021 were preexisting laws of general or regulatory application, including the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act, New York Penal Law section 240.45, the California nuisance provisions, the District of Columbia strict liability act of 1990, and the Indiana statutes in the Gary litigation. No State had enacted a statute drafted to operate as a predicate for firearms industry liability by May 15, 2021. Assembly Bill 6762, introduced in the New York Assembly on March 29, 2021 by Assemblymember Patricia Fahy, was a proposal described by its terms, declaring the illegal use of firearms a public nuisance and defining duties of reasonable controls and procedures for industry members. The bill had been introduced and referred to committee and had not been enacted at that date. Whether a statute written in that form would satisfy clause (iii) is a question the record through May 15, 2021 does not answer, because no enacted statute of that design had been construed by a court.

Whether the exceptions are meaningful in practice is an open question on the record through May 15, 2021, stated here as open and without a verdict. The ledger that frames the question contains material pointing in more than one direction. No municipal plaintiff obtained a judgment, and most first wave actions were dismissed, withdrawn, or ended by State statute. Two cases reached juries under the exceptions, one for the plaintiffs in the Badger Guns litigation, with a verdict on October 13, 2015 and an award of just over five million dollars in compensatory and punitive damages, and one for the dealer in the Alaska Kim litigation, after the Alaska Supreme Court vacated a dismissal that had rested on the federal statute and held that a negligent entrustment claim could fall within the exceptions. The defect channel produced the Carter class settlement. The predicate channel produced opposed appellate readings rather than a uniform construction. The record supports the statement of the dispute and the inventory of outcomes. The record does not support a single conclusion about the practical sufficiency of the exceptions, and that assessment remains where the decided cases and the statutory text leave it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does PLCAA actually bar?

The act bars a qualified civil liability action, and no other category of action, in any Federal or State court under section 7902(a) of title 15. Section 7903(5)(A) defines that category as a civil action or proceeding or an administrative proceeding brought by any person, including a governmental entity, against a manufacturer or seller of a qualified product, or a trade association, for damages, punitive damages, injunctive or declaratory relief, abatement, restitution, fines, or penalties, or other relief, resulting from the criminal or unlawful misuse of a qualified product by the person or a third party. Section 7902(b) separately directs immediate dismissal of an action within the category that was pending on October 26, 2005.

### Q: What are the six exceptions in PLCAA?

Section 7903(5)(A) states that a qualified civil liability action does not include six categories. Clause (i) covers an action against a transferor convicted of a knowing transfer to a prohibited person, brought by a party directly harmed by the conduct underlying the conviction. Clause (ii) covers an action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se. Clause (iii) covers an action based on a knowing violation of a statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, with proximate causation. Clause (iv) covers breach of contract or warranty in connection with the purchase. Clause (v) covers death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture. Clause (vi) covers enforcement proceedings by the Attorney General.

### Q: What is the predicate exception in PLCAA?

The predicate exception is clause (iii) of section 7903(5)(A). The exception preserves an action in which a manufacturer or seller knowingly violated a State or Federal statute applicable to the sale or marketing of the product, and the violation was a proximate cause of the harm for which relief is sought. Clause (iii)(I) addresses false entries, missing entries, and false or fictitious statements material to the lawfulness of a sale in records required under Federal or State law. Clause (iii)(II) addresses a manufacturer or seller who aided, abetted, or conspired with another person to sell or dispose of a product to an actual buyer known, or reasonably believed, to be prohibited under section 922(g) or section 922(n) of title 18.

### Q: Can you still sue for a defective gun under PLCAA?

A defect claim falls outside the bar when the action meets clause (v) of section 7903(5)(A). That clause preserves an action for death, physical injuries, or property damage resulting directly from a defect in design or manufacture of the product, when used as intended or in a reasonably foreseeable manner. Clause (iv) separately preserves breach of contract or warranty claims in connection with the purchase, and contract or warranty law may address a related dispute about the condition of a product. Clause (v) contains one qualification: where the discharge was caused by a volitional act that constituted a criminal offense, that act is considered the sole proximate cause of the resulting death, personal injuries, or property damage. The Carter class settlement documents the defect channel in operation.

### Q: What did Soto v. Bushmaster do to PLCAA?

Soto v. Bushmaster Firearms International, LLC, 331 Conn. 53, 202 A.3d 262 (2019), was a four to three decision of the Connecticut Supreme Court construing the predicate exception. The court held that the trial court had properly struck the negligent entrustment and general negligence claims, that a first theory under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act was time barred, and that a narrower wrongful marketing theory could proceed because that statute is applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms within clause (iii), with standing recognized for the plaintiffs on that claim. The Supreme Court of the United States denied certiorari on November 12, 2019, at 140 S. Ct. 513. The case returned to the Connecticut Superior Court, and no judgment had been entered at May 15, 2021.

### Q: Why did Congress pass PLCAA?

Congress stated its reasons in the findings and purposes at section 7901, and those statements are attributed to the enacted text. The findings state that lawsuits had been commenced against manufacturers, distributors, dealers, and importers of firearms that operate as designed and intended, seeking relief for harm caused by the misuse of firearms by third parties, including criminals, in a field heavily regulated by Federal, State, and local law. The findings state that businesses engaged in lawful commerce in such products are not, and should not be, liable for harm caused by criminal or unlawful misuse, and describe the described actions as an unreasonable burden on interstate and foreign commerce and an attempt to use the judicial branch to circumvent the legislative branch. The purposes include prohibiting such causes of action and preserving access to a supply of firearms and ammunition for lawful purposes.

### Q: Do states write laws to trigger the PLCAA predicate exception?

No State had enacted a statute drafted to operate as a predicate for firearms industry liability by May 15, 2021. The statutes litigated as predicates up to that date were preexisting laws of general or regulatory application, including the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act, New York Penal Law section 240.45, the California nuisance provisions, the District of Columbia strict liability act, and the Indiana statutes in the Gary litigation. One proposal may be described by its terms. Assembly Bill 6762, introduced in the New York Assembly on March 29, 2021 by Assemblymember Patricia Fahy, would have amended the General Business Law in relation to dangers to safety and health and the creation of a public nuisance caused by the sale, manufacturing, distribution, importing, and marketing of firearms, declaring the illegal use of firearms a public nuisance and defining duties of reasonable controls and procedures for industry members. The bill had been introduced and referred to committee and had not been enacted.

### Q: Is PLCAA immunity unique among industries?

No. Congress had limited or restructured civil liability for other industries before 2005, using instruments that differ from the categorical bar and conduct based exceptions in sections 7901 to 7903. The Price-Anderson Act channels and limits liability for nuclear incidents within a compensation and indemnification regime. The National Childhood Vaccine Injury Act created a no fault compensation program, with a capped pain and suffering element and a limited tort route after rejection of a program result. The General Aviation Revitalization Act imposed an eighteen year statute of repose. The Biomaterials Access Assurance Act limits liability for suppliers of raw materials and component parts for implanted devices absent culpable conduct. The SAFETY Act provides protections and damages caps for designated anti terrorism technologies. The federal statute for firearms uses none of those instruments in its operative text.

### Q: What is a qualified product under PLCAA?

Section 7903(4) defines a qualified product as a firearm as defined in section 921(a)(3)(A) or (B) of title 18, including any antique firearm as defined in section 921(a)(16), or ammunition as defined in section 921(a)(17)(A), or a component part of a firearm or ammunition, that has been shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce. The definition therefore has a product element and a commerce element. A firearm, a round of ammunition, or a component part must fall within one of the title 18 definitions the clause incorporates, and the product must have the shipment or transport connection the closing words of the definition require. A product lacking either element does not satisfy the definition.

### Q: Who counts as a manufacturer or seller under PLCAA?

Section 7903(2) defines a manufacturer as a person engaged in the business of manufacturing the product in interstate or foreign commerce and licensed as a manufacturer under chapter 44 of title 18. Section 7903(6) defines a seller through three branches: a licensed importer engaged in the business as such an importer, a licensed dealer engaged in the business as such a dealer, and a person engaged in the business of selling ammunition in interstate or foreign commerce at the wholesale or retail level. Section 7903(1) gives engaged in the business the meaning of section 921(a)(21) of title 18, with a specified livelihood and profit meaning for ammunition sellers. Licensed status, business engagement, and commerce therefore all enter the defendant definitions.

### Q: What happened to lawsuits that were already pending when PLCAA took effect?

Section 7902(b) states the rule for pending actions in direct terms. A qualified civil liability action that was pending on October 26, 2005, the date of enactment, shall be immediately dismissed by the court in which the action was brought or is pending. The provision therefore reached actions filed before passage, including actions filed during the municipal litigation wave that began in 1998, rather than governing only filings made after enactment. The command applies to an action within the defined category and outside every exclusion in section 7903(5)(A). An action that met an exclusion was not a qualified civil liability action under the definition, so the dismissal command addressed to that category had no object in such a case.

### Q: Has PLCAA been held unconstitutional by any court?

No. The constitutional challenges in the appellate record through May 15, 2021 were rejected. In City of New York v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 524 F.3d 384 (2d Cir. 2008), the Second Circuit rejected Commerce Clause, Tenth Amendment, and separation of powers challenges to the federal statute. In District of Columbia v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 940 A.2d 137 (D.C. 2008), the District of Columbia Court of Appeals upheld the federal statute against separation of powers and due process challenges brought in litigation over the District strict liability act. Those holdings resolve the challenges presented in those cases under the reasoning each court gave. The record described contains no holding that the federal statute is unconstitutional.

### Q: What does the negligent entrustment exception require a plaintiff to show?

Clause (ii) preserves an action against a seller for negligent entrustment or negligence per se, and section 7903(5)(B) defines negligent entrustment for the chapter. The definition requires the supplying of a qualified product by a seller for use by another person, at a time when the seller knows, or reasonably should know, that the person supplied is likely to, and does, use the product in a manner involving unreasonable risk of physical injury to the person or others. The knowledge element has a subjective limb and an objective limb. The likelihood element and the actual use element are separate, and both must be present under the paired words of the definition. The governing tort law outside the chapter supplies the claim itself, because the federal statute creates no cause of action.

### Q: Did PLCAA end the New York City lawsuit against gun manufacturers?

The City of New York action, filed in federal court in 2000, was dismissed under the federal statute. In City of New York v. Beretta U.S.A. Corp., 524 F.3d 384 (2d Cir. 2008), the Second Circuit held that New York Penal Law section 240.45, the criminal nuisance provision the City invoked, is not within the predicate exception. The court described the statutes the exception encompasses as statutes that expressly regulate firearms, statutes that courts have applied to the sale and marketing of firearms, and statutes that clearly can be said to implicate the purchase and sale of firearms, and the court placed section 240.45 outside those categories. The Second Circuit also rejected the constitutional challenges in the case, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 9, 2009.

### Q: What happened to the City of Gary lawsuit under PLCAA?

The Gary litigation is described here only through decided rulings. In City of Gary v. Smith & Wesson Corp., 801 N.E.2d 1243 (Ind. 2003), the Indiana Supreme Court held that the city public nuisance claim could proceed, reversing the dismissal of that claim in an action filed in September 1999. In Smith & Wesson Corp. v. City of Gary, 875 N.E.2d 422 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), the Indiana Court of Appeals held that the claims fell within the predicate exception at the pleading stage, because the complaint alleged knowing violations of Indiana statutes applicable to the sale or marketing of firearms, and transfer was denied in January 2009. In City of Gary v. Smith & Wesson Corp., No. 18A-CT-181 (Ind. Ct. App. May 23, 2019), the court held that the 2015 amendment making the State statute retroactive to August 26, 1999 did not bar all of the city claims.

### Q: Can a gun dealer be sued for a straw sale under PLCAA?

An action against a dealer over a straw sale can fall within the exceptions addressed to a seller's own conduct at the counter, principally negligent entrustment under clause (ii) and the statutory violation theory under clause (iii), including clause (iii)(II) for participation in a sale to an actual buyer known, or reasonably believed, to be prohibited under section 922(g) or (n) of title 18. The Badger Guns litigation in Milwaukee County Circuit Court documents that route at trial. On October 13, 2015, a jury found that the dealer had violated Federal law and acted negligently in completing what the evidence showed was a straw sale, in an action by two police officers injured with a handgun the dealer had sold, and awarded just over five million dollars in compensatory and punitive damages.

### Q: Does PLCAA block the federal government from enforcing gun laws?

No. Clause (vi) of section 7903(5)(A) excludes an action or proceeding commenced by the Attorney General to enforce the provisions of chapter 44 of title 18 or chapter 53 of title 26 from the definition of a qualified civil liability action. An enforcement proceeding of that kind is therefore outside the bar in section 7902(a) and outside the dismissal direction in section 7902(b). The exclusion turns on both the plaintiff and the purpose: the proceeding must be commenced by the Attorney General, and the proceeding must be brought to enforce one of the two chapters the clause cites. A private or municipal damages action does not become a clause (vi) proceeding merely because the complaint discusses those chapters.

### Q: What is the Child Safety Lock Act of 2005 and how does it relate to PLCAA?

The Child Safety Lock Act of 2005 formed part of the same public law, Public Law 109-92, that enacted sections 7901 to 7903 of title 15. That portion of the enactment amended chapter 44 of title 18 to require that handguns transferred by licensees be accompanied by a secure gun storage or safety device, subject to exceptions. The Senate adopted amendments that included the child safety lock requirements before passing S. 397 on July 29, 2005, and the House of Representatives passed the Senate bill in that form on October 20, 2005. The lock requirement attaches at transfer through the licensing structure of chapter 44. The relationship between the two parts is therefore enactment in a single measure, with the liability provisions in title 15 and the lock provisions operating through title 18.

### Q: How does PLCAA differ from the liability limits Congress gave other industries?

The difference lies in the instrument Congress selected. The Price-Anderson Act channels and limits liability for nuclear incidents inside a compensation and indemnification regime, and the vaccine statute routes a claimant through a no fault program before a limited tort action. The General Aviation Revitalization Act closes claims after an eighteen year repose period, the biomaterials statute protects upstream suppliers absent culpable conduct, and the SAFETY Act caps damages for designated technologies. Sections 7901 to 7903 create no fund, cap no damages, set no repose period, channel no claims into a Federal forum, and require no administrative designation. The operative instrument is a prohibition on one defined category of action, joined to six exclusions that preserve claims based on a defendant's own conduct or on the condition of a product.

### Q: What should a student remember about PLCAA for an exam?

The definition decides the case. An examination answer should begin with section 7903(5)(A), identify the plaintiff, defendant, product, relief, and causation elements of a qualified civil liability action, and then test the claim against clauses (i) through (vi) before reaching the merits of any underlying theory. The six exceptions preserve a convicted transferor action, negligent entrustment and negligence per se against a seller, a knowing statutory violation with proximate causation, contract and warranty claims, defect claims under stated conditions, and Attorney General enforcement. The predicate exception carries the principal dispute, with narrow readings in the Second and Ninth Circuits and broader readings in Connecticut and Indiana. Section 7902(b) and its immediate dismissal command for actions pending on October 26, 2005 complete the framework.
