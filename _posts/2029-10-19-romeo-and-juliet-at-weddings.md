# The Play at Weddings and in Vows: Irony, Readings and Ritual

Romeo and Juliet is the only tragedy in the language that doubles as wedding stationery. Its speeches are read aloud at ceremonies, copied into vows, printed on invitations and table cards, and staged as decor, while its plot, two teenagers married in secret on a Monday and dead before the week ends, is the precise opposite of the event the words are hired to bless. No other canonical text is asked to perform this double duty. Couples do not read from Hamlet at christenings or borrow Macbeth for anniversary cards. The lovers of Verona alone are summoned, week after week, to preside over the rite their own story renders impossible, and the summons is issued with full knowledge of how the story ends. Every guest at every ceremony where the bounty speech is read already knows the ending. The reading proceeds anyway. That persistence, not the irony alone, is the phenomenon this article examines: what couples take from the play, what they leave behind, what the trade that supplies the words does to the text in transit, and whether the borrowing is a falsification or a defensible act of editing.

![A wedding reading drawn from the balcony scene of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-101.webp)

The verdict, stated at the outset and defended through the evidence that follows, divides the practice into two operations that are usually confused. The first operation is excerpting: a couple takes the balcony scene's pledge language, words that are in their original setting reciprocal, future-facing and spoken as the substance of a betrothal, and re-performs them as the substance of a marriage. That operation is defensible, and the strongest evidence for the defence sits inside the scene itself, where Juliet proposes the marriage, sets its terms and supplies the words the trade keeps reprinting. The second operation is branding: the sales layer of guides, venues and municipalities that presents the lovers not as speakers of fine vows but as proof that intense love succeeds, an emblem of the outcome the couple hopes to copy. That operation is falsification, because the play's outcome is the one fact about it that cannot be excerpted away, and because the play's own first advertisement, the Prologue, refuses the frame in advance. The charge of amputation, which this article was commissioned to weigh, therefore lands unevenly. It convicts the marketing. It largely acquits the marrying couples, whose documented behaviour, reading the warnings and proceeding, keeping the poison bottles on the tables at themed receptions, marrying in the friar's own convent with the tomb a stair below, shows editors at work rather than an audience deceived.

The evidence base matters, because claims about wedding fashion inflate easily. What follows rests on the reading trade's own documents: the wedding-reading guides that print the passages and grade them, the styled shoots and reported real weddings that built ceremonies around the play, the municipal record of Verona's marriage programme since 2009, and the text of the play in the Folger Shakespeare Library edition, cited by act, scene and line. Where a claim rests on a guide's list, the guide is named. Where a claim rests on Verona's tariff, the schedule's year is named. Where the record runs out, the article says so and stops.

## The Play's Only Wedding Is a Secret Kept from Every Guest

Begin with the ceremony the play actually contains, because the wedding use of the text borrows everything except it. The marriage of Romeo and Juliet takes place offstage, in Friar Laurence's cell, at the close of Act 2, and the audience is not shown it. No family attends. No friend attends. There are no witnesses beyond the officiant, no ring is mentioned, no feast follows, and the scene that precedes the rite is a warning delivered by the man about to perform it. The Friar greets the dawn with a lecture on the poison and medicine that share every plant, agrees to the marriage for a political reason, that the alliance may "turn your households' rancor to pure love" (2.3.91-92), and then, at the cell, cautions the groom in the play's most quoted piece of wedding advice and its least quoted at weddings: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9). The ceremony itself occupies no lines at all. Shakespeare stages the negotiation, the warning and the exit, "Come, come with me, and we will make short work" (2.6.35), and withholds the rite. A full account of that withheld rite, its brevity and its missing witnesses, belongs to the companion study of [Act 2 Scene 6: the secret wedding at the cell](/2023/09/01/act-2-scene-6-secret-wedding/), and is taken here as read.

The wedding the play does stage, at full length and with the household in festival dress, is the one that never happens. Capulet's preparations for Juliet's marriage to Paris fill Act 4 with cooks, musicians and a father awake all night in supervision, and the bride is found dead on the morning appointed for it. Capulet's own summary converts the two ceremonies into each other in a single breath: "Our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast; / Our solemn hymns to sullen dirges change" (4.5.87-88). The play thus offers the wedding industry a precise inventory: one real wedding, invisible, unwitnessed and fatal within days; one visible wedding, feasted, witnessed and converted into a funeral before the bride reaches the church. Neither resembles the ceremony at which the play's words are read. The public, licensed, witnessed, feasted rite that modern couples perform has no counterpart anywhere in the text. Its nearest relative in the play is the Paris wedding, the one the heroine dies to escape.

History explains why the secret cell wedding was not, to its first audiences, the irregularity it appears to modern eyes. The historian John R. Gillis, in For Better, For Worse: British Marriages, 1600 to the Present (1985), reconstructs a world in which betrothal, the spoken exchange of promises before witnesses, carried a binding force close to marriage itself, and in which clandestine unions, contracted without banns or parental stage management, were a persistent feature of the marriage system rather than an exotic crime. The lovers' arrangement, a private contract ratified by a friar, sits inside that older grammar: irregular, punishable, but intelligible as a marriage in a way a modern elopement to a registry office, fully legal and merely private, is not. Gillis's larger finding governs this whole article's method. Across four centuries of evidence, he argues, marital ideals and marital practices stand in perpetual tension, each generation denouncing the gap and each generation living in it. The couple in the cell and the couple at the altar rail of a modern ceremony are not separated by sincerity. They are separated by which half of the tension, the ideal or the practice, their rite is built to display.

What the play's wedding lacks, measured against the modern rite, is not love or even validity. It lacks an audience and a future. The modern ceremony is engineered around both: witnesses are legally required, and the vows are drafted in the future tense, pledging conduct across decades neither party can inspect. The lovers' vows, by contrast, are spoken in a garden at night to an audience of one concealed listener, and the marriage they found lasts from Monday afternoon to Thursday. When a modern couple reads the balcony speeches aloud before two hundred witnesses, the borrowing reverses every term of the original transaction except the words. Privacy becomes publicity. Haste becomes deliberation, since the reading was chosen months in advance from a printed list. The unwitnessed contract becomes the most witnessed moment of the day. And the future the original speakers never received is the entire point of the exercise. The words survive the reversal intact, which is the first clue that the words, rather than the plot, are what the ceremony is buying.

The play does eventually give its marriage a congregation, and the congregation's identity completes the inversion the ceremony performs. The union is made public in the tomb, in Act 5, before both fathers, the Prince, the Friar and the watch, the full civic assembly the wedding lacked, gathered around the bodies to hear the marriage announced as evidence in a catastrophe inquiry. Verona's families learn of the wedding at the funeral, from the officiant's confession, and ratify it posthumously in gold statues. The lovers' story thus contains exactly one fully witnessed, publicly proclaimed celebration of the marriage, and it is the obsequies. A modern couple reading the speeches before two hundred guests is supplying, four centuries late and at the correct end of the story, the congregation the marriage was denied. The borrowing can be described, in this light, as restitution: the rite the couple performs is the one the play's world owed its lovers and delivered only to their corpses.

## The Balcony Scene Is a Betrothal, and the Guides Are Right About Its Words

The standard objection to the wedding use begins from genre: a tragedy cannot supply a wedding. The objection dissolves on inspection of the scene the trade actually mines, because Act 2 Scene 2 is not a love scene in the loose sense. It is a contract negotiation, conducted with a precision about terms that any registrar would recognise, and it ends with a marriage proposed, accepted and scheduled. Romeo opens beneath the window with the soliloquy the decor trade prints, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3), but the scene's business begins when the speakers find each other and start, in earnest, to settle what their words commit them to. A scene-by-scene account of that negotiation is the subject of the companion analysis of [the balcony scene: Act 2 Scene 2 explained](/2023/07/21/balcony-scene-act-2-scene-2-analysis/); the argument here needs only its contractual skeleton.

The skeleton is reciprocal. Romeo asks for an exchange, not a tribute: "The exchange of thy love's faithful vow for mine" (2.2.127). Juliet answers that the exchange has already occurred and regrets only that it cannot be performed again for the pleasure of the performing: "I gave thee mine before thou didst request it: / And yet I would it were to give again" (2.2.128-129). Then comes the passage the guides print, the speech on which the play's whole wedding afterlife rests: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133-135). Read in place, the speech is not a compliment. It is a theory of the marital economy, stated as a paradox of non-rivalrous goods: love, unlike land or money, increases by expenditure. A wedding reading asks a couple to pledge exactly that theory before witnesses. The pledge is grammatically and ritually complete. It names no condition, sets no term and depends on no sequel. Of all the speeches in the play, it is the one that least needs its context to function, which is a fact about its construction, not about the carelessness of its borrowers.

### Is the balcony scene a betrothal or only a love scene?

The balcony scene is a betrothal in substance: the lovers exchange vows in terms (2.2.127-129), Juliet proposes marriage outright and fixes the mechanism, a messenger sent the next day (2.2.143-151), and the pair part on a contract the cell ceremony ratifies. The love language surrounds a legal act, which is why the scene transfers to a wedding without changing function.

Juliet, moreover, audits the transaction as it proceeds, and her audit is the passage the guides omit. Offered Romeo's oath by the moon, she refuses the instrument: "O, swear not by the moon, the inconstant moon, / That monthly changes in her circled orb, / Lest that thy love prove likewise variable" (2.2.109-111). Offered the contract itself, she accepts the man and distrusts the speed: "Although I joy in thee, / I have no joy of this contract tonight: / It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden; / Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be / Ere one can say 'It lightens'" (2.2.116-120). Then, having issued the warning, she issues the proposal, in the scene's plainest prose-like verse: "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow" (2.2.143-144). The sequence deserves emphasis, because it reverses the wedding trade's picture of the speaker. The heroine of the guides is a girl overwhelmed. The heroine of the scene is a negotiator who rejects an unstable oath, names the risk in the contract, and then sets the contract's terms, its timetable and its channel of communication. The bounty speech sits inside that negotiation as its emotional capital, the fund on which the contract draws. Couples who read the whole scene, as some do, are not borrowing a swoon. They are borrowing a negotiation in which the bride does the drafting.

René Weis, editor of the Arden Third Series edition of the play (2012), prints the scene from the Second Quarto text in which Juliet's word for the night's work is "contract," and the editorial tradition he represents insists on the point the anthologies blur: the scene's vows are preliminary to a marriage the speaker herself schedules, and her "too rash" speech is not a lover's modesty but a risk assessment the play proceeds to vindicate in full. On the editorial view, excerpting the bounty speech without its neighbouring caution is like printing a bond without its covenants; the instrument still circulates, but its terms have been altered by the cut. This article accepts the editorial description of what the cut removes. Whether the removal invalidates the borrowing is a separate question, and it is the question the remaining evidence, from the guides, the ceremonies, the decor studios and Verona, is assembled to answer.

One further feature of the scene fits it for ceremonial reuse, and it is structural rather than verbal. The vows of Act 2 are spoken without any officiant, liturgy or form of words supplied by an institution. The lovers invent the rite as they go: an overheard soliloquy becomes a dialogue, the dialogue becomes an exchange, the exchange becomes a proposal, and the proposal generates its own logistics. The modern ceremony, in the form couples increasingly build for themselves from readings, music and personal vows, follows the same architecture for the same reason. Where the state or the church supplies a complete script, couples add nothing. Where the script leaves licensed gaps, as civil ceremonies do, couples fill them with borrowed literature, and they reach for texts in which lovers speak for themselves rather than texts in which institutions speak for lovers. The balcony scene is the supreme case of lovers speaking for themselves. Its migration into the ceremony is, at this level, not an accident of fame but a fit of form.

## A Duet in the Text: The Feast Sonnet as Ceremony Script

One passage in the play is built, formally, as a ceremony for two voices, and its near-absence from the guides' lists is the selection pattern's most instructive anomaly. At the Capulet feast, Romeo and Juliet's first conversation takes the shape of a sonnet spoken between them, fourteen lines divided between two speakers who have known each other for the length of a dance. Romeo opens with a pilgrimage conceit, his hand an unworthy pilgrim at a holy shrine; Juliet answers inside the same form, correcting the conceit's theology while extending it, and the sonnet's structure performs the courtship it describes: proposition, response, negotiation, and a close that bargains for a kiss as the form's own completion. The exchange is the only place in the play where the lovers speak in a single shared form, neither soliloquising nor overhearing, and it demonstrates in miniature the quality the wedding trade keeps certifying in the balcony scene, two people composing a rite between them in real time, without script, officiant or precedent.

The sonnet's ceremonial fitness is exact. A reading for two voices is a standing option in the modern rite, chosen by couples who want the community's witness work shared between friends or between the partners themselves, and the feast sonnet is the only famous love dialogue in the language that arrives pre-scored for the format: balanced parts, a theological conceit a secular congregation can hear as devotion rather than doctrine, and a dramatic situation, strangers at a party, closer to most couples' actual first meetings than a garden at midnight. Its neglect by the guides, against the bounty speech's ubiquity, measures the trade's preference for extractable monologue over dialogue: a listicle can print three lines of Juliet; a duet requires the compiler to imagine a performance. The couples who find the sonnet anyway, in editions rather than in guides, recover the play's most wedding-shaped artifact entire, a form in which the vow's reciprocity is not described but enacted, line answering line until the form closes. The wedding note records the first-sight couplet that the film carried into the decor trade; the sonnet behind it waits in the text as the ceremony's unused best evidence that the play contains, alongside its warnings, a working model of two people talking themselves into a union in public.

## The Reading Trade: What the Guides Recommend, and What They Warn

The documentary record of the reading trade is thinner and more honest than the irony-first account of it suggests. The Wedding Secret's Shakespeare page, the fullest of the guides consulted, introduces the play with its genre stated plainly: the work is a tragedy, the deaths at the end may not be lines a couple wants at a wedding, and the romantic material sits early in the text. The same page then prints two passages from the play, the bounty speech, carefully labelled with Juliet's name and its line numbers, and the little-stars speech from Act 3 Scene 2, and closes its advice with a warning that couples should take care to understand the context behind any lines they choose. SoGlos, listing thirteen Shakespeare readings, gives the play a single entry, the bounty speech, described as a conversation in which Juliet figures her love as limitless, and reserves its popularity language for Sonnet 116, which it calls arguably the most popular of the playwright's wedding readings and labels the marriage sonnet. WeddingBee's 2018 selection of seven romantic passages places the bounty speech third, in company with Maya Angelou and Elizabeth Barrett Browning. Offbeat Bride offers the same three lines for vows, readings, speeches and what it frankly calls ironic ramblings. Shakespeare Online's readings page prints the bounty speech among sonnets and excerpts, on a page whose attributions elsewhere wander, assigning lines to plays they do not come from, a reminder that the anthology layer of the trade is compiled for use rather than for scholarship.

Three conclusions follow from the record, and each disciplines a lazy version of the irony argument. First, the play is a standard but not a dominant wedding author-text: within the Shakespeare list, the sonnets outrank it, and Sonnet 116, a poem about marriage that contains no plot at all, holds the position the lazy account assigns to the tragedy. The play's wedding presence is one famous speech, not a canon. Second, the guides do not, on the evidence, deceive their readers. The fullest guide names the genre, locates the deaths, prints line numbers and warns about context in terms. The information a couple would need to make the amputation knowingly is printed on the same page as the extract. Third, the trade's extracts cluster with remarkable discipline on Juliet's voice. Romeo supplies the window soliloquy, which circulates as decor; the speeches read aloud as pledges are Juliet's: the bounty speech, the vow exchange, the little-stars speech. The ceremony, without ever announcing the fact, has adopted the play's own distribution of eloquence, in which the bride drafts, proposes and pledges while the groom admires. A couple reading the guides' selection is re-enacting the scene's actual power structure, a fidelity no one involved appears to have planned.

The demand side of the trade confirms the guides' picture from the couples' own side of the counter. In August 2018 a couple planning an ocean-themed ceremony asked the Ask MetaFilter community for readings, and the recommendation that drew the thread's favour was not the bounty triad alone but the whole vow exchange that contains it, quoted entire: Romeo's request for the exchange of faithful vows, Juliet's answer that hers was given before it was asked, and the bounty speech as the exchange's climax. The instance is small and exactly probative. Given a free choice and a knowledgeable crowd, the borrowers reached past the anthology's excerpt to the scene's contract, taking the negotiation with the pledge. The trade's standard product may be three lines; the documented demand, at least in this thread, was for the whole instrument, covenants included. Selection, in the record, runs in both directions, and not always toward amputation.

### Why does Sonnet 116 outrank the play at the weddings where both are offered?

Sonnet 116 outranks the play because it performs the ceremony's work without importing a plot: it defines love as constancy under alteration, addresses marriage in its first line, and attaches its claims to no speaker contradicted by a sequel. The play's speeches carry speakers, and the speakers carry endings. The guides register the difference in their rankings.

The counterfactual sharpens the point. If couples borrowed from the play in ignorance of its ending, the guides' warnings would be news to them and the themed decor examined later in this article, poison bottles and potion scenes included, would be inexplicable. The record instead shows a trade and a clientele who know the ending and negotiate with it: the guide warns, the couple proceeds, the stylist restages the potion scene as a tableau. Janice A. Radway's Reading the Romance (1984), the foundational ethnography of romance reading, documented the same posture in a neighbouring medium. Her Smithton readers were not passive recipients of the novels they consumed; they operated as an interpretive community with enforceable requirements, chief among them that the story end with the heroine's reward secured, and they discarded, reread and revalued texts against those requirements with full knowledge of the conventions involved. Radway's readers imposed their ending on the genre. The wedding readers impose their ceremony on the play. In both cases the use is an act of editing performed by people who know exactly what the unedited text contains, and in both cases the charge of ignorance, levelled by outside observers, mistakes a deliberate selection for a failure of comprehension.

### Why do the guides print a speech that begins by imagining the beloved dead?

The little-stars speech survives in the lists because its core, the beloved's face outshining the sun and converting the world to night-worship, detaches cleanly from its clause, "When he shall die" (3.2.21), in which Juliet imagines possessing Romeo in a future beginning at his death. The image works as flattery; the deletion of the premise is the anthology method's cost.

The Act 3 speech also exposes what the ceremony is structurally unable to use from the play, and the inability is instructive. Juliet's wedding night is the play's one consummated joy, and its poetry is the most erotically explicit celebration in the text, opening with the summons to the horses of the sun to hurry the dark in. A ceremony is a public rite conducted before grandparents and registrars; its readings are filtered for what a mixed congregation can hear as blessing. The filter, not ignorance of the play, explains the selection pattern across the whole table: declarations pass, negotiations pass in shortened form, and the wedding night's great speeches, the most nuptial poetry Shakespeare wrote for the couple, fail the filter on grounds of decorum and premise alike. The play at weddings is therefore edited twice over, once by the tragedy and once by the congregation, and the second edit is the one no critic of the practice ever mentions.

## Readings Inside the Modern Ceremony: Why a Tragedy Passes the Registrar

The reading occupies a peculiar office in the modern ceremony, and the office explains the play's admission ticket better than any theory of romance does. A wedding reading is neither vow nor prayer. It binds nobody, asserts nothing on the couple's behalf in law, and is delivered, usually, by a third voice, a friend or sibling standing briefly between the couple and the congregation. Its function is witness work: the community, through a chosen speaker, lends the couple language larger than the couple's own, and the couple stands inside the borrowed language while it is spoken. The reading is the ceremony's quotation mark. What gets quoted inside it is therefore selected for resonance rather than for truth conditions, the way a motto is selected, and the selection criteria visible across the guides, quotability, brevity, a first-person pledge or a cosmic compliment, follow from the office. A passage fails as a reading not when its source is unhappy but when it cannot be stood inside for ninety seconds by two people being watched.

The civil ceremony sharpens the selection pressure in a documented direction. In England and Wales, where civil marriage was established by statute in 1836 and is conducted on approved premises under regulations restated in 2005, the proceedings must not be religious in nature: no hymns, no extracts from sacred texts, no act of worship may form part of the ceremony, a restriction whose application to readings is a standing topic in the legal commentary on registrars' practice. The rule creates a vacancy shaped exactly like Shakespeare. A couple barred from the Song of Songs and from the liturgy still requires language of sufficient altitude for the rite's central moment, and the national dramatist's love poetry is the largest body of elevated secular English available. The tragedy's speeches enter the ceremony not despite the secular rule but through it: the play is the scripture a registrar will allow. The little-stars speech and the bounty speech are, in this jurisdiction, beneficiaries of a legal filter that excludes their only real competitors in the language of erotic devotion, the biblical love poems. Irony arguments that treat the borrowing as a failure of taste miss the institutional fact: for a large class of ceremonies, the choice was never between the play and a happier book of comparable altitude. It was between the play and silence at the level the rite requires.

### What does a reading do at a wedding that a vow cannot?

A reading testifies while a vow commits. The vow is performative and binding, spoken by the couple against the future; the reading is declarative and witnessed, spoken about love in borrowed words while the couple stands inside them. The distinction licenses the borrowing: a couple may stand inside the bounty speech as testimony to feeling's scale without pledging its content.

Gillis's history supplies the longer arc into which this office fits. The early modern wedding, on his evidence, was thick with communal speech: the peer group fetched the bride, the congregation ratified the match, and the rite's words were surrounded by customary noise, games and sanctions that made the marriage a public manufacture. The privatised modern ceremony, scripted by professionals and performed before seated guests, thinned that communal voice to applause. The reading restores it in the only form the modern rite permits: a member of the couple's community rises, speaks a canonical text over the pair, and sits down, and for the length of the passage the wedding recovers the structure of the older rite, in which the community talked the couple into their new state. That the text so often comes from a tragedy is, at this level of analysis, beside the point. The office requires amplitude, antiquity and a community voice. The play supplies all three, and its plot stays outside the door with the coats.

The seasonal pattern of the borrowing deserves a sentence, because it connects this article's subject to its neighbour's. The play's occupation of Valentine's Day, the February feast the lovers annexed without a single line of textual warrant, is treated in the companion study of [Romeo and Juliet and Valentine's Day](/2029/09/14/romeo-and-juliet-and-valentines-day/) and is noted here only for its consequence in the ceremony calendar: engagements announced in February ripen into weddings across the following seasons carrying the feast's iconography with them, so that the lovers preside twice over the couple's year, once as patrons of the proposal and once as authors of the rite. The two occupations share a mechanism. In February as at the altar, the culture assigns the lovers an office their story never sought, and the assignment holds because the names, detached from the plot, have become the language's common property for the subject of young love itself.

## Vows in Borrowed Words: The Exchange as Template

Where the play enters the vows themselves, rather than the readings around them, the borrowing changes character, and the change deserves a colder assessment than the readings receive. A vow is not stood inside; it is spoken in the first person and it binds. Couples who draft personal vows from the play are therefore not quoting testimony but adopting undertakings, and the text they adopt from was written for speakers who undertake everything and schedule nothing. The contrast with the liturgy's own words is exact. The marriage service of the Book of Common Prayer, in print since 1549 and descended from the medieval Sarum rite, pledges a conditional catalogue: for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health. The traditional vow is actuarial. It prices adversity in advance and signs anyway. Juliet's pledge prices nothing. The bounty speech promises increase without condition, an economy in which giving manufactures having, and it is silent on sickness, poverty and time because its speaker has no experience of any of them and, in the event, acquires none: she is dead before the first condition could be tested. A couple that vows in her words adopts the unconditional register and drops the actuarial one, trading the liturgy's hard-won pessimism for the garden's arithmetic.

Yet the scene offers the vow-writer something the liturgy does not, and the offer explains the drafting choice better than naivety does. The traditional vow is asymmetrical in its history, spoken by each party in identical words supplied by the institution, a form in which the couple's individuality appears only in the names. The balcony exchange is composed, by contrast, as a negotiation between two specific people, in which the terms emerge from the exchange itself: Romeo proposes the swap of vows, Juliet corrects the oath's instrument, refuses the moon as a variable security, and sets the standard, constancy of the self, "swear by thy gracious self, / Which is the god of my idolatry, / And I'll believe thee" (2.2.112-114). A couple drafting personal vows wants precisely that artifact: words that sound negotiated rather than recited, terms that appear to have been found between two people instead of issued to them. The play is the language's most famous record of a couple finding its own terms. That the found terms are then never tested is the tragedy's business. The drafting is the wedding's, and as drafting it is genuine: mutual, specific and formally inventive, a vow-writing masterclass embedded in a catastrophe.

Juliet's moon speech deserves its own verdict within the section, because it is the one passage where the play instructs the ceremony in technique rather than supplying it with text. Her objection to the moon oath is an objection to variable collateral: an oath sworn by an inconstant body invites inconstant performance. Every vow-writing guide in the modern trade, whatever its source texts, teaches the same lesson in prose, that vows should pledge the self's conduct rather than invoke cosmic guarantees. The heroine arrives at the principle unaided, at thirteen, in the dark, within an hour of meeting the man. The wedding trade that prints her bounty speech and omits her oath criticism keeps the poetry and drops the doctrine, and couples drafting from the full scene get both. The distribution of the two passages across the trade is therefore a clean measure of what the trade sells: feeling, excerpted; method, left in the scene. An officiant building a ceremony from the play whole would read the moon speech as the rite's instruction manual and the bounty speech as its anthem, and the conjunction, which no guide consulted performs, is the strongest available evidence that the borrowing, even at its most defensible, proceeds by selection against the text's own advice about how promises should be made.

## The Advice Nobody Prints: The Friar at the Wedding

Every wedding use of the play borrows the couple and ignores the officiant, and the omission is systematic enough to count as the trade's most revealing editorial decision. Friar Laurence is the only character in the play who speaks about the lovers' marriage from outside it, the only one who officiates, and the author of the play's complete doctrine of erotic prudence, delivered across two scenes in advance of the rite. None of it circulates. His greeting of Romeo in the garden, the herb speech's doctrine that "Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied, / And vice sometime by action dignified" (2.3.21-22), is a treatise on dosage, the principle that the same substance heals or kills according to measure. His response to the marriage request is the doctrine applied: consent given for policy, haste noted and warned against, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94). His threshold speech at the cell converts the doctrine into the ceremony's own prologue, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9), followed by the marriage's terms stated as a prayer against sequel: "So smile the heavens upon this holy act / That after-hours with sorrow chide us not" (2.6.1-2). A wedding that wanted the play's wisdom rather than its register would read the Friar. No guide consulted for the wedding note prints him, at any length, in any role.

The reasons are structural, and they complete the map of the ceremony's exclusions drawn in the musicians' section. The Friar's speeches are all conditionals and cautions; they price the delight in the act of blessing it. A ceremony can absorb a warning in its liturgy, where the institution speaks with authority over the couple, as the older services absorbed their charges and exhortations. It cannot easily absorb a warning in its readings, where the voice is borrowed testimony and the congregation hears the words as the couple's chosen self-description. No couple chooses to describe its marriage, before witnesses, as a violent delight with a violent end, even when the description arrives wearing the authority of the officiant who married the speakers. The Friar is therefore unusable in the office the readings fill, and his exclusion is not evidence that couples flinch from the play's darkness, since the decor record shows them embracing it. His exclusion is evidence that the ceremony distinguishes, precisely, between darkness as atmosphere, which it welcomes, and darkness as prognosis, which it refuses. The potion can appear on the table because it is past and fictional. The warning cannot be read aloud because it is future and addressed, grammatically, to whoever is being married in the room.

There is an irony inside the irony, and it belongs to the play rather than to the trade. The Friar's counsel fails in the play not because it is wrong but because it is unheeded at the speed the plot requires; the lovers marry within the day, and every caution he utters is verified by the event. The wedding trade, declining to print him, performs the lovers' own editorial act: it hears the warning, judges the occasion too joyful for it, and proceeds. Couples who marry under the bounty speech are in this respect the Friar's truest audience, not because they follow his advice but because they repeat his congregation's behaviour toward it. The play's officiant stands at the edge of every modern ceremony where its words are used, uninvited and unquoted, having said the one thing the rite cannot use: that the delight's violence is not a refutation of the marriage but a property of it, to be governed by slowness. The ceremony that finally printed "Wisely and slow" on its order of service would be the first to take the play whole, and it would be, by the play's own arithmetic, the wedding most likely to last.

## Decor: The Weddings That Keep the Poison

If the reading trade edits the play by selection, the decor trade, on the documented evidence, does something stranger and more honest: it stages the tragedy as atmosphere. The styled shoots and reported real weddings built around the play do not confine themselves to the balcony and the bounty. They reach, repeatedly and with evident deliberation, for the death imagery, and they reach for it through the mediation of Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film, whose visual vocabulary of neon crosses, votive candles and Catholic iconography made the tragedy's funereal splendour available as a palette.

The record begins with a styled shoot published by The Perfect Palette in January 2015, in which the Toronto events studio Renata De Thomasis Events dressed the Berkeley Church in Toronto as what its designer described as a dark and dreamy editorial. The designer's stated elements were, in her phrase, intense passion combined with the romanticism of Romeo and Juliet, worked out as a sustained contrast of light and dark, and the shoot's centrepiece recreated the film's sleeping-potion tableau, the heroine laid out as if in the tomb, as a piece of wedding imagery. A wedding editorial that restages the bride's apparent corpse as its hero image has not set the tragedy aside. It has priced the tragedy's darkness as the luxury good, the intensity that distinguishes the theme from generic romance, and sold the couple a wedding photographed inside the catastrophe's most recognisable frame.

The Cheltenham styled shoot published by Wedding Chicks, built around Luhrmann's film at the No.131 venue, announces its editorial policy in terms: the team took inspiration from the film and put a slightly more uplifting spin on it. The spin, documented in the shoot's own account, is a study in how much darkness an uplift can retain. The florist built the arrangements around amaranthus, the flower marketed as Love Lies Bleeding, chosen, the account says, because the love and death theme running through the story made it perfectly apt; roses were selected with names symbolic of the same theme; and the stationer Bee Davies contributed hand-painted poison bottles as the place cards and favours, each painted with sacred-heart imagery or quotations. The poison bottle as a wedding favour is the decor trade's verdict on the amputation charge, delivered in glass: the instrument of the heroine's feigned death and the hero's real one, miniaturised, personalised with each guest's name and sent home as a keepsake. No one at that shoot had failed to read to the end of the play. The ending was the merchandise.

Real couples commission the same vocabulary. Rock n Roll Bride's report of the wedding of Jenna and Chad, held at Chateau Lill in Redmond, Washington, records that the couple's main visual reference was the final church scene of Luhrmann's film, the tomb scene, with its banks of candles and neon crosses, and that the bride wanted no white flowers anywhere in the venue, preferring gothic drapery, dripping amaranthus and candelabra, with name cards handmade as small vials filled with black tea beside scrolls bearing each guest's name. Green Wedding Shoes' account of Megan and Gavin's wedding at San Juan Capistrano in California likewise builds the day on Luhrmann's film, its primary colours and iconography, with the angel wings of the film's ball carried into the design. In each case the reference is not the play in the abstract but the film's final movement, the church of candles around the bodies. The decor trade's couples are not borrowing the romance and discarding the tragedy. They are borrowing the tragedy's look and discarding its causality, which is a different operation from the reading trade's, and in one respect a more candid one: the image admits the deaths are the point of the glamour, even as the event being celebrated declines to rehearse them.

Set against this record, the Prologue's phrase completes its own migration. The Chorus introduces the lovers as "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" who "take their life" (Prologue, line 6), a formulation whose full grammar is a death notice, and the phrase in its truncated form circulates on wedding stationery, programmes and signage as an emblem of fated compatibility, its meaning reversed by the cut. What the phrase promises, once its verb is removed, is destiny; what it states, whole, is doom. The migration is the purest instance in the wedding economy of the operation this article calls branding, since no couple needs the phrase and no reading requires it: it is chosen as a logo. The full account of that phrase, its astrology and its long career as the lovers' label, is given in the companion study of [star-crossed lovers: what the phrase really means](/2022/03/25/star-crossed-lovers-meaning-explained/); here it stands as the decor section's closing exhibit, the point at which the wedding use stops editing speeches and starts editing a sentence against its own grammar.

### Why do themed weddings keep the poison bottles and the potion scene?

The weddings keep the death imagery because intensity is the product bought: the shoots state the logic, darkness distinguishing the theme from generic romance and the love and death theme named as the attraction by the florists who serve it. Without the poison bottle the theme collapses into candles and roses. With it, the wedding cites the story in miniature.

## Beyond the Ceremony: Speeches, Stationery and the Toast

The play's wedding service extends well past the ceremony's edge, into the printed matter that frames the day and the speeches that close it, and the extension follows the same editorial law as the readings, with one instructive variation. The Wedding Secret's guidance states the trade's own map of the territory: the playwright's lines may be recited at the altar, used in vows and speeches, or used to decorate the venue, three licences that between them cover the day entire. Offbeat Bride's list is issued under the same general warrant, for vows, readings, speeches and ceremony generally. Within that territory the play's phrases do different work at different stations, and grading the stations separately refines the verdict the readings earned.

The invitation and the programme take the emblems. Star-crossed is the stationery's favourite, for the reasons the decor section established: truncated, the phrase promises destiny, and an invitation is a promise-making document in which destiny is the one claim a couple is licensed to print about itself. The bounty speech appears on orders of service as an epigraph, where it functions as the day's motto rather than as a reading, stating the economy the couple proposes to run. Both uses are branding in this article's sense, and both are, in the stationery's defence, the conventional rhetoric of the form: no invitation prints its couple's actuarial risks, and the Sarum catalogue of conditions is reserved, by a division of labour centuries old, for the rite itself. The stationery's amputation is therefore the least culpable in the system, since the documents that carry the emblems never claimed to carry the story.

The speeches take the philosophy. A best man's or father's toast that quotes the name speech, or Romeo's first-sight couplet, borrows the play as ornament for an argument about the couple, and the borrowing is governed by the toast's own contract, which promises affection and wit rather than textual fidelity. Here the play competes with every other quotable author on equal terms, and its advantage is recognition: the name speech is the one argument about identity that every guest has already heard, so the toast can spend its ninety seconds on application rather than exposition. The reception's use of the play is thus the most purely rhetorical, and the least ironic, in the whole economy, since a toast that reaches for Juliet on names makes no claim about the marriage's prospects at all. It borrows her cleverness, not her fate.

The favours and the table take the farewell. The parting line, the idiom whose independent career the companion study of the parting phrase traces in full, circulates at weddings as an inscription on keepsakes and leaving gifts, a goodbye printed for an event whose entire purpose is that the couple not part. The choice looks perverse until its logic is stated: the line is the play's one farewell that promises reunion by morning, sorrow scheduled to expire at dawn, and a wedding favour is likewise a farewell engineered to be temporary, the guests departing with the couple's permanence as the souvenir's whole message. Even here, at the economy's most decorative station, the excerpt keeps the shape of the scene it came from: a parting that expects to be undone. The survey of stations therefore ends in a refinement rather than a reversal of the article's verdict. The further a use sits from the ceremony's centre, the more freely it brands; the nearer the centre, the more carefully it borrows instruments, pledges and arguments whose forms survive the journey. The wedding day's printed margin tells the culture's favourite lie about the lovers. Its spoken centre tells, on the evidence assembled here, a more disciplined truth: that the words were built for promising, and that promising is what they are still doing.

## Verona: Marrying Inside the Fiction

Verona converts the wedding use of the play from quotation into jurisdiction. Since May 2009, under the municipality's Sposami a Verona programme, couples of any nationality have been able to marry in a real civil ceremony at Juliet's House, the Casa di Giulietta on Via Cappello, with the famous balcony, the courtyard and the frescoed rooms as the setting, and the wedding operator Veronissima's account of the product specifies the choreography: exclusive use of the rooms and balcony for the ceremony's duration, ten minutes for a proposal on the balcony and up to sixty for the full ceremony, while the courtyard, a public museum space, remains open to its visitors below. The launch was reported by Italy Magazine in March 2009 under the headline of a city drive to wed the world's couples in Verona: the tourism councillor Daniele Polato declared the ambition of making Verona a wedding capital, and the report printed the tariff that the companion audit of Verona's economy built on the fictional lovers examines in full, a marriage licence at the house priced at 600 euros for Verona residents, 700 for the surrounding catchment, 800 for citizens of the European Union and 1,000 for couples from beyond it, against the ordinary civil fee of the time. That audit owns the schedule and its later revisions, the 2019 ceremony prices and the balcony proposal's doubling in 2023; the figures are cited here, at passage, for what they certify about the ceremony's nature. A municipality priced the rite by the couple's distance from Verona, charging most to those for whom the fiction had travelled furthest. The product was never the room. It was the address.

For the argument of this article, the Verona ceremony's decisive property is its legal reality. Every other wedding use of the play is quotation: words, images, phrases. The Verona use is incorporation. The marriage registered at the Casa binds in Italian law exactly as a registry-office marriage binds, and the couple's vows, spoken in a museum assigned to a fictional girl, alter their legal state in every court that recognises the register. Verona therefore sells the one commodity in the entire lovers' economy that is not counterfeit in any particular: a genuine legal act staged inside an invented room. The irony that governs the readings, devotion's words drawn from a doomed marriage, inverts at Verona into something harder to mock. The doomed marriage was fictional. The marriages at its address are real, and their reality is not diminished by the warrant's invention, any more than a christening in a film-set chapel would wet the child less. What the couple buys is co-presence at the fiction's address for the one act of their lives that most resembles the fiction's subject, and the purchase is rational on its own terms in a way the star-crossed stationery is not.

### Why does the courtyard stay open to strangers during a wedding at the Casa?

The courtyard stays open because it is a working museum whose public cannot be displaced for every booking. The arrangement produces the ceremony's image: a legal wedding in the balcony rooms while pilgrims circulate below, queuing for the statue and the wall of letters. The couple marries before two congregations, invited above, uninvited below, inverting the play's unwitnessed wedding exactly.

The municipal calendar adds a resonance no marketer appears to have planned. The wedding operator Distinctive Italy Weddings, in its account of the house, records that the Casa celebrates its civil ceremonies on Monday mornings, the hall given over to the rite at the week's opening while the museum week is young. The play's lovers, by the text's compressed chronology, met on a Sunday night and were married on the Monday, in the cell, in secret, at the week's opening. The Casa's couples marry on the lovers' own weekday, in public, with the courtyard open and the register signed, the same day of the week carrying the opposite ceremony. Coincidence is the likeliest explanation, since a museum's diary has its own logic of quiet mornings. The record does not require the resonance to be intended for it to be exact, and the couples who notice it are reading the calendar the way the decor studios read the film, as a found correspondence between their rite and the story's.

The tomb extends the same logic one register darker, and it is documented in the companion study of the tomb of Juliet in Verona, whose record this article follows at passage. Verona celebrates civil marriages inside the tomb's museum, the former convent of San Francesco al Corso, in two rooms the municipality maintains: the Sala Galtarossa, seating around a hundred guests, and the Sala Guarienti, a smaller frescoed room seating around sixty. The municipality's promotion of the venue leans on the fictional warrant without embarrassment: the convent is offered as the monastery of Friar Laurence, the place where the story's wedding was set, so that couples marry, by the city's own account, where the secret wedding happened, with the heroine's sarcophagus in the crypt below the celebration. The play's Act 4 conversion, wedding cheer turned to burial feast, runs in reverse as a civic service, a burial shrine converted into a marriage hall, and the couples who book the Guarienti room do so, on the record, in full knowledge of the grave downstairs. The grave is not the venue's embarrassment. It is the venue's distinction, the thing the neoclassical register offices across town cannot offer: narrative position, a marriage registered inside the story's geography rather than merely decorated with its phrases.

The courtyard of the Casa adds the final Verona exhibit, and it belongs by rights to the companion study of the bronze Juliet and the superstition of touch, whose findings this article cites at passage. Couples married at the Casa descend to the courtyard for photographs in which the bronze Juliet stands as the marriage's presiding figure, and the luck rite of the statue, the touched breast polished by generations of petitioners asking for the state the couple has just entered, is performed in wedding clothes before the assembled families, where it reads as blessing rather than superstition. The bronze attends every ceremony the house hosts, the only wedding guest who is always present, and the photographs make her the marriage's witness in the folk sense: the figure who was there, touched by everyone, believed in by the pose. A wedding at the Casa thus assembles, in one courtyard, every layer of the play's afterlife examined in this article: the excerpted words in the ceremony, the emblem on the stationery, the invented room, the real register, and a statue worn smooth by the petitions of strangers hoping to arrive where the couple in the photograph has arrived. Verona is the wedding use's limiting case, the place where quotation hardens into real estate, and its evidence cuts against the simplest version of the irony argument. The couples in the Guarienti room and the courtyard photographs are not confused about the story. They have travelled, in many cases across oceans and at the tariff's highest tier, to marry in the tragedy's own buildings. Confusion does not explain the demand. Devotion to a fiction, openly priced, does.

## The Bride's Voice: Why the Ceremony Reads Juliet

A distribution so consistent across independent guides cannot be accidental, and it repays a section of its own. The wedding canon drawn from the play is overwhelmingly Juliet's. She supplies the pledge (the bounty speech), the contract's acceptance (the vow exchange), the wedding night's celebration (the little-stars speech), the farewell that decorates the favours (the parting line), and the philosophy quoted in speeches (the name speech). Romeo supplies the window soliloquy and the first-sight couplet, both of which circulate as images rather than as spoken pledges, printed on signage and quoted in write-ups. The asymmetry follows the play's own allocation of verbal power. In the balcony scene Romeo performs admiration; Juliet performs acts. She names, refuses, audits, proposes and schedules. His speeches describe her; hers do things, and a ceremony, which is a machine for doing things with words, naturally recruits the speeches that do things. A vow is a performative utterance, and the play's performative utterances are the bride's.

The point extends beyond one scene. Across the play, Juliet's language repeatedly takes the form of instruments: the name argument is a legal brief against the feud's title deeds; the potion speech is a risk assessment conducted aloud, itemising failure modes before she drinks; the tomb speeches are decisions executed in sequence. Romeo's great speeches, by contrast, are reports of feeling, magnificent and inert. The wedding trade, riffling the text for language that can be stood inside and spoken as an act, extracts the instruments and leaves the reports for the decor. Critics of the practice who picture the ceremony swooning over Romeo's rapture have the wrong speaker in view. The ceremony's Juliet is the play's most competent drafter of binding language, and the couples who read her words aloud are, perhaps without knowing it, honouring the tragedy's own judgement about which of its lovers could be trusted with a document.

There is a further consequence for the irony ledger. The standard objection imagines the wedding borrowing the lovers as a unit, two children whose fate refutes the rite. The documented selection divides the unit. The ceremony takes Juliet's pledges, which are adult in structure whatever their speaker's age: reciprocal, unconditional in register, and attached to a proposal she originates and terms she sets. It leaves behind, almost entire, the play's machinery of male haste: Romeo's instant transfer of devotion from Rosaline, the Friar's political calculation, Capulet's auction of his daughter, Paris's courtship by contract. What the wedding use amputates, in other words, is not only the ending. It amputates the play's men and keeps its woman, and the resulting text, Juliet's speeches arranged as a liturgy, is less a falsification of the play than a severe abridgement with a thesis: that the tragedy's credible marriage was the one its bride designed, and that the design failed through no defect in the drafting. Abridgements with theses are arguments, and arguments can be answered, but they cannot be dismissed as ignorance, which is the dismissal the irony-first account keeps attempting.

The distribution has a negative space that confirms it. The play's other great voices never reach the ceremony at all. The Nurse, who carries the marriage's logistics and knows its secret, speaks a prose of errands, appetites and bawdy that no list prints; Mercutio, the play's wittiest speaker, supplies the Queen Mab set piece and the bawdy hand of the dial, and is likewise unquotable in a rite whose congregation spans three generations. Their exclusion is usually credited to decorum, and decorum is part of it. The deeper reason is functional: the Nurse and Mercutio neither pledge nor propose. They comment, facilitate and mock, the three speech acts a ceremony has no office for, since its borrowed voices are hired to perform the couple's commitments, not to annotate them. The wedding canon is therefore not merely the play with its ending removed. It is the play filtered by speech act, pledges and proposals admitted, commentary and logistics refused, which is why its table of contents reads as a liturgy rather than as an anthology of beauties.

## Thirteen: The Age the Ceremony Never Mentions

One fact about the speakers is absent from every document in the wedding record, and its absence is the trade's quietest edit. Juliet is thirteen. The play states her age with bureaucratic exactness: her father tells Paris that "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.9), and the Nurse's Lammas Eve arithmetic fixes her birthday a fortnight ahead. The bounty speech, the vow exchange and the marriage proposal, the entire liturgy the ceremony borrows, are spoken by a girl of thirteen to a boy whose age the text leaves unstated and whom the tradition ages only a few years higher. No guide consulted prints the age beside the speech. No styled shoot mentions it. The Verona programme's marketing does not raise it. The lovers circulate through the wedding economy as adults of an unspecified ripeness, their speeches' authority borrowed without their speaker's biography attached, and the biography's first fact is the one no borrower wants.

The omission deserves a more careful charge than the trade usually receives. Every anthology omits; the question is whether the omitted fact would alter the borrowed words' meaning if restored. Juliet's age plainly would, in both directions at once, which is why the ceremony cannot afford to print it. In one direction, the age aggravates the irony the critics prosecute: the rite's model pledges are a child's, uttered inside a marriage the play presents as a catastrophe of adult failure, of fathers auctioning, friars scheming and a city pricing its children into graves. A congregation that heard the bounty speech introduced with its speaker's age would hear the pledge differently, and the difference would not flatter the borrowing. In the other direction, the age is the source of the speeches' one quality the ceremony cannot source elsewhere: their uncalculating scale. The bounty paradox, love increasing by expenditure, is a thirteen-year-old's economics, innocent of scarcity because innocent of experience, and its authority at a wedding derives from exactly that innocence. Adults who have kept accounts know that giving depletes; the speech persuades because its speaker does not. The ceremony wants the arithmetic without the arithmetician, the innocence as a style rather than as a biography, and so the age is edited out not from squeamishness alone but because the borrowing's mechanism requires the words to arrive as if spoken by the couple themselves, at the couple's own age, on the couple's own day. The reading's fiction is ventriloquism, and the ventriloquism fails if the original voice is allowed to be a child's.

The play, characteristically, has already staged the adults' version of the same edit. Capulet, negotiating with Paris, first pleads his daughter's age as a reason for delay, "My child is yet a stranger in the world" (1.2.8), and then, three acts and one street killing later, overrides the plea entirely and sets the wedding for Thursday, the age unchanged and the argument discarded. Verona's adults cite Juliet's thirteen years when the citation serves delay and drop them when it obstructs a marriage they want. The wedding trade's silence about the age is therefore not a modern squeamishness imposed on an innocent text. It is the text's own adult behaviour, repeated by the text's borrowers, and the repetition closes the section's account of what the ceremony edits: the ending, the warning, the officiant, the musicians, and the bride's age, five excisions that together convert a tragedy about what adults do to children's marriages into a liturgy for adults marrying as they choose.

The stage colludes in the same silence, and its collusion is the passage-level link between this article and the casting record. The play's text leaves Romeo's age unstated and fixes Juliet's at thirteen, an asymmetry productions have exploited for centuries by ageing the pair upward: adult Juliets were the stage's norm for most of its history, and even the famous film castings that made youth their selling point worked with performers older than the text's figure or at its very edge, as the companion study of the Zeffirelli casting documents. The wedding ceremony inherits the production tradition's solution entire. It casts the couple themselves in the lovers' roles, at the couple's own ages, and lets the casting do the editing: no programme note announces that the words were written for a child, because the bodies speaking them have already answered the objection. The age is the one fact about the source that every borrower, guide, stylist, municipality and couple, agrees without discussion to leave in the scene.

## What the Ceremony Cannot Use: Musicians at the Funeral

The play's own wedding professionals supply the counter-image to every argument in this article, and they have been waiting in Act 4 for their section. When Juliet is found apparently dead on her wedding morning, the household's grief runs its course in lamentation, and then the text does something extraordinary: it keeps the hired musicians on stage and gives them a comedy scene. Peter, the Nurse's servant, asks the fiddlers for a tune to relieve his heart, is refused on the ground that it is no time to play, trades insults with them about silver sounds and musicians' wages, and dismisses them: the wedding band, sent home unheard. Shakespeare, in other words, stages the exact transaction the modern ceremony refuses to imagine, the wedding's suppliers confronting the wedding's conversion into a funeral, and he stages it as farce, the professionals haggling over their fee while the bride lies dead upstairs. No reading list prints the musicians. No styled shoot restages Peter and the fiddlers. The play's wedding industry, its cooks and musicians and its father awake all night among the preparations, exists in the text precisely to be converted, at the moment of its completion, into the funeral's staff. The modern wedding industry that quotes the play declines, understandably, to quote its own portrait.

The unused material maps the ceremony's true exclusions more accurately than the used material maps its taste. The ceremony cannot use Capulet's feast, because the play's public wedding is a father's transaction, the bride displayed as an asset in a negotiation the text anatomises. It cannot use the Paris courtship, a marriage arranged by contract between men, although that courtship is the play's only normative one. It cannot use the Nurse's wedding bustle, the errands and clothing and drudge work by which the household's labour underwrites the festival, because the modern rite prefers its labour invisible and its suppliers, pace Peter, unquoted. And it cannot use the one passage in the play actually addressed to a marrying couple by their officiant, the Friar's threshold warning, because the warning predicts the rite's failure in the rite's own hearing. Each exclusion is defensible in isolation; a wedding is not a seminar. Together the exclusions describe the ceremony's editorial policy with precision: the play may enter the rite as the bride's voice and as atmosphere, and may not enter as structure, as warning, or as the story of what weddings in its world are for. The policy is coherent. It is also a policy, chosen and maintained, and the choosing is what the critics of the practice, and its defenders, should be arguing about.

Set beside the musicians, even the Verona ceremonies acquire a sharper outline. The couples in the Sala Guarienti are not merely marrying near a tomb; they are reversing, by deliberate booking, the conversion Capulet names when the wedding cheer turns to a burial feast. The play performs the conversion in one direction, in horror, inside a single house and a single morning. Verona performs it in the other, as a civic service, in the same architectural complex, and charges for the reversal at rates graduated by the couple's devotion. Whether the reversal is a defiance of the play or a refusal of it depends on the verdict reached about editing in general, which is the business of the next section. The musicians, at least, are neutral witnesses: they testify that the play itself understood weddings as an industry staffed by professionals with fees to protect, and that it found the industry's collision with death funny as well as terrible, a doubleness the modern industry, which takes the play very seriously as a brand, has not inherited.

## Three Witnesses: The Historian, the Romance Critic and the Editor

The disagreement this article is required to adjudicate can be stated cleanly at this point, because the evidence has assembled it. Three witnesses, each expert in one layer of the phenomenon, give three incompatible accounts of what a couple is doing when the bounty speech is read at a wedding.

The cultural historian speaks first. Gillis's history of British marriage establishes that the ceremony itself is a layered construction, assembled across centuries from betrothal customs, communal rites, liturgical forms and commercial services, and that the gap between marital ideals and marital practices is not a modern corruption but the system's permanent condition. On the historian's evidence, there has never been a wedding rite whose texts meant, in use, exactly what their authors meant in composition: the liturgy itself is a patchwork of a medieval Salisbury rite wearing a Tudor translation, and the congregation that recited it across the centuries included couples marrying for land, for pregnancy, for alliance and for love, all speaking the same words into different facts. Borrowing, on this account, is not what weddings do to texts. Borrowing is what weddings are. The historian therefore acquits the practice in advance, as one more instance of the rite's constitutive method, and regards the irony argument as a category error made by literary people who imagine that ceremonies elsewhere are pure.

The critic on the romance speaks second, and Radway's ethnography complicates the acquittal in a useful direction. Her Smithton readers knew the conventions of their genre cold, imposed their requirements on it, and used its products as instruments for needs the texts were not written to serve, replenishment, escape, the rehearsal of being cherished. Use, in her account, is a skilled practice with its own integrity, and the user's knowledge is not in question. But Radway's readers also exacted a price from the genre in return: the romance had to end correctly, with the heroine rewarded, or it was judged a failure and its author abandoned. The users' freedom ran one way, toward the guaranteed outcome. Applied to the ceremony, her account convicts a specific segment of the practice rather than the whole: the segment that requires the lovers' story to function as a guarantee, the emblem that promises the couple's own plot will follow the speeches' register into a matching destiny. The star-crossed stationery, the venue marketing that sells the address as love's proof, the guide rhetoric that presents the pair as the patron saints of successful passion: these are Radway's enforced happy ending, imposed on a text that famously refuses it. The readings themselves, where the couple stands inside a pledge without claiming the plot, fall outside her indictment. Her readers, after all, never demanded that the novel's middle be other than it was. They demanded jurisdiction over the ending, and it is the claim of jurisdiction over the play's ending, not the borrowing of its speeches, that her evidence condemns.

The editor speaks last, and Weis's position, as the editorial tradition's representative, is the least forgiving. The text, on this view, is a determinate object whose meanings are fixed by its structures: the bounty speech is a move in a negotiation, flanked by a refused oath and a scheduled proposal, inside a scene whose speed its own heroine flags as the danger, inside a play whose Prologue announces the outcome and whose Friar prices the delight in advance. To circulate the speech as a free-standing emblem of love's economics is to alter its terms, and an altered instrument is a different instrument, whatever its users intend. The editorial witness does not deny that the ceremony may do as it likes. He denies that what the ceremony does with the speech is the speech. Between the editor and the historian there is no reconciliation available, because they are describing different objects: the historian describes a rite, which has always been a collage; the editor describes a text, which is a whole. Radway's ethnography supplies the court's judgement between them, because her evidence shows what users actually do with wholes: they excerpt, they enforce, and they know. The verdict of this article follows her evidence and divides the practice accordingly, as the opening promised. The excerpting of the pledges is a use, defensible and knowingly performed, and the editor's objection, while accurate as textual description, does not reach it, because a reading does not claim to be the scene; it claims to borrow the scene's words for a rite the scene never imagined, and the borrowing is announced as borrowing by the ceremony's own quotation marks. The branding of the outcome is a falsification, and the historian's acquittal does not reach it, because the stationery and the sales copy do claim the plot: they present the lovers as evidence that love like theirs succeeds, which is the one proposition the text exists to refute.

## The Verdict on the Play at Weddings

The play at weddings is an amputation, and the amputation is, in its central instance, justified by the anatomy. The balcony scene is a betrothal conducted in pledge language of unusual completeness, drafted by the play's most competent speaker of binding words, and a ceremony that borrows the bounty speech borrows an instrument built for the purpose it is put to. The borrower is not deceived: the guides print the genre and the warning on the same page as the extract, the stylists restage the potion scene in full knowledge, and the couples in Verona marry a stair above the tomb with their eyes open and the tariff's highest tier paid. Deception exists in the system, but it lives in the sales layer, in the truncated Prologue sentence on the stationery and in the marketing that presents two dead teenagers as the proof of love's success, and it is refuted not by abandoning the play but by finishing its sentences: the star-crossed phrase whole, the bounty speech with its caution attached, the wedding at the Casa understood as a real act in an invented room rather than a blessing conferred by a fictional one.

The verdict is offered with its falsification conditions attached, because a judgement about a documented practice should state what evidence would overturn it. The acquittal of the couples would fall if the guides were shown to conceal the play's genre or its ending, if officiants reported congregations surprised by the source of the readings, or if the themed record showed the death imagery entering weddings by accident rather than by commission. None of that evidence appears in the record assembled here; the genre is printed beside the extracts, the warnings are printed with them, and the potion tableau is restaged by designers who name it as their centrepiece. The conviction of the branding layer would fall, in turn, if the stationery printed the Prologue's sentence whole or the venue marketing carried the tomb in its imagery, and the record shows the opposite at every point examined. Judged against its own stated tests, the division stands: the borrowing knows, and the branding truncates.

What the practice finally reveals about the cultural appetite for the romance is more interesting than the irony it is usually charged with. The appetite is not for the lovers' story, which the culture consumes separately and constantly in theatres and films. The appetite is for their register: a pitch of feeling, stated in the first person, without actuarial discount, at the exact moment a modern rite requires two people to say what the scale of their commitment feels like from inside. The liturgy offers the rite its wisdom about adversity. The play offers it its arithmetic of abundance. A ceremony that can hold both, the Sarum catalogue of conditions and Juliet's paradox of increase, speaks a fuller truth about marriage than either text speaks alone, since marriages are in fact entered in the garden's register and survived, when they are survived, in the liturgy's. The couples who reach for the play are reaching for the half of the truth their own service omits. The reaching is the verdict's final evidence: judged by what it supplies, rather than by what it omits, the play at weddings is not the culture misreading a tragedy. It is the culture completing a ceremony, with the tragedy's own words, in full knowledge of the tragedy's price, and the completion stands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why do couples choose readings from a tragedy for a wedding ceremony?

Couples choose the play because the ceremony needs what the tragedy happens to stock: first-person pledge language of maximum scale, written for lovers speaking for themselves rather than for institutions speaking about them. The balcony scene is a betrothal negotiation whose central speeches are performative, reciprocal and future-facing, so the extraction costs less than the genre label suggests. The documented guides register no embarrassment about the source: the fullest of them names the play a tragedy on the same page that prints its speeches. The choice is therefore rarely the blunder it is painted as. Couples are buying a register, the garden's unconditional arithmetic of love increasing by expenditure, which the liturgy's conditional catalogue does not supply, and they are buying it with the ending in plain view.

### Q: Which passage from the play do the wedding guides print most consistently?

The bounty speech, Juliet's three lines beginning "My bounty is as boundless as the sea" (2.2.133-135), is the play's standard entry: every guide consulted for the wedding note prints it, including The Wedding Secret, SoGlos, WeddingBee's 2018 selection and Offbeat Bride. The consistency should be graded carefully. Within the playwright's whole wedding canon the play does not lead; the guides reserve their strongest popularity claims for Sonnet 116, which SoGlos calls arguably the most popular of the playwright's wedding readings. The play's position is that of a permanent shortlist entry rather than a chart leader, one famous speech that every compiler includes and none promotes above the sonnet. Its durability comes from construction: the pledge is complete in three lines, names no condition, and requires no narrative introduction for a congregation to stand inside it.

### Q: What does the bounty speech pledge, in plain terms?

The speech pledges a paradox about the economics of giving. Juliet figures her bounty as the sea, boundless, and her love as its depth, then states the law of the system: the more she gives, the more she has, because both the giving and the having are infinite. In plain terms the pledge promises that love is not a depleting asset, that expenditure on the beloved manufactures rather than consumes the fund. As a wedding reading the speech functions as a couple's founding economic policy, a public commitment to run the marriage on increase rather than on rationing. Its force at a ceremony depends on its unconditional form. The liturgy's traditional vows arrive with their conditions priced in, sickness, poverty and adversity named in advance. Juliet's pledge names none, which is precisely the lack the ceremony hires it to fill.

### Q: Did the lovers exchange vows before the ceremony in the Friar's cell?

Yes, in substance, and the exchange is the balcony scene's contractual core. Romeo asks for "the exchange of thy love's faithful vow for mine" (2.2.127), and Juliet answers that she gave her vow before it was requested and wishes only that it were hers to give again for the pleasure of the giving (2.2.128-129). What the cell adds the next afternoon is ratification, not commitment: an officiant, a rite and the Church's form wrapped around a contract the garden had already executed. The distinction matters for the wedding use of the text, because it locates the play's true vow moment in the scene the guides print rather than in the rite the play withholds. Couples who read the exchange aloud are re-performing the play's actual vow exchange, in its actual setting of mutual negotiation, and borrowing the cell ceremony not at all.

### Q: Why do the wedding guides warn couples about the context of the lines?

The fullest guides warn because their compilers know the extracts travel with sequels the ceremony cannot use. The Wedding Secret's Shakespeare page states the play's genre, notes that the deaths at the end may not be lines a couple wants quoted at a wedding, and advises care in understanding the context behind any chosen lines, all on the page that prints the bounty speech and the little-stars speech. The warning performs two services. It protects the couple from the one genuine accident the trade can cause, the unknowing use of a passage whose premise contradicts the occasion, as the little-stars speech's opening clause does. And it converts the borrowing from an oversight into a decision, documenting that the selection was made with the context available. A warned couple that proceeds is an editor, and the guide's caution is the paperwork of that editorship.

### Q: Why does Juliet supply nearly all of the play's wedding readings?

Juliet supplies the readings because her speeches do things while Romeo's describe things. Across the balcony scene she names, refuses, audits, proposes and schedules; Romeo performs admiration. Her utterances are instruments, a pledge, a contract's acceptance, a marriage proposal with logistics attached, and a ceremony is a machine for performing acts with words, so it recruits the play's performative voice. Romeo's great speeches circulate instead as images, the window soliloquy on signage, the first-sight couplet in write-ups, because admiration decorates better than it pledges. The distribution follows the play's own allocation of competence: its bride is the drafter, and the wedding trade, without ever announcing the discovery, has adopted her as its author. The ceremony's Juliet is consequently less the tragedy's victim than its one credible maker of binding language.

### Q: What is left out when the bounty speech is read on its own?

The reading leaves out the speech's neighbourhood, and the neighbourhood is the scene's conscience. Immediately around the pledge stand Juliet's refusal of the moon oath, her objection that an inconstant security invites inconstant performance, and her audit of the whole contract as too rash, too unadvised and too sudden, followed by her marriage proposal with its messenger logistics. Read whole, the scene teaches a method: pledge largely, but audit the instrument and distrust the speed. Read as the guides print it, the scene teaches only the scale. The omission also removes the feud, the concealed listener's danger and the Nurse's interruptions, the pressure system that makes the pledge an act of courage rather than a sentiment. What remains is genuine but partial, a bond printed without its covenants, and the wedding note grades the cut accordingly: the lowest irony in the table, and a real loss all the same.

### Q: Can a couple legally marry at Juliet's House in Verona?

Yes. Since May 2009 the municipality's Sposami a Verona programme has offered civil ceremonies at the Casa di Giulietta to couples of any nationality, and the marriage registered there binds in Italian law exactly as a registry-office marriage does. The operator Veronissima's account specifies the choreography: exclusive use of the house's rooms and balcony for the ceremony, up to sixty minutes for the full rite and ten for a balcony proposal, with the courtyard remaining open to the museum's public below. Italy Magazine reported the launch in March 2009, printing the tourism councillor Daniele Polato's declared ambition to make Verona a wedding capital and the tariff graduated by the couple's residence, a schedule whose figures and later revisions the companion audit of Verona's economy examines in full. The ceremony is the wedding use's limiting case: a real legal act staged inside a fictional address.

### Q: Why would a couple choose to marry in the museum that holds Juliet's tomb?

The documented answer is narrative position. Verona's register offices include grander rooms, yet the former convent of San Francesco al Corso holds its share of the marriage trade because its two wedding halls, the Sala Galtarossa and the frescoed Sala Guarienti, stand inside the building the city promotes as Friar Laurence's monastery, the setting of the story's secret wedding, with the heroine's sarcophagus in the crypt below. A couple marrying there is not hiring a beautiful room with a story attached. The couple is registering a marriage inside the fiction's own geography, at the address where the invented marriage was joined, and the grave downstairs is the venue's distinction rather than its embarrassment, the one asset no palace across town can match. The choice reverses the play's own conversion, in which wedding cheer turns to a burial feast, and performs it as a civic service with a registrar present.

### Q: Why is the playwright permitted in civil ceremonies that exclude religious readings?

In England and Wales the civil ceremony is regulated to be secular in every part: the approved-premises regulations restated in 2005 bar hymns, extracts from sacred texts and acts of worship from the proceedings, a restriction whose application to readings is a standing subject in the legal commentary on registrars' practice. The rule creates a vacancy shaped like the national dramatist. A couple denied the Song of Songs and the liturgy still requires language of altitude for the rite's centre, and the love poetry of the plays and sonnets is the largest body of elevated secular English in existence. The tragedy's speeches therefore enter the ceremony through a legal filter rather than around one: the play functions as the scripture a registrar will allow. The irony usually charged against the borrowing, that couples reach for a tragedy in a moment of joy, is incomplete without the institutional fact that for a large class of ceremonies the shelf of permitted alternatives was short.

### Q: How do personal vows drafted from the play differ from the traditional liturgy's vows?

The difference is actuarial. The traditional vow, printed in the Book of Common Prayer since 1549 and descended from the medieval Sarum rite, pledges a conditional catalogue: for better, for worse, for richer, for poorer, in sickness and in health. It prices adversity in advance and signs anyway, a form built by an institution that had buried many of the couples it married. Vows drafted from the play take the opposite register. Juliet's pledge prices nothing, promises increase without condition, and is silent on sickness, poverty and time, because its speaker has no experience of any of them. Couples who draft from the scene are choosing the garden's arithmetic over the liturgy's pessimism, and choosing also the scene's form, terms that sound negotiated between two people rather than issued to them. The trade is intelligible and the cost is real: the borrowed vow omits the only part of the traditional form that the future is guaranteed to test.

### Q: Do any wedding guides print the Friar's warning as a ceremony reading?

None consulted for the wedding note prints it. The guides print the lovers' declarations and pledges exclusively; the officiant's doctrine, "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9), "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94), and the threshold prayer that after-hours not chide the act with sorrow, appears in no list examined, in any role. The exclusion is structural rather than squeamish. A warning can be absorbed where an institution speaks over the couple, as the older liturgies absorbed their charges, but a reading is borrowed testimony that the congregation hears as the couple's chosen self-description, and no couple describes its own marriage, in advance and before witnesses, as a violent delight awaiting its end. The Friar is the play's wedding professional, and the wedding trade's refusal of him is its most consistent editorial act, repeated by every compiler without discussion.

### Q: Why is Juliet's age never mentioned beside her wedding speeches?

Because the borrowing's mechanism requires the words to arrive as if spoken by the couple. Juliet is thirteen, a fact the play states exactly through her father's "She hath not seen the change of fourteen years" (1.2.9) and the Nurse's Lammas Eve arithmetic, and the pledge, the audit and the proposal are all a thirteen-year-old's work. Restoring the age would alter the borrowed speeches' meaning in both directions at once: it would aggravate the irony, since the rite's model vows are a child's, and it would explain the speeches' authority, since the bounty paradox persuades through an innocence of scarcity that experience would correct. The ceremony wants the arithmetic without the arithmetician, innocence as a style rather than a biography, and so the age is edited out by every guide, shoot and programme in the record. The play's own adults behave identically, Capulet pleading the age when delay serves him and discarding it when the wedding does.

### Q: Why do couples pose with the bronze Juliet after marrying at the Casa?

The photograph completes the courtyard's rite. Couples married at the house descend to the courtyard where Costantini's bronze stands, the figure whose breast generations of petitioners have polished with requests for luck in love, and the wedding party arranges itself around her for the portrait, as the companion study of the statue and its superstition records. Performed in wedding clothes before the assembled families, the touch that the queue performs as petition reads as blessing: the crowd's accumulated hope, made visible as worn metal, is claimed by contact for the pair who have reached the state the petitioners were asking for. The bronze is the only guest who attends every ceremony the house hosts, and the photographs install her as the marriage's witness in the folk sense, the figure who was present, touched by everyone, and believed in by the pose. The custom costs the couple nothing and certifies the address, which is why no Verona wedding album in the record omits it.

### Q: Is quoting the lovers at a wedding treated as bad luck anywhere in the documented record?

The superstition economy around the play is real but located elsewhere, at the courtyard statue, where touch is held to purchase luck in love; the readings themselves carry no documented taboo. The distinction is worth keeping because it separates the practice's actual risks, which are editorial, a passage whose premise contradicts the occasion, from its imagined risks, which are magical. Couples who hesitate over the bounty speech are conducting literary criticism, not fleeing a curse, and the guides' warnings address the hesitation in exactly those terms.

### Q: Why does the star-crossed phrase appear on wedding stationery with its ending removed?

Because the whole sentence is a death notice and the truncated phrase is a promise. The Chorus introduces "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" who "take their life" (Prologue, line 6), a single grammatical unit in which the destiny and the doom are the same fact. Stationery prints the destiny and drops the doom, converting the Prologue's announcement into an emblem of fated compatibility, the one claim an invitation is licensed to make about its couple. The cut is the wedding economy's purest act of branding, since no rite requires the phrase and no reading includes it: it is chosen as a logo, and a logo cannot carry its own refutation. The full study of the phrase, its astrology and its career as the lovers' label, belongs to the companion article on the star-crossed lovers' meaning; the stationery's use of it stands here as the limiting case that separates branding from borrowing, the point where editing stops serving a ceremony and starts selling one.

### Q: What happens to the play's wedding musicians, and why does no ceremony quote them?

The musicians are sent home unheard, in the play's strangest tonal swerve. Juliet is found apparently dead on her wedding morning; the lamentations run their course; and the text keeps the hired fiddlers on stage for a comedy scene in which Peter begs a tune for his heart, is refused because it is no time to play, trades insults about musicians and silver sounds, and dismisses the band. Shakespeare stages the wedding industry confronting the wedding's conversion into a funeral and stages it as farce, the professionals haggling while the bride lies dead upstairs. No reading list prints the scene and no shoot restages it, because it is the play's portrait of the wedding trade itself, suppliers with fees to protect at the edge of a catastrophe they cannot use. The ceremony that quotes the play everywhere else falls silent at the one scene about ceremonies, and the silence is the trade's most honest review of its own position in the text.
