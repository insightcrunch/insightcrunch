The Right to Education Act, formally the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, is the statute that converted a constitutional promise into an enforceable claim a parent can make at the gate of a school. It is also a statute with unusually precise edges. Inside those edges it is generous and detailed: it fixes the size of a class, the hours of a school year, the qualifications of a teacher and the acts a school is forbidden to perform. Outside those edges it is silent, and the silence is deliberate. A child of five has no claim under it. A child of fifteen has no claim under it. A child studying in a minority institution has, by judicial construction, no claim under it at all. The claim this article defends throughout is the one the text supports: this is a right with two open ends and one large exemption, a guarantee of eight years of schooling for children between six and fourteen in a defined subset of the schools of the country.

![The Right to Education Act 2009, showing the entitlement of children aged six to fourteen, the norms binding every school and the prohibitions a parent can enforce - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-65.webp)

That shape produces the practical questions the Act generates every admission season. A parent is told a seat cannot be given because a birth certificate is missing, although the statute forbids exactly that refusal. A school demands a donation at admission under another name, although the statute makes the demand punishable. A teacher is sent away on administrative work for weeks, although the statute confines such deployment to three named purposes. A child reaches the end of class eight and the family discovers that the entitlement they relied upon has simply run out, because the statute ends where secondary education begins. Each of those situations is governed by a specific provision, and the purpose of a statute pillar is to put the provision, its limits and its enforcement route in one place. What follows reads the Act as a working instrument: the entitlement first, then the duties it casts on governments, schools and teachers, then the prohibitions and the complaint machinery, then the courts that redrew its reach, and finally the amendments and the record.

## The constitutional road to the Act

For the first half century of the Republic, elementary education sat in the part of the Constitution that courts cannot enforce. The original Article 45, a directive principle of state policy, recorded that the state shall endeavour to provide free and compulsory education for all children until they complete the age of fourteen years within ten years of the commencement of the Constitution. The ten years passed. The decades passed. The Education Commission of 1964 to 1966, chaired by D. S. Kothari, recommended a common school system and a far larger public investment in schooling, and its recommendations shaped policy documents without ever becoming a claim a citizen could take to court. The National Policy on Education of 1986, and its revision in 1992, repeated the commitment in programme language. The entitlement remained an instruction to governments rather than a right of children.

The judiciary moved first. In Mohini Jain v State of Karnataka in 1992 and then in Unni Krishnan v State of Andhra Pradesh in 1993, the Supreme Court read a right to education into the right to life in Article 21, holding in the second of those cases that a child has a right to free education until the age of fourteen, after which the obligation of the state depends on its economic capacity. The doctrinal lineage of that reading, and the way Parliament answered it, belongs to the companion analysis of the amendment itself, and the full constitutional and judicial history of [the 86th Amendment and the road to Article 21A](/2015/01/15/86th-amendment-right-to-education/) is set out there. The short version needed here is that the Constitution (Eighty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2002 wrote the judicial position into the constitutional text in a particular and consequential form. It inserted Article 21A, under which the state shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years in such manner as the state may, by law, determine. It substituted Article 45 with a direction that the state shall endeavour to provide early childhood care and education for children below the age of six. And it added a fundamental duty, in Article 51A(k), on every parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to their child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years.

Two features of that settlement matter for everything in the 2009 Act. The first is that Article 21A is not self-executing: it promises education in such manner as the state may by law determine, so the content of the fundamental right is whatever the implementing statute says it is. Parliament took seven years to enact that statute, and the drafting choices it made in 2009 are therefore not administrative detail beneath a constitutional right; they are the right itself, in its only enforceable form. The second is the age band. The Constitution Bench in Unni Krishnan had spoken of education until fourteen, and the original Article 45 had covered the years from birth to fourteen as an aspiration. The 86th Amendment narrowed the promise at both ends: the enforceable right begins at six, and early childhood, from birth to six, was moved into the substituted Article 45 as an endeavour rather than an entitlement. The Act of 2009 inherited that band exactly, and no reading of it can extend the claim to a five year old or a sixteen year old. Whether the band should be widened is a live policy question, addressed later in this article; as a matter of law it is closed.

## The Right to Education Act: identity and commencement

### When did the Right to Education Act come into force?

The Act received the assent of the President on 26 August 2009 and was brought into force on 1 April 2010. It is Act 35 of 2009. From the date of commencement, its norms, its prohibitions and its complaint machinery applied across India, and the deadlines the statute sets for schools and governments run from that date of commencement.

The formal identity deserves a paragraph because shorter accounts regularly get it wrong. The statute is the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009. Section 1(2) extends it to the whole of India. Section 1(3) left commencement to notification, and the Central Government appointed 1 April 2010. The Act contains thirty-eight sections arranged in seven chapters, together with a Schedule of norms and standards. Chapter I carries the definitions. Chapter II states the right itself. Chapter III distributes duties among the appropriate government, local authorities and parents. Chapter IV governs schools and teachers. Chapter V addresses curriculum and the completion of elementary education. Chapter VI creates the protection and monitoring machinery. Chapter VII carries the miscellaneous provisions, including the power of the Central Government to issue guidelines under Section 35 and the rule-making power in Section 38. Two features inserted by later amendment belong to the identity of the Act as it stands: Section 1(4), added in 2012, provides that the Act applies subject to Articles 29 and 30 of the Constitution, and Section 1(5), added at the same time, provides that nothing in the Act applies to madrasas, vedic pathshalas and educational institutions primarily imparting religious instruction.

## What the right itself contains

Section 3(1) is the operative sentence of the statute: every child of the age of six to fourteen years shall have the right to free and compulsory education in a neighbourhood school till the completion of elementary education. Each element of that sentence is a defined term doing work. A child, under Section 2(c), means a male or female child of the age of six to fourteen years. Elementary education, under Section 2(f), means education from first class to eighth class. A neighbourhood school is a school within the limits of neighbourhood the state prescribes, a concept Section 6 obliges governments to give physical form. Free, under Section 3(2), means that no child shall be liable to pay any kind of fee or charges or expenses which may prevent the child from pursuing and completing elementary education. Compulsory carries the obligation in the other direction: the compulsion in this statute falls on the state to provide, admit and retain, rather than on the parent to send, and the only parental provision, Section 10, states a duty without attaching any penalty to its breach.

Section 4 deals with the child the system has already failed. Where a child of the relevant age has not been admitted to any school, or having been admitted could not complete elementary education, the child shall be admitted to a class appropriate to the age of the child. The school must then arrange special training, in the manner and within the time limits prescribed, so that the child can be brought level with the class, and a child admitted under this route remains entitled to free education until completion of elementary education even after crossing the age of fourteen. This provision is the statute's repair mechanism, and in enrolment terms it is among its most used: the child who enters school for the first time at nine goes to the class other nine year olds attend, not to the first class, and the catching up is the school's obligation rather than the child's problem.

Section 5 protects movement between schools. A child has the right to seek transfer to another school for completing elementary education, and the head teacher or teacher in charge of the school the child is leaving must immediately issue a transfer certificate. A delay in producing the certificate from the previous school cannot be a ground to delay or deny admission in the new school, and a head teacher who delays issuing the certificate is liable to disciplinary action under the applicable service rules. Section 14 closes the documentary door that schools habitually use against poor families: no child shall be denied admission in a school for lack of age proof, and age is to be determined on the basis of the birth certificate issued under the births and deaths registration law or such other document as may be prescribed. Section 15 completes the admission protections by accommodating the child who arrives late: admission may be sought beyond the start of the academic year, within the extended period prescribed, and a child admitted late must be enabled to complete studies in the manner the academic authority prescribes.

## The duties cast on governments and local authorities

The Act is built on a distinction between two duty holders. The appropriate government, defined in Section 2(a), is the Central Government for schools it establishes or controls and for territories without a legislature, and the state government elsewhere. The local authority, defined in Section 2(h), is the municipal corporation, municipal council, zila parishad, nagar panchayat, panchayat or equivalent body in charge of the area where the school stands. Most of what the Act demands of the state runs through these two channels, with the Centre carrying the standard-setting and financing role and the state and local bodies carrying delivery.

Section 6 requires the appropriate government and the local authority to establish, within the area or limits of neighbourhood prescribed, a school where one does not exist, within three years of commencement. The neighbourhood limits are fixed by state rules rather than by the Act itself, and the pattern adopted across states places a primary school within about one kilometre of a habitation and an upper primary school within about three kilometres, adapted for terrain and sparse settlement. The duty sounds simple and has proved to be one of the harder ones: the obligation is not met by a school on a map, but by a functioning school with teachers, and the distance norm interacts with transport, safety and the age of the child in ways a bare figure does not capture. Section 7 governs money. The Central and state governments bear concurrent responsibility for providing funds, the Centre prepares estimates of capital and recurring expenditure, and it may make grants to states as a percentage of expenditure on the recommendation of the Finance Commission or otherwise; the states must then provide the balance, taking into account their own resources and funds available from other sources. The Act deliberately fixes no ratio in its text, which is why the sharing pattern between Centre and states has been set in programme documents and revised from time to time, and why underfunding arguments in this field are arguments about budget heads rather than about breach of a stated figure.

Section 8 collects the duties of the appropriate government. It must provide free and compulsory elementary education to every child, ensure the availability of a neighbourhood school, ensure that a child belonging to a weaker section or a disadvantaged group is not discriminated against or prevented from completing elementary education, provide infrastructure including school buildings, teaching staff and learning material, provide special training under Section 4, ensure good quality elementary education conforming to the norms in the Schedule, ensure the timely prescription of curriculum and courses of study, and provide training facilities for teachers. Section 9 assigns the parallel duties to the local authority, which must provide free and compulsory education within its jurisdiction, maintain records of children up to the age of fourteen residing in its area, monitor the functioning of schools, decide the academic calendar, and ensure admission and attendance; it may also perform additional prescribed functions and establish pre-school provision. Section 11 adds that the appropriate government may make necessary arrangements for providing free pre-school education and early childhood care for children above the age of three until they complete six, a discretionary provision that mirrors the endeavour language of the substituted Article 45 and confers no individual claim.

Because education sits in the Concurrent List, having been moved there from the State List by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, both Parliament and the state legislatures can legislate on it, and the Act works through state rules, state academic authorities and state commissions at almost every operating point. The competence question, the repugnancy rules and the way central framework legislation coexists with state school law are treated in the companion piece on [education and the Concurrent List](/2015/05/01/education-concurrent-list-federalism/), and the reader who needs the federal mechanics should go there. The point for the pillar is narrower: almost every number a parent will meet in practice, from the neighbourhood distance to the extended admission period to the composition rules for committees, arrives through a state rule made under Section 38 rather than from the central text, and the first step in any concrete dispute is to find the state rule that fills the central frame.

## Which schools the Act binds, and the recognition gate

Section 2(n) divides schools into four categories, and the obligations of the Act distribute differently across them. The first category is a school established, owned or controlled by the appropriate government or a local authority. The second is an aided school, receiving aid or grants to meet the whole or part of its expenses from the appropriate government or local authority. The third is a school belonging to a specified category, namely the Kendriya Vidyalayas, the Navodaya Vidyalayas, the Sainik Schools and any other school having a distinct character which the government may notify. The fourth is an unaided school, receiving no aid or grants of that kind. Government and aided schools must provide free education to every child admitted. Specified category and unaided schools carry the admission obligation described later in this article. Minority institutions, after the judgments examined below, stand outside the Act altogether, and institutions of religious instruction stand outside by the terms of Section 1(5).

### Which schools does the Right to Education Act bind?

The Act binds government schools, aided schools, specified category schools such as the Kendriya Vidyalayas and Navodaya Vidyalayas, and unaided private schools, each with different obligations. It does not bind minority institutions, aided or unaided, after the Constitution Bench judgment of 2014, nor madrasas and pathshalas primarily imparting religious instruction.

Section 18 is the gate through which every school must pass. No school, other than a school established, owned or controlled by the appropriate government or a local authority, shall, after the commencement of the Act, be established or function without obtaining a certificate of recognition from the prescribed authority, granted where the school fulfils the norms and standards in the Schedule and the additional conditions the section prescribes. A person who establishes or runs a school without obtaining a certificate of recognition, or continues to run a school after recognition is withdrawn, is liable to a fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, and in the case of a continuing contravention to a further fine of ten thousand rupees for each day during which the contravention continues. Section 19 ties recognition to the Schedule: a school shall not be granted recognition unless it fulfils the norms and standards specified there, schools established before commencement were given three years to bring themselves up to the required norms at their own expense, and where a school fails to fulfil the norms within that period the authority must withdraw recognition, with the children of a school whose recognition is withdrawn continuing their education in a neighbouring school.

The recognition regime generated one of the Act's genuine unintended consequences, and the pillar should record it plainly. Large numbers of low-fee private schools, many of them the only school within reach of the families who used them, could not meet infrastructure norms written with the government school in mind, particularly land and playground requirements in dense urban areas. Enforcement of the recognition gate in several states produced closure notices against such schools, and the children displaced were entitled on paper to places in neighbouring schools that parents had not chosen and sometimes could not reach. The defence of the gate is equally plain: a right to education that says nothing about the conditions in which education happens would permit the state to discharge its duty through premises that are unsafe or unfit, and the norms in the Schedule are the content of the quality floor. Both points are true, and the practical literature of the Act is full of the friction between them. The statute's own answer is the three-year compliance window and the staged enforcement of recognition, and whether administrators used the window as a ladder or as a cliff is a question of record rather than of text.

## The Schedule: norms and standards

The Schedule is the engineering drawing of the Act, and it is the part most often misquoted, so the principal norms are worth stating exactly as they stand. For the building, every school must have an all-weather building consisting of at least one classroom for every teacher, an office-cum-store-cum-head-teacher room, barrier-free access, separate toilets for boys and girls, safe and adequate drinking water, a kitchen where the mid-day meal is cooked in the school, a playground, and arrangements for securing the school building by boundary wall or fencing. The Schedule further requires a library in each school providing newspapers, magazines and books on all subjects including story books, and play material, games and sports equipment as required.

For staffing, the Schedule sets the pupil teacher ratio by enrolment bands. In classes one to five, a school with up to sixty admitted children must have two teachers; with sixty-one to ninety children, three teachers; with ninety-one to one hundred and twenty, four teachers; with one hundred and twenty-one to two hundred, five teachers; above one hundred and fifty children, five teachers and a head teacher; and where admitted children exceed two hundred, the pupil teacher ratio, excluding the head teacher, shall not exceed forty. In classes six to eight, there must be at least one teacher for every class so that there is at least one teacher each for science and mathematics, for social studies and for languages, and at least one teacher for every thirty-five children; where admission exceeds one hundred, the school must have a full-time head teacher and part-time instructors for art education, health and physical education, and work education. For the school year, the Schedule prescribes a minimum of two hundred working days for classes one to five and two hundred and twenty working days for classes six to eight, a minimum of eight hundred instructional hours per academic year at the primary stage and one thousand instructional hours at the upper primary stage, and a minimum of forty-five teaching including preparation hours per week for a teacher.

### What pupil teacher ratio does the Schedule prescribe?

At the primary stage the Schedule works by enrolment bands, from two teachers for up to sixty children to five teachers and a head teacher above one hundred and fifty, with the ratio not to exceed forty children per teacher in the largest schools. At the upper primary stage it requires at least one teacher for every thirty-five children.

Section 25 operationalises the ratios by requiring the appropriate government and the local authority to ensure that the pupil teacher ratio specified in the Schedule is maintained in each school, and Section 26 adds the discipline that vacancies of teachers in a school established, owned or controlled by the appropriate government or local authority shall not exceed ten per cent of the total sanctioned strength. The gap between these provisions and the staffing position in government schools has been a standing feature of the implementation record: the norm is unambiguous, the measurement is public, and the shortfall in several large states has been measured in tens of thousands of sanctioned posts unfilled and in single-teacher schools that cannot, by construction, satisfy a banded ratio at all. The statute anticipated part of the problem: its answer to a vacancy or a single teacher is not a defence available to the duty holder, because the obligation in Sections 25 and 26 is framed as a duty to ensure and a cap that shall not be exceeded.

## The prohibitions: what a school may not do

The prohibitions are the provisions a parent is most likely to need, and they are scattered across the Act in a way that makes them hard to assemble from the bare text. Section 13(1) provides that no school or person shall, while admitting a child, collect any capitation fee and subject the child or the parents or guardian to any screening procedure. Section 13(2) attaches the teeth: whoever contravenes by receiving a capitation fee is punishable with a fine which may extend to ten times the capitation fee charged, and whoever subjects a child to a screening procedure is punishable with a fine which may extend to twenty-five thousand rupees for the first contravention and fifty thousand rupees for each subsequent contravention. Screening procedure is a defined term in Section 2(o): the method of selection for admission of a child, in preference to another child not selected by the method of random selection. An interview of the child, a test of the child, or an assessment of the parents' income, education or occupation used to pick one child over another falls inside the prohibition; admission by lottery or other random method does not.

Section 17 addresses the treatment of the child inside the school: no child shall be subjected to physical punishment or mental harassment, and whoever contravenes is liable to disciplinary action under the service rules applicable to that person. The absence of a fine in this section is deliberate drafting rather than oversight: the sanction runs through employment discipline, which for a government teacher can be severe, and the provision sits alongside the general criminal and child protection law rather than replacing it. Section 28 forbids a teacher to engage in private tuition or private teaching activity, again enforced through the service framework. Section 30(1) provides that no child shall be required to pass any board examination till the completion of elementary education, and Section 30(2) requires that a child who completes elementary education be awarded a certificate in the prescribed form and manner. Above them all sits the free education rule of Section 3(2): a demand that is dressed as a voluntary contribution, an activity charge or a development fee still breaches the Act if its practical effect is to prevent a child from pursuing or completing elementary education in a school obliged to provide that education free.

The complaint route it refers to is the machinery of Section 32, examined in its own section below: a written complaint to the local authority having jurisdiction, which must decide the matter within three months after giving a reasonable opportunity of being heard to the parties, with an appeal from that decision to the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights or the authority prescribed for states where no such commission functions.

## The admission obligation on private schools, in outline

Section 12 distributes the cost of the entitlement across school types, and one limb of it has dominated public discussion of the Act. Section 12(1)(a) requires a government or local authority school to provide free and compulsory elementary education to all children admitted. Section 12(1)(b) requires an aided school to provide free and compulsory education to a proportion of children at least equal to the proportion its annual recurring aid bears to its annual recurring expenses, subject to a minimum of twenty-five per cent. Section 12(1)(c), the provision at the centre of the litigation, requires a specified category school and an unaided school to admit in class one, and in the case of a pre-primary class in that class, children belonging to weaker sections and disadvantaged groups in the neighbourhood, to the extent of at least twenty-five per cent of the strength of that class, and to provide free and compulsory elementary education to them till completion. Section 12(2) requires the state to reimburse a school providing education under the twenty-five per cent limb to the extent of the per-child expenditure incurred by the state or the actual fee charged, whichever is less, and a school receiving reimbursement for a child cannot charge that child any fee for the elementary education provided.

This pillar states the provision and stops there, deliberately. The working record of the twenty-five per cent obligation, the pattern of applications and allotments, the reimbursement disputes between schools and state governments, the question of which classes and which categories of disadvantage the state definitions capture, and the reasons the seats that exist on paper are not always the seats filled in a given year, belong to the specialist analysis of [the twenty-five per cent quota in operation](/2015/02/01/rte-25-percent-quota-implementation/), and the questions that record raises are answered in that article rather than here. What the pillar must settle is the place of the provision in the statute's logic: Section 12(1)(c) is the point where the Act conscripts private capacity into a public duty, on the reasoning that the state's obligation to provide a neighbourhood school can be discharged in part by purchasing places in schools that already exist, and it is precisely that conscription, applied to institutions a community maintains for itself, that the minority-institution litigation put in issue.

## Teachers: qualifications, duties and deployment

Chapter IV turns from the school to the person teaching in it. Section 23(1) provides that any person possessing the minimum qualifications laid down by an academic authority authorised by the Central Government, by notification, shall be eligible for appointment as a teacher. The Central Government designated the National Council for Teacher Education as that academic authority by notification issued on 25 August 2010, and the Council laid down the minimum qualifications for appointment, which include, in addition to the academic and training qualifications for the relevant stage, a pass in the teacher eligibility test conducted by the appropriate government in accordance with the Council's guidelines. The institutional history of the Council, the structure of teacher education and the working of the eligibility test are the subject of the companion article on [the NCTE Act and teacher education](/2015/03/01/ncte-act-teacher-education-india/); the pillar's concern is the place of the qualification rule inside this Act, where it operates as a condition of lawful appointment rather than as a professional aspiration.

### Who lays down teacher qualifications under the Act?

Teacher qualifications are laid down by an academic authority the Central Government authorises by notification under Section 23(1), and the authority designated is the National Council for Teacher Education. Its qualifications include a pass in the teacher eligibility test conducted by the appropriate government under the Council's guidelines.

Section 23(2) contained the Act's first built-in confession of difficulty. Where a state did not have adequate institutions offering teacher education courses, or did not have enough trained persons for appointment, the Central Government could relax the minimum qualifications for a period not exceeding five years, and teachers who at commencement did not possess the qualifications were given five years to acquire them. That period ran out on 31 March 2015 with the task unfinished. The Ministry of Human Resource Development stated in 2017 that of about 66.41 lakh teachers at the elementary level, about 11 lakh remained untrained, of whom 5.12 lakh were in government and aided schools and 5.98 lakh in private schools. Parliament responded with the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (Amendment) Act, 2017, Act 24 of 2017, which inserted a further proviso in Section 23(2) giving every teacher appointed or in position on 31 March 2015 who lacked the prescribed qualifications four further years from the commencement of the amendment to acquire them, a window that closed on 31 March 2019, with training delivered at scale through open and distance learning under the National Institute of Open Schooling. The episode matters beyond its dates: it is the clearest demonstration in the Act's history that a statutory standard does not conjure the institutions needed to meet it, and that when the two collide, it is the standard that is amended.

Section 24 prescribes the duties of a teacher appointed under Section 23: to maintain regularity and punctuality in attending school; to conduct and complete the curriculum in accordance with Section 29(2); to complete the entire curriculum within the specified time; to assess the learning ability of each child and supplement additional instruction as required; to hold regular meetings with parents and guardians and apprise them of attendance, ability to learn and progress; and to perform such other duties as may be prescribed. Default in these duties exposes the teacher to disciplinary action under the applicable service rules, after a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and the grievances of teachers themselves are to be redressed in the prescribed manner. Section 27 then protects teaching time from the administration: no teacher shall be deployed for any non-educational purposes other than the decennial population census, disaster relief duties and duties relating to elections to the local authority, the state legislatures or Parliament. The three exceptions are exhaustive in the text, and the long administrative habit of requisitioning teachers for surveys, campaigns and office work sits outside them. Section 28, already noted among the prohibitions, bars private tuition by teachers altogether.

## Curriculum, evaluation and the detention question

Chapter V is short and carries two of the Act's most debated choices. Section 29(1) provides that the curriculum and the evaluation procedure for elementary education shall be laid down by the academic authority specified by the appropriate government by notification, which for the central sphere is the National Council of Educational Research and Training. Section 29(2) requires that authority, in laying down curriculum and evaluation, to take into consideration that the curriculum conforms to the values enshrined in the Constitution; that it develops the child all round, building knowledge, potentiality and talent; that it develops physical and mental abilities to the fullest extent; that learning proceeds through activities, discovery and exploration in a child-friendly and child-centred manner; that the medium of instruction shall, as far as practicable, be the mother tongue of the child; that learning is made free of fear, trauma and anxiety, with the child helped to express views freely; and that there is comprehensive and continuous evaluation of the understanding of the child and the ability to apply it. Section 30, noted among the prohibitions, bars any board examination until elementary education is complete and substitutes a completion certificate.

As enacted, Section 16 completed this design with a single sentence: no child admitted in a school shall be held back in any class or expelled from school till the completion of elementary education. The reasoning behind automatic progression was empirical before it was ideological. Repetition in the early classes was strongly associated with children leaving school altogether, the children held back were disproportionately first-generation learners whose families could least absorb an extra year, and a system that assessed a seven year old and then repeated the seven year old was treating the failures of teaching as the failures of the child. Against that stood the argument that progression without any checkpoint removed accountability for learning from every actor in the system at once: a teacher could move a class through the year without ensuring the class could read, a school could report enrolment and completion while its pupils learned very little, and parents received no signal about a child's difficulty until the child reached a stage where the gap could not be closed. The sub-committee of the Central Advisory Board of Education that examined the provision recommended that it be modified, and state governments pressed the same view in consultation.

### Did the Act abolish examinations in elementary school?

The Act as enacted barred holding a child back in any class and separately barred any board examination until elementary education was complete. It did not bar school-level assessment, which Section 29 requires to be comprehensive and continuous. The amendment of 2019 added regular examinations in classes five and eight, with re-examination before any holding back.

Parliament settled the argument by compromise in the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (Amendment) Act, 2019, Act 1 of 2019, which received assent on 10 January 2019 and was brought into force on 1 March 2019. The amendment substituted Section 16 with a provision headed Examination and holding back in certain cases. There shall be a regular examination in the fifth class and in the eighth class at the end of every academic year. A child who fails shall be given additional instruction and an opportunity for re-examination within a period of two months from the declaration of the result. If the child fails the re-examination, the appropriate government may allow schools to hold the child back in the fifth class or the eighth class or in both classes, in the manner and subject to the conditions prescribed; and the same provision expressly preserves the power of the appropriate government to decide not to hold back a child in any class until the completion of elementary education. The bar on expulsion is retained without qualification in Section 16(4). The result is a federalised compromise: the statute supplies the examination and the re-examination as the default structure, while the decision to detain at all is left to each government, so the lived rule differs between states in a way the bare central text will not reveal. Describing the no-detention bar as absolute, as many summaries still do, is a plain error after 2019; describing the amendment as having restored detention everywhere is the opposite error.

## School management committees and the monitoring machinery

Section 21 requires every school other than an unaided school to constitute a school management committee, composed of elected representatives of the local authority, parents or guardians of children admitted in the school, and teachers. Three-fourths of the members must be parents or guardians, proportionate representation must be given to parents of children belonging to disadvantaged groups and weaker sections, and one half of the members must be women. The committee monitors the working of the school, prepares and recommends the school development plan, and monitors the utilisation of grants received. Section 22 requires the committee to prepare a school development plan in the prescribed manner, which forms the basis for the plans and grants made by the appropriate government and the local authority. The 2012 amendment adjusted this machinery for aided minority schools, in which the committee functions in an advisory capacity only. The design intent is legible: the Act tries to move the first level of accountability out of the inspectorate and into a body dominated by the parents whose children attend the school, meeting in the school itself, with the school's own plan and grants as its agenda. Where committees are elected, trained and given real papers to examine, the mechanism bites; where they exist as a signature on a form, it does not, and the distance between those two states is an administrative fact rather than a statutory one. For a parent maintaining the papers of an admission, a complaint and its receipts, or a committee examining a school's grants and utilisation certificates, keeping those records in a form that can be opened and checked on any machine is a practical necessity, and an administrator assembling such a file can open the spreadsheet and presentation records in the browser itself with [an office file viewer covering Excel, Word and PowerPoint formats](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) rather than depend on a single office installation.

Chapter VI builds the external machinery. Section 31 entrusts the monitoring of the child's right under the Act to the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and to the State Commissions for Protection of Child Rights constituted under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005. In addition to their functions under that Act, the commissions must examine and review the safeguards for rights provided by this Act and recommend measures for their effective implementation, inquire into complaints relating to a child's right to free and compulsory education, and take necessary steps in accordance with the inquiry provisions of the 2005 Act. Where a state has no commission, the appropriate government may authorise an authority to perform these functions. Sections 33 and 34 add advisory councils at the national and state levels, constituted by notification, to advise on implementation of the Act in an effective manner.

Section 32 is the provision that turns all of this into a remedy. Any person having a grievance relating to the right of a child under the Act may make a written complaint to the local authority having jurisdiction. On receipt, the local authority shall decide on the matter within a period of three months, after affording a reasonable opportunity of being heard to the parties concerned. A person aggrieved by the decision of the local authority may prefer an appeal to the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, or to the authority prescribed where no commission functions, and that body shall decide the appeal. Three features of this route deserve emphasis. It is free and written, requiring no court fee and no lawyer. It is time-bound at the first stage, which almost no other Indian grievance mechanism of this scale is. And it is a remedy about the right itself: admission refused, fee demanded, child expelled, teacher absent as a pattern, school unrecognised. It does not displace the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226, which remains the route for systemic failures and for disputes the local machinery cannot resolve, but the statute plainly intends the local authority to be the first forum and expects most grievances to end there. A general account of this kind cannot substitute for advice on an individual admission dispute, where the state rule, the school category and the dates of application decide the outcome.

## The courts redraw the map: 2012, 2014 and the reference of 2025

The Act was challenged almost immediately, and the challenges went to its centre rather than its edges. In Society for Un-aided Private Schools of Rajasthan v Union of India, decided in 2012, a three-judge Bench of the Supreme Court upheld the Act by a majority of two to one. The majority held that the Act, including the admission obligation in Section 12(1)(c), was constitutionally valid in relation to government and aided schools and to unaided non-minority schools, resting the obligation on unaided schools on the reasonable restrictions permissible under Article 19(6) on the freedom to practise a profession or carry on an occupation under Article 19(1)(g). The same majority held the Act inapplicable to unaided minority schools, whether they impart secular education or not, because the obligation could not be imposed on institutions protected by Article 30(1). Justice Radhakrishnan dissented in part. The structure of the holding is what matters for the map of the Act: after 2012, the twenty-five per cent obligation bound every unaided school except a minority one, and the remaining provisions bound aided minority schools along with all others.

In Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v Union of India, decided on 6 May 2014 and reported at (2014) 8 SCC 1, a Constitution Bench of five judges went the remaining distance. The Bench upheld the 86th Amendment itself, and upheld Article 15(5), inserted by the 93rd Amendment, which enables special provisions for admission to educational institutions other than minority institutions. But it held that the Act, insofar as it applies to minority institutions whether aided or unaided, abridges the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice under Article 30(1), and is therefore unconstitutional in that application. The consequence is categorical: the Act does not apply to minority institutions at all. Not the admission obligation alone, but the norms, the prohibitions, the committee machinery and the monitoring structure fall away together for that entire category, because the Bench treated the statute as a single scheme imposed on the administration of the institution. Children in minority institutions retain the protections of other law, of the recognition conditions of the state and of Article 21 in its general form, but the specific entitlements of this Act are not theirs to claim.

The tension in that holding is real and should be stated as the Court's critics and defenders each state it. The defence of Pramati is that Article 30(1) is itself a fundamental right held by communities that the Constitution singles out for protection precisely because they are vulnerable to majoritarian standardisation, that the right to administer would be hollow if the state could dictate admissions, staffing norms and committee structure in the name of a general welfare statute, and that a Constitution Bench does no more than take seriously the text that grants minorities institutions of their choice. The criticism is that the exemption is not proportioned to its rationale: a holding aimed at the admission obligation was extended to provisions, such as the bar on physical punishment and the requirement of safe buildings and qualified teachers, that protect children without touching the minority character of a school, and the result is that a child's statutory protection against being beaten or being taught in an unsafe building depends on the management identity of the school the child happens to attend. On 1 September 2025, a two-judge Bench of the Supreme Court in Anjuman Ishaat-E-Taleem Trust v State of Maharashtra expressed exactly this doubt, questioned whether the blanket exemption in Pramati was justified by the reasoning that supported it, and referred the questions it framed to the Chief Justice for consideration by a larger Bench. Until a larger Bench decides otherwise, Pramati remains the governing authority and the exemption stands; the reference records that its foundations have been questioned from within the Court itself, and any account of the Act that omits either half of that position misdescribes the law.

## The amendment arc and the unamended frontier

The Act has been amended three times, and the three amendments pull in three different directions, which is itself a finding about the statute. The first amendment, the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (Amendment) Act, 2012, Act 30 of 2012, widened the Act's inclusiveness and narrowed its institutional reach at the same time. It brought the child with disability within the definition of a child belonging to a disadvantaged group in Section 2(d), inserted a definition of child with disability in Section 2(ee) drawing on the disabilities legislation of 1995 and the National Trust Act of 1999, and inserted a provision in Section 3 entitling a child with multiple disabilities or severe disability to opt for home-based education. The same amendment inserted Sections 1(4) and 1(5), making the Act expressly subject to Articles 29 and 30 and excluding madrasas, vedic pathshalas and institutions primarily imparting religious instruction, and made the school management committee advisory in aided minority schools. The second amendment, Act 24 of 2017, has been described with the teacher qualification deadline: it extended time, conceding that a standard the system could not meet would be met later or not at all. The third, Act 1 of 2019, qualified the no-detention rule, conceding ground to the learning-accountability argument while preserving the expulsion bar and the governmental discretion to detain nobody.

The frontier that has not moved is the age band. The National Education Policy of 2020 recommended that the entitlement be extended so that the right to education covers children from the age of three to the age of eighteen, aligning the statute with the policy's restructuring of schooling into a single continuum from the foundational stage through secondary education. That recommendation cannot be implemented by notification or by amendment of this Act alone, because the band is written into Article 21A itself: extending the fundamental right downward to three or upward to eighteen requires a constitutional amendment altering Article 21A, followed by amendment of the Act, and neither has been enacted. The demand recurs in policy debate because the case for it is strong on its own terms. Early childhood is the period in which learning gaps open and in which intervention is cheapest per unit of effect, and the years from fourteen to eighteen are the years in which children, girls above all, leave education for work and marriage. The case against movement is fiscal and administrative rather than principled: each year added to the band multiplies the state's obligation in buildings, teachers and reimbursement, and the state that has not yet staffed the existing band to its own norms is being asked to promise four more years at both ends. A pillar should record the position exactly: the recommendation exists, the route exists, the amendment does not.

One further structural point belongs to the amendment arc. The Act the reader consults in a bare text is not quite the Act of 2009: Sections 1, 2 and 3 carry the 2012 changes, Section 23 carries the 2017 proviso, and Section 16 is wholly the creature of 2019, with Section 38 enlarged at the same time to give rule-making power over the manner and conditions of holding back. Reading the statute without its amendment history produces the three errors this series flags wherever it finds them: treating the no-detention bar as absolute, treating the Act as covering children up to eighteen, and omitting the minority institution exclusion altogether.

## The record: inputs legislated, outcomes contested

The standing criticism of the Act, and the one any honest pillar must state at full strength, is that it legislates inputs and is silent about learning. The Schedule measures classrooms, ratios, hours and toilets. Section 23 measures qualifications. Nothing in the Act sets a standard for what a child in class five should be able to read, and the evaluation design of Section 29, with its emphasis on continuous assessment and freedom from fear and trauma, deliberately avoided converting schooling into a sequence of pass and fail gates. The learning surveys that run independently of the state give the criticism its evidence. The Annual Status of Education Report, the household survey conducted by the organisation Pratham, found in its 2022 round that enrolment of children between six and fourteen stood at 98.4 per cent, a figure that represents the near completion of the access project the Act was built to finish. The same survey found that only 42.8 per cent of children in class five could read a text written for class two, and that 69.6 per cent of children in class eight could read such a text, figures that had fallen back from the corresponding 2018 round. Enrolment and learning are both facts, and they point in opposite directions, which is why the debate about this Act is so durable.

The strongest form of the criticism, made over many years by educationist Anil Sadgopal among others, is that the Act constitutionalised a low-quality settlement: it guaranteed schooling rather than education, defined the guarantee through facilities that a poor state could claim to provide, diluted the common school idea that the Kothari Commission had placed at the centre of reform, and accommodated private provision through the reimbursement route instead of building a public system in which the children of officials and the children of labourers attend the same school. On this reading the input focus is not an oversight but the essence of the compromise, and the learning figures are its predictable product.

The strongest form of the defence is that the criticism asks of a statute something no statute can deliver. Law can forbid a fee, fix a ratio, require a toilet and disqualify an untrained teacher; it cannot enact comprehension. On this reading, enforceable minimum inputs are precisely the contribution legislation makes to quality, because a class of eighty with an absent teacher defeats any pedagogy, and the learning crisis has causes, in nutrition, in household poverty, in the language distance between home and school and in the training institutions that supply teachers, that sit outside the four corners of an education Act. The defenders add that the access achievement is not trivial: a system that enrols 98.4 per cent of its six to fourteen year olds has solved the problem the twentieth century could not solve, and the remaining problem, of what happens inside the classroom, is addressed by curriculum, training and assessment policy, some of it built on the hooks this Act supplies in Sections 23, 29 and 30.

The verdict this pillar takes is that both accounts are right about different things, and that the text supports a narrower conclusion than either camp usually draws. The Act is best understood as a floor statute: it defines the minimum conditions under which the state may claim to have educated a child, and it makes those conditions individually enforceable, which no Indian education measure before it had done. Its silence on outcomes is a genuine design limitation, not a drafting accident, and the 2019 amendment is the system's own acknowledgment that a floor without any checkpoint had begun to undermine the floor itself. But a reader measuring the Act against the learning survey alone will miss what it actually changed in law, which is the conversion of schooling from a programme a government runs into a set of claims a parent holds.

## The definitions that decide disputes

Statutes of entitlement are won and lost in their definition clauses, and Section 2 of this Act repays close reading because several of its definitions quietly determine the outcome of ordinary disputes. Capitation fee, in Section 2(b), means any kind of donation or contribution or payment other than the fee notified by the school, which is collected in consideration for admission of a child to a school. The breadth is deliberate: the definition captures the substance of the transaction rather than its documentation, so a payment extracted as the price of a seat is a capitation fee whether the receipt calls it a building fund contribution or a voluntary donation, and the only payments outside the definition are the fees the school has notified in the ordinary way. Child belonging to a disadvantaged group, in Section 2(d), means a child with disability or a child belonging to the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, the socially and educationally backward classes, or such other group having disadvantage owing to social, cultural, economic, geographical, linguistic, gender or such other factor as may be specified by the appropriate government by notification. Child belonging to a weaker section, in Section 2(e), means a child belonging to such parent or guardian whose annual income is lower than the minimum limit specified by the appropriate government by notification.

The consequence of those two definitions is that the social reach of the Act is set by notification. Parliament fixed the constitutional categories, the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes and the backward classes, and then handed governments the power to name additional disadvantaged groups and to fix the income ceiling that marks a weaker section household. Two neighbouring states can therefore operate different versions of the same central entitlement, with different income limits and different notified groups, and a family entitled in one state may fall outside the definitions in another. That is not a defect in drafting so much as a consequence of the federal design discussed earlier, but it is the single most common source of confusion in the admission season, because public discussion treats the categories as if they were printed in the Act when the operative lists sit in state notifications.

Other definitions work the same quiet machinery. Parent in Section 2(l) means the natural or step or adoptive father or mother of a child, and guardian carries its ordinary legal sense, which matters when a school must decide whose signature and whose income count. Screening procedure in Section 2(o) has already been noted. School, in Section 2(n), produces the four categories on which the distribution of duties depends, and specified category in Section 2(p) identifies the Kendriya Vidyalayas, Navodaya Vidyalayas, Sainik Schools and other notified schools of distinct character. Appropriate government and local authority, in Sections 2(a) and 2(h), allocate every duty in the Act between the Centre, the state and the local body. A reader who masters Section 2 can predict the answer to most questions the Act generates, because the operative sections almost never add a new category; they attach consequences to the categories the definitions create.

## The child with disability inside the Act

As enacted in 2009, the Act largely passed over disability, and the omission was conspicuous in a statute whose disadvantaged group definition is its principal instrument of inclusion. The amendment of 2012 repaired the point in three moves. It wrote the child with disability into the definition of a child belonging to a disadvantaged group. It supplied a definition of child with disability by reference to the Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities, Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act, 1995 and to the National Trust for Welfare of Persons with Autism, Cerebral Palsy, Mental Retardation and Multiple Disabilities Act, 1999, covering disability, and the categories of autism, cerebral palsy, mental retardation and multiple disabilities, including severe disability. And it inserted into Section 3 the option for a child with multiple disabilities or severe disability to pursue home-based education, a provision debated at the time on the ground that a home-based route, however necessary for a small number of children, sits uneasily beside the Act's premise that education happens in a school with other children.

The Schedule carries the physical side of inclusion in a single phrase, barrier-free access, which obliges every school building to be accessible, and the norms on toilets and drinking water apply to children with disabilities as they do to all children. The later general statute, the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, built its own education chapter on top of this foundation, including a right of a child with benchmark disability between six and eighteen to free education in a neighbourhood school or a special school of choice, and duties on recognised institutions to admit children with disabilities without discrimination and to provide accessible infrastructure and reasonable accommodation. The two statutes must be read together for a child with disability, because the 2016 Act extends the age band to eighteen for that class of children while the 2009 Act supplies the school-level norms and the admission machinery. The pillar records the junction without attempting the full analysis of disability law, which belongs elsewhere in this series; the practical point is that the exclusion of children with disabilities from the original text is a defect Parliament itself corrected within three years, and accounts of the Act that omit the 2012 amendment misdescribe who the Act covers.

## Autonomy against regulation: the private school question behind the cases

The litigation of 2012 and 2014 cannot be understood without the two decisions that frame the constitutional position of private education in India. In T.M.A. Pai Foundation v State of Karnataka in 2002, an eleven-judge Bench held that the right to establish and administer educational institutions flows from the freedom of occupation in Article 19(1)(g) for citizens generally and from Article 30(1) for minorities, subject in both cases to reasonable regulation in the interest of the institution's educational character and standards. In P.A. Inamdar v State of Maharashtra in 2005, a seven-judge Bench held that the state could not impose its reservation policy on unaided professional institutions, minority or non-minority, and that admissions in such institutions could not be appropriated by the state, though a common entrance framework and fee regulation against profiteering remained permissible. Those holdings set the terrain on which the Act was drafted: Parliament knew that an admission quota imposed on unaided schools would be tested against Article 19(1)(g), and that the same quota imposed on minority schools would be tested against the harder wall of Article 30(1).

The judgments on the Act itself map exactly onto that terrain. For unaided non-minority schools, the 2012 majority treated Section 12(1)(c) as a reasonable restriction in the interest of the general public under Article 19(6), emphasising that education is a charitable occupation, that the obligation attached to a fraction of entry-level seats, and that the state reimburses the cost, so the institution is regulated rather than expropriated. For minority schools, Article 30(1) contains no internal restriction clause of the Article 19(6) kind, and the 2014 Constitution Bench treated the protection as correspondingly stronger. The doctrinal lineage of these cases, and their place in the longer argument about Article 21A, belongs to the companion article owning the amendment's judicial history; the pillar's point is the structural one. The Act's reach over private schooling is a gradient, not a switch: full application to government schools, application with reimbursement to aided and unaided non-minority schools, and no application to minority schools, with the gradient fixed by the constitutional text the courts were interpreting rather than by any choice of the draftsman of 2009.

## The deadlines the Act set for itself, and what their passing showed

The Act is unusual among Indian social statutes for the number of dates it writes into its own text, and the history of those dates is the shortest honest summary of its implementation. Section 6 gave governments three years from commencement to establish a neighbourhood school wherever one did not exist, a period that closed on 31 March 2013. Section 19 gave schools established before commencement three years to meet the Schedule norms at their own expense, the same closing date, after which recognition was liable to be withdrawn. Section 25 required the pupil teacher ratios to be in place within three years of commencement. Section 23(2) gave serving unqualified teachers five years to acquire the prescribed qualifications, a period that closed on 31 March 2015. Every one of those dates passed with the obligation incompletely discharged, and none of the consequences the text attaches to default, withdrawal of recognition for schools, ineligibility for teachers, operated at anything like the scale the defaults would have implied.

The pattern deserves a precise name, because it recurs across Indian welfare legislation: the Act's deadlines functioned as planning targets rather than as legal triggers. A state that had failed to build the schools could hardly close the schools that existed for failing the norms, and a state short of trained teachers could hardly dismiss the untrained ones who were teaching its classes, which is why the 2017 amendment extended the teacher deadline rather than enforce it. The finding for the pillar is not that the deadlines were dishonest. It is that the Act's enforcement design assumes the duty holder and the defaulter are different parties, whereas in the largest category of default, the unfilled teacher post and the unbuilt classroom, the duty holder and the defaulter are the same state, and the statute contains no mechanism by which a state can be made to fine itself into compliance. The mechanism that exists for that failure is political and judicial rather than statutory: budget scrutiny, commission review, and the writ jurisdiction exercised at the level of the system rather than the individual school.

## Enforcement beyond the complaint: appeals, writs and recognition proceedings

Section 32 is the front door of enforcement, but three other routes complete the picture and a pillar should map them. The first is internal to the recognition system. Section 18 requires the recognition authority to communicate its order, whether granting or refusing a certificate of recognition, and the Act's scheme, completed by state rules, gives a person aggrieved by the refusal or withdrawal of recognition an appeal to the appellate authority prescribed. For a school, recognition proceedings are existential in a way a fine is not, and the staged structure of notice, opportunity to comply and withdrawal, with the children of a closed school continuing in a neighbouring school, is the part of the Act that school managements litigate most often. The second route is the inquiry power of the commissions under Section 31, which can be set in motion by complaint or taken up by the commission itself and which produces findings and recommendations addressed to governments; the commissions cannot themselves admit a child or fine a school, and their product is pressure and record rather than a decree. The third route is the constitutional one: the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Article 226 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32, through which the systemic litigation on admissions, on schools functioning without recognition, on teacher recruitment and on reimbursement arrears has largely run. The relationship among the three routes is hierarchical in practice: the local authority decides individual grievances quickly, the commissions aggregate grievances into findings about the system, and the courts intervene where the machinery itself has failed or where the dispute is about the validity of a rule or a policy rather than about one child's seat.

## The money in practice: programme, sharing pattern and reimbursement

Section 7 states the principle of concurrent financial responsibility and leaves the arithmetic to the programme framework, and the programme through which the Act was financed in its first decade was the Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan, the flagship elementary education programme that predated the Act and was aligned to its norms after commencement, supplying the school grants, teacher salaries support, civil works and entitlements such as free textbooks through which much of the Schedule was funded. In 2018 the Centre subsumed the Abhiyan, together with the secondary education programme and teacher education, into the integrated scheme of Samagra Shiksha, which carries the Act's entitlements within a single school education programme running from pre-school to the senior secondary stage. The cost sharing between the Centre and the states under these programmes has been fixed by the programme framework rather than the Act, stood at sixty-five parts Centre to thirty-five parts state in the years immediately after commencement for most states, was later revised to sixty to forty for most states with a more favourable pattern for the north-eastern and Himalayan states, and is renegotiated as programme periods turn. The figures matter to the pillar for one reason: the obligations of the Act are unconditional in the text, while the money arrives through a negotiated programme, and the distance between those two facts is where most state-level default is actually produced.

Reimbursement under Section 12(2) is the sharpest point of that friction. The state must reimburse a school that educates a child under the twenty-five per cent obligation to the extent of the per-child expenditure incurred by the state or the actual amount charged by the school, whichever is less. Because the per-child expenditure of a state school system is an average across very different schools, and because the fee of an urban private school frequently exceeds it, the formula caps the school's recovery below its fee in precisely the cases where the obligation bites hardest, and arrears in payment have been a recurring subject of dispute between private school associations and state governments. The statute's answer is built into Section 12(2) itself: a school that has received reimbursement for a child cannot charge that child any fee, so the shortfall, where it exists, is borne by the institution as the price of the obligation, a distribution the 2012 judgment accepted as a reasonable incident of the restriction on the occupation of running a school.


## The school the Act imagines, assembled from its parts

No single section describes a compliant school, because the description is distributed across the Act, and assembling it shows how exacting the composite is. The school stands within the prescribed neighbourhood distance of the children it serves and holds a certificate of recognition granted on the footing of the Schedule. Its building is all-weather, with a classroom for every teacher, an office and store and a room for the head teacher, ramps or other barrier-free access, separate usable toilets for boys and girls, safe drinking water, a kitchen in which the mid-day meal is cooked, a playground, a boundary wall or fencing, a library stocked with newspapers, magazines and books including story books, and play and sports equipment. Its staffing satisfies the banded ratios of the Schedule, its teacher vacancies in the public system remain within the ten per cent cap of Section 26, and every teacher holds the qualifications laid down by the National Council for Teacher Education, including the eligibility test. Its year runs for the prescribed two hundred or two hundred and twenty working days, its timetable delivers the prescribed eight hundred or one thousand instructional hours, and its teachers work the prescribed forty-five hours a week including preparation, undisturbed by administrative requisition outside the three purposes Section 27 permits.

Inside that school, the admission register was filled without a test, an interview or a donation; a quarter of the entry class in a private unaided school arrived under Section 12(1)(c) and pays nothing; the child who entered at nine sits with nine year olds and receives special training; no child has been struck or shamed as punishment; no teacher takes private tuition in the evening; the management committee, three-fourths parents and half of them women, has examined the grants and signed the development plan; assessment has proceeded continuously through the year, a regular examination has been held at the end of class five, and no board examination has been imposed on anyone. The composite is not a fantasy of the drafting pen; it is the literal sum of the obligations in force. Stating it whole is the fairest way to see both the achievement of the Act and the size of the claim it makes on administrative capacity, because every element of the composite is separately measurable, and the measurement of most elements, in most states, is published. The distance between the composite school and the median school is the implementation problem of Indian elementary education stated in statutory units.

## Three disputes worked through the sections

The first dispute is the commonest. A private unaided school tells a family that admission to class one requires an interaction session for the child and an interview of the parents, and that a development charge, described as voluntary, must accompany the form. Three provisions answer the family. The interaction and the interview are a screening procedure within Section 2(o) if they are used to prefer one child over another otherwise than by random selection, and Section 13(1) prohibits them, with the fine in Section 13(2) rising from twenty-five thousand rupees for the first contravention. The development charge, if it is in substance the price of the seat, is a capitation fee within Section 2(b), punishable with a fine up to ten times the amount charged. The remedy is the written complaint to the local authority under Section 32, which must be decided within three months, and the school cannot lawfully condition the seat on either practice in the meantime. If the child is being admitted under the twenty-five per cent obligation, a fourth provision completes the answer: Section 12(2) bars the school from charging the child any fee at all once reimbursement is claimed for the seat.

The second dispute concerns the child the system missed. A girl of eleven who has worked at home and never enrolled is brought to the neighbourhood school in the middle of the year. The school proposes class two, on the ground that she cannot read. Section 4 forbids that placement: she must be admitted to the class appropriate to her age, which is the class her age cohort attends, and the school must arrange special training to bring her to the level of that class. Section 15 permits the late admission itself within the extended period prescribed, Section 14 removes any objection based on the absence of a birth certificate, and Section 3(2) removes any fee. If she crosses fourteen while completing elementary education, Section 4 keeps her entitlement alive until completion. Every element of the instinctive response of the school, to place her by attainment, to demand documents, to treat the year as lost, is answered by a provision, which is why Section 4 is best read as the Act in miniature.

The third dispute is the detention case. A boy in class five fails the annual examination, and the school informs the parents that he will repeat the year. Under the substituted Section 16 that announcement is premature twice over. The failure triggers, first, additional instruction and a re-examination within two months of the declaration of the result; only failure in the re-examination opens the question of holding back at all. Even then, the school may hold him back only if the appropriate government has allowed schools to do so, in the manner and on the conditions prescribed, and the same government may have decided that no child is to be held back in any class. The first question for the parents is therefore not about the marks but about the state prescription: whether holding back is permitted in that state, in that class, on those conditions. What the school may never do, before the amendment and after it, is expel him: Section 16(4) is unqualified, and a school that converts failure into removal has committed the one act in this area that the statute forbids without exception.

## What the Act does not regulate

A pillar owes the reader the negative map as well as the positive one, because a large share of the grievances brought to this Act belong to other law. The Act does not regulate the fees an unaided school charges the children outside the twenty-five per cent obligation and outside its other free seats; fee regulation of private schools, where it exists, is the work of state legislation and state fee committees, and the jurisprudence on profiteering and capitation in higher and professional education runs on a separate track. The Act does not govern secondary or senior secondary education at all, and the conditions of classes nine to twelve, their examinations and their boards stand outside it. It does not create a transport entitlement: the neighbourhood principle assumes a school within walking distance, and the bus fare of a child assigned to a distant school is a matter for state schemes rather than for Section 3. It does not set the salary of teachers in unaided schools, beyond the recognition framework and state rules. It does not measure or mandate learning outcomes, for the reasons examined in the record section. And it does not penalise the parent who does not send a child to school: Section 10 states the parental duty in the language of obligation, but the compulsion of the title is directed at the state, and the absence of a parental offence is a considered feature of the Indian design, distinguishing it from the attendance offences of older compulsory education laws that the Act displaced in practice.

## The state rules layer

Because Section 38 distributes rule-making between the appropriate governments, the Act a family actually deals with is a composite of the central text and a state rule book, and the rule book decides questions the Act leaves open: the limits of the neighbourhood for the purposes of Section 6, the extended period within which late admission may be taken under Section 15, the manner of special training under Section 4, the form of the completion certificate under Section 30, the conduct of school management committee elections, the authority that grants recognition and hears appeals from its refusal, and the specifications that supplement the Schedule. The income ceiling that defines a weaker section household and the list of notified disadvantaged groups are likewise state instruments. Two consequences follow for any reader using this guide in a live matter. The central answers given here are the frame within which every state answer must fit, and they are reliable as far as they go; but the operative figure in a concrete case, the distance, the deadline, the income limit, the appellate authority, is found in the rules of the state where the school stands, and the first professional task in a dispute under this Act is to read the central section and the state rule together. The second consequence is comparative: the same statute produces noticeably different admission seasons in different states, and much of what is reported nationally as the working of the Act is in fact the working of one state rule book under it.

## The older compulsory education laws and what the Act displaced

The Act of 2009 was not the first Indian law to use the word compulsory. Several states carried compulsory primary education statutes of their own, among them the Delhi Primary Education Act, 1960 and corresponding enactments in other states, built on the older model in which the parent was the primary duty holder and non-attendance could attract a penalty after notice and enquiry by an attendance authority. Those statutes were little enforced and largely symbolic, and their model explains by contrast what is distinctive in the central design. The Act of 2009 reversed the polarity of compulsion: the enforceable duties run against the state, the local authority, the school and the teacher, while the parent appears in Section 10 under a duty with no sanction behind it. The choice was deliberate and of a piece with the constitutional settlement of 2002, which placed the obligation in Article 21A on the state. It also explains a recurring criticism and its answer. The criticism is that a compulsory education law that compels nobody to attend is compulsory in name alone. The answer is that the Indian evidence ran the other way: children were out of school because schools were absent, distant, costly or hostile, not because parents were defying attendance officers, and a statute that fined the poorest households for the failures of provision would have punished the victims of the default it existed to cure. Enrolment rose to near universality under the state-duty model without a single parental prosecution, which is the strongest empirical reply the design can offer.

## Guidelines, advisory councils and the soft machinery

Two provisions of Chapter VII and Chapter VI do quieter work than the complaint route and complete the governance picture. Section 35 empowers the Central Government to issue guidelines to the appropriate governments and local authorities for the implementation of the Act, and empowers the appropriate government to issue guidelines and directions, consistent with the Act, to local authorities and school management committees. It was under this power that the Central Government issued its clarification of 23 November 2010 on the position of institutions serving religious and linguistic minorities, the clarification the Supreme Court noticed in the litigation of 2012 before Parliament wrote the substance of it into Sections 1(4) and 1(5). Guidelines under Section 35 are the instrument by which the Centre steers a concurrent subject without legislating afresh, and their legal force is the force of executive instruction within the four corners of the Act: they can clarify and coordinate, and they cannot add a norm the Act does not contain or subtract one it does.

The advisory councils of Sections 33 and 34 are the deliberative counterpart. The National Advisory Council constituted by the Central Government, and the State Advisory Council constituted by each state government, advise on implementation of the Act in an effective manner, with membership drawn in the prescribed manner from persons having knowledge and practical experience in the field of elementary education and child development. Their product is advice rather than direction, and their significance is institutional: the Act creates a standing forum in which the education departments must periodically explain the state of the entitlement to members who are not officials. Read together with the monitoring commissions, the councils show the draftsman distributing the governance of the right across four kinds of body, a duty holder that delivers, a local authority that records and decides complaints, a commission that inquires and recommends, and a council that advises, with the courts above them all. The design is elaborate, and the honest assessment is that its performance has tracked the energy of the particular body in the particular state far more than the elegance of the allocation on paper.

## Entitlements inside the free seat: textbooks, meals and the content of provision

What a child in a government school actually receives under the word free is specified partly by the Act and partly by the programme norms through which the Act is financed, and the composite is worth stating because parents are frequently told that items inside it are chargeable. The Act itself secures the place, the teaching and freedom from any fee or expense that would prevent completion, together with the completion certificate at the end. The programme framework built on the Act supplies free textbooks to children in government and local body schools, support for uniforms, and the school grants that fund the library and the play material the Schedule requires. The mid-day meal, which the Schedule assumes by requiring a kitchen where the meal is cooked in the school, rests on its own line of authority: the Supreme Court's continuing mandamus in the right to food litigation begun by the People's Union for Civil Liberties in 2001 converted the meal from a programme benefit into an enforceable entitlement to a cooked meal in primary schools, and the meal scheme operates as the nutritional companion of the education entitlement. The junction matters practically. A school that charges for the textbook set, or demands a contribution toward the meal, is not merely breaching a programme condition; it is imposing an expense of exactly the kind Section 3(2) forbids where the charge prevents a child from pursuing elementary education, and the complaint route of Section 32 is available for it on that footing.

## The nursery admission confusion

Each winter, the most visible education litigation in the capital concerns admissions of three and four year olds to nursery classes, and a reader could be forgiven for assuming that the Act of 2009 governs it. It does not, and the reason is the lower open end of the statute. The Act's admission protections, its bar on screening and its neighbourhood principle attach to elementary education beginning in class one, and the only point at which the Act reaches below class one is the twenty-five per cent obligation in Section 12(1)(c), which expressly extends to a pre-primary class where the school has one, so that a child admitted under that obligation enters at the school's entry level. Outside that obligation, the criteria for nursery admission, the point systems for sibling and alumni and distance, and the disputes they generate are governed by state executive action and by the recognition conditions of the state education law, tested in court against the autonomy jurisprudence of T.M.A. Pai and P.A. Inamdar and against Article 14, rather than by the sections of this Act. The confusion is instructive about the statute's architecture: the public argument treats education as one continuous field, while the Act governs a defined segment of it, and the segment below six was left to the endeavour language of Article 45 and Section 11 precisely because Parliament in 2002 and 2009 chose not to constitutionalise it. The recommendation of the National Education Policy of 2020 to fold the years from three onwards into the structure of schooling is aimed at exactly this seam, and until the constitutional and statutory amendments follow, the seam remains where the litigation finds it.

## The distinctions an examination candidate must hold apart

Because this cluster is examined heavily, the pillar closes its analysis with the confusions that recur in papers and in print, each of which this article has resolved in its place. The Act covers children from six to fourteen, not to eighteen, and its education is elementary education, classes one to eight, not schooling generally. The no-detention rule is no longer absolute: since the amendment of 2019 there are regular examinations in classes five and eight, additional instruction, a re-examination within two months, and holding back only where the appropriate government allows it, while expulsion remains barred without exception. The Act does not apply to minority institutions, aided or unaided, after the Constitution Bench judgment of 2014 in Pramati, and it does not apply by its own terms to madrasas, vedic pathshalas and institutions primarily imparting religious instruction. Teacher qualifications are laid down by the National Council for Teacher Education as the designated academic authority, not by the National Council of Educational Research and Training, which is the academic authority for curriculum under Section 29. The eligibility test is a condition within the qualifications, not a substitute for them. The twenty-five per cent obligation binds specified category and unaided schools at the entry class; the aided school obligation is proportionate to aid with a floor of twenty-five per cent; and reimbursement is the lesser of the state per-child expenditure and the fee charged. The complaint under the Act goes first to the local authority, which must decide in three months, and not in the first instance to the commission, whose role is monitoring, inquiry and appeal. A candidate who holds those eight distinctions apart holds the skeleton of the Act.

## The administrative record in the official data

The official measurement system for school education is the Unified District Information System for Education Plus, the annual census of schools conducted through the education departments, and its successive reports let the Act be audited against its own norms without relying on advocacy figures. The picture those reports present across the years since commencement has a consistent structure, and the structure is more informative than any single year. Enrolment at the elementary stage is effectively universal in gross terms, and the gender gap at that stage has narrowed to a small margin. The stock of school infrastructure has moved steadily toward the Schedule: the shares of schools reporting a library, a playground, usable toilets for girls and boys, drinking water and barrier-free access have all risen across the reporting period, with some items approaching saturation and others, ramps and libraries among them, lagging in particular states. The persistent failures sit in staffing and in the smaller school. UDISE Plus reporting has recorded of the order of a lakh of schools functioning with a single teacher, a configuration in which the banded ratios of the Schedule and the subject-teacher requirements of the upper primary stage cannot be satisfied by construction, whatever the aggregate ratio for the state may show. Aggregate pupil teacher ratios at the national level have stood within the Schedule's outer limits for years, while the distribution beneath the aggregate, across states, districts and school sizes, contains the actual breaches. That divergence between the average and the distribution is the single most important statistical fact about the Act's implementation, because the entitlement belongs to the individual child in the individual school, and an average satisfied at state level is no answer to the parent whose school has one teacher for five classes.

Two further features of the record belong to the pillar's audit. The first is the recognition backlog: the number of private schools operating without recognition, or under proceedings for it, has remained substantial in several states long after the three-year window closed, which is why the recognition gate operates in practice as a rolling negotiation rather than as the single deadline the text announces. The second is the vacancy position in government schools, where sanctioned posts unfilled have coexisted with the ten per cent cap of Section 26; the cap is expressed as a share of sanctioned strength, so a state can comply with the percentage while understating the establishment itself, and the honest measure of default is the ratio in the classroom rather than the ratio on the sanction register. None of this is hidden. It is published annually by the duty holder itself, which is perhaps the Act's most underappreciated institutional effect: by specifying the norm in a Schedule, the statute made the state's own statistics into an audit of the state.

## The committee trail behind the amendments

The amendments of 2017 and 2019 did not arrive unheralded; each followed a committee or consultative process that stated the problem in terms Parliament then adopted, and the trail is part of the pillar's record. The detention question was examined by a sub-committee of the Central Advisory Board of Education, the apex advisory body in education, which reported in favour of modifying the no-detention provision with safeguards, and state education ministers pressed the same conclusion in the consultative process that preceded the amendment Bill. The larger review of education policy by the Committee for Evolution of the New Education Policy, chaired by T. S. R. Subramanian, which reported in 2016, recommended both the modification of the detention rule and the extension of the right to education downward to pre-primary and upward through the secondary stage, a pair of recommendations the National Education Policy of 2020 later carried into policy language. The teacher qualification extension of 2017 followed the ministry's own enumeration of the untrained teacher stock, stated in Parliament and in the Press Information Bureau material of that year, which put the figure at about eleven lakh teachers and made the case that dismissal of the unqualified at that scale would injure the children the standard existed to protect. The pattern across all three episodes is the same, and it is the pattern of the deadline history examined earlier: the standard is legislated at the level of principle, the system reports that the standard cannot be met on the stated timetable, and Parliament adjusts the timetable or the rule while keeping the principle in the text. A reader who understands that rhythm can predict how the remaining frontiers, the age band above all, will move if they move: first the committee recommendation, then the policy document, then, if the fiscal settlement follows, the amendment.

## The anatomy of Section 12(1)(c)

The most litigated limb of the Act rewards a slow reading, because three of its drafting choices decided the cases before they were argued. The first choice is the entry point: the obligation attaches in class one, and where the school has a pre-primary class, in that class, so the child admitted under the obligation travels with the cohort through the whole of elementary education rather than being inserted at an arbitrary point. The second choice is the beneficiary definition by cross-reference: the children are those belonging to weaker sections and disadvantaged groups in the neighbourhood, which ties the obligation to the Section 2 definitions and therefore to the state notifications that fix the income ceiling and the additional groups, and ties it geographically to the neighbourhood the school serves. The third choice is the fraction and its floor: at least twenty-five per cent of the strength of that class, a minimum the school cannot dilute by expanding the class definition, since strength is measured against the class as constituted. Around those choices the reimbursement rule of Section 12(2) closes the financial circuit and the bar on charging the child prevents the cost from being passed back to the beneficiary. The litigation then asked the only questions left open by so complete a text: whether the Constitution permits the obligation to be imposed on an unaided school at all, answered affirmatively in 2012 under Article 19(6), and whether it permits the obligation to be imposed on a minority school, answered negatively in 2014 under Article 30(1). The implementation of the obligation in the states, the admission cycles, the lotteries and the reimbursement disputes occupy the specialist article linked from the outline section of this pillar, and are deliberately not rehearsed here; the anatomy matters to the pillar because it shows that the cases turned on constitutional permission, not on any ambiguity in what the section commands.

## The Act among the entitlement statutes

Placed beside the other great entitlement measures of its period, the employment guarantee of 2005 and the food security statute of 2013, the Act of 2009 shares a recognisable design grammar and one significant departure. The grammar is common: a benefit defined in the text, a duty cast on identified levels of government, a local institution, the panchayat there and the school management committee here, through which the beneficiary community watches the delivery, a grievance route that begins locally, and a monitoring body above it. The departure is in the nature of the benefit. Employment under the guarantee is demanded year by year and food under the security statute is delivered month by month, so default is visible immediately and the entitlement renews itself for enforcement. Education is delivered continuously over eight years to a beneficiary who cannot assess its quality and who will have left the institution before the consequences of default are measurable, which is why the Act leans so heavily on input norms that can be inspected at any visit rather than on the outcome that can only be known years later. The design choice examined critically in the record section of this article is, on this comparison, less a failure of imagination than the predictable shape of a statute guaranteeing a long-duration service to beneficiaries who cannot monitor it. The comparison also locates the Act's enforcement weakness precisely: its grievance machinery is built for the visible defaults, the fee, the refusal, the expulsion, and has little purchase on the invisible one, the year in which a child was enrolled, attended and certified, and learned very little. No Indian entitlement statute has solved the invisible default, and the contribution of the 2019 amendment was to install, at two points in the eight years, a checkpoint at which the invisible becomes briefly visible, with consequences attached.

## Section 3 dissected: free, compulsory, neighbourhood, completion

The operative sentence of the Act repays word by word attention, because litigation and administration both turn on its components. Free is defined by consequence in Section 3(2): the bar is on any fee, charge or expense which may prevent the child from pursuing and completing elementary education, so the question in any dispute is not what the demand is called but whether it stands between the child and completion. Compulsory is the word most often misread as a direction to parents. In the scheme of Chapter III it is the state that is compelled: Section 8 opens by obliging the appropriate government to provide free and compulsory elementary education to every child, and the machinery of neighbourhood schools, records of children and monitoring of attendance is the machinery of that compulsion. Neighbourhood converts geography into entitlement: the right is not to a school place in the abstract but to a school within the prescribed limits, which is why Section 6 is a duty and not a programme description. Completion is the terminal word and the one that carries Sections 4, 16 and 30: the right runs till the completion of elementary education, which is why the late entrant keeps it past fourteen, why expulsion before completion is barred, and why the completion certificate under Section 30(2) is the documentary end of the right. The certificate itself has a practical importance out of proportion to its single subsection, because it is the instrument by which the child passes into secondary schooling in systems that ask for proof of elementary completion, and a school that withholds it reproduces, at the exit gate, the same obstruction the transfer certificate rule forbids at the point of movement between schools.

## Language of instruction and the limits of state prescription

Section 29(2)(f) records that the medium of instruction shall, as far as practicable, be in the mother tongue of the child, and the qualifying phrase carries a constitutional settlement that the courts supplied. In State of Karnataka v Associated Management of English Medium Primary and Secondary Schools, decided by a Constitution Bench in 2014, the Supreme Court held that the state could not compel the choice of mother tongue or of the regional language as the medium of instruction at the primary stage against the wishes of the parent, locating the choice in the freedoms of speech and occupation and in the rights of minorities, and treating the imposition as an unreasonable restriction. The consequence for the Act is that Section 29(2)(f) operates as a direction to the academic authority in designing curriculum, to be applied as far as practicable and consistently with parental choice, and not as a power in a school or a state to override the medium a parent selects for a child in an unaided institution. The episode is a useful miniature of the Act's general legal character: its provisions frequently state the educational consensus of the drafting moment in strong language, and their enforceable content is fixed by the constitutional rights that surround them, so the pillar reader should always ask of a ringing phrase in this Act whether a judgment has already marked the point at which it stops.

## Discipline inside the school and the reach of Section 17

Section 17 is two sentences long, and they carry the whole of the Act's position on the treatment of the child's person: no child shall be subjected to physical punishment or mental harassment, and whoever contravenes the provision shall be liable to disciplinary action under the service rules applicable to that person. Three features deserve emphasis in a pillar. The prohibition is absolute in form; it admits no exception for reasonableness of chastisement, and it therefore displaced, within the schools the Act governs, the older common law position under which a teacher stood in the place of a parent for purposes of moderate correction. The sanction is disciplinary rather than penal, which means its severity depends on the service rules of the employer and its pursuit depends on the management and the department acting; the section creates no offence a police officer can register, and conduct that amounts to an offence under the general criminal law or the juvenile justice legislation must travel under those instruments. And the prohibition, after Pramati, does not run in minority institutions as a matter of this Act, which is the point at which the 2025 reference directed its sharpest question: the protection of a child against being beaten in school is difficult to connect to the minority character of the institution's management, and the extension of the exemption to provisions of this kind is the part of the 2014 holding its critics find hardest to defend on the judgment's own reasoning. The state education law and the criminal law continue to govern conduct in such schools, but the statutory sentence itself does not reach them, and an accurate pillar must record the gap rather than smooth it over.

## The eligibility test as a continuing controversy

The teacher eligibility test deserves its own passage in the pillar because it is the point where the qualification policy of the Act meets the livelihoods of serving teachers, and the meeting has repeatedly produced litigation. The test entered the Act through the qualifications laid down by the National Council for Teacher Education under Section 23(1), which require a candidate for appointment to have passed the test conducted by the appropriate government in accordance with the Council's guidelines; the central test and the state tests that followed are eligibility screens, testing the academic and pedagogical basics of the relevant stage, and a pass is a threshold for appointment rather than a ranking for selection. For teachers already in service when the qualifications arrived, the Act's own proviso and then the amendment of 2017 supplied the time to acquire them, and the litigation of the following years largely concerned the consequence of not acquiring them: whether an unqualified serving teacher could be terminated, and whether the qualification could be demanded for promotion as well as for appointment. In the Anjuman Ishaat-E-Taleem Trust judgment of September 2025, the same decision that questioned Pramati, a two-judge Bench treated the eligibility test as a requirement reaching teachers in service and not only new entrants, with protection indicated for teachers close to retirement, reasoning that the right of the child under Article 21A includes a right to be taught by a qualified teacher. The pillar states the position at that level of generality deliberately: the detailed service consequences are being worked out in the High Courts and in the reference the judgment generated, and an aspirant or administrator should take the operative rule from the service law of the state read with the judgments, rather than from a summary. What is not in doubt is the direction of travel across fifteen years: the qualification floor has never been lowered, every extension has been of time rather than of standard, and the courts have consistently treated the floor as an incident of the child's right rather than as a mere service condition.

## The head teacher and the school office: where the Act is actually administered

Statutes of this kind are administered less by commissions than by a single office in each school, and the Act quietly loads that office with its paperwork of entitlement. The head teacher or teacher in charge issues the transfer certificate under Section 5 and answers in discipline for delaying it. The school maintains the admission record within the prohibitions of Sections 13, 14 and 15, produces the completion certificate under Section 30, convenes and services the school management committee under Section 21, prepares with the committee the school development plan under Section 22 on which grants depend, and supplies the returns from which the district information system builds the annual record that audits the whole structure. Recognition is renewed and norms are inspected through the same office. When commentators describe the Act as bureaucratic, this is the layer they mean, and the burden is real: in a small school the head teacher is also a full-time class teacher, and every hour of return and record is an hour taken from the instructional time the Schedule counts. The defence of the layer is the defence of the Act itself, that an entitlement which is nowhere recorded is nowhere enforceable, and that the parent's complaint under Section 32 succeeds or fails on the documents this office keeps. The practical conclusion for the reader is unglamorous and true: under this statute, rights travel on paper, and the family that keeps its copies of the admission form, the receipts and the correspondence holds the means of its own remedy.

## Questions of proof: age, income and neighbourhood

Three factual questions recur in disputes under the Act, and the statute answers each with a deliberate relaxation of ordinary proof, because the families the Act serves are those least able to produce documents. The first is age. Section 14(1) provides that for the purposes of admission, the age of a child shall be determined on the basis of the birth certificate issued in accordance with the registration of births and deaths law, or on the basis of such other document as may be prescribed; Section 14(2) then forbids the refusal that the first clause might invite, providing that no child shall be denied admission for lack of age proof. State rules complete the machinery by prescribing alternative documents and, in many states, by accepting a declaration where documents do not exist, since a regime that demanded a birth certificate from precisely the households least likely to have registered a birth would defeat the entitlement at its first step. The second question is means. Entitlement under the twenty-five per cent obligation and the weaker section category turns on the income of the parent or guardian against the ceiling the state notifies, and proof travels through the income certificate machinery of the revenue administration, with the familiar consequences: delay, discretion and occasional rent at the point of issue. The Act does not create that machinery and cannot be blamed for its frictions, but the pillar should record that the gateway to its most redistributive provision is a document issued outside the education system altogether. The third question is neighbourhood. Whether a child lives within the neighbourhood of a school for the purposes of the obligation is decided by the state rules and the maps and distance measurements the local authority maintains, and disputes about a few hundred metres at the boundary are among the commonest in the admission season. In each of the three, the pattern of the Act is the same and worth naming: the central text fixes the right and forbids the obvious obstruction, the state instrument fixes the measure, and the local authority decides the individual case subject to the appeal the statute provides. A parent or school that argues from the central text alone, without the state measure, is arguing with half the law.

## The aided school proportion, worked

Section 12(1)(b) is the least discussed limb of Section 12 and the one that governs the large aided sector, and its arithmetic is worth working once, slowly. An aided school must provide free and compulsory elementary education to such proportion of children admitted as is at least equal to the proportion of its annual recurring expenses met by the aid or grants it receives, subject to a minimum of twenty-five per cent. Take a school whose annual recurring aid from the state meets sixty per cent of its annual recurring expenses: the school must educate free of cost at least sixty per cent of the children admitted. Take a school whose aid meets only a fifth of its recurring expenses: the proportional calculation yields twenty per cent, but the statutory floor overrides it, and the school must educate at least twenty-five per cent free of cost. The design is a sliding scale with a ratchet: the public contribution and the public obligation rise together, and no aided school, however small the grant, falls below a quarter of its admissions free. The comparison with the unaided limb is instructive. The unaided school receives nothing and owes a flat twenty-five per cent at the entry class, reimbursed at the capped rate; the aided school receives a continuing share of its costs and owes a proportional share of its seats, unreimbursed child by child because the aid itself is the payment. Between them the two limbs express the Act's general theory of the mixed school system it inherited: public money, in whatever measure it flows into a school, carries public obligation in the same measure, and the obligation never falls to zero while the money flows at all.

## Why this Act produced so much litigation

Few Indian statutes have been litigated as continuously as this one, and the volume is itself diagnostic. The Act distributes money, seats, jobs and status at enormous scale, and it does so through definitions and proportions that invite dispute at every joint: the income ceiling of a weaker section, the boundary of a neighbourhood, the strength of a class, the recurring expenses of an aided school, the recognition of a small private school, the qualification of a serving teacher, the composition of a management committee. Each joint is a point where a disappointed applicant, a school management or a teachers' association can frame a legal question, and the forums the Act supplies, the local authority, the commissions and the constitutional courts, have all been used at every level. The deeper cause is that the Act legislated into a mixed system it did not create: a vast public sector, a large aided sector carrying the obligations of its grants, a growing unaided sector carrying the autonomy jurisprudence of the decade before, and a minority sector carrying a constitutional protection of its own. A single framework statute laid across four different institutional logics will generate friction at the seams indefinitely, and the seams are exactly where the case law has accumulated. The pillar's map of the Act is therefore best read as a map of those seams, because that is where the next decade of disputes under it will also arise. The volume of litigation should not be mistaken for failure of the statute as such: a rights statute that nobody invokes is usually one that grants nothing worth invoking, and the docket this Act has generated is the record of parents, schools and teachers treating its provisions as real constraints on real decisions, which is the condition to which every entitlement law aspires and which the programme documents that preceded it never achieved in a single admission season, in any state, under any government, across the whole period in which elementary education remained a directive principle rather than a right.

## How a reader should use this Act

The provisions fall into a natural order for each of the three readers the pillar serves. The parent needs, in sequence, the entitlement in Section 3, the admission protections in Sections 4, 5, 14 and 15, the prohibitions in Sections 13 and 17 with the table that assembles them, and the complaint route in Section 32, and can stop there with a complete equipment for the admission season. The teacher and the administrator need the Schedule and Sections 18 and 19 for the institution, Sections 23 to 28 for their own position, Sections 21 and 22 for the committee through which the school is governed, and the substituted Section 16 for the examination duties of classes five and eight. The aspirant needs the whole map but should weight it as the examiners do: the constitutional route through Article 21A and the 86th Amendment, the distribution of duties in Sections 6 to 12, the prohibitions and their penalties, the two great judgments and the reference that has questioned one of them, and the three amendments with their dates and their directions. Read in any of those orders, the Act rewards the same discipline: take the answer from the section, check the state rule that fills the section, and distrust any summary, including every shorter summary of this statute in circulation, that reports the law without its exclusions.

## Verdict

The Right to Education Act is the rare Indian statute whose practical content can be stated in a single breath and whose limits matter as much as its content. A child between six and fourteen is entitled to free education in a neighbourhood school that meets stated norms, taught by qualified teachers, without fee, screening, capitation, physical punishment or expulsion, with a written complaint route that must be answered in three months. The same child has no entitlement before six or after fourteen, no entitlement enforceable against a minority institution, and no entitlement that measures whether anything was learned. Within the wider family of entitlement statutes, from food security to employment guarantee, the pattern of a defined benefit, a defined duty holder and a grievance route is the pattern examined across the cluster in [the guide to welfare legislation in India](/2017/07/01/welfare-laws-india-complete-guide/). The sections that follow answer the questions parents, teachers and aspirants put to this Act most often, in the terms the statute itself supplies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Right to Education Act guarantee?

The Act guarantees every child between the ages of six and fourteen free and compulsory education in a neighbourhood school until the completion of elementary education, meaning classes one to eight. Free means the child cannot be charged any fee, charge or expense that would prevent the child from pursuing or completing that education. The guarantee carries supporting rights: admission to an age-appropriate class for a child who has missed schooling, protection against screening, capitation fees, physical punishment and expulsion, a school that meets the norms in the Schedule, and teachers who hold the prescribed qualifications. The guarantee is enforceable through the complaint machinery of Section 32 rather than being a statement of policy.

### Q: Which children are covered by the Right to Education Act?

Children between the ages of six and fourteen are covered, a category Section 2(c) defines as a male or female child of that age, and the entitlement runs until the completion of elementary education in class eight. A child admitted late into an age-appropriate class under Section 4 remains entitled to free education even after turning fourteen, until elementary education is complete. Children below six are outside the Act; for them the Constitution speaks only of an endeavour to provide early childhood care and education. Children above fourteen are outside it as well, because Article 21A and the Act both stop at fourteen, leaving secondary education to other schemes and state provision. Children with disabilities are expressly included within the disadvantaged group since the amendment of 2012.

### Q: What norms must a school meet under the Right to Education Act?

A school must meet the norms and standards in the Schedule to the Act, which cover both premises and staffing. The building must be all-weather, with at least one classroom for every teacher, an office-cum-store-cum-head-teacher room, barrier-free access, separate toilets for boys and girls, safe drinking water, a kitchen for the mid-day meal, a playground and a boundary wall or fencing, together with a library and play material. Staffing must satisfy the pupil teacher ratios in the Schedule: banded ratios at the primary stage ending at a maximum of forty children per teacher in the largest schools, and one teacher for every thirty-five children at the upper primary stage. The school year must run for at least two hundred working days at the primary stage and two hundred and twenty at the upper primary stage, with the prescribed minimum instructional hours.

### Q: Can a school refuse admission or hold a screening test?

A school cannot subject a child, or the parents or guardian, to any screening procedure while admitting the child, and screening is defined to mean selecting one child in preference to another by any method other than random selection. An entrance test, an interview of the child, or an assessment of the parents used to choose between applicants is prohibited, and the fine for a first contravention is twenty-five thousand rupees, rising to fifty thousand rupees for each subsequent one. Refusal of admission has separate bars: a child cannot be denied admission for lack of age proof under Section 14, admission can be taken within the extended period under Section 15, and a child seeking transfer cannot be delayed for want of a transfer certificate under Section 5. A school that refuses on a prohibited ground can be taken before the local authority under Section 32.

### Q: Is capitation fee illegal in India?

Within elementary education governed by the Act, yes. Section 13(1) provides that no school or person shall, while admitting a child, collect any capitation fee, and Section 13(2) punishes whoever receives a capitation fee with a fine that may extend to ten times the capitation fee charged. The prohibition is aimed at the payment demanded as a condition of admission, whatever name it is given, and it operates alongside the free education rule in Section 3(2), under which a child in a school obliged to educate free cannot be charged any fee or expense that prevents completion of elementary education. The complaint route is a written complaint to the local authority under Section 32. The Act governs elementary education; admission payments in higher education are regulated under different instruments and state laws.

### Q: What happened to the no detention policy?

As enacted, Section 16 barred holding back a child in any class, or expelling a child, until elementary education was complete, so progression through classes one to eight was automatic. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (Amendment) Act, 2019, Act 1 of 2019, substituted Section 16. There is a regular examination in class five and in class eight at the end of every academic year. A child who fails must be given additional instruction and a re-examination within two months of the result. Only if the child fails the re-examination may the appropriate government allow schools to hold the child back in class five or class eight, and the government may instead decide to hold back no child at all. The bar on expulsion remains absolute.

### Q: Does the Right to Education Act apply to minority schools?

After the Constitution Bench judgment in Pramati Educational and Cultural Trust v Union of India in 2014, the Act does not apply to minority institutions at all, whether aided or unaided. The Bench held that applying the Act to such institutions would abridge the right of minorities under Article 30(1) to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, and the exemption covers the whole statute rather than the admission obligation alone. The position had been reached in stages: the judgment of 2012 in the Society for Un-aided Private Schools of Rajasthan case had already held the Act inapplicable to unaided minority schools while upholding it for unaided non-minority schools. A two-judge Bench in 2025 questioned the breadth of the exemption in Pramati and referred the issue to a larger Bench, but Pramati remains the governing decision unless a larger Bench revisits it.

### Q: Who monitors implementation of the Right to Education Act?

Section 31 assigns the monitoring of the child's right under the Act to the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions for Protection of Child Rights constituted under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005. The commissions examine and review the safeguards provided by the Act, recommend measures for effective implementation, and inquire into complaints relating to the right to free and compulsory education. Below them, the local authority carries the working machinery: it maintains records of children, monitors the functioning of schools in its area and decides written complaints under Section 32 within three months. National and state advisory councils constituted under Sections 33 and 34 advise the governments on implementation. Day-to-day supervision of admissions and norms also runs through the school management committees in each school.

### Q: What does free education mean under the Right to Education Act?

Free education has the meaning Section 3(2) gives it: no child shall be liable to pay any kind of fee or charges or expenses which may prevent the child from pursuing and completing elementary education. The test is the effect of the demand, not its label. A charge described as a development fee, an activity charge or a voluntary contribution falls foul of the provision if paying it is in practice a condition of the child continuing in a school that must educate the child free of cost. In a government school the education itself, the textbooks and the mid-day meal sit inside the public obligation. In an aided school the proportion fixed by Section 12(1)(b) applies, and in a school admitting a child under the twenty-five per cent obligation, Section 12(2) bars charging that child because the state reimburses the school instead.

### Q: What is a neighbourhood school under the Right to Education Act?

A neighbourhood school is the school the Act requires the appropriate government and the local authority to establish within the area or limits of neighbourhood prescribed, under Section 6, where one does not exist. The Act leaves the distance limits to state rules, and the pattern states have adopted places a primary school within about one kilometre of a habitation and an upper primary school within about three kilometres, with adjustments for difficult terrain and scattered settlement. The idea carries the older common school aspiration in statutory form: the school a child attends is the school of the locality, reached on foot, rather than a distant institution chosen by examination or fee. The entitlement in Section 3 is expressed as a right to education in such a neighbourhood school, which is why the existence and distance of the school are themselves part of the right and not background detail.

### Q: What happens when a child who has never attended school seeks admission?

Section 4 governs the case. A child above six years of age who has not been admitted to any school, or who was admitted but could not complete elementary education, shall be admitted to a class appropriate to the age of the child, rather than being placed in class one with much younger children. The school must then provide special training, in the manner and within the time limits prescribed, so that the child can reach the level of the class, and the child remains entitled to free education until the completion of elementary education even after crossing the age of fourteen. The provision reverses the traditional practice of treating the missed years as the child's disqualification. It also answers the common school objection that a late entrant can simply be refused: refusal is not an option the statute gives, and the catching up is structured as the school's duty.

### Q: What is a school management committee under the Right to Education Act?

A school management committee is the body Section 21 requires every school other than an unaided school to constitute, made up of elected representatives of the local authority, parents or guardians of children admitted in the school, and teachers. Three-fourths of its members must be parents or guardians, with proportionate representation for parents of children from disadvantaged groups and weaker sections, and one half of the members must be women. The committee monitors the working of the school, prepares and recommends the school development plan required by Section 22, and monitors the utilisation of grants the school receives. In aided minority schools the committee functions only in an advisory capacity, a change made by the amendment of 2012. The committee is the first level of accountability the Act creates, located inside the school rather than in a distant inspectorate.

### Q: What duties does the Right to Education Act impose on teachers?

Section 24 lists the duties of a teacher. The teacher must maintain regularity and punctuality in attending school, conduct and complete the curriculum in accordance with Section 29, complete the entire curriculum within the specified time, assess the learning ability of each child and supplement additional instruction where required, hold regular meetings with parents and guardians to apprise them of attendance, ability to learn and progress made, and perform such other duties as may be prescribed. A teacher who defaults in these duties is liable to disciplinary action under the applicable service rules, after being given a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and teachers may themselves have grievances redressed in the prescribed manner. Two further provisions shape the working life the Act imagines: Section 27 confines deployment away from teaching to three named purposes, and Section 28 prohibits private tuition altogether.

### Q: How can a parent complain about a violation of the Right to Education Act?

Section 32 supplies the route. Any person with a grievance relating to the right of a child under the Act may make a written complaint to the local authority having jurisdiction over the area. The local authority must decide the matter within three months of receiving the complaint, after giving the parties concerned a reasonable opportunity of being heard. A person aggrieved by that decision may appeal to the State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, or, where a state has no such commission, to the authority prescribed for the purpose, and that body decides the appeal. The route needs no court fee and no lawyer, and it covers the typical grievances: admission refused, a fee or capitation amount demanded, a child expelled, a transfer certificate withheld. Systemic failures can also be taken to the High Court under Article 226, but the statute intends the local authority to be the first forum.

### Q: Does the Right to Education Act guarantee education after class eight?

No. The entitlement in Section 3 runs until the completion of elementary education, which Section 2(f) defines as education from first class to eighth class, and the age band in Article 21A and Section 3 ends at fourteen. A child who completes class eight has exhausted the right the Act creates, and education from class nine onwards rests on other foundations: state schooling provision, scholarship and incentive schemes, and the general framework of secondary education policy, none of which gives the individual child an enforceable fundamental right of the same kind. The single carry-over is the child admitted late under Section 4, who remains entitled to free education until elementary education is complete even after turning fourteen. The National Education Policy of 2020 recommended extending the right to the age of eighteen, but that requires a constitutional amendment and has not been enacted.

### Q: Does the Right to Education Act cover pre-primary education for children under six?

The Act does not create an enforceable right for children under six. The fundamental right in Article 21A begins at six, and for the years below it the substituted Article 45 records only that the state shall endeavour to provide early childhood care and education. Section 11 of the Act mirrors that softness: the appropriate government may make necessary arrangements for providing free pre-school education and early childhood care and education for children above the age of three until they complete the age of six, in order to prepare them for elementary education. The word is may, not shall, so the provision authorises and encourages provision through anganwadis and pre-schools without giving an individual child a claim a court or a local authority can enforce. The gap is one of the two open ends of the statute, and the recommendation in the National Education Policy of 2020 to bring the foundational years inside the entitlement addresses precisely this end.

### Q: What is continuous and comprehensive evaluation under the Right to Education Act?

Continuous and comprehensive evaluation is the mode of assessment Section 29(2) directs the academic authority to build into the curriculum and evaluation procedure for elementary education. Section 29(2)(h) requires that there be comprehensive and continuous evaluation of the understanding of the child and of the ability to apply it, and the surrounding clauses explain the philosophy: development of the child all round, learning through activities, discovery and exploration in a child-friendly and child-centred manner, the medium of instruction in the mother tongue as far as practicable, and learning made free of fear, trauma and anxiety. In practice it means assessment spread across the year through the work of the classroom rather than a single terminal examination, recording progress in scholastic and co-scholastic areas. It coexists with the bar in Section 30 on board examinations before elementary education is complete, and the class five and class eight examinations introduced in 2019 operate within schools rather than as board examinations.

### Q: Can teachers be deployed for non-educational work under the Right to Education Act?

Only within narrow limits. Section 27 provides that no teacher shall be deployed for any non-educational purposes other than the decennial population census, disaster relief duties, and duties relating to elections to the local authority, the state legislatures or Parliament. The three exceptions are stated as a closed set, so the routine requisitioning of teachers for surveys, enrolment drives, office work and administrative campaigns that characterised school administration falls outside what the section permits. The provision protects teaching time, which the Schedule counts in instructional hours and working days: every week a teacher spends on outside duties is a week in which the statutory school year is not being delivered. Enforcement runs through the service framework and the complaint machinery, and parents and committees can raise persistent diversion of teachers as a failure of the school to function as the Act requires.

### Q: Can a school withhold a transfer certificate under the Right to Education Act?

The Act treats the transfer certificate as the child's document rather than the school's leverage. Section 5 gives a child the right to seek transfer to another school for completing elementary education where the school has no provision for completion or for any other reason, and requires the head teacher or teacher in charge of the school the child is leaving to issue the transfer certificate immediately. A delay in producing the certificate from the previous school cannot be a ground for delaying or denying admission in the school the child moves to, and a head teacher or teacher in charge who delays issuing the certificate is liable to disciplinary action under the service rules applicable. The rule answers a familiar practice in which certificates were held back over fee disputes or to prevent a child from leaving, and it makes mobility between schools a right the receiving school must honour first and verify later.

### Q: How are the costs of the Right to Education Act shared between the Centre and the states?

Section 7 gives the Central Government and the state governments concurrent responsibility for providing funds for carrying out the Act. The Central Government prepares estimates of the capital and recurring expenditure required, may make grants to state governments, and the percentage of expenditure it bears may be determined with reference to the recommendations of the Finance Commission or otherwise under the section. Each state government must then provide its share, taking into account its own resources and any funds available from other sources. The Act itself fixes no permanent ratio, so the sharing pattern has been set through the programme framework for elementary education and revised between plan periods, which is why arguments about underfunding of the Act are conducted over budget allocations and release patterns rather than over a figure written into the statute. The concurrent responsibility reflects the place of education in the Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule.
