Understanding how a Bill becomes law in India requires separating two things that civics accounts habitually merge: the steps the Constitution commands, and the steps that exist only because Parliament, the Cabinet or the government of the day chooses to observe them. A Bill travels from a policy decision inside a ministry to a printed Act in the Gazette of India, and along that road it passes through drafting, Cabinet approval, introduction, possible committee scrutiny, three readings in each House, resolution of any disagreement between the Houses, assent, publication and commencement. Of those stages, the Constitution regulates only the middle portion, from introduction to assent, and even there it regulates with a light hand. It prescribes no period within which a Bill must be drafted, none within which a committee must report, none within which the second House must act on an ordinary Bill, and none within which the President or a Governor must decide a Bill presented for assent. The single deadline in the entire process is the fourteen days allowed to the Council of States for returning a Money Bill, a deadline that binds the chamber with the least power over that class of Bill. Every other stage can absorb any amount of time without any provision being breached.

That structural fact explains the two controversies that dominate the subject. The first is the near disappearance of the joint sitting, the Constitution's chosen device for resolving disagreement between the Houses, which has been convened three times since 1950 and has left disagreement to be settled by politics, by delay or by the Bill dying with the House that passed it. The second is the assent litigation of 2025, in which the Supreme Court first laid down timelines for Governors and the President and then, through a Constitution Bench answering a Presidential Reference, held that timelines of that kind cannot be imposed at all. Both controversies arise from the same silence. Where the Constitution fixes no period, the question becomes who, if anyone, may supply one: the other House, the courts, or nobody. This article follows a Bill through every stage, identifies at each stage who acts, whether the step is required or discretionary, and what happens if the step never occurs, and it ends with the assent question stated as the two decisions of 2025 leave it.

## The constitutional spine: Articles 107 to 111

The Union legislative process rests on five articles. Article 107 sets out the provisions for the introduction and passing of Bills generally: a Bill may originate in either House of Parliament, subject to the special rules for Money Bills and for Bills requiring the President's recommendation, and a Bill is not deemed to have been passed by the Houses unless it has been agreed to by both Houses, either without amendment or with such amendments only as are agreed to by both. Article 108 provides for a joint sitting where the Houses disagree. Article 109 lays down the special procedure for Money Bills. Article 110 defines a Money Bill. Article 111 governs assent at the Union level: when a Bill has been passed by the Houses, it is presented to the President, who declares either that assent is given or that assent is withheld, with a power to return a Bill that is not a Money Bill for reconsideration. Article 117 deals with financial Bills and with the President's recommendation. Article 118 empowers each House to make rules regulating its procedure, and it is those rules, the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business in the Lok Sabha and in the Rajya Sabha, that supply most of the mechanics described in this article. Article 122 bars courts from calling in question the validity of proceedings in Parliament on the ground of an alleged irregularity of procedure.

Two features of this scheme deserve emphasis before the stages are traced. The first is that the Constitution's text begins at introduction. Everything before a Bill is laid before a House, the policy decision, the drafting, the Cabinet's approval and the public consultation that executive policy prescribes, sits outside the constitutional text altogether, governed by the Rules of Business of the Government of India, by departmental practice and by a consultation policy adopted in 2014. The second is that the Constitution's text ends at assent. What follows assent, publication in the Gazette and the commencement of the Act on a date the Act itself or a notification appoints, is governed by the Act's own commencement clause and by the General Clauses Act, 1897, and the series to which this article belongs has found across many clusters that enactment and commencement are separate events separated, in some instances, by years or by decades.

The special procedures stand apart from the ordinary road and are owned by companion articles in this series. The procedure for Money Bills and the contested boundary between a Money Bill and a Financial Bill are treated in [the comparison of Money Bills and Financial Bills](/2022/02/15/money-bill-vs-finance-bill-india/), and legislation by ordinance, the executive's parallel route when Parliament is not in session, is treated in [the account of the ordinance-making power](/2022/03/01/ordinance-making-power-india/). Both are noticed here only at the level of the map, because the ordinary Bill that this article follows is defined partly by the routes it does not take.

### Which stages of the journey are required by the Constitution?

The Constitution requires only that a Bill be agreed to by both Houses in identical form and be assented to, for Money Bills under a special procedure and for other Bills under Articles 107 and 111. Drafting, Cabinet approval, consultation and committee scrutiny are executive or parliamentary practice, valuable and settled, but none of them is a constitutional condition of a valid Act.

## Before Parliament: policy, drafting and the Cabinet

A government Bill begins as a decision, not as a text. The administrative ministry responsible for the subject concludes that the existing law is inadequate, whether because a court has exposed a gap, a committee has recommended reform, a policy programme requires statutory authority, or the ordinary administration of a scheme has outgrown the rules under which it runs. That conclusion is tested inside the government before any drafting starts: the ministry consults the other ministries whose subjects the proposal touches, the Ministry of Finance where expenditure is involved, and the Ministry of Law and Justice on questions of legislative competence and form. A Cabinet note is prepared, and the proposal travels to the Cabinet, because under the Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961, proposals for legislation stand among the matters reserved for the Cabinet's decision. Only after the Cabinet approves the proposal in principle does the drafting begin in earnest, although drafting and policy formation in practice inform each other throughout.

The drafting itself is the work of the Legislative Department of the Ministry of Law and Justice, acting on the instructions of the administrative ministry. The administrative ministry supplies the legislative proposals, the policy in structured form; the Legislative Department converts them into clauses, definitions, commencement and repeal provisions, and the machinery of offences, penalties, rule-making powers and delegations that a workable statute requires. The drafter's task is constrained by the Constitution in ways the sponsoring ministry sometimes discovers late: the Bill must fall within the legislative competence of Parliament under the Seventh Schedule, it must survive Part III, and where it trenches on a State subject or requires the President's recommendation, those facts must be identified before introduction rather than after. The vetted draft returns to the administrative ministry, is settled with it, and goes to the Cabinet again for approval of the Bill in its final form, together with the Statement of Objects and Reasons, the notes on clauses, the financial memorandum where the Bill involves expenditure, and the memorandum on delegated legislation. Cabinet approval of the text is the point at which a proposal becomes the government's Bill.

Superimposed on this internal process is the Pre-legislative Consultation Policy, adopted by the Government of India in February 2014 through the Ministry of Law and Justice. The policy directs that a department proposing legislation should publish the draft legislation or at least a summary of its salient features, together with an explanatory note, on its website and by other means, ordinarily for a period of thirty days, so that the public may comment; that the comments received should be considered and placed before the competent authority; and that a summary of the feedback should accompany the proposal sent for the Cabinet's consideration. Two qualifications define the policy's real weight. It is an executive policy, not a statute and not a rule made under the Constitution, and it creates no enforceable right in any person: a Bill introduced without the prescribed consultation does not fail for that reason, and no court will arrest its passage or invalidate the resulting Act for want of consultation. Assessments of compliance, including assessments recorded in studies of the policy's working, describe observance as uneven, with many Bills introduced without prior publication of a draft and others published for periods shorter than the policy contemplates. The pre-legislative stage is therefore the least regulated part of the entire process: it is the stage at which the text is most open to influence and the stage at which the citizen's participation rests entirely on the government's adherence to its own declared practice.

The consultation stage is also where the documentary trail of a Bill begins, and that trail matters beyond the legislative process itself. Draft Bills, consultation papers and the comments they attract are the raw material from which journalists, researchers and litigants later reconstruct what a provision was meant to do, and the discipline of keeping such records intact and verifiable is the same discipline described in [the account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), where preserving a document and proving its integrity are treated as one task. A researcher following a Bill across its stages needs the draft as published for consultation, the Bill as introduced, the Bill as passed and the Act as notified, and needs to be able to show that each is what it purports to be.

Private members enter the process at a different point. A member of either House who is not a minister may seek the House's leave to introduce a Bill on a subject of the member's choosing, after giving the notice the rules prescribe. The private member drafts the Bill without the Legislative Department's machinery, although the secretariat of the House assists on form. The constitutional requirements are the same: competence, the President's recommendation where Articles 110 and 117 demand it, and passage by both Houses followed by assent. The practical course is very different, because private members' business is taken up in the limited time the rules reserve for it, ordinarily a portion of one sitting day in a week, and a private member's Bill that the government does not support rarely travels beyond introduction. The distinction matters for the map of the process: the stages before introduction that consume months of a government Bill's life, Cabinet approval and departmental drafting, are replaced for a private member's Bill by a single member's initiative, while every stage after introduction is common to both.


## Introduction and the first reading

Introduction is the first constitutional event in a Bill's life, and the rules surround it with notice and formality. A minister who wishes to introduce a Bill gives notice of the motion for leave to introduce it, and the Bill, with its Statement of Objects and Reasons, is published in the Gazette before or at introduction, unless the presiding officer permits otherwise. On the appointed day the member in charge moves for leave to introduce the Bill. The motion is ordinarily formal and unopposed. Where the introduction is opposed, the presiding officer may permit a brief statement from the member who opposes and a brief reply from the member in charge, and the question is then put to the House. Opposition at this stage is directed, where it is raised seriously, at legislative competence: the objection is that the Bill lies outside the House's power to make, and the presiding officer's practice is to allow the objection its hearing while leaving the substantive question of competence to the courts after enactment. When leave is granted, the Bill is introduced, and that introduction constitutes the first reading.

The first reading is therefore a threshold rather than a debate. No discussion of the Bill's merits, its clauses or its policy takes place at that stage, and the House commits itself to nothing beyond receiving the Bill into its business. What introduction accomplishes is threefold. It fixes the text that the House will consider, because amendments at later stages are measured against the Bill as introduced. It starts the procedural clocks and listings that the rules attach to a pending Bill. And it makes the Bill a public document of the House, published and available, so that the scrutiny which committees, the press and affected persons will conduct proceeds on the authoritative text rather than on summaries. Bills requiring the President's recommendation receive it before or at this stage: Article 117 provides that a Bill or amendment making provision for matters of the Money Bill class shall not be introduced or moved except on the recommendation of the President, and shall not be introduced in the Council of States at all, and that a Bill which, if enacted, would involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India shall not be passed by either House unless the President has recommended to that House the consideration of the Bill. The recommendation is a condition of the stage, not of the Bill's validity in the abstract, and its absence is a defect the House itself polices at the point of introduction.

After introduction, the member in charge has the options the rules give for the next step, and the choice among them is one of the consequential discretions of the process. The member may move that the Bill be taken into consideration, either at once or on a future day; that it be referred to a Select Committee of the House or a Joint Committee of the Houses; or that it be circulated for the purpose of eliciting opinion. The motion chosen determines whether the Bill next meets debate, scrutiny or public comment at the parliamentary stage, and because referral is discretionary, the same formal introduction can lead to months of committee examination or to consideration within the same week.

## The committee stage: scrutiny the Constitution does not command

Nothing in the Constitution requires a Bill to be examined by a committee. Referral is a creature of the rules of the Houses and of parliamentary practice, and its discretionary character is the point on which the quality of Indian legislation is most often debated. A Bill referred to a Select Committee or a Joint Committee is examined clause by clause by a body smaller than the House, constituted in proportion to party strength, chaired ordinarily by a member of the ruling side in the case of a Select Committee, and empowered to take evidence from the ministry, from experts and from affected interests. The committee reports to the House, recommending amendments or reporting the Bill back in the form in which it was referred. A parallel system operates through the Departmentally Related Standing Committees, to which Bills are referred by the presiding officer of the House in which they are introduced, subject to the decision of the House and the practice of the ministry concerned. The scrutiny function of committees, the evidence they gather and the record of their declining or rising use across Parliaments are examined in [the article on parliamentary committees and Bill scrutiny](/2022/04/01/parliamentary-committees-bill-scrutiny/), which owns that subject; here the committee stage matters for its place in the sequence and for its timing.

### Must a Bill pass through a committee before the House debates it?

No stage of the constitutional text requires committee scrutiny, and a Bill may lawfully travel from introduction to passage without any committee seeing it. Referral lies in the discretion of the House on the motion of the member in charge, or of the presiding officer to a standing committee, and Bills taken up without referral are passed on the strength of debate alone.

The timing consequence is direct. A committee has no constitutional deadline for its report. The House fixes a period when it refers the Bill, and committees seek and obtain extensions as a matter of routine. A referred Bill therefore leaves the timetable of the House and enters a timetable that the process itself does not discipline: evidence may be taken over months, the report may be adopted by the committee long after the session in which the Bill was introduced has ended, and the Bill returns to the House only when the report is presented. Because a Bill pending before a House does not lapse merely because a session is prorogued, the committee stage can extend across sessions without the Bill dying, and prorogation, which ends pending notices and motions, leaves Bills untouched. The committee stage is thus the clearest illustration, before the assent stage repeats it, of the article's central observation: the process contains stages whose duration is governed by convention and convenience rather than by any enforceable rule, and a government that wishes to slow a Bill, or an opposition that wishes to force scrutiny, works through this discretion rather than through any constitutional lever.

## The second reading: general discussion and clause by clause consideration

The second reading is the substantive heart of the process in each House, and it unfolds in two phases. In the first phase the House discusses the Bill generally: the member in charge moves that the Bill be taken into consideration, or, where a committee has reported, that the Bill as reported be taken into consideration, and the debate ranges over the principle of the Bill, its objects and its broad scheme. Amendments hostile to the principle, such as motions to circulate the Bill further or to refer it again, are in order at this phase, and the debate closes with the House deciding whether the Bill proceeds. In the second phase the House considers the Bill clause by clause. Each clause is put to the House in turn, amendments to the clause are moved, debated and disposed of, and the clause, as amended or in its original form, is voted to stand part of the Bill. The schedules, the preamble and the long title are taken up in their order, and the enacting formula is agreed to at the end. It is at this phase that the text of the future Act is actually settled, amendment by amendment, and the voting on clauses, rather than the speeches on principle, is what a reader of the finished Act must reconstruct in order to understand why the text reads as it does.

Two disciplines govern amendments, and both shape the legislation that emerges. An amendment must be relevant to the subject-matter of the Bill and within the scope of the Bill as introduced; an amendment that enlarges the scope beyond the Bill's declared objects invites objection, and where an amendment would bring the Bill within the provisions requiring the President's recommendation, the recommendation must cover it. Amendments of which notice has not been given require the presiding officer's permission, and the presiding officer's ruling on admissibility is final within the House. The second discipline is political rather than procedural: an amendment moved by the government carries the government's majority behind it, while an amendment moved against the government's wishes succeeds only where the government's majority is absent, divided or unwilling to be recorded. Clause by clause consideration in the House of the People therefore tends to confirm the text the Cabinet approved, and the amendments that alter a Bill in substance are more often the product of the committee stage, where the ministry negotiates with a smaller body, or of the second House, where the government's numbers may be weaker.

## The third reading and passage

When every clause has been disposed of, the member in charge moves that the Bill be passed. That motion opens the third reading, the final stage in the House. Debate at the third reading is confined by the rules to the Bill as it stands after the second reading: the member may argue for or against the Bill as a whole, but detailed criticism of particular clauses and the moving of amendments are out of order, save for amendments of a verbal or consequential character that correct drafting errors disclosed by the consideration stage. The House then votes on the motion, and if the motion is carried, the Bill is passed by that House. The simplicity of the third reading misleads many accounts of the process, which describe three readings as if they were three debates of equal weight. In the Indian procedure derived from the rules of the two Houses, the first reading is a formality of introduction, the second reading contains both the debate on principle and the settlement of the text, and the third reading is a final vote on the settled text. A Bill is defeated in the House, where it is defeated, at the consideration motion or on its clauses, and the third reading registers a conclusion the House has already reached.

Passage by the first House transforms the Bill's position without completing it. The Bill, in the form in which the first House passed it and authenticated by the secretariat, is transmitted to the other House, where it is laid on the table and enters that House's list of business. Article 107 requires agreement by both Houses, and agreement means agreement to the same text: the second House must pass the identical Bill, and any amendment it makes must return to the first House for agreement before the requirement is met.

## The other House, disagreement and the joint sitting

The second House takes the transmitted Bill through the same stages: introduction there is constituted by the laying of the Bill as passed, the member in charge gives notice of the motion for consideration, committee referral is available on the same discretionary terms, and the readings proceed as in the first House. The second House has four courses open to it on an ordinary Bill. It may pass the Bill without amendment, in which case the requirement of Article 107 is satisfied. It may pass the Bill with amendments, in which case the Bill returns to the first House, which may agree to the amendments, whereupon the Bill is passed, or disagree, whereupon the disagreement must be resolved. It may reject the Bill outright. Or it may simply not pass it, taking no decision while the Bill remains on its pending business. The Constitution attaches consequences to the second, third and fourth courses alike, because Article 108 treats rejection, the passage of more than six months without the Bill being passed, and final disagreement on amendments as the three situations in which a deadlock exists that a joint sitting may resolve.

The joint sitting is the President's to summon. Where any of the three situations arises, the President may notify the intention to summon the Houses to meet in a joint sitting for the purpose of deliberating and voting on the Bill, and at the joint sitting the Bill, with such amendments as are agreed to by a majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting, is deemed to have been passed by both Houses. The Speaker of the House of the People presides, under Article 118, and the rules for joint sittings regulate the amendments that may be moved there: amendments relevant to the matters on which the Houses have disagreed, and amendments made necessary by the delay. The arithmetic of the joint sitting favours the House of the People, whose membership is more than twice that of the Council of States, and a government commanding the first House will ordinarily command the joint sitting as well.

### How many times has the joint sitting actually been used?

Three times since the Constitution came into force. A joint sitting passed the Dowry Prohibition Bill in 1961 after the Houses disagreed on amendments; a joint sitting passed the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill in 1978 after the Council of States rejected it; and a joint sitting passed the Prevention of Terrorism Bill on 26 March 2002 after the Council of States rejected it. No other Bill has been taken to a joint sitting.

The rarity is itself the finding. A mechanism designed as the ordinary resolution of inter-House disagreement has been used three times in more than seven decades, and the reasons are structural. A government facing resistance in the Council of States can wait, because the Council's composition changes as State legislatures renew it, and a Bill that cannot pass in one year may pass in another without the confrontation of a joint sitting. It can redraft and reintroduce, conceding in a new text what it would not concede by amendment. It can route the measure, where the subject permits, through the Money Bill procedure, where no deadlock can arise. Or it can let the Bill lapse and accept the loss. Each alternative is politically cheaper than summoning both Houses to a sitting whose outcome advertises the disagreement it resolves. The consequence for the map of the process is that the Council of States, on an ordinary Bill, holds a real but wasting power: it can delay, amend and embarrass, and it can reject, but its rejection sets in motion either a joint sitting it will probably lose or a waiting game in which the Bill may die with the House of the People rather than with the Council's objection.

The exclusions from Article 108 are as important as the power. A joint sitting is not available for a Money Bill, because the special procedure of Article 109 leaves nothing for a joint sitting to resolve: the Council of States may only recommend amendments within fourteen days, and the House of the People may accept or reject them. A joint sitting is equally unavailable for a Bill to amend the Constitution, because Article 368 constitutes a self-contained code for such Bills: each House must pass the amendment Bill separately by the special majority that article prescribes, and disagreement between the Houses is fatal to the Bill, with no device for overriding it. The amendment procedure, including the further requirement of ratification by the States for amendments touching the matters listed in the proviso to Article 368, is traced in [the article on the constitution amendment process](/2010/01/01/constitution-amendment-process-india/). The exclusions show the design clearly: the joint sitting is a device for ordinary legislation, where the directly elected House is meant in the end to prevail, and it is withheld precisely where the Constitution wants the second House's concurrence, or the States' concurrence, to be a genuine condition.


## The special routes noticed on the map

Three classes of Bill depart from the ordinary road, and the reader of the map needs their outlines without their detail. A Money Bill, defined by Article 110, can be introduced only in the House of the People and only on the President's recommendation; after the House passes it, the Council of States must return it within fourteen days with such recommendations as it chooses to make, and the House of the People may accept or reject any recommendation, whereupon the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses in the form in which the first House finally settles it. A Financial Bill shares some of the Money Bill's features without attracting the deeming procedure, and travels the ordinary road subject to the recommendation requirements of Article 117. The boundary between the classes, the Speaker's certificate that concludes the question of classification within Parliament, and the litigation that boundary has generated are the subject of the companion comparison of Money Bills and Financial Bills, and they are not re-argued here. What matters for the ordinary process is the contrast the special procedure exposes. The fourteen days of Article 109 stand as the only period the Constitution fixes for any House in the handling of a Bill, and it is fixed against the House whose disagreement the procedure is designed to survive. For every other Bill and every other stage, the text fixes nothing, and the six months of Article 108, after which inaction by the second House ripens into a deadlock the President may resolve, is a trigger for a discretion rather than a deadline for the House: nothing obliges the President to summon a joint sitting when six months have passed, and nothing happens to the Bill if no sitting is summoned.

A Bill to amend the Constitution departs in the opposite direction: it may originate in either House, it requires no recommendation, but it must be passed by each House separately by a majority of the total membership of the House and by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, and for the amendments enumerated in the proviso it must also be ratified by the legislatures of not less than half the States. There is no joint sitting and no deeming, so the second House's power over an amendment Bill is the strongest it holds over any class of legislation. An Appropriation Bill and the annual Finance Bill follow the budget procedure of Articles 112 to 116, with demands for grants voted only by the House of the People, and they share the Money Bill's immunity from the ordinary deadlock rules. The ordinary Bill traced in this article is thus the residual and the standard case, and its features, bicameral agreement, possible deadlock, discretionary scrutiny and unbounded assent, are the features the special procedures modify one at a time.

## Every stage and who can stop it

The table below sets the whole journey in one view. Its final column carries the article's argument: at most stages nothing happens if the step does not occur, because no period is prescribed and no consequence is attached to the delay, and the steps at which delay is impossible or self-defeating can be counted on one hand.

| Stage | Who acts | Required or discretionary | Time limit | What happens if the step does not occur |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Policy decision | Administrative ministry and Cabinet | Required by the Rules of Business for government Bills | None | The proposal remains a file; no Bill exists |
| Pre-legislative consultation | Administrative ministry under the 2014 policy | Discretionary executive policy | Thirty days ordinarily contemplated by the policy | Nothing in law; the Bill may still be introduced |
| Drafting and vetting | Legislative Department on ministry instructions | Required in practice for government Bills | None | The Bill cannot be introduced in settled form |
| Cabinet approval of the text | Cabinet | Required by the Rules of Business | None | The Bill is not the government's Bill and is not introduced by a minister |
| Introduction and first reading | Member in charge, with the House's leave | Constitutionally required as the start of passage | None | The Bill is not before the House |
| Committee reference | House on motion, or presiding officer to a standing committee | Discretionary | None fixed by the Constitution | The Bill proceeds to consideration without committee scrutiny |
| Second reading | The House | Constitutionally required in substance, as agreement to the text | None | The Bill is not passed by the House |
| Third reading and passage | The House | Required | None | The Bill is not passed by the House |
| Consideration by the other House | The second House | Required by Article 107 | None for ordinary Bills; fourteen days for a Money Bill under Article 109 | The Bill remains pending; after six months a joint sitting becomes possible |
| Joint sitting | President summons; both Houses vote together | Discretionary with the President | None | The disagreement persists and the Bill remains pending or lapses |
| Assent | President, or Governor in a State | Required by Articles 111 and 200 | None | The Bill does not become law |
| Publication in the Gazette | Government after assent | Required in practice as the Act's promulgation | None | The Act is not promulgated in the ordinary manner |
| Commencement | The government, by notification where the Act so provides | Required before the Act operates | None, unless the Act itself fixes a date | The Act remains enacted but uncommenced |

## Dissolution and the lapsing rules

The end of a House is the one event that kills Bills by operation of the Constitution, and Article 107 states the rules with a precision that public discussion habitually blurs. Prorogation kills nothing: a Bill pending before either House survives the end of a session, because Article 107 provides that a Bill pending in Parliament shall not lapse by reason of the prorogation of the Houses. Dissolution of the House of the People is different, and its effects turn on where the Bill stands when the House is dissolved. A Bill pending in the House of the People lapses, whether it originated there or was transmitted from the Council of States and awaits consideration. A Bill passed by the House of the People and pending in the Council of States also lapses, because the House that passed it has ceased to exist and the agreement the Constitution requires can no longer be completed on that text. Two classes survive. A Bill pending in the Council of States which has not been passed by the House of the People does not lapse, because the Council is a continuing body and the Bill awaits a House of the People that the election will supply. And a Bill passed by both Houses and pending assent does not lapse, because the legislative work is complete and only the assent stage remains. Article 107 adds a fourth survival: a Bill in respect of which the President has notified the intention to summon a joint sitting does not lapse on dissolution, so that the new House takes its place in the joint sitting the notification promised.

The rules repay statement in this form because the common error, that every pending Bill lapses on dissolution, inverts the design for the two surviving classes that matter most in practice. Government Bills pending in the Council of States after introduction there, a frequent position for Bills the government chose to start in the second House, survive a general election intact and can be taken up by the new House of the People without reintroduction. Bills awaiting assent survive as well, which means the assent stage is insulated from the electoral cycle: a President or Governor holding a Bill when the House dissolves holds a Bill that remains alive, and the absence of a time limit at that stage operates across the dissolution and into the term of the successor House. In the States, Article 196 states the corresponding rules for State Legislatures, with the Legislative Council, where one exists, in the position of the continuing chamber, and the same distinctions between prorogation and dissolution apply.

## Assent at the Union: Article 111

When a Bill has been passed by the Houses of Parliament, it is presented to the President, and Article 111 gives the President three declarations. The President may declare assent to the Bill, in which case it becomes an Act. The President may declare that assent is withheld, in which case the Bill fails. Or, where the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President may return it to the Houses with a message requesting reconsideration of the Bill or of specified provisions, and recommending amendments. The return is a suspensive veto, not an absolute one: if the Bill is passed again by the Houses, with or without amendment, and presented to the President for assent, the President shall not withhold assent therefrom. The text therefore contemplates a single return, a reconsideration, and an assent that the second presentation obliges. For a Money Bill there is no return; the choice is assent or withholding.

Three features of Article 111 have shaped its working. The first is the absence of any period. The article does not say within what time the President must declare one of the three courses, and the Supreme Court held in Purushothaman Nambudiri v State of Kerala, a decision of 1962 on the corresponding State provision, that the Constitution prescribes no time limit for assent and that a Bill pending assent does not lapse on the dissolution of the legislature that passed it. The second is the level at which the discretion is exercised. The President acts, in the ordinary course, on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, and the return of a Bill for reconsideration is in practice a decision of the government of the day about a Bill of its own or of its predecessor, rather than a personal act of censure by the head of State. The third is the practice that has grown in the space the silence leaves. Bills have remained pending without a declaration for long periods, neither assented to, withheld nor returned, a course the text does not name and does not forbid in terms, since withholding is a declaration and inaction is not. It was this space, at the State level where it had been used most visibly, that the litigation of 2025 was fought over.

The Union practice also includes one refinement the map should record. Because the President's return carries a message and recommended amendments, the reconsideration it triggers is a structured one: the Houses reconsider the Bill in the light of the message, and the Bill they pass again may incorporate the recommended amendments, reject them, or adopt others, and in every case the obligation on the second presentation is the same. The device resembles a negotiation between the executive head and the legislature conducted through the text of the Bill, and its rarity in practice reflects the fact that a government commanding the Houses rarely needs its own President to return its own Bill, while a Bill passed against the government's wishes by Houses it does not command presents the harder case in which withholding, rather than return, has been the course associated with the provision's early decades.

## Assent in the States: Articles 200 and 201

The State scheme mirrors the Union scheme with two additions that have made it the litigated one. Under Article 200, when a Bill has been passed by the Legislative Assembly of a State, or by both Houses where a Legislative Council exists, it is presented to the Governor, who declares assent, declares that assent is withheld, returns the Bill, if it is not a Money Bill, with a message for reconsideration, or reserves the Bill for the consideration of the President. The first proviso states the consequence of a return: if the Bill is passed again by the House or Houses, with or without amendment, and presented to the Governor for assent, the Governor shall not withhold assent therefrom. A second proviso obliges the Governor to reserve certain Bills, namely a Bill which, in the Governor's opinion, would so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position the Constitution designs that Court to fill. The text of Article 200 also carries, in its main clause, words that the Union article does not: the Governor is to make the declaration, in the case of the first option, and the structure of the article frames the courses as alternatives among which the functionary must choose.

Article 201 governs the reserved Bill. When a Bill is reserved by a Governor for the consideration of the President, the President declares either assent or that assent is withheld; where the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State legislature with a message for reconsideration, and the legislature must reconsider it within six months of receiving the message. If the Bill is passed again, with or without amendment, it is presented again to the President for consideration, and at that point the State scheme departs from the Union scheme in a way of the first importance: Article 201 contains no provision obliging the President to assent to the re-passed Bill. A reserved Bill can therefore be returned, re-passed by the State legislature, and still refused at the second presentation, a asymmetry between the Governor's position under Article 200, where re-passage binds, and the President's position under Article 201, where it does not. Reservation also connects the legislative process to the federal scheme of repugnancy, because a State law on a Concurrent List subject, reserved for the President's consideration and assented to, prevails in that State over an earlier Union law under [the doctrine of repugnancy under Article 254](/2018/09/01/article-254-repugnancy-explained/), so that the assent stage doubles as a federal clearing house for State legislation that would otherwise fail.

The Governor's discretion under Article 200 has been the subject of a line of authority on the aid and advice question. The Constitution Bench in Nabam Rebia v Deputy Speaker, decided in 2016 on the Governor's powers generally, treated the Governor's functions as exercisable on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers save where the Constitution requires discretion, and the question which of the Article 200 courses, if any, lies within the Governor's own discretion ran through the litigation of 2025. That litigation, although it concerned State Bills, was argued and decided as a question about the assent stage everywhere, because Articles 111, 200 and 201 share the same structure of named courses and unspoken time.


## The rules of the Houses: the procedure code the Constitution delegates

Article 118 leaves each House to make its own rules, and the consequence is that the working law of the legislative process is found in two rulebooks rather than in the Constitution. The rules govern notice of motions, the days and hours allotted to government and private members' business, the admissibility of amendments, the selection of amendments by the presiding officer, the closure, the guillotine on financial business, voting by voice and by division, and the authentication and transmission of a passed Bill. The Business Advisory Committee of each House translates the government's legislative programme into allotted time, and the presiding officer's power to select amendments and to allocate the debate determines, in practice, how much of the second reading's theoretical scrutiny a given Bill receives. None of this machinery is unconstitutional for its informality, because Article 118 authorises it, but the reader following a Bill needs to know that the sequence in this article describes the frame within which the rules operate, and that the rules can compress the stages severely: a Bill introduced, considered and passed on the same day has passed through every constitutional stage, and the courts will not inquire into the compression, for reasons the section on Article 122 gives.

The rules also govern the devices by which the House disciplines its own consideration. A member may raise a point of order on the procedure being followed, and the presiding officer's decision on it is final within the House. The member in charge may move the closure when debate has run its course, and the House may accept it. On financial business the rules permit the Speaker to fix time limits for the stages of a Bill and to put all outstanding clauses to the vote when the time expires, the device known as the guillotine, by which clauses never debated are passed all the same in the form the government proposed. Suspension of a rule is itself available on motion, so that even the rulebook's protections for deliberation can be displaced by the majority the deliberation is meant to check. These features do not make the stages fictitious; the second reading still settles the text, and amendments carried or negatived there are recorded in the proceedings. They do mean that the distance between the formal map and a particular Bill's actual journey can be large, and that the honest description of the process is the one that separates what the Constitution demands, which is little, from what the rules make possible, which is a great deal, and from what practice delivers, which varies with the government's majority and its appetite for scrutiny.

## Messages between the Houses and the settling of a single text

Bicameral agreement is achieved through messages, and the mechanics of the message stage explain many outcomes that accounts of the process leave mysterious. When the second House passes a Bill with amendments, the Bill returns to the first House with a message requesting its concurrence in the amendments. The first House considers the amendments and may agree to them, agree to some with further amendments of its own, or disagree. Further amendments proposed by the first House travel back to the second House in their turn, and the exchange continues until one House accepts the position of the other or the disagreement hardens into the kind that Article 108 addresses. A House may also insist on its amendments, the formal step by which a disagreement is declared final. The important procedural truth is that the Houses never meet to negotiate a text, except in the joint sitting; the text converges, if it converges, by each House voting on the other's amendments within its own chamber and under its own presiding officer.

This structure gives the second House its characteristic strength and weakness. Its strength is that agreement must be to its text or to a text it accepts, so a determined second House can force the first House to vote repeatedly on amendments the first House dislikes, and can make the political cost of rejecting them visible. Its weakness is that the exchange has no end imposed on it by anything except the first House's choices among the exits: accepting the amendments, letting the Bill rest, seeking a joint sitting after the Article 108 conditions are met, or allowing the Bill to lapse at dissolution. Where the two Houses are controlled by opposing political formations, the message stage can extend for the life of a Parliament, and Bills have remained in that exchange, passed by one House and amended by the other, through successive sessions without resolution. The process has no conference committee and no mediation; the only reconciling body the Constitution provides is the joint sitting, and the joint sitting decides by numbers rather than by compromise, which is why governments prefer the slower exits.

## The States' second chambers: Article 197 and the contrast it draws

The State scheme contains, in Article 197, the provision that throws the Union scheme's silence into relief. Where a State has a Legislative Council, a Bill passed by the Legislative Assembly and transmitted to the Council can be delayed by the Council only within fixed periods: if the Council rejects the Bill, fails to pass it for more than three months, or passes it with amendments the Assembly does not accept, the Assembly may pass the Bill again, and on the second transmission the Council's further delay is confined to one month, after which the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form in which the Assembly passed it the second time, with only such amendments as the Council made or recommended and the Assembly agreed to. The State second chamber is thus placed under deadlines by the constitutional text itself, and the deemed passage at the end of the second period is automatic, requiring no assent-stage intervention and no joint sitting. No corresponding provision exists at the Union level for the Council of States in relation to ordinary Bills: the Union second chamber's delay ripens, after six months, only into the possibility of a joint sitting that the President may or may not summon.

The contrast is instructive for the assent debate, because it shows the framers fixing periods where they intended periods to bind. Article 109 fixes fourteen days for a Money Bill in the Council of States. Article 197 fixes three months and one month for a Legislative Council. Article 108 uses six months as the point at which delay becomes a deadlock justifying a summons. Article 201 fixes six months for a State legislature to reconsider a reserved Bill returned to it. Where the Constitution intends a legislative stage to be time-bound, it says so in figures. The assent articles say nothing in figures, and that pattern of speech and silence across the legislative chapter is the textual foundation on which the Constitution Bench's answer to the Presidential Reference rests, just as the words requiring the Governor to act, and the imperative form of the first proviso to Article 200, are the foundation on which the case for enforceable timelines was built.

## The assent litigation of 2025: the April judgment

The question whether any timeline governs assent came before the Supreme Court in proceedings brought by the State of Tamil Nadu against its Governor. A number of Bills passed by the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly had been presented to the Governor and had remained without a declaration, and after the Assembly passed a group of them again, the Governor reserved them for the consideration of the President. The State invoked the Court's jurisdiction, and in State of Tamil Nadu v Governor of Tamil Nadu, decided on 8 April 2025 by a Division Bench of Pardiwala and Mahadevan JJ, the Court held the Governor's course illegal. The Bench reasoned that the three courses open under Article 200 are exhaustive alternatives among which the Governor must choose, that there is no fourth course of inaction, and that the constitutional expression requiring the declaration to be made without delay admits of no pocket veto by silence. It held that once a Bill had been returned, reconsidered, passed again and presented afresh, it was not open to the Governor to reserve that Bill for the President, because the first proviso's command that the Governor shall not withhold assent left no room for a reservation that would achieve indirectly what withholding could not achieve directly. Exercising the power under Article 142 to do complete justice, the Bench declared the ten Bills before it to be deemed to have received the Governor's assent on the date they were re-presented, and it laid down timelines for the future: periods within which a Governor should assent, withhold and return, or reserve a Bill, differentiated according to whether the course was taken on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers or against it, and a period of three months within which the President should decide Bills reserved for consideration under Article 201. The Bench added that a State aggrieved by inaction beyond those periods could seek a writ of mandamus.

The case for that judgment, stated at its strongest, begins from the legislative fact. A Bill that has passed both Houses of an elected legislature represents the completed exercise of the legislative power the Constitution assigns to that body, and a functionary who neither assents, nor withholds, nor returns, nor reserves does not exercise a veto the Constitution grants; the vetoes the Constitution grants are declared ones. On this view the pocket veto is not a discretion but an abdication, and it defeats responsible government at its root, because the ministry answerable to the Assembly for the legislative programme cannot answer for a Bill that has vanished into an office the ministry does not control. The words of the article support the reading: Article 200 frames the courses as declarations to be made, and the first proviso's peremptory language for re-passed Bills shows that the framers contemplated decision, not suspension. The remedy follows the wrong. Where inaction is the violation, a court must be able either to compel a decision within a stated period or, where the record shows the decision was in substance taken by the legislature twice over, to declare the legal consequence itself; the timelines are not invented numbers but the content given to the Constitution's own requirement of action without delay, and the mandamus the judgment contemplated is the ordinary instrument by which public law compels a duty-bearer to perform a duty.

## The Presidential Reference and the opinion of 20 November 2025

The Union government took a different view of the judgment, and the President, acting under Article 143, referred fourteen questions to the Supreme Court in May 2025, asking in substance whether the Governor's and the President's courses under Articles 200 and 201 are discretionary, whether their decisions and their inaction are open to judicial review, whether courts may prescribe timelines where the Constitution prescribes none, and whether Article 142 empowers the Court to declare a Bill deemed assented to. A Constitution Bench of five judges, presided over by Gavai CJ and including Surya Kant, Vikram Nath, P S Narasimha and A S Chandurkar JJ, heard the reference and delivered the Opinion of the Court on 20 November 2025, reported as In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India, 2025 INSC 1333. The Opinion holds that the Governor does exercise a discretion in choosing among the courses Article 200 offers and is not confined, in that choice, to the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. It holds that the merits of the Governor's or the President's decision on a Bill are not justiciable, and that a Bill does not become law without assent, so that no court can declare assent to have been deemed. It holds, centrally, that courts cannot impose strict or universal timelines under Articles 200 and 201: the articles are framed, the Opinion reasons, to allow elasticity, the constitutional language imposing a duty of timely action cannot be judicially converted into a fixed number of days, and the prescription of timelines and of deemed assent in the April judgment cannot stand as the general law. Two of the fourteen questions, those concerning the composition of benches under Article 145 and the reach of the Court's jurisdiction in federal disputes outside Article 131, were returned unanswered as falling outside the reference's functional scope.

The Opinion is not, however, a charter for indefinite delay, and its limits are part of its holding. The Bench accepted that prolonged, unexplained and indefinite inaction by a Governor is not immune from scrutiny: a court may issue a limited mandamus directing the Governor to take a decision within a reasonable time, without reviewing the merits of the decision taken and without fixing the content of the decision in advance. The immunity that Article 361 confers on the Governor personally does not place the constitutional office beyond that limited scrutiny. The President's position under Article 201 attracts the same reasoning: no timeline may be imposed, the decision itself is not justiciable, and the President is not obliged to seek the Court's opinion whenever a Bill is reserved. Article 142, the Opinion holds, cannot be used to substitute a judicial act for a constitutional function or to create a regime of deemed assent contrary to the constitutional scheme.

### What is the position on assent after the two decisions of 2025?

As the position stands on the authorities, no fixed timeline binds a Governor or the President on a Bill, and no Bill becomes law by deemed assent. Indefinite and unexplained inaction remains open to a limited judicial response: a court may direct that a decision be taken within a reasonable time, while leaving the choice among assent, withholding, return and reservation to the functionary the Constitution names.

The case against timelines, stated at its strongest and as the Opinion substantially accepts it, is a case about the location of constitutional choices. The framers fixed periods elsewhere in the legislative chapter, in figures, wherever they intended a stage to be time-bound, and their silence in the assent articles is a deliberate allocation of latitude to the assenting functionary, who may need to consult, to consider a Bill's interaction with Union law, or to weigh a reservation. A court that fixes one month or three months does not interpret that silence; it fills it with a rule of the court's own making, binding every future case regardless of the Bill's character, and the deemed assent that enforced the April timelines went further still, converting a discretionary constitutional act into an automatic consequence of a calendar and enacting, by judicial declaration, Bills whose text the assenting authority had never approved. Separation of powers is the name for the objection: the assent is assigned to the President and the Governor, and a judiciary that can supply both the deadline and the decision has taken the function over. The federal objection reinforces the institutional one, because reservation is the channel through which State legislation touching Union interests receives a Union-level examination, and timelines calibrated to State impatience would compress a scrutiny the federal scheme deliberately interposes.

The resulting position, stated without prediction, is therefore a divided one. The April judgment's declarations in the case before it, including the treatment of the ten Tamil Nadu Bills, were the exercise of jurisdiction in a decided case; the November Opinion, delivered by a larger bench in the Court's advisory jurisdiction, states the general law on timelines, discretion and review, and advisory opinions under Article 143, while not judgments in a lis, carry the authority the Court's pronouncements on the Constitution ordinarily command. A Governor or a President who leaves a Bill undecided for an extended period acts within a text that prescribes no period, and a State that complains of the delay has a remedy confined to compelling a decision, not to obtaining the assent it wants or a date by which assent must come. The question the litigation has settled is who may not supply the missing deadline. The question it has left where the Constitution left it is how long is too long, and that question will be answered, under the Opinion's framework, case by case, on the length and the explanation of each delay.


## The President's recommendation: the executive's gate at the entrance

Article 117 deserves separate treatment on the map because it places a condition at the entrance of the process rather than along its course. A Bill or amendment making provision for any of the matters that Article 110 enumerates, the taxing, borrowing and Consolidated Fund matters, shall not be introduced or moved except on the recommendation of the President, and such a Bill shall not be introduced in the Council of States. A Bill that does not fall within Article 110 but involves expenditure from the Consolidated Fund may be introduced without that recommendation, yet it shall not be passed by either House unless the President has recommended its consideration to that House. The two limbs operate at different stages: the first at introduction, the second at passage. Their purpose is the financial initiative of the executive, the principle that the authority answerable for the public finances must consent before the legislature commits them, and their effect is to give the government of the day a blocking power over financial legislation by private members and by the opposition, since the recommendation issues, in practice, on ministerial advice.

The recommendation requirement interacts with classification. A Bill that contains Article 110 matters alongside other matters is a Financial Bill of the first kind, which follows the ordinary road after a recommended introduction in the House of the People alone. A Bill containing only Article 110 matters is a Money Bill and follows the special procedure. A Bill involving expenditure alone is a Financial Bill of the second kind, which may originate in either House and needs the recommendation only for its consideration. Errors at this gate are not theoretical: a Bill introduced without a recommendation the Constitution requires is open to a point of order in the House, and the presiding officer's ruling at introduction determines whether the defect stops the Bill at the threshold. The courts, for the reasons given under Article 122, will not revisit the House's internal handling of the point after enactment, so the gate is policed where it stands, at the entrance, by the House itself.

## How a State Bill travels: one House or two

In a State with a single House, the Legislative Assembly performs both parts of the bicameral function in reduced form: it introduces, considers and passes the Bill, and the Bill goes directly to the Governor under Article 200. In a State with a Legislative Council, the Bill may originate in either House, subject to the Money Bill rules, and must be agreed by both, but the Council's power is the delaying power of Article 197 described in this article, so the Assembly can always complete the passage by passing the Bill a second time after the prescribed intervals. The State process therefore reproduces the Union process with the second chamber weakened by design and its delays fixed by the text. What the State process does not weaken is the assent stage, which at the State level is doubled: the Governor's courses under Article 200, and behind them the President's courses under Article 201 for reserved Bills, so that a State Bill can face two assenting authorities in sequence, each holding a discretion without a period, a structure with no counterpart for Union Bills and the reason the assent litigation arose in the States rather than at the Centre.

## Publication and commencement: the stages after assent

Assent makes an Act; it does not, without more, make the Act operative law that officials may enforce and citizens must obey. The assented Bill is published in the Gazette of India, or in the State Gazette for a State Act, and publication is the Act's promulgation, the point from which its existence as law is public and provable. The Act then commences. Where the Act fixes its own commencement date, that date governs. Where the Act provides that it shall come into force on a date the government appoints by notification, commencement waits on the notification, and different provisions of the same Act may be brought into force on different dates, a technique of staggered commencement that the series has encountered in statute after statute. Where the Act is silent, the General Clauses Act, 1897 supplies the default: the Act commences on the day on which the assent is first published in the Gazette. The distinction between enactment and commencement is the thread this article shares with the series as a whole. The legislative process examined here produces an enacted text. Whether that text governs anyone's conduct depends on a further executive act that, where the Act leaves the date to notification, carries no deadline either, so that the absence of enforceable timing which marks the process before enactment continues, in commenced-by-notification statutes, after enactment as well.

### When does assent turn a Bill into working law?

Assent completes the enactment, and publication in the Gazette promulgates the Act. The Act operates from its commencement, which is the date the Act itself fixes, or the date appointed by notification where the Act leaves commencement to the government, or, where the Act is silent, the day the assent is first published in the Gazette under the General Clauses Act, 1897.

Commencement practice rewards attention because it redistributes power after the legislative contest is over. A government that has carried a Bill through both Houses and assent may still choose the moment the Act begins to bite, and a government of a different complexion, inheriting an enacted but uncommenced statute from its predecessor, may choose never to appoint the day. Provisions have remained uncommenced for the whole life of a Parliament and beyond, and some Acts in the Indian statute book have never been commenced at all in whole or in part, without any provision being breached, because the commencement clause committed the date to the executive and fixed no outer limit. The courts have been reluctant to compel a commencement the Act leaves to notification, treating the choice of date as part of the legislative design the Houses enacted, which places commencement alongside assent as a stage where the text's silence is the law. For the citizen reading a newly enacted statute, the operative questions are therefore two and distinct: has the Act been assented to and published, and has the provision relied on been brought into force. The first is a question about the legislative process. The second is a question about a notification that may or may not exist.

## The courts and the process: Article 122

Article 122 closes the process against one kind of judicial inquiry while leaving another open, and the distinction between the two is where the courts' role in the legislative process is defined. The validity of proceedings in Parliament shall not be called in question on the ground of any alleged irregularity of procedure, and no officer or member in whom powers are vested for regulating procedure, for the conduct of business or for maintaining order is subject to the courts' jurisdiction in respect of the exercise of those powers. Article 212 states the corresponding bar for State Legislatures. The bar is on the ground: an irregularity, a departure from the rules the House made for itself, is the House's own affair, correctable by the House and by nobody else. What the bar does not protect is illegality, an act the Constitution forbids or a step the Constitution requires and that was not taken. The Supreme Court's decisions on the corresponding privileges and on legislative procedure, including Raja Ram Pal v Hon'ble Speaker, decided in 2007, maintain the line: a proceeding tainted by substantive illegality or by the violation of a constitutional mandate is open to judicial review on those grounds, while the internal regularity of the proceeding is not. It is on this distinction that the Money Bill litigation turns, because the challenge there is not that the House broke its own rules but that a Bill was carried through a procedure the Constitution reserves for a narrower class, a want of constitutional compliance rather than an irregularity, and that question belongs to the companion article that owns it.

For the ordinary process the consequence is that the stages described in this article are policed at three different levels, and the citizen who alleges that a stage went wrong must identify the right one. Compliance with the Constitution's own requirements, agreement by both Houses, the special procedures, assent itself, is examinable by the courts after enactment, because those requirements are the conditions of a valid law. Compliance with the rules of the Houses is examinable by the Houses alone, and the presiding officer's rulings within that field are final. Compliance with the executive's own pre-legislative practice, the consultation policy and the internal approvals, is examinable by nobody with power to undo the result, because those stages create no legal conditions at all. The map of the process is therefore also a map of remedies, and its lesson is that most of what can go wrong in the making of a law goes wrong at a level where no court can follow it.

## Where Bills die: a reading of the whole process

Set out in sequence, the process has the appearance of a corridor with doors that open only forwards, and the natural assumption is that a Bill introduced will in time emerge as an Act or be voted down. The record of any Parliament shows a different picture. Bills die at every stage and by silence more often than by vote. They die before introduction, when the Cabinet declines the proposal or the ministry loses its appetite for it, and no trace of the death is public. They die in committee, where a reference without a reporting deadline can hold a Bill for the remainder of a Parliament. They die between the Houses, in the message exchange that no rule forces to a conclusion. They die at dissolution, in the two classes Article 107 condemns. And they die, or sleep without dying, at the assent stage and the commencement stage, the two stages at which the Bill is complete and the system is waiting on a single decision that no period governs. Defeat on a vote, the death the civics account imagines, is the rarest of these endings for a government Bill, because a government that can count does not bring to a vote a Bill it cannot pass, and the stages of silence offer it cheaper ways to let a measure go.

The One Test of the process, for the reader who has followed the stages, is the ability to say of any Bill, at any moment, three things: where it stands, what the next step is and whose act that step is, and whether anything in law compels that act to happen within any period. For almost every Bill and almost every stage, the answer to the third question is that nothing does. The Constitution of India, which specifies the composition, powers and privileges of the legislatures in minute detail, specifies the timetable of law-making in one figure, the fourteen days of Article 109, and leaves the rest to politics. The litigation of 2025 asked whether the courts would write the missing figures into the assent articles. The answer the Constitution Bench gave preserves the design: the timetable of an Indian law is a political fact before it is a legal one, and the stages at which a Bill can be stopped are, with the exceptions this article has marked, stages at which it can be stopped for as long as the actor who holds it chooses.


## Sessions, prorogation and the legislative calendar

The stages operate inside a calendar the Constitution frames loosely. Article 85 requires that six months shall not intervene between the last sitting of Parliament in one session and the date appointed for its first sitting in the next, so Parliament must meet at least twice a year, and by practice it meets in three sessions. Within a session, the Houses sit on the days their presiding officers fix, and the government's legislative business competes for that time with financial business, questions, debates and private members' business. Prorogation, the President's act that terminates a session, ends the session's pending notices but, as Article 107 provides, does not touch pending Bills. Adjournment sine die by the presiding officer ends the sittings of a session without prorogation and likewise leaves Bills where they stand. The practical consequence is that a Bill's progress is measured in sessions rather than in days, and a Bill that misses its place in one session's business simply waits, alive, for the next. Governments announce their legislative programme for a session in advance, and the Bills that reach the statute book in a session are, in the ordinary case, the Bills the government chose to list and to keep listed when the session's time ran short.

The calendar also explains the end-of-session rush that observers of Parliament remark on. Because prorogation does not kill Bills, there is no constitutional necessity driving it; the rush is political, a product of the government's wish to report a session's enactments and of the opposition's corresponding wish to deny it the record. Bills passed in the closing days of a session have passed through every constitutional stage in compressed form, and the compression, for the reasons given in the discussion of Article 122, is not a ground on which the resulting Act can be impeached. The calendar's one hard edge is dissolution, and it falls only on the House of the People and only on the classes of Bill that Article 107 names, which is why a government approaching a general election attends to the location of its pending Bills: a Bill safe in the Council of States survives the election, while the same Bill, passed by the House of the People and awaiting the Council's consideration, dies with the House and must be introduced afresh in the new Parliament.

## Voting, division and the meaning of passage

Passage is a vote, and the Constitution fixes the majority for the ordinary case in Article 100: questions in either House are determined by a majority of votes of the members present and voting, the presiding officer voting only in the exercise of a casting vote where the votes are equal. The special majorities the Constitution requires for particular business, the amendment majority of Article 368, the effective-majority and two-thirds requirements for removals and impeachments elsewhere in the text, are exceptions enumerated where they apply, and an ordinary Bill is not among them. A Bill may therefore be passed by a House in which a minority of the total membership is present, subject to the quorum of one-tenth of the total number of members that Article 100 prescribes, and the vote may be taken by voice, the presiding officer judging the cry of the House, unless a division is demanded and allowed, in which case members record their votes and the numbers are published. Voice votes and divisions produce the same legal result, agreement by the House, and the distinction between them is one of record and of politics rather than of validity.

The vote that matters most in a Bill's life is rarely the final one. The consideration motion at the second reading decides whether the Bill proceeds at all; the votes on clauses and on amendments settle its content; the vote on the motion that the Bill be passed records a conclusion the earlier votes have reached. In the second House the pattern repeats, with the additional possibility, unavailable in the first, that the House's most consequential act is an omission, the decision not to take the Bill up. Students of the process who count only final votes miss the distribution of power the stages create: the power to amend in the second reading, the power to delay in the second House, the power to hold at assent and the power to withhold commencement are each held by different actors, and a Bill's journey is the sum of their separate decisions, most of which are never recorded as votes at all.

## Assent in practice at the Union: return, withholding and silence

The recorded practice under Article 111 illustrates each of the courses the text names, and one it does not. Return for reconsideration has been used where the President, acting on ministerial advice, sought to put the Houses in possession of objections the government wished aired: in 2006 the President returned the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Amendment Bill, the office of profit Bill, to the Houses with a message seeking reconsideration, the Houses passed the Bill again without change in substance, and assent followed, as the text obliges, on the second presentation. Withholding has been the rarer declared course, since a government that commands the Houses seldom allows a Bill it opposes to reach the stage of presentation. The unnamed course, silence, has the most instructive history. The Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, passed by both Houses in 1986, was presented to the President and was neither assented to, withheld nor returned during that President's tenure, passing to a successor under whom it was returned and eventually allowed to lapse from the legislative agenda. The episode is the standard illustration, in the literature on Article 111, of the pocket veto the text does not confer: because withholding is a declaration and the article fixes no period for making it, the absence of a declaration produces the effect of a veto with none of its constitutional incidents, no message, no reconsideration and no obligation on a second presentation. It was the same effect, produced by Governors in the States on a larger scale, that the Tamil Nadu litigation challenged and that the Constitution Bench's Opinion has left standing subject only to the limited mandamus against prolonged and unexplained inaction.

## Reservation in depth: the Governor's second door

Reservation deserves a fuller account than the map alone gives, because it is the course through which the assent stage performs federal work. A Governor reserves a Bill where the Governor considers that the Bill requires the President's examination, and the habit of reservation has settled around recognisable categories: Bills that trench on a Union law on a Concurrent List subject, where the President's assent under Article 254 gives the State law its protection against repugnancy; Bills affecting subjects on which the Constitution or the Union's interests require a central view, such as Bills derogating from the High Court's position, for which reservation is obligatory; and Bills on which the Governor, or the Union government whose attention the reservation invites, considers the State legislature's judgment should be tested at the Centre. Reservation converts a State question into a Union decision, and the President's courses under Article 201 then govern, including the direction to return the Bill for reconsideration with a six-month period for the legislature to complete that reconsideration. The six months of Article 201 bind the legislature, not the President, an allocation consistent with the pattern this article has traced throughout: where the text fixes a period in the assent region, it fixes it against the elected legislature, and against the assenting authority it fixes none.

The political significance of reservation has grown with its use in States governed by parties opposed to the party in power at the Centre, because a reserved Bill leaves the State's legislative process and enters the Union's, where the timetable is the Union executive's and the reasons for the eventual decision need not be given. The April judgment's holding that a re-passed Bill could not be reserved at all addressed the sharpest form of the grievance, the reservation that follows a return and a re-passage, and it reasoned from the first proviso's command. The November Opinion, in restoring the Governor's discretion among the courses of Article 200, leaves the re-passage question to be governed by that discretion read with the proviso, and States and Governors will work out the boundary in practice under the shadow of the limited mandamus. What neither decision does is attach a period to the President's consideration of a reserved Bill, so a State Bill can rest at the last stage of its journey, its legislature's work complete and twice confirmed, for a duration that no provision measures.

## The enrolled text and the evidence of passage

A process that runs on texts must be able to prove its texts, and the closing mechanics of passage deserve record. When both Houses have agreed to a Bill, the secretariat of the House in which it originated prepares the fair copy incorporating every amendment agreed to, the presiding officer of that House authenticates it, and the Bill is presented for assent in that authenticated form. After assent, the Act is printed and published in the Gazette, and the Gazette text is the authoritative evidence of the law. Where a question later arises about what the Houses passed, the enrolled and assented text answers it, and the debates, the committee reports and the consultation papers serve the different purpose of interpretation, available to a court construing an ambiguous provision but incapable of displacing the text they explain. The distinction disciplines researchers as well as courts: the stages of this article produce a hierarchy of documents, from the consultation draft, which binds nobody, through the Bill as introduced and as passed, to the Act as published, which alone is the law, and each document's authority is fixed by the stage that produced it.


## The three joint sittings in detail

Each of the three joint sittings illustrates a different trigger of Article 108. The Dowry Prohibition Bill reached a joint sitting in 1961 because the Houses disagreed on amendments: the Bill had passed through both Houses, but the form in which each was willing to accept it differed, and the disagreement on the text, rather than on the principle, was what the sitting resolved, passing the Bill with the amendments the combined House agreed to. The Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill reached a joint sitting in 1978 because the Council of States rejected a Bill the House of the People had passed, in the period after the Emergency, when the composition of the two Houses reflected different electoral moments and rejection was a real exercise of the Council's power rather than a formality. The Prevention of Terrorism Bill reached a joint sitting on 26 March 2002 because the Council of States rejected it days after the House of the People had passed it, and the government of the day chose the sitting over the waiting game, carrying the Bill in the combined House on the strength of the larger chamber's numbers. In each case the sitting did what Article 108 designs it to do, converting the first House's majority into the Parliament's decision, and in each case the rarity of the occasion made it a political event of the first order, debated as a confrontation rather than as a procedure.

The sittings also mark the boundaries of the device. No joint sitting has been summoned on the six-month trigger alone in a case that reached a vote, because a government content to wait six months is usually content to wait longer. No joint sitting has been used for a Bill whose difficulty lay in the government's own ranks in the first House, because the arithmetic that rescues a Bill in a joint sitting presupposes a first House majority to begin with. And no joint sitting has been attempted for a class of Bill the article excludes, so the exclusions for Money Bills and amendment Bills have never needed judicial exposition. The device stands in the text as a standing threat that disciplines the second House in negotiation: a Council of States that knows its rejection can be overridden by numbers will price its amendments accordingly, and much of Article 108's real work is done in the message exchanges that settle because the sitting exists behind them.

## Amendment Bills: the road with no exit

A Bill to amend the Constitution travels the ordinary road with three alterations that change its politics entirely. It may be introduced in either House without the President's recommendation, and a private member may introduce one, subject to the same stages as any Bill. It must then be passed by each House by the special majority of Article 368, a majority of the total membership of the House and not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, a threshold that requires the government to carry members beyond its own ranks in most Parliaments or to command an exceptional majority of its own. And where the amendment touches the federal matters the proviso enumerates, the federal structure, the judiciary, the Seventh Schedule, State representation in Parliament and allied subjects, it must also be ratified by the legislatures of not less than one-half of the States, by resolution, before the Bill is presented for assent. There the alterations end the resemblance: no joint sitting can rescue a deadlocked amendment Bill, a State legislature asked to ratify faces no period within which it must act, and the President, since the Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971, is obliged to give assent to an amendment Bill duly passed and, where required, ratified, the one point in the entire legislative process at which the assent itself is commanded rather than discretionary.

The amendment road thus inverts the ordinary Bill's distribution of power at both ends. At the passage end the second House is at its strongest, holding an absolute and unmediated veto. At the assent end the head of State is at the weakest, holding no discretion at all. The framers' calibration is visible in the contrast: where the measure changes the Constitution itself, concurrence must be real and the final act is a formality; where the measure is ordinary legislation, the second House's concurrence can be overridden by numbers and the final act retains a discretion whose limits two decisions of 2025 were needed to state.

## After the Act: delegated legislation and the process continued

Commencement is not the end of law-making under a statute, because most Acts delegate power to make rules, regulations and notifications, and the delegated legislation that follows is produced by an executive process that mirrors, in miniature, the stages of the Bill itself. The Act fixes the rule-making authority and the subjects the rules may govern; the ministry drafts the rules; and the rules, when made, are published in the Gazette and laid before each House for the period the Act or the general practice prescribes, during which either House may modify or annul them without prejudice to action already taken under them. The laying requirement is Parliament's retained hold over the delegated product, exercised through the Committees on Subordinate Legislation of the two Houses, which examine rules for excess of the delegated power, for retrospective effect the Act did not authorise and for delay in publication. The parallel matters to the map of the process for one reason: the citizen affected by a modern statute is governed, in most particulars, by the rules under it, and those rules travel a road with no readings, no second House agreement and no assent, acquiring force from the parent Act and from publication alone. The rigour of the Bill's journey is the justification offered for the lightness of the rules' journey, which is why the scrutiny stages of the Bill, and the committee stage in particular, carry a weight beyond the Bill's own text: they are the only examination most of the law under the Act will ever receive.

## Tracing a Bill: the record the process leaves

For the aspirant, the researcher and the journalist with whom this series is concerned, the process is knowable through its paper. The Bill as introduced, with its Statement of Objects and Reasons, its financial memorandum and its memorandum on delegated legislation, states the government's case for the measure in the government's own words. The committee report, where one exists, records the evidence and the dissent. The debates on the consideration motion and on the clauses record the arguments the Houses heard and the amendments they accepted or refused. The list of amendments agreed between the Houses, and the Gazette text of the Act with its commencement notifications, complete the record. Read in that order, the documents answer the questions litigation later asks: what the provision was aimed at, what alternatives were pressed and rejected, and when the enacted words began to govern. Read out of order, beginning with the Act and its commencement position, they answer the citizen's prior question, whether the provision relied on is law in operation at all. The stages of this article are, among other things, a finding aid for that record, and the discipline of the record, the preservation of each stage's document in a form that can be produced and proved, is the condition on which the whole reconstruction depends.


## The Governor and the advice question before 2025

The 2025 litigation did not begin the argument about the Governor's discretion; it sharpened an argument the Court had conducted for fifty years. In Shamsher Singh v State of Punjab, decided in 1974 by a Bench of seven judges, the Court held that the Governor, like the President, is a constitutional head who acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in the exercise of the executive and legislative functions the Constitution assigns, save in the exceptional fields where the Constitution expressly requires the Governor to act in discretion. The holding made ministerial advice the default and discretion the exception requiring a textual warrant. The assent articles supplied the battleground because their text names no adviser: Article 200 says the Governor shall declare one of the courses, and the question whether the declaration is the Governor's own act or the ministry's, channelled through the Governor, divides the authorities. Nabam Rebia, in 2016, restated the Shamsher Singh default for the Governor's discretionary sphere generally and confined discretion to the areas the Constitution marks out. The April 2025 judgment worked within that tradition, treating the choice among the Article 200 courses as structured by advice and by the proviso, and treating sustained inaction as outside any conception of the office. The November 2025 Opinion moved the boundary, holding that the choice among the courses is itself discretionary and not controlled by advice, while withholding from that discretion any power to defeat the process by indefinite silence. A reader tracing the argument across the half-century can see the question narrow as the benches enlarged: not whether the Governor is a cipher, and not whether the Governor is a rival executive, but which specific acts within Article 200 are the Governor's own, and what a court may do when the act is not performed at all.

## The opposition within the process

A complete map must mark the points at which those who oppose a Bill can act, because the process distributes obstruction as deliberately as it distributes power. At introduction, the opposition may contest leave on competence and force the government's majority to declare itself before the Bill exists as a text of the House. At the consideration stage it may move to circulate the Bill for eliciting opinion, or to refer it to a committee against the government's preference, motions that rarely carry against a majority but that place the government's refusal of scrutiny on record. At the clause stage it may move amendments in numbers that consume the time allotted, forcing the government to choose between accepting delay and using the closure or the guillotine, each of which carries a reputational price. In the second House, where the government's majority is least assured, it may amend, delay or reject, with the consequences Article 108 provides. Outside the Houses, it may litigate the finished Act on competence and Part III grounds, the one challenge the process cannot insulate, and it may make the assent and commencement stages politically expensive by publicising a Bill's residence at those stages. None of these instruments can stop a determined government with a stable majority in the House of the People, which is the design's intention for ordinary legislation. All of them can raise the cost, consume the calendar and build the record on which elections and litigation are later fought, which is the design's counterweight.

Measured against that distribution, the assent stage stands out as the one point of obstruction that is neither majoritarian nor accountable. The opposition's instruments work through votes, records and courts. The Governor's or the President's inaction works through silence, leaves no record to answer and, until the November Opinion's limited mandamus, admitted no remedy proportioned to it. That asymmetry, rather than any single episode, is what carried the question to a Constitution Bench, and it explains why the Opinion, while refusing timelines, was careful to preserve a judicial response to inaction that is prolonged and unexplained: a process in which every other actor's delay is visible and answerable could not easily leave one actor's delay invisible and unanswerable without conceding that the last stage of law-making had passed out of the constitutional scheme altogether.

## The pace of the stages in practice

No general statistics are needed to see the pattern the stages produce, because the variance is visible in individual journeys that the record documents. Bills of the first political importance have travelled from introduction to assent within days where the government commanded both Houses and the calendar, the second reading compressed, the committee stage waived and the second House concurring at once. Bills of comparable importance have spent years in the committee and message stages where the second House was hostile, emerging transformed or not emerging at all. State Bills have rested at the assent stage for periods longer than the Assembly that passed them took to consider them, and Acts duly assented to have waited for commencement notifications across changes of government. The same formal sequence accommodates all of these journeys without any of them presenting a procedural irregularity, because the sequence regulates order and not pace. The reader who asks how long a Bill takes is therefore asking a question the law does not answer: the answer is a function of the majority the government holds in each House, of the second House's disposition, of the assenting authority's view of the Bill and of the executive's readiness to commence what it has enacted. The Constitution supplies the road and the order of its stages. Politics supplies the speed, and the decisions of 2025 confirm that, at the stage where the question was pressed hardest, politics is where the Constitution intends the speed to be set, subject to a court's power to insist that a decision, of some content, eventually be taken.


## The anatomy of the text the Houses consider

The readings operate on a document of a settled anatomy, and the anatomy explains what the stages can and cannot change. A Bill opens with its long title, which states its objects in a sentence and fixes the scope within which amendments are relevant, and a preamble where the drafter has thought one necessary. The enacting formula follows, recording that the Bill is enacted by Parliament in the year of the Republic specified, a formula whose year changes with the session and which the third reading stage settles. The clauses follow in an order that legislative drafting has standardised: the short title and commencement clause first, then definitions, then the operative provisions creating rights, duties, authorities and offences, then the machinery of enforcement, appeals and penalties, then the rule-making power and the laying requirement, and finally the repeals, savings and removal-of-difficulty provisions with which the statute takes its place in the existing book. Schedules carry matter too detailed or too tabular for clauses, lists of repealed enactments, amendments to other Acts and forms. Each element has its moment in the second reading: the clause-by-clause stage takes the clauses in order, the schedules after the clauses they serve, and the long title and preamble last, because amendments to the statement of objects are intelligible only when the operative text they describe has been settled. A member's amendment, to be in order, must attach to that anatomy at a point, and the presiding officer's rulings on relevance are rulings about the relation between the amendment and the Bill's declared scope.

The Statement of Objects and Reasons that accompanies the Bill is not part of the text the Houses pass, and the distinction is one courts enforce when the statement is later pressed into interpretive service: it may explain the circumstances in which the Bill was introduced and the mischief it addresses, but it is not enacted, and it cannot control the meaning of words the Houses did enact. The notes on clauses and the memoranda serve the members during the readings and then join the record. The text that travels to the other House, and onward to assent, is the clauses and schedules alone in their agreed form, which is why the laborious clause-by-clause stage, and not the speeches, is where an Act is actually written.

## Withdrawal, revival and the inheritance of a new Parliament

A Bill's journey can also end by its author's hand. The member in charge may, with the leave of the House, withdraw a Bill at any stage before passage, and governments withdraw Bills that have lost their purpose, been overtaken by a replacement measure or attracted a resistance the government no longer wishes to pay for. Withdrawal in one House of a Bill that originated there ends it; a Bill transmitted from the other House and withdrawn from consideration simply ceases to be proceeded with. The converse operation is revival after dissolution. The classes of Bill that lapse under Article 107 cannot be revived as the same Bill: the measure must be introduced afresh in the new House, passing every stage again, although the drafting, the consultation and the committee work of the earlier Parliament may be reused in substance, and a committee of the earlier House that had reported may spare the new House some of its labour by the persuasion of its printed record. Bills that survive dissolution need no revival and are taken up from the stage they had reached, which is one reason governments late in a Parliament's term attend to the chamber in which their unfinished measures stand.

The inheritance question has a State analogue and a federal wrinkle worth noting. A State Bill reserved for the President and pending at the dissolution of the State Assembly that passed it does not lapse, because the assent stage is complete so far as the legislature is concerned, and the incoming Assembly inherits a predecessor's Bill at the President's desk rather than on its own order paper. The new Assembly cannot withdraw it by any proceeding of its own, and its remedy, if it dislikes the measure its predecessor twice passed, lies in the ordinary legislative power to repeal or amend the Act if assent comes, not in any recall of the Bill. The assent stage thus detaches a Bill from the legislature that made it more completely than any earlier stage does, and the detachment is a further reason the duration of that stage matters: a Bill held long enough at assent is decided, in effect, in the time of a different legislature from the one that passed it.

## The one deadline, restated

The argument of the artifact table can be stated in its final form. The Constitution of India prescribes the composition of the legislatures, the qualifications and disqualifications of their members, the majorities for their several kinds of business, the privileges that protect their proceedings and the procedures by which their Acts may be impeached or sustained. For the time within which law-making is to occur it prescribes, in figures, the fourteen days of Article 109 for the Council of States on a Money Bill, the three months and one month of Article 197 for a Legislative Council, the six months of Article 201 for a State legislature reconsidering a returned reserved Bill, and the six months of Article 108 after which, and only after which, a deadlock in the Council of States may be taken to a joint sitting. Against the assenting authorities, against the committees, against the drafting departments and against the governments that must commence what they have enacted, it prescribes no figures at all. The Supreme Court's two engagements with the question in 2025 divided on whether that silence could be filled by judicial timelines, and the later and larger authority holds that it cannot, leaving in place only the insistence that silence itself, prolonged and unexplained, may be brought to an end by a direction to decide. The process by which a Bill becomes law in India is therefore complete in its stages and open in its durations, and the reader who understands both halves of that sentence understands the process as the Constitution made it and as the litigation of 2025 has left it.


## What the process borrows and what it invents

The stages bear the marks of their origin in the procedure of the House of Commons, transmitted through the legislatures of British India and the Constituent Assembly's own rules, and the resemblances are real: the three readings, the committee of selection, the message between the Houses and the form of assent all descend from that practice. The departures are the parts that matter for understanding the Indian process on its own terms. The joint sitting has no Westminster counterpart, where disagreement between the Houses is resolved by the Parliament Acts' device of allowing the elected House, after delay, to present a Bill for assent without the other House's agreement; the Indian device decides the disagreement by voting the two Houses together, which preserves a role for the second House's members in the very act that overrides their chamber. Assent in India is a constitutional act of a head of State bound into a written text, where the corresponding act in the United Kingdom has, by convention settled over centuries, ceased to be a discretion at all, while the Indian text retains the words of choice and has required litigation to fix their meaning. The Governor's reservation, and behind it the President's consideration of State Bills, are federal devices without a Westminster analogue, answering to a distribution of legislative power between two levels of government that the unitary model never faced. And the written Constitution itself changes the character of every borrowed stage, because each stage's requirements, where the text states them, become conditions of legal validity examinable in court, so that a procedure inherited as parliamentary practice operates in India as constitutional law, with the courts stationed at the boundary Article 122 draws between the irregularity they must ignore and the illegality they must correct.

## When the enacted text is wrong: correction and repair

The process must also account for its own errors, because a document produced by amendment and vote across two Houses can carry mistakes into the statute book. Errors divide by the stage at which they are found. A printing or transcription error in the Gazette text, where the authenticated Bill and the published Act diverge, is corrected by corrigendum, because the assented text is the law and the publication is its evidence. An error inside the assented text itself, a wrong cross-reference, a clause that contradicts the scheme the Houses plainly enacted, stands as law until Parliament repairs it, ordinarily by a short amending Act that travels the full road of readings and assent like any other Bill, there being no procedure by which the executive or the courts may rewrite an enacted clause to match an intention the text does not express. Courts construing such a text may correct an obvious drafting slip where the intended meaning is clear and the error is plain on the face of the provision, a narrow doctrine exercised with reluctance, but the systematic repair of the statute book belongs to the legislature, and the annual crop of amending and repealing Bills, including the periodic repealing and amending Acts that clear spent and obsolete statutes from the book in bulk, is the process maintaining its own product. The reader of an Act should therefore treat its text as the Houses left it, errors included, and look for the repair, where one exists, in a later Act rather than in any corrected reprint.


## One ordinary Bill from end to end

The stages assemble into a single journey when an ordinary government Bill is followed through them, and a composite journey, drawn from the standard course the rules produce, shows where the time goes. A ministry settles its legislative proposals after inter-ministerial consultation and places the draft, settled with the Legislative Department, before the Cabinet. The Cabinet approves the introduction of the Bill; the draft has been published for comment under the consultation policy, or the ministry records its reasons where the policy's course is not followed, and the comments received are summarised for the Cabinet with the proposal. Notice of introduction is given in the House of the People; the Bill is published in the Gazette; leave is granted without a division, and the first reading is complete on the day the Bill is introduced. The member in charge moves reference to the Departmentally Related Standing Committee, or the presiding officer refers the Bill, and the committee takes the ministry's evidence, hears affected interests, considers the Bill clause by clause and adopts its report with a dissent note appended by members who disagree. The report is presented in a later session than the one in which the Bill was introduced, the committee having taken the extension the House allowed.

The consideration motion is then moved in the House of the People, the general debate ranges over the Bill's principle across the time the Business Advisory Committee allotted, and the House agrees to take the Bill into consideration. Clause by clause, the House accepts two amendments the committee recommended and the government accepted, rejects the opposition's amendments on divisions, and agrees to the schedules and the long title. The motion that the Bill be passed is carried at the third reading. Transmitted to the Council of States, the Bill is considered after its own committee stage is waived by the House's decision, amended in one clause the government of the day cannot accept, and returned with a message. The House of the People disagrees to the amendment; the Council, on the Bill's return to it, does not insist, weighing the joint sitting that insistence could invite, and the Bill is deemed passed by both Houses in the form the House of the People settled. The authenticated Bill is presented to the President; assent is declared; the Act is published in the Gazette with a commencement clause appointing a day to be notified, and the operative provisions are brought into force by notification when the rules under the Act have been framed and laid. At no point in that journey was any stage performed out of its order, and at no point did any provision fix the time the journey took: the committee's extension, the interval between the sessions, the Council's deliberation and the interval before the commencement notification were each, in law, as long as the actor concerned chose to make them. The journey is unremarkable in every particular, and it is the unremarkable case, rather than the litigated one, that shows how completely the timetable of Indian law-making belongs to practice and politics rather than to the constitutional text.


## The reverse journey: a Bill that begins in the Council of States

An ordinary Bill may begin in either House, and a Bill that begins in the Council of States reverses the journey without altering its law. Introduced there, referred there, considered and passed there, it travels to the House of the People, which may pass it, amend it and return it, reject it or leave it pending, and the consequences of each course mirror those of the forward journey with the chambers exchanged. Two consequences of the reversal deserve note. The first concerns dissolution: a Bill passed by the Council and pending in the House of the People lapses when that House is dissolved, while a Bill still in the Council, unpassed by the House of the People, survives, so the government's choice of the originating House is also a choice about the Bill's exposure to the electoral calendar, and ministries that expect a dissolution have reason to hold their unfinished measures in the continuing chamber. The second concerns the joint sitting's arithmetic, which does not change with the direction of travel: whichever House originated the Bill, a joint sitting decides by the combined numbers, and the originating Council's Bill is as exposed to the larger House's majority at a sitting as a transmitted one. Governments accordingly use the Council as the originating chamber for measures they expect to be uncontroversial or to benefit from the Council's committee scrutiny first, and for measures introduced late in a Parliament's life, where survival matters more than speed. The private member's Bills of members of the Council likewise begin there, and the stock of such Bills pending in the Council at any dissolution is the visible residue of the reverse journey's protection.

The symmetry between the journeys is itself a feature of the design worth stating. Bicameralism in the Indian scheme is not a sequence in which the second House merely reviews the first House's work; it is a duplication of the whole process in two chambers of different composition and different electoral rhythms, with agreement between them, rather than the priority of either, as the condition of a law. The exceptions prove the rule by naming themselves: the Money Bill, where the Council reviews without power to insist; the amendment Bill, where each House's concurrence is separately and specially required; and the joint sitting, where disagreement is resolved by dissolving the boundary between the chambers for a single vote. Outside those exceptions, a Bill becomes law by being enacted twice, once in each House, in identical words, and the identity of the words, secured by the message exchange and the authenticated text, is the substance of what bicameral agreement means.


## Competence: the question that shadows every stage

Behind the stages stands a condition none of them can cure. Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India on the matters in the Union List, and on Concurrent List matters subject to the repugnancy rule, and a State legislature may make laws on State and Concurrent matters for its State; a Bill outside the enacting legislature's competence is not saved by being passed in perfect form, and the resulting Act is void to the extent of the incompetence however regular its journey. The stages interact with competence at two points only. At introduction, the objection may be raised in the House, where the presiding officer permits it to be aired while leaving the question itself to the courts, so that a Bill of doubtful competence proceeds through the readings with the doubt recorded rather than resolved. After enactment, the question belongs to the courts, and it is decided on the Act's pith and substance, the true character of the legislation, rather than on the entries the legislature recited. Between those two points, the process neither tests nor certifies competence, and a Bill may complete every stage, receive assent and commence, carrying a defect that only litigation will expose. The map of the process is therefore a map of form: it shows how a law is made, and the question whether the law was the maker's to make at all is answered elsewhere, by the Seventh Schedule and by the courts that police it.


## The process as the series leaves it

This article closes the cluster in which the series turns from individual statutes to the machinery that produces them, and its finding is the mirror image of the finding with which the series began. The statute-by-statute examination found, again and again, that enactment was not the operative event: commencement notifications were delayed, provisions were left unborn, and repeal schedules outlived the Acts they repealed. The process examination finds the same quality at the near end. Before a Bill is enacted, its progress depends at every stage on actors whom no period binds: the ministry that drafts when it chooses, the committee that reports when it chooses, the second House that decides when it chooses, and the assenting authority whose duty to decide the Constitution Bench has affirmed while refusing to number its days. The single discipline the system knows is order: the stages must come in their sequence, the texts must agree, the assent must follow the agreement and the publication must follow the assent. Within that order, time is unregulated. A reader who has absorbed the point will read news of a Bill's introduction, its passage and its assent with the questions this article supplies permanently attached: which stage has it reached, whose act comes next, and does anything in law say when that act must occur. For the great majority of Bills at the great majority of stages, the honest answer to the last question is that nothing does, and that answer, rather than any defect of drafting or of doctrine, is the deepest fact about how a Bill becomes law in India.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What stages does a Bill go through?

A government Bill begins with a policy decision in the administrative ministry, drafting by the Legislative Department on the ministry's instructions, and approval of the text by the Cabinet. It is then introduced in either House of Parliament, which constitutes the first reading. The House may refer it to a committee, a discretionary step, before the second reading, which comprises general discussion on the Bill's principle and clause by clause consideration of its text. The third reading is the final vote on the settled text. The Bill then travels to the other House for the same stages. Once both Houses agree to an identical text, the Bill is presented for assent, published in the Gazette after assent, and brought into force on its commencement date.

### Q: What types of Bill are there?

Bills divide by who introduces them and by their subject. A government Bill is introduced by a minister; a private member's Bill is introduced by a member who is not a minister. By subject, an ordinary Bill is the standard case, travelling the full bicameral road with a joint sitting available on deadlock. A Money Bill, defined by Article 110, can originate only in the House of the People and follows the special procedure under which the Council of States may only recommend amendments within fourteen days. A Financial Bill shares some features of a Money Bill without the special procedure. A Constitution amendment Bill must be passed by each House separately by the special majority of Article 368, with State ratification for specified subjects.

### Q: Can the Rajya Sabha block a Bill?

For an ordinary Bill, the Council of States can delay, amend and reject, but it cannot impose a permanent block against a determined House of the People: rejection, or the passage of six months without a decision, or final disagreement on amendments entitles the President to summon a joint sitting, where the combined numbers favour the larger House. For a Money Bill the Council can do no more than recommend amendments within fourteen days, which the House of the People may refuse. For a Constitution amendment Bill, by contrast, the Council holds an absolute veto, because Article 368 requires each House to pass the Bill separately by special majority and provides no joint sitting. Its blocking power therefore depends entirely on the class of Bill.

### Q: When is a joint sitting held?

The President may summon a joint sitting under Article 108 when a Bill passed by one House has been rejected by the other House, when the other House has not passed it within more than six months from the date of its reception, or when the Houses have finally disagreed on amendments. The sitting is presided over by the Speaker of the House of the People, and the Bill is decided by a majority of the members of both Houses present and voting. A joint sitting cannot be held for a Money Bill or for a Constitution amendment Bill. The device has been used three times: for the Dowry Prohibition Bill in 1961, the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill in 1978 and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill in 2002.

### Q: Which options does the President have on a Bill?

Under Article 111, when a Bill passed by both Houses is presented, the President may declare assent, in which case the Bill becomes an Act; may declare that assent is withheld, in which case the Bill fails; or, if the Bill is not a Money Bill, may return it to the Houses with a message requesting reconsideration and recommending amendments. The return is suspensive rather than final: if the Houses pass the Bill again, with or without amendment, and present it a second time, the President shall not withhold assent. A Money Bill cannot be returned. The article prescribes no period within which the President must choose among these courses, and inaction is a course the text neither names nor in terms forbids.

### Q: What happens to a Bill when the House is dissolved?

Prorogation of the Houses lapses no Bill. Dissolution of the House of the People lapses a Bill pending in that House and a Bill passed by that House and pending in the Council of States. Two classes survive dissolution: a Bill pending in the Council of States which has not been passed by the House of the People, because the Council is a continuing body and the Bill awaits the new House; and a Bill passed by both Houses and pending assent, because the legislative work is complete. A Bill for which the President has notified the intention to summon a joint sitting also survives. These rules, stated in Article 107, mean that where a Bill stands at the moment of dissolution decides whether it lives or dies.

### Q: What must a Governor do with a state Bill?

Under Article 200, a Bill passed by the State legislature and presented to the Governor must receive one of four declarations: assent; assent withheld; return of the Bill, if it is not a Money Bill, with a message requesting reconsideration; or reservation of the Bill for the consideration of the President. If a returned Bill is passed again by the legislature, with or without amendment, and presented afresh, the Governor shall not withhold assent. Reservation is obligatory for a Bill that would derogate from the powers of the High Court so as to endanger its constitutional position. A reserved Bill is governed by Article 201, under which the President may assent, withhold assent, or direct its return to the State legislature for reconsideration.

### Q: Is there a time limit for assent?

The Constitution prescribes no period within which the President or a Governor must decide a Bill. A Division Bench of the Supreme Court, in the Tamil Nadu Governor case decided on 8 April 2025, laid down timelines for Governors and for the President and treated Bills held beyond them as deemed assented to. A Constitution Bench of five judges, answering a Presidential Reference by its Opinion of 20 November 2025, held that courts cannot impose strict or universal timelines under Articles 200 and 201 and that deemed assent is impermissible, because the articles allow elasticity and a fixed number of days cannot be extracted from the duty of timely action. Prolonged and unexplained inaction may still attract a limited direction to decide within a reasonable time.

### Q: Where does a government Bill begin before it is introduced in Parliament?

It begins inside the executive. The administrative ministry formulates legislative proposals, consults the ministries affected and the Ministry of Law and Justice, and obtains the Cabinet's approval in principle. The Legislative Department of the Ministry of Law and Justice then drafts the Bill on the ministry's instructions, settling clauses, definitions, penalties, rule-making powers and repeal provisions, and vets the draft for legislative competence and consistency with the Constitution. The settled draft returns to the Cabinet for approval of the final text, together with the Statement of Objects and Reasons, the notes on clauses and, where expenditure is involved, a financial memorandum. Only after that approval does a minister give notice of introduction in the House.

### Q: What is a private member's Bill, and how does its course differ from a government Bill?

A private member's Bill is introduced by a member of either House who is not a minister, on a subject of the member's choosing, after the notice the rules of the House prescribe. The member drafts the Bill without the Legislative Department's machinery and without Cabinet approval, although the House secretariat assists on form. From introduction onward the stages are identical to those of a government Bill: readings in both Houses, agreement on an identical text, and assent, with the President's recommendation required where the Bill involves the financial matters covered by Articles 110 and 117. The practical difference is time and support: private members' business occupies a limited portion of the Houses' sittings, and a Bill the government does not support rarely advances beyond introduction.

### Q: What happens at the second reading of a Bill?

The second reading has two phases. In the first, the House debates the Bill's principle and objects, on the motion that the Bill be taken into consideration, or that the Bill as reported by a committee be taken into consideration. In the second phase, the House considers the Bill clause by clause: each clause is put in turn, amendments are moved, debated and disposed of, and each clause is voted to stand part of the Bill in its original or amended form. The schedules, preamble and long title are taken in their order, and the enacting formula is agreed at the end. The second reading is where the text of the future Act is settled, and most substantive change to a Bill occurs at this phase or in the committee stage that precedes it.

### Q: What happens at the third reading of a Bill?

At the third reading the member in charge moves that the Bill be passed. Debate is confined to the Bill as a whole in the form settled at the second reading: members may argue for or against the Bill, but detailed criticism of clauses is out of order and no amendment may be moved except amendments of a verbal or consequential character that correct errors disclosed during consideration. The House then votes on the motion, and if it is carried the Bill stands passed by that House and is transmitted to the other House. Despite its name, the third reading is not a third substantive debate; the first reading is a formality of introduction, the second reading settles principle and text, and the third reading records the House's final decision on the settled text.

### Q: Why is there no joint sitting for a Constitution amendment Bill?

Article 368 is a self-contained code for constitutional amendments. It requires an amendment Bill to be passed in each House separately, by a majority of the total membership of that House and by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, and it provides no mechanism for resolving a disagreement between the Houses. The omission is deliberate in design: because an amendment alters the Constitution itself, the framers required the genuine concurrence of both Houses rather than permitting the larger House to override the smaller by combined numbers, which is the function a joint sitting performs for ordinary Bills. Disagreement or failure to reach the special majority in either House is therefore fatal to an amendment Bill, which must be introduced afresh if it is to be pursued.

### Q: Can the President return a Bill a second time?

No. Article 111 gives the President one return. When a Bill that is not a Money Bill is returned with a message and the Houses pass it again, with or without amendment, and present it to the President for assent, the President shall not withhold assent therefrom. The second presentation therefore obliges assent, and a second return is not among the courses the article offers. The same structure appears in Article 200 for a Governor, whose re-passed State Bill likewise cannot be withheld. Article 201 is different for reserved State Bills: where the President directs the return of a reserved Bill and the State legislature passes it again, the article contains no corresponding obligation, and the President's courses on the second presentation remain assent or withholding.

### Q: What recommendation does a Bill involving expenditure need before Parliament can pass it?

Article 117 draws two lines. A Bill or amendment making provision for the matters listed in Article 110, taxation, borrowing and the Consolidated and Contingency Funds, may not be introduced or moved except on the recommendation of the President, and may not be introduced in the Council of States at all. A Bill that falls outside those matters but would, if enacted, involve expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India may be introduced without the recommendation, yet neither House may pass it unless the President has recommended to that House the consideration of the Bill. The recommendation is obtained on ministerial advice, and in practice it gives the government of the day a gatekeeping power over financial legislation proposed by private members and by the opposition.

### Q: When does an Act begin to operate after the President assents to the Bill?

Assent makes the Bill an Act, and publication of the assented Act in the Gazette of India promulgates it, but the Act operates from its commencement. If the Act fixes its own date, that date governs. If the Act provides that it shall come into force on a date appointed by the government by notification, the Act operates from the notified date, and different provisions may be commenced on different dates. If the Act is silent, the General Clauses Act, 1897 supplies the default rule that the Act commences on the day the assent is first published in the Gazette. Because many Acts leave commencement to notification and fix no deadline for it, an enacted Act may remain inoperative in whole or in part for an extended period.

### Q: What does publication in the Gazette do, and what does it not do?

Publication in the Gazette of India, or in the State Gazette for a State Act, is the promulgation of the assented Act: it makes the authoritative text public and provable, and the Gazette text is the evidence of the law that courts and officials use. Where an Act is silent on commencement, publication also starts the Act, because the General Clauses Act, 1897 ties the default commencement to the day the assent is first published. Publication does not, however, cure a defect in the Act's passage or enact an Act that was never assented to, and where an Act appoints commencement by notification, publication alone leaves the Act enacted but not yet in operation. The Gazette records the stages of assent and commencement; it performs neither of them by itself.

### Q: What is the pre-legislative consultation policy, and can a court enforce it?

The Pre-legislative Consultation Policy was adopted by the Government of India in February 2014, through the Ministry of Law and Justice. It directs departments proposing legislation to publish the draft legislation, or a summary of its salient features with an explanatory note, ordinarily for a period of thirty days, to receive public comments, to consider the comments received and to place a summary of the feedback before the Cabinet with the proposal. It is an executive policy, not a statute and not a rule under the Constitution. It creates no right in any citizen, and no court will restrain the introduction of a Bill, or invalidate an Act, because the consultation was not held or was held for a shorter period. Assessments of its working describe compliance as uneven across ministries and Bills.

### Q: Can a court set aside a law because Parliament followed the wrong procedure in passing it?

It depends on whose procedure was breached. Article 122 provides that the validity of proceedings in Parliament shall not be called in question on the ground of an alleged irregularity of procedure, and Article 212 states the same protection for State legislatures. A departure from the rules a House made for itself is therefore the House's own affair and is not a ground for invalidating the Act. What remains open to the courts is illegality: a failure to comply with a requirement the Constitution itself imposes, such as agreement by both Houses, the special procedure for a class of Bill, or assent, and a want of legislative competence. The distinction between irregularity, which is protected, and illegality or unconstitutionality, which is reviewable, runs through the case law on legislative proceedings, including Raja Ram Pal v Hon'ble Speaker, decided in 2007.

### Q: What is the Statement of Objects and Reasons, and is it part of the Act?

Every Bill introduced in Parliament is accompanied by a Statement of Objects and Reasons, a document prepared by the sponsoring ministry that explains the circumstances that made the legislation necessary, the defects in the existing law it addresses and the objects the Bill seeks to achieve. The statement is published with the Bill and is the government's own account of its measure. It is not, however, part of the text the Houses pass: the clauses and schedules alone are enacted, the statement is not voted on, and it acquires no legal force from assent. Courts may consult the statement when construing an ambiguous provision, as evidence of the setting in which the Act was made and the mischief it addresses, but the statement cannot control or contradict the meaning of the enacted words.
