The Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 is the statute under which India prosecutes bribery in public office, and it contains a paradox that most summaries flatten into a single direction. In July 2018 Parliament amended the Act in ways that pulled it both ways at once. For the first time, giving a bribe became a substantive offence in its own right, carrying up to seven years of imprisonment, and companies became liable when persons associated with them bribed a public servant to win or keep business. In the same amending Act, the definition of criminal misconduct was cut down to two categories, a limb under which a public servant could be prosecuted for abusing his position to secure a benefit for another person was deleted, and a new provision, Section 17A, barred the police from even beginning an enquiry or investigation into an offence connected with an official decision until the government employing the official had given its approval. The statute after 2018 therefore reaches more people than it reached before, and is harder to set in motion against the one category of person it was written for.

![The Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 and the two directions of its 2018 amendment, giver criminalised and investigation gated - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-89.webp)

That double movement is the fact to hold on to, because nearly every public account of the 2018 amendment reports only half of it. Accounts written from the enforcement side describe a tightening: supply-side bribery criminalised, corporate liability introduced, punishments severe, trials put on a statutory clock. Accounts written from the administration side describe a necessary loosening: honest officials protected from prosecution over decisions that later look wrong, a filter against frivolous complaints, an end to the paralysis that senior civil servants had protested against for decades. Both accounts are accurate about the provisions they cite and wrong about the amendment as a whole. Only a provision-by-provision reading shows the shape of the trade Parliament actually made, and that reading is what this article performs. It states what the Act criminalises in its present form, reconstructs what the law was before 2018 and why it changed, examines the two gates the statute places in front of an investigation and a prosecution, compares Section 17A with the earlier prior-approval provision that the Supreme Court struck down in 2014, and closes with an assessment of which failure mode the amended Act handles well and which it handles badly: the corrupt decision or the harassed official. Where the argument turns on a contested question, both sides are given in their strongest form and attributed to the people and institutions that actually make them.

## What existed before 1988, and why it was judged inadequate

The Act of 1988 did not invent the Indian law of official corruption; it consolidated a scheme that had been built in layers over four decades and found wanting at each layer. The first layer was the Indian Penal Code of 1860, which treated bribery as a set of offences by the public servant who took: Sections 161 to 165A of the Code punished a public servant who accepted gratification other than legal remuneration as a motive or reward for an official act, whoever abetted that acceptance, and the taking of a valuable thing without adequate consideration from a person concerned in the servant's official business. The second layer was the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1947, passed in the last months of British rule as wartime procurement corruption exposed the limits of the Code. The 1947 Act created the offence of criminal misconduct, including the possession of assets disproportionate to known sources of income, introduced a presumption that assisted the prosecution once acceptance of gratification was proved, and required previous sanction before a court could take cognizance against a public servant. Its criminal misconduct clause, Section 5 of that Act, went wider than bribery: it reached habitual corrupt conduct, the acceptance of gifts from persons having business relations with the official, and, in a limb whose descendants matter later in this article, the obtaining of a valuable thing or pecuniary advantage by corrupt or illegal means or by otherwise abusing the official position. Its presumption clause went further still, providing that where a public servant was found in possession of resources disproportionate to his known sources of income, for which he could not satisfactorily account, the court could presume criminal misconduct until the contrary was proved. Almost every structural feature of the present statute, the special judge, the presumption, the sanction gate, the disproportionate assets offence, is visible in embryo in the 1947 Act, and so is the recurring constitutional and policy argument about how much of the prosecution's work presumptions and gates may properly do.

The third layer was institutional rather than statutory. In 1962 the Government of India appointed the Committee on Prevention of Corruption, chaired by K. Santhanam, to examine the growth of corruption in public administration and recommend remedies. The Santhanam Committee reported in 1964. Its diagnosis was that corruption was fed by discretionary controls, by delay, and by the absence of a dedicated vigilance machinery inside government. Its most durable recommendation was the creation of a Central Vigilance Commission as an apex vigilance body, and the Commission was set up by a government resolution in February 1964, acquiring statutory status only decades later under the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. The Committee also pressed for a stronger and more coherent penal law, and the case for consolidation built from there: the corruption offences were scattered between the Penal Code and the 1947 Act, the two texts used different vocabularies for the same conduct, and prosecuting agencies had to charge under both.

### What did the Santhanam Committee change?

The Santhanam Committee, appointed in 1962 and reporting in 1964, changed the institutional map rather than the offences. Its central recommendation produced the Central Vigilance Commission in February 1964, giving the Union a standing vigilance apex. Its wider argument, that corruption flourishes where discretion is wide and supervision thin, supplied the intellectual case for the consolidated Act of 1988.

By the time Parliament took up consolidation, two defects in the older scheme were conceded across the political spectrum. The first was fragmentation: a single transaction could attract the Penal Code offences, the 1947 Act's criminal misconduct, or both, with different punishments and different procedural incidents, and the resulting charge-sheets invited technical objections that consumed trials. The second was reach: the older law was built almost entirely around the taker. The giver entered the picture, if at all, as an abettor of the taker's offence or as a witness whose statement the 1947 scheme protected in order to secure his evidence. That asymmetry had been defensible when the object was to extract testimony against officials, but it left the supply side of bribery, the contractor, the tout, the firm buying a licence or a contract, outside the statute's direct prohibition. Both defects were named in the debates and reports that preceded the 1988 Act, and both were still being argued about thirty years later, because the 1988 Act repaired the first and largely preserved the second.

The investigative lineage matters as much as the statutory one, because the institutions that work this Act were built for an earlier emergency. The Special Police Establishment was set up in 1941 to investigate bribery and corruption in the war and supply departments of the Government of India, and the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946 gave that force a statutory footing and a name whose acronym, DSPE, survives inside the present Act's investigation chapter. The Establishment grew into the Central Bureau of Investigation by an executive resolution of 1963, and it is the DSPE, not the CBI by name, that Section 17 of this Act still addresses when it fixes the rank of an investigating officer in Delhi at Inspector of Police. The point is structural: the machinery for investigating corruption in India descends from a wartime procurement police, was generalised into a federal investigative agency by executive action rather than by a constituent statute, and operates a central Act in a federal system in which police is a state subject, which is why state anti-corruption bureaux and vigilance departments carry the larger half of the Act's working life. The tension between a central investigative inheritance and a state police reality runs under every later argument about who should hold the approval and sanction gates.

## The making of the 1988 Act and the shape of the statute

The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, Act 49 of 1988, received assent and came into force on 9 September 1988. It repealed the 1947 Act, and the corresponding Penal Code offences, Sections 161 to 165A, were omitted at the same time, so that from 1988 the whole of the general law of official bribery sat in one statute. The architecture it created has five chapters. Chapter I carries the definitions. Chapter II provides for special Judges, sessions judges designated to try offences under the Act and conspiracies, attempts and abetments connected with them. Chapter III contains the offences and penalties, Sections 7 to 16. Chapter IV governs investigation, including the ranks of officer who may investigate and, after 2018, the prior-approval gate in Section 17A. Chapter IV A, inserted by the 2018 amendment, connects the Act to the attachment machinery of the Criminal Law Amendment Ordinance, 1944. Chapter V contains the sanction requirement in Section 19, the presumption in Section 20, and the remaining miscellaneous provisions. A reader who understands that the statute is organised around offences, then investigation, then sanction, then trial before a special Judge, already understands where its pressure points lie: the contest over this Act is almost never about whether bribery should be an offence, and almost always about the width of the offences and the height of the two gates.

The definitional section does heavy work. Section 2(c) defines "public servant" through twelve categories, beginning with any person in the service or pay of the Government or remunerated by fees or commission for the performance of a public duty, and extending to employees of local authorities and of corporations and bodies owned, controlled or aided by government, judges and others discharging adjudicatory functions, arbitrators, persons holding electoral offices, office-bearers of cooperative societies and of educational and other institutions receiving government aid, and members and employees of service commissions and universities. Two explanations widen the net further: persons in the listed categories are public servants whether appointed by government or not, and wherever the expression occurs it is to be understood of every person in actual possession of the situation of a public servant, whatever legal defect there may be in his right to hold it. The definition is functional rather than formal. What matters is the public duty, defined in Section 2(b) as a duty in the discharge of which the State, the public or the community at large has an interest, and not the letter of appointment.

### Who counts as a public servant under the Prevention of Corruption Act?

A public servant under the Act is anyone brought within the twelve categories of Section 2(c): government servants, employees of local authorities and government-controlled corporations, judges, arbitrators, electoral officers, and office-bearers of aided cooperative societies, universities and institutions. The test is functional: a person in possession of the situation of a public servant counts, whatever the defect in his appointment.

The second definition that matters arrived only in 2018. Before the amendment, the operative word in the offences was "gratification", a term the courts had read broadly but which the statute nowhere defined. The 2018 amendment inserted Section 2(d), which defines "undue advantage" as any gratification whatever, other than legal remuneration, and adds that "gratification" is not limited to pecuniary gratifications or to gratifications estimable in money, while "legal remuneration" includes all remuneration the public servant is permitted by the government or the organisation he serves to receive. The substitution matters for scope. A favour that cannot be priced, a job promised to a relative, hospitality, an advantage routed to a third party, all sit comfortably inside "any gratification whatever", and the offences rebuilt in 2018 around this expression inherit that breadth. The Act binds, on the taking side, public servants and those who trade in influence over them; on the giving side, after 2018, it binds everyone, including commercial organisations; and it extends to all citizens of India outside India. Its territorial history carries one mark of the old constitutional settlement: as enacted, the Act did not extend to the State of Jammu and Kashmir, which had its own prevention of corruption statute, and the exclusion was removed only by the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, with effect from 31 October 2019, when the central Act was carried into the successor Union territories and the state statute's regime was folded into it. Its exemptions are not of persons but of situations: the compelled giver who reports in time, the person who assists a trap, and the conduct that no provision captures because it involves no undue advantage and no entrusted property, a gap whose significance is examined in the discussion of criminal misconduct.

## Continuity through repeal: what survived from 1947 and from the Penal Code

Consolidation did not restart the law, and the point is more than antiquarian. Section 30 of the Act repealed the 1947 Act while saving what had been done under it, and the ordinary savings doctrine preserved pending investigations and prosecutions, so that cases begun under the old statute continued to their conclusions in the courts for years after 1988. The deeper continuity is doctrinal. Because the 1988 offences were drafted as successors to the Penal Code and 1947 Act offences, the case law under the predecessor provisions travelled with them: the constructions of "gratification", of the presumption, of sanction and of known sources of income that the courts had built under the 1947 Act and under Sections 161 to 165A of the Penal Code are cited in argument under the 1988 Act as a matter of course, and the Supreme Court's modern authorities, P. Nallammal and Krishnanand Agnihotri among them, were themselves decided under the 1988 text while reasoning in a line that runs back through the older statutes. The 2018 amendment disrupted that continuity at identified points, and only there: where Parliament substituted new text, in Sections 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 19 and 20 and their companions, the older authorities must be re-read against the new words, which is why the demand cases decided under the unamended Section 7 required the Constitution Bench's attention in Neeraj Dutta, and why the pre-2018 learning on Section 13(1)(d) is, after the deletion, of historical and comparative value only. A researcher using this Act professionally therefore works in three strata at once: the pre-1988 authorities that still gloss the carried-over language, the 1988 to 2018 authorities that gloss the original text of this Act, and the post-2018 text, which governs conduct after 26 July 2018 while the older text continues, by the savings and by the constitutional bar on retrospective penal liability, to govern the trials of earlier conduct that still fill the special courts.

## Who the Act reaches: the public servant question in the courts

The breadth of Section 2(c) has been tested at its edges, and the edge cases define the Act's real jurisdiction. In K. Veeraswami v. Union of India, decided in 1991, the Supreme Court held that judges of the superior courts are public servants within the Act, while constructing a safeguard appropriate to judicial independence: no case could be registered against a superior court judge without the prior sanction of the President, to be given after consultation with the Chief Justice of India. The holding does two things at once, and both matter for reading the Act: it refuses to exempt the highest offices from the definition, and it concedes that for some offices the sanction stage, rather than the definition stage, is where protection must be calibrated.

Legislators produced the harder problem. In P. V. Narasimha Rao v. State, decided in 1998 in the prosecution arising from the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha bribery affair, the Supreme Court held that members of Parliament and of state legislatures are public servants under the Act, since they hold an office by virtue of which they are required to perform public duties and are remunerated for it. The same judgment held, by majority, that a legislator who took a bribe for voting or speaking in the House was protected by the parliamentary privilege in Articles 105(2) and 194(2) from prosecution in respect of that vote or speech, and it wrestled with the further difficulty that no authority exists who is competent to remove a legislator and therefore to grant sanction. That privilege holding stood for a quarter of a century and was then overruled. In Sita Soren v. Union of India, a seven-judge bench held in 2024 that bribery is not protected by legislative privilege: the offence of accepting a bribe is complete on acceptance, it is not part of the deliberative function the privilege exists to shield, and a legislator who takes money to vote or speak in a particular way may be prosecuted under this Act like any other public servant. The sequence is a compact lesson in how this statute's reach is fixed by constitutional adjudication rather than by its text alone: the definition never changed between 1998 and 2024, but its application to the most politically sensitive class of public servants reversed.

At the other end of the system, the commercial edge of the definition mattered more. In Central Bureau of Investigation v. Ramesh Gelli, decided in 2016, the Supreme Court held that the chairman, directors and officers of a private bank are public servants for the purposes of this Act, reasoning from the duties banking law casts on them and from the public character of the banking function, with the support of the deeming provision in Section 46A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, which treats bank officers as public servants for the purposes of the Penal Code's public servant chapter. The holding closed what would otherwise have been a conspicuous hole after 1988: the Penal Code offences had been omitted on the footing that the 1988 Act consolidated the field, and had the Act's definition not reached private bank management, corruption in the post-nationalisation and post-liberalisation banking sector would have sat between two statutes and inside neither. The through-line of this case law is consistent with the definitional design. The courts have read Section 2(c) functionally and expansively, attaching the Act to the duty rather than to the employer, and the classes of accused who escape the Act do so, in the main, because no undue advantage, no entrusted property and no public duty can be shown, rather than because their employer was private.

## The taking offences: Sections 7, 7A and 11

Section 7 is the core of the Act and the provision a reader should master first. As substituted in 2018, it punishes any public servant who obtains, accepts or attempts to obtain from any person an undue advantage with the intention to perform, or cause the performance of, a public duty improperly or dishonestly, or to forbear from performing it; who obtains or accepts such an advantage as a reward for improper or dishonest performance already rendered; or who performs or induces another public servant to perform a public duty improperly or dishonestly in anticipation of, or in consequence of, accepting an undue advantage. The punishment is imprisonment for not less than three years and up to seven years, with fine. Three features of the text repay attention. The first is Explanation 1, which provides that the obtaining, accepting or attempting to obtain the undue advantage is itself the offence even if the performance of the public duty was not in fact improper: the clerk who demands five thousand rupees to process a routine ration card application on time, the illustration the section itself gives, commits the offence although the applicant was entitled to the card and received exactly what the law owed him. The second is Explanation 2, which provides that obtaining includes obtaining by abusing one's position as a public servant, by using personal influence over another public servant, or by any other corrupt or illegal means, and that it is immaterial whether the advantage is taken directly or through a third party. The abuse of position therefore survives inside Section 7 even after its removal from criminal misconduct, a point that became central in argument before the Supreme Court and is taken up again in the assessment. The third is what Section 7 requires the prosecution to prove: a demand or acceptance tied, by intention or by reward, to the performance of a public duty. Money that changes hands with no such tie is not within the section.

Clause (c) of Section 7 deserves separate notice because it is the 2018 draftsmen's answer to the timing problem in bribery. Clauses (a) and (b) look at the advantage: taken with the intention of performing improperly, or taken as a reward for performance already rendered. Clause (c) looks at the conduct: a public servant who performs, or induces another public servant to perform, a public duty improperly or dishonestly, or to forbear from performing it, in anticipation of or in consequence of accepting an undue advantage, commits the offence. The sequence may therefore run favour first and payment later, on an understanding rather than a bargain struck in advance, and the prosecution need not force the facts into the inducement pattern of clause (a). Read with Explanation 2, which brings obtaining by abuse of position or personal influence within the section, clause (c) is the textual ground on which the Union argued, in the Section 17A challenge, that the amended Act still reaches the official who bends a decision for gain although the freestanding abuse-of-position limb of criminal misconduct had been deleted: the conduct is covered where an advantage is in the picture, and uncovered only where the official acts from favour, ideology, indolence or loyalty with no advantage in prospect. That is a coherent line for a bribery statute to draw; whether it is the right line for an anti-corruption statute is the question the misconduct discussion takes up.

Section 7A covers the middleman. It punishes whoever accepts, obtains or attempts to obtain an undue advantage as a motive or reward to induce a public servant, by corrupt or illegal means or by exercise of personal influence, to perform a public duty improperly or dishonestly or to forbear from performing it, again with imprisonment of three to seven years and fine. The section consolidates two offences that stood separately before 2018, taking gratification to influence a public servant and taking gratification for the exercise of personal influence over one, and it is the provision that reaches the liaison agent, the fixer and the consultant whose stock in trade is access. Section 11 is the quieter provision. It punishes a public servant who accepts, obtains or attempts to obtain, for himself or another, any undue advantage without consideration, or for a consideration he knows to be inadequate, from a person whom he knows to be concerned in a proceeding or business transacted or about to be transacted by him, or connected with his official functions or public duty, or with those of a superior, or from a person interested in or related to the person so concerned. The punishment, six months to five years with fine, is lower, because the section captures the compromised relationship rather than the proved bargain: the officer who takes a valuable gift at a steep discount from a contractor whose file sits on his desk is guilty under Section 11 although no specific act was bought.

Section 11 is the provision that polices the gift economy around public office, and its drafting repays a closer look because it criminalises a state of affairs rather than a transaction. Its elements are a public servant; an acceptance or obtaining of an undue advantage; the absence of consideration, or the presence of a consideration the servant knows to be inadequate; and a giver who is known to the servant to be concerned in a proceeding or business transacted or about to be transacted by the servant, or connected with the servant's public duty or that of a superior, or interested in or related to a person so concerned. Nothing need be asked for and nothing need be promised in return. The section thereby overlaps deliberately with the conduct rules that govern civil servants, which restrict the gifts an official may accept and require reporting above modest thresholds, but it converts the gravest breaches of that etiquette into crime: the flat sold at half its value by a developer with projects awaiting clearance, the vehicle made available indefinitely by a licensee, the loan on terms no bank would offer, each from a person within the section's description. Its breadth is also its vulnerability in argument, since an advantage from a relative who happens to have business before the government can fall within the literal text, and the courts' insistence on the servant's knowledge, that he knows the giver to be so concerned and knows the consideration to be inadequate, is what keeps the section a corruption offence rather than a strict-liability trap for the ill-advised.

Section 7A's middleman deserves a parallel word, because his trade changed character across the period the Act covers. The classic tout sold access to a clerk or an inspector and took his cut of a cash transaction; the modern intermediary sells something less tangible, an introduction, a reputation for being able to deliver a ministry, a consultancy agreement that launders the advantage into fees. Section 7A reaches both, because its verbs are accepts, obtains and attempts to obtain, and its object is an undue advantage taken as a motive or reward to induce a public servant by corrupt or illegal means or by exercise of personal influence. The retired official who monetises his former office by undertaking to move his successors sits squarely within the description, and the section's indifference to whether the influence succeeds, since the offence is complete on the taking as a motive or reward, makes it one of the few provisions of the Act that can be proved without the underlying decision ever being traced.

Around these sections sits the evidentiary machinery that makes the Act distinctive. Section 20 provides that where, in a trial for an offence under Section 7 or Section 11, it is proved that the accused public servant accepted, obtained or attempted to obtain an undue advantage, it shall be presumed, unless the contrary is proved, that he accepted it as a motive or reward for improper performance, or, under Section 11, without consideration or for inadequate consideration. The presumption is rebuttable and operates only after the prosecution has proved the foundational fact of acceptance, but it reverses the ordinary forensic burden on the most difficult element, the corrupt purpose. The Supreme Court's treatment of that foundation matters as much as the presumption itself. In P. Satyanarayana Murthy v. District Inspector of Police, decided in 2015, the Court held that proof of demand of illegal gratification is the sine qua non of offences under Section 7 and the old criminal misconduct clause, so that mere recovery of tainted money from the accused, without proof of a demand, will not sustain a conviction. In Neeraj Dutta v. State (Government of NCT of Delhi), a Constitution Bench decided in December 2022 reviewed the authorities and held that the foundational facts of demand and acceptance may be proved by circumstantial evidence and not only by the direct testimony of a complainant, that the absence or death of the complainant does not end the prosecution's case, and that once demand and acceptance stand proved, the Section 20 presumption arises and the burden of displacing it rests on the accused. Between them, the two decisions fix the modern law of proof under the Act: demand remains indispensable, but it may be inferred, and a trap proceeding in which marked currency is recovered is evidence within a structure, not a conviction by itself.

The demand doctrine has a history that explains its strictness. In B. Jayaraj v. State of Andhra Pradesh, decided in 2014, the Supreme Court set aside a conviction where the complainant had not supported the prosecution's account of a demand, holding that proof of acceptance of illegal gratification, without proof of demand, was insufficient, and that the Section 20 presumption could not be used to supply the missing foundation. Read widely, Jayaraj threatened every trap case in which the complainant resiled, and it was principally that reading that the Constitution Bench in Neeraj Dutta was convened to examine. The Bench's resolution kept Jayaraj's core and discarded its widest implication: the prosecution must prove demand and acceptance as foundational facts beyond reasonable doubt, but it may prove them by the testimony of witnesses other than the complainant and by circumstantial evidence, including the trap's own artefacts, and the failure or unavailability of the complainant's evidence does not disable it from doing so. The doctrinal settlement is characteristic of this Act's judicial life: the courts protect the accused against conviction on recovery alone, because recovery is the element a corrupt investigator could fabricate, while refusing to make the prosecution hostage to the one witness most exposed to pressure.

### How does a corruption case reach a court under this Act?

A case travels through four filters. An authorised officer investigates, subject to the Section 17A approval gate where an official decision is impugned. On the investigation report, the competent authority decides whether to grant sanction under Section 19. Only then may the special Judge take cognizance, and the trial proceeds before that Judge on a day-to-day basis.


## The statute before 2018: a taker-only scheme and its punishment escalations

Because the amended sections can only be measured against what they replaced, the pre-2018 text deserves a record of its own. The unamended Section 7 punished a public servant who accepted or obtained, or agreed to accept or attempted to obtain, for himself or any other person, any gratification other than legal remuneration as a motive or reward for doing or forbearing an official act, or for showing favour or disfavour in the exercise of official functions. Two features of that drafting are worth fixing. The offence was organised around the official act rather than around improper performance: an advantage taken as a motive or reward for doing what the official should in any event do was within the section, and Explanation 3 stated expressly that "gratification" was not restricted to pecuniary gratifications or to gratifications estimable in money, a breadth the 2018 definition of undue advantage reproduces almost word for word. The middleman offences stood separately, as Section 8, taking gratification in order to influence a public servant by corrupt or illegal means, and Section 9, taking gratification for the exercise of personal influence with a public servant; the 2018 Act fused those two into Section 7A and turned the vacated numbers to the new offences of giving and corporate bribery, which is why a reader comparing texts across 2018 must be careful: Sections 8, 9 and 10 changed their subjects entirely, and commentary written before the amendment that cites Section 8 is speaking of the tout, not of the giver.

Punishments climbed in two steps. As enacted in 1988, the principal offences carried imprisonment from six months to five years: that band covered taking gratification, the middleman offences, Section 11's taking without consideration, and criminal misconduct under Section 13(2), while the habitual offender provision, Section 14, carried two to seven years. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, whose substantive amendments to this Act took effect in January 2014, raised the scheme substantially: the taking and middleman offences moved to a band of three to seven years, criminal misconduct to one to seven years, and the attempt provision's ceiling to five years. The 2018 amendment then restructured the offences, kept the three-to-seven band for bribery on both sides of the transaction, and set criminal misconduct apart at four to ten years and the habitual offender at five to ten years. The direction of travel across thirty years is unmistakable and rarely contested in itself: Parliament has repeatedly made the surviving offences more serious. The 2018 controversy is not about the severity of the punishments but about the narrowed field to which they attach and the gates through which a case must pass before any punishment is in prospect.

One more piece of the unamended scheme must be recorded because the 2018 Act removed it and its removal is part of the rebalancing. Section 24 of the unamended Act, descended from the 1947 Act, provided that a statement made by a person in a proceeding against a public servant, to the effect that he offered or agreed to offer or gave gratification, would not subject him to a prosecution under Section 12 for abetment. The provision was an evidence-gathering device: it told the bribe-giver that his testimony against the official could not be turned into his own prosecution, and generations of trap complainants and approvers spoke under its shelter. Its omission in 2018, in the same amendment that made the giving itself a substantive offence, means that the giver who comes forward is protected only by the seven-day compulsion proviso or by the trap-assistance clause, and a giver who speaks later, in proceedings begun by others, has no statutory shelter for his admissions as such. Whatever the theoretical merits of punishing collusive giving, the exchange of a broad testimonial immunity for a narrow seven-day reporting window is a contraction of the informant's position, and it sits on the contraction side of the table in the rebalancing section of this article.

## How a trap case is built, and the ways it fails

The Act's most familiar proceeding is the trap, and understanding its mechanics explains both the case law on proof and the design of the exception in Section 17A. A trap begins with a complainant who reports a demand. The investigating agency records the complaint, arranges for the demanded sum to be produced and treated, classically with phenolphthalein powder, so that the notes and the hands that receive them can be chemically shown to have met, summons independent witnesses, often public servants from another department, rehearses the complainant in the pre-arranged signal, and positions a shadow witness within hearing of the exchange. At the moment of acceptance the team closes in, the accused's hands are washed in a solution that turns pink if the treated notes were handled, the notes are recovered and their serial numbers matched against the recorded list, and contemporaneous memoranda are drawn up and signed. The evidentiary theory is that demand, acceptance and recovery, proved together, engage the Section 20 presumption and leave the accused to explain innocent receipt, which, in the face of the chemical test and the serial numbers, is difficult to do.

The failure modes are as instructive as the mechanics. The complainant who turns hostile at trial, bought, intimidated or simply exhausted, was once thought to end the case; Neeraj Dutta removes that assumption by allowing the prosecution to prove demand and acceptance circumstantially through the shadow witness, the recorded conversation, the recovery and the conduct of the parties. The trap that proves acceptance but not demand fails under P. Satyanarayana Murthy, because money thrust on an official, or planted, or accepted for onward transmission is not the offence. The trap laid without prior verification of the complaint exposes the agency to the charge of manufacturing the crime it then detects, and courts scrutinise the preliminary verification, or its absence, closely. And the trap is structurally available only against transaction corruption: it catches the clerk at the counter and the inspector at the site, the demand made in the present tense for cash, while the decision corruption at which Section 13 and Section 17A aim, the favour banked, the advantage deferred, the benefit routed to a relative's venture, produces no moment of acceptance for any trap to catch. That asymmetry, between the offence the Act can prove theatrically and the offence it struggles to reach, is the deepest practical fact about the statute, and it explains why enforcement statistics under the Act, such as they are, skew heavily towards petty transactional bribery at the lower levels of the administration, although the Act's text is written for corruption at every level.

## The giving offences: Sections 8, 9 and 10, and the position of the coerced giver

Before 26 July 2018, a person who paid a bribe committed no offence under this Act by the payment itself. He could be prosecuted for abetting the public servant's offence, and the Penal Code offences through which that route ran had been omitted in 1988, leaving abetment under Section 12 of this Act as the principal instrument. The scheme relied on the giver in another role: Section 24 of the unamended Act provided that a statement made by a person in proceedings against a public servant, that he offered or gave gratification, would not subject him to prosecution for abetment. The giver was treated as the prosecution's natural witness, and the law bought his evidence with immunity for his admissions. Section 8 as substituted in 2018 reverses the premise. Any person who gives or promises to give an undue advantage to another person, with the intention of inducing a public servant to perform a public duty improperly, or of rewarding such a servant for improper performance, commits an offence punishable with imprisonment up to seven years, or fine, or both. The section's explanation makes it immaterial whether the advantage is given to the public servant or to someone else, and whether it is given directly or through a third party. Collusive bribery, the payment made willingly to buy an outcome the payer wants, is therefore squarely criminal for the first time, and the change was among the amendment's stated purposes: the government's case, in Parliament and in the Statement of Objects and Reasons accompanying the 2013 Bill, was that a law which punished only the demand side left half of every corrupt transaction untouched and fell short of the standard India had accepted on ratifying the United Nations Convention against Corruption in 2011, whose provisions on active bribery of public officials the amendment was designed to meet.

The difficult question was never the collusive giver but the coerced one: the citizen who pays because a file will not move, a connection will not be released or a licence will not issue without payment, and who is the person most likely to report the demand. Section 8 answers with two provisos of very unequal generosity. The first proviso disapplies the section where a person is compelled to give the undue advantage. The second proviso then conditions the protection: the person so compelled shall report the matter to a law enforcement authority or investigating agency within a period of seven days from the date of giving the undue advantage. A citizen who pays under compulsion on a Monday and reports by the following Monday is outside the offence. A citizen who pays under the same compulsion, thinks the matter over, takes advice, gathers the courage or the documents, and reports on the eighth day is, on the text, within the offence, although the compulsion is identical. Defenders of the window, including the government in the parliamentary debates on the Bill, argue that a short period is what gives the report its evidentiary value and prevents the compulsion claim from becoming a retrospective excuse raised by collusive givers only after an investigation begins. Critics, among them the anti-corruption campaigners who gave evidence during the Bill's long passage, answer that the persons the provision most needs to reach are precisely those least able to find a law enforcement authority, articulate a complaint and accept the consequences within a week, and that the window will be missed by the frightened, the poor and the poorly advised, the very citizens whose reports the enforcement system depends on. Both arguments are about the same seven days. The window is also narrower than the protection it replaced: Section 24 had attached no time limit to the giver's immunity for statements made in proceedings, and its omission by the 2018 amendment means the older, broader shelter is gone.

A drafting tension inside Section 8 should also be recorded, because practitioners noticed it at once and it has not been judicially resolved. The offence requires the giver to act with the intention of inducing a public servant to perform a public duty improperly, or of rewarding improper performance. Section 7, on the taking side, is drafted more widely: its first Explanation makes the obtaining of an undue advantage an offence even if the performance of the duty was not improper, so the clerk who demands money to issue on time the certificate the citizen is entitled to commits the taking offence. Whether the citizen who pays that demand commits the giving offence is a harder question on Section 8's text, since the performance he seeks, the timely issue of his own certificate, is not an improper performance of the official's duty, however corrupt the demand that extracted the payment. One reading confines Section 8 to collusive payments for improper outcomes and leaves the speed-money payer to the compulsion proviso, which fits his case if he reports in time. Another reading treats a performance procured by payment as improperly performed in the manner of its doing, whatever its content. The choice between them will decide the liability of the most common class of bribe-giver in the country, the citizen paying for his own entitlement, and it illustrates the price of rebuilding four sections of a statute in a single amending clause: the giver's offence and the taker's offence were drafted to different measures of impropriety, and the courts have been left to reconcile them.

Two further provisions complete the giving side. Section 8(2) protects a different kind of cooperator: nothing in the section applies to a person who, after informing a law enforcement authority or investigating agency, gives or promises an undue advantage in order to assist that authority in its investigation. That is the statutory footing of the trap, in which the complainant hands over marked currency under police supervision so that the demand can be proved at the moment of acceptance. And the third proviso to Section 8(1) makes a commercial organisation that commits the offence punishable with fine, which leads into Sections 9 and 10. Section 9 provides that where an offence under the Act has been committed by a commercial organisation, the organisation shall be punishable with fine if any person associated with it gives or promises an undue advantage to a public servant intending to obtain or retain business for the organisation, or to obtain or retain an advantage in the conduct of its business. The associated person need not have been prosecuted, an employee is presumed to be a person performing services for or on behalf of the organisation unless the contrary is proved, and capacity does not matter: employee, agent and subsidiary are all within the net. The organisation's defence is procedural. It is a defence for the commercial organisation to prove that it had in place adequate procedures, in compliance with such guidelines as may be prescribed, designed to prevent persons associated with it from undertaking such conduct. Section 9(5) obliges the Central Government, in consultation with stakeholders, to prescribe those guidelines, and the rule-making power in Section 29A extends to them. The model is recognisably the corporate offence in the United Kingdom's Bribery Act 2010, with its adequate procedures defence: liability follows the associated person's act, and the company's answer is the quality of its compliance system, not the innocence of its board. Two consequences of that drafting deserve emphasis. The first is that the defence's content is delegated: what counts as adequate is measured against guidelines the Central Government prescribes, so the practical standard for companies will be set by subordinate legislation and by the compliance practice that grows around it, covering risk assessment for intermediaries, gifts and hospitality registers, training, contractual controls on agents, and escalation routes for demands reported by staff. The second is that the offence relocates the moment of legal significance: for the company, the legally important events happen years before any bribe, in the design and honest operation of its procedures, and a paper programme adopted for display and ignored in the sales process is the precise case the defence is drafted to refuse. Section 10 then attaches personal liability: where an offence under Section 9 is committed by a commercial organisation and is proved to have been committed with the consent or connivance of any director, manager, secretary or other officer of the organisation, that officer is guilty and punishable with imprisonment of three to seven years and fine. Sections 7A, 8 and 9 offences are cognizable.

Who reports bribery, and what protects the reporter, is therefore a question the amended Act answers in fragments. The compelled giver has seven days. The trap participant is protected by Section 8(2). A complainant who is an employee reporting corruption inside his own organisation looks to a different statute altogether, the protection framework examined in [the account of the Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014](/2012/01/01/whistle-blowers-protection-act-2014/), with its own history of delayed commencement. Anyone who does report, citizen, employee or journalist assisting an investigation, faces a practical problem the statute does not solve: the proof of the demand usually lives in the reporter's own records, messages, recordings and notes, and those records are themselves sensitive, since they identify sources and expose the reporter if they leak. Keeping such material in a private, encrypted file rather than scattered across devices and inboxes is the elementary precaution, and it is the discipline described in [this account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), which is as relevant to a complainant assembling a bribery file as to a reporter protecting a contact. The general position set out in this article is legal information about the statute's structure; it is not advice on any individual case, and a person who has paid under compulsion should take advice on the reporting window promptly, because that window does not wait.

## Criminal misconduct: the five limbs, the deletion, and the two that remain

Section 13 was, for thirty years, the most feared provision in the Act, because it converted positions into offences. As enacted in 1988, criminal misconduct had five limbs. A public servant committed it if he habitually accepted gratification other than legal remuneration as a motive or reward; if he habitually obtained a valuable thing without consideration or for inadequate consideration from a person concerned in his official business; if he dishonestly or fraudulently misappropriated or converted property entrusted to him or under his control as a public servant, or allowed another to do so; if he obtained for himself or any other person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage by corrupt or illegal means, by abusing his position as a public servant, or, while holding office, for any person without any public interest; or if he or any person on his behalf was in possession of pecuniary resources or property disproportionate to his known sources of income, for which he could not satisfactorily account. The fourth limb, Section 13(1)(d) of the unamended Act, was three alternative routes in one clause, and its third route, obtaining a pecuniary advantage for any person without any public interest, was the one that haunted the higher civil service. It required no bribe, no dishonesty in the ordinary sense and no personal gain. An official who took a decision that conferred a benefit on a company, a licensee or a class of applicants could be charged, years later, on the footing that the benefit had been conferred without public interest, with the content of the public interest assessed by an investigating officer and a prosecutor rather than by the government that had made the policy.

The objection to that limb was not corrupt in origin, and the stance of this article requires it to be stated at full strength. The Central IAS Association and associations of retired civil servants campaigned against Section 13(1)(d)(iii) for years, arguing that it criminalised error of judgment: that complex decisions on spectrum, minerals, land and procurement are taken on files, under time pressure, on the advice of committees, and that a decision which proves mistaken, or which a successor government dislikes, is not a corrupt decision. Writers from within the service, among them the former officer K. B. S. Sidhu, made the argument publicly at the time of the amendment: a public servant could face conviction for criminal misconduct although no quid pro quo was shown, merely because a benefit had passed to a third party and an investigator later pronounced it contrary to the public interest. The Law Commission of India, which examined the amendment Bill in its 254th Report, and the Select Committee of the Rajya Sabha, which reported on the Bill in August 2016, both worked on texts that removed or recast the offending limb.

The clause's career in the courts had, by then, given both sides of the argument their evidence. In Rajiv Kumar v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided in 2017, the Supreme Court confirmed that the three sub-clauses of the old Section 13(1)(d) were independent and disjunctive: a prosecution could rest on any one of them, including the obtaining of a pecuniary advantage for a person without public interest, without alleging the others. The clause's most consequential deployment was in the prosecutions that followed the allocation scandals of the period, in which the theory of loss to the exchequer and benefit to chosen allottees was constructed substantially on the third limb, dispensing with the need to prove that money had reached the deciding officials. The collapse of the flagship prosecution is part of the record the amendment's supporters cite: in December 2017 the special court acquitted all the accused in the principal spectrum allocation case, holding that the prosecution had failed to prove its case, a result reached under the old law and widely read, by officials who had watched the proceedings, as proof that the limb invited prosecutions built on hindsight valuations rather than on evidence of corruption. The amendment's critics answer that an acquittal proves the failure of a prosecution, not the vice of a provision, and that Parliament legislated the clause away in the acquittal's shadow rather than on a measured assessment of its deterrent value in cases never brought. Both readings are available on the same record, and the reader should notice that the deletion commanded support well beyond the services: few participants in the 2018 debates, in either House, rose to defend the words "without any public interest" as a satisfactory definition of a serious crime. The 2018 amendment deleted clause (d) altogether, along with the two habitual-conduct limbs, and substituted a Section 13(1) with two categories only. A public servant commits criminal misconduct if he dishonestly or fraudulently misappropriates or otherwise converts for his own use any property entrusted to him or under his control as a public servant, or allows any other person so to do; or if he intentionally enriches himself illicitly during the period of his office. The punishment rose at the same time, from a band of one to seven years as the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 had left it, to imprisonment of not less than four years and up to ten years, with fine. Section 15, the attempt provision, was narrowed to match: it punishes an attempt to commit the misappropriation limb, with two to five years.

The illicit enrichment limb carries the old disproportionate assets offence inside it, translated into a presumption. The first Explanation to Section 13(1) provides that a person shall be presumed to have intentionally enriched himself illicitly if he, or any person on his behalf, is in possession, or has at any time during the period of his office been in possession, of pecuniary resources or property disproportionate to his known sources of income which the public servant cannot satisfactorily account for. The second Explanation defines known sources of income as income received from any lawful source; the older phrase had carried the qualifier "known to the prosecution", and its replacement by "any lawful source" settles the drafting in the accused's favour on paper, since income the prosecution did not know of is not excluded from the account merely for that reason, although the official must still bring it forward and prove its lawful character. The burden structure was settled under the predecessor clause and survives the translation. In P. Nallammal v. State, decided in 1999, the Supreme Court held that the prosecution must first prove that the accused is a public servant and that he possesses resources or property disproportionate to his known sources of income; only then does the onus shift to the public servant to account satisfactorily for the disproportion. The ingredients were listed in M. Krishna Reddy v. State Deputy Superintendent of Police, Hyderabad: the prosecution must establish the public servant status, the nature and extent of the resources found in his possession, his known sources of income, and that the resources are disproportionate to those sources, before the accused is called on to explain. In Krishnanand Agnihotri v. State of Madhya Pradesh, the Court accepted that known sources of income means sources known to the prosecution after a thorough investigation, and allowed a margin of tolerance: a disproportion in the region of ten per cent was treated as not, without more, criminal. That margin has no statutory text and is an act of judicial moderation, and the debate about whether the offence reverses the burden unfairly, which the brief for this article requires to be faced, turns on it. The prosecution's answer is that the facts of a man's income and assets are peculiarly within his knowledge, that the State cannot prove a negative across a career, and that the accused is asked only to account, not to disprove dishonesty. The defence's answer is that conviction follows from an arithmetic gap plus an explanation a court finds unsatisfying, that family assets, agricultural income and gifts are routinely swept into the gap, and that the presumption of illicit enrichment does the work that proof of a corrupt act should do. Both descriptions of the same machinery are accurate.

The working method of a disproportionate assets investigation deserves statement, because the offence's burden structure only makes sense inside it. The investigator fixes a check period, usually the span of the official's service or the segment of it under suspicion, and reconstructs three figures: the assets held at the start, the assets held at the end together with expenditure during the period, and the total income from known lawful sources across the period. The disproportion is the excess of assets and expenditure over income, after crediting what the official can document: a spouse's salary from an independent employer, agricultural income supported by records, loans actually raised and repaid, gifts whose occasion and donor can be shown. Cases are won and lost on the documentation of ordinary family finance, on whether a house built over a decade was valued at construction cost or at a later market figure, on whether a daughter's wedding expenditure is estimated or proved, and on whether the prosecution's arithmetic survives the ten per cent tolerance the courts allow. The offence therefore behaves less like a bribery charge and more like a forensic audit with a criminal sanction attached, which is precisely why the second proviso to Section 17 requires the investigation to be ordered by an officer of Superintendent of Police rank: Parliament recognised that an audit of a colleague's whole financial life is an investigative step that ought not to be available at junior discretion.

### Can a public servant be prosecuted for a bad decision if no bribe was paid?

After the 2018 amendment, a decision that turns out badly, or that benefits a private party, is not by itself an offence under the Act. The old clause that punished obtaining a pecuniary advantage for any person without public interest is gone. Prosecution requires an undue advantage, entrusted property misappropriated, or illicit enrichment the official cannot explain.

What the deletion leaves outside the Act should be stated exactly, because both camps in the debate exaggerate it. Conduct that survives: a public servant who bends a decision in exchange for an undue advantage is within Section 7, whose second Explanation expressly covers obtaining an advantage by abusing his position, and during the hearings in the constitutional challenge to Section 17A the Union's argument, accepted at that point by counsel for the petitioner Prashant Bhushan, was that the amended Section 7 continues to cover the situations of abuse of position to obtain a pecuniary advantage that the deleted limb had reached. Conduct that falls outside: favouritism, nepotism in allocation, and the deliberate conferral of a benefit on a chosen party where no advantage flows to the official or his nominees, no entrusted property is converted, and no unexplained wealth results. Before 2018 such conduct could be prosecuted under Section 13(1)(d)(ii) and (iii), controversially and at the price of exposing every hard decision to later criminal audit. After 2018 it is a matter for departmental proceedings, vigilance action, audit and politics. Whether that is the statute's honest recognition that bad judgment is not crime, or the removal of the only provision that reached corruption without cash, is the evaluative core of the 2018 amendment, and reasonable readers of the same text divide on it. The verdict section of this article returns to the question with a position.

## The 2018 rebalancing of the Prevention of Corruption Act

The Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018, Act 16 of 2018, was the end of a five-year passage. The Bill had been introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 19 August 2013, examined by the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Personnel, Public Grievances, Law and Justice, referred to the Law Commission, whose 254th Report dealt with it, and then to a Select Committee of the Rajya Sabha, which reported on 12 August 2016. The Rajya Sabha passed it on 19 July 2018 and the Lok Sabha on 24 July 2018; presidential assent followed on 26 July 2018 and the amendments came into force the same day. The stated objects, in the government's presentation, were to align the Act with the United Nations Convention against Corruption, to punish the bribe-giver and the commercial organisation, to protect honest officials from harassment over bona fide decisions, and to put corruption trials on a clock. The table below is the findable record of what the amendment actually did, direction by direction.

Read down the direction column and the series thesis of this cluster is proved on one page: the same Act that expanded the statute's reach across givers, companies, definitions and punishments also contracted it across the definition of misconduct, the protection of givers who report late, and both procedural gates. Any single-direction account of the amendment is therefore not a summary but an error.

## The investigation gate: Section 17, Section 17A, and the provision the Court struck down

Investigation under the Act has always been rank-bound. Section 17 provides that no police officer below the rank of Inspector of Police in the Delhi Special Police Establishment, Assistant Commissioner of Police in the metropolitan areas, or Deputy Superintendent of Police or equivalent elsewhere may investigate an offence under the Act without the order of a magistrate, and a second proviso added in 2018 requires that an offence of illicit enrichment under Section 13(1)(b) be investigated only under the order of an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police. The structure reflects a legislative judgment that corruption investigation is intrusive and error-prone and should begin at a senior level. Corruption investigation itself sits with the Central Bureau of Investigation for central government servants and with state anti-corruption bureaux and vigilance organisations for state servants, a distributed model quite unlike the single-agency design Parliament chose for scheduled terrorism and organised crime offences in [the National Investigation Agency Act 2008](/2011/06/15/nia-act-2008-explained/), which that article examines.

Section 17A, inserted as Section 12 of the amending Act's scheme and operative from 26 July 2018, adds a gate before the investigation rather than within it. No police officer shall conduct any enquiry or inquiry or investigation into any offence alleged to have been committed by a public servant under the Act, where the alleged offence is relatable to any recommendation made or decision taken by that public servant in the discharge of his official functions or duties, without the previous approval of the government of the Union or of the State in whose affairs he was employed, or, for any other person, of the authority competent to remove him from office at the time of the alleged offence. Two provisos cut the gate down. No approval is necessary for cases involving the arrest of a person on the spot on the charge of accepting or attempting to accept an undue advantage for himself or another: the trap case, in which the official is caught with the money, proceeds without permission. And the authority must convey its decision within three months, extendable by a further month for reasons recorded in writing. The gate is therefore not unlimited, and it does not cover the classic bribery transaction caught in the act. What it covers is the decision: the file noting, the recommendation, the approval, the policy choice, which is precisely the category in which the complaint of harassment over bona fide decisions had been made. Note also what the gate protects against: not prosecution, for which Section 19 has always required sanction, but the enquiry itself, the preliminary stage at which reputations are damaged and at which, the investigators answer, evidence is gathered before it can be managed.

How the gate works in practice is a matter of files. The investigating agency assembles a proposal setting out the allegation and its connection with the recommendation or decision, and the competent authority decides on that record whether an enquiry may proceed. In the constitutional challenge described later in this article, the Solicitor General placed a standard operating procedure before the Supreme Court and described reasoned, detailed orders being passed on such proposals, adding that where ministers are involved, the approving authority under the rules of business would be the Governor or the President rather than a colleague in the same council of ministers. That answer meets the crudest form of the conflict objection and leaves the refined form standing: the authority remains an organ of the same executive, advised by the same administrative machinery, and its refusal needs no confirmation from any independent body. The provision's defenders reply, with some force, that investigation agencies are themselves organs of the executive, so that the alternative to an executive gate is not an independent investigator but an unfiltered executive investigator, and that the choice is between two locations of executive judgment, one before the enquiry and one within it. That is the honest framing of the dispute, and readers evaluating Section 17A should hold the argument in that form rather than in the slogans of either camp.

To see why Section 17A drew the charge that it restores a struck-down protection, the earlier history must be set out accurately. Executive government had long sought a screening device for investigations against senior officers. The Central Vigilance Commission's Single Directive, clause 4.7.3 of the government instructions governing the CBI, required prior permission of the government before an enquiry or investigation could be initiated against officers of the level of Joint Secretary and above. In Vineet Narain v. Union of India, decided in 1998, the Supreme Court struck that directive down as part of its directions for insulating the CBI from executive control. Parliament then enacted the screening device in statute, and the route it chose is instructive: the protection was not placed in this Act but in the agency's own statute, and it was carried there by the very legislation that gave the Vigilance Commission statutory status. Section 6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946, inserted by the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003 with effect from 11 September 2003, provided that the Delhi Special Police Establishment could conduct no enquiry or investigation into an offence under this Act alleged against an employee of the Central Government of the level of Joint Secretary and above, and officers of equivalent rank in government corporations and societies, without the previous approval of the Central Government. In Dr Subramanian Swamy v. Director, Central Bureau of Investigation, a Constitution Bench of five judges, in a judgment delivered on 6 May 2014 with Chief Justice R. M. Lodha presiding, held Section 6A(1) invalid and unconstitutional. The reasoning is the analytical heart of this article, and it must be stated precisely. The Court held that Section 6A created a classification of public servants by status and rank which had no rational nexus with the object of the anti-corruption law: corruption is corruption whoever commits it, the offence is the same, and a provision that shields one class of officials from even being investigated, on the basis of the level they occupy, offends the equality guarantee in Article 14 of the Constitution. The Court added that such a threshold barrier impedes the fight against corruption at precisely the levels where grand corruption occurs. A later Constitution Bench, in Central Bureau of Investigation v. R. R. Kishore, held that the declaration operates retrospectively, so that Section 6A is to be treated as never in force from its insertion in 2003.

The comparison with Section 17A can be drawn in exact terms, and it cuts in a direction many summaries miss. Section 6A was vulnerable because it classified by rank: it protected Joint Secretaries and above and nobody else. Section 17A does not classify by rank at all. It classifies by subject matter, applying to every public servant, senior or junior, where the allegation is relatable to a recommendation or decision in the discharge of official functions. Practitioners who defend the provision, and the Union in its defence of the section before the Supreme Court, make the argument in this form: the equality defect identified in Subramanian Swamy was the status-based line, a provision drawn on functional lines, covering the peon and the secretary alike and only where an official decision is impugned, does not reproduce that defect, and screening of decision-based allegations serves the legitimate object of protecting decision-making from the chilling effect of investigative exposure. The counter-argument, pressed by the petitioners in the challenge and by anti-corruption campaigners since 2018, is that the functional breadth is the problem rather than the answer: the gate stands in front of every official decision by every public servant, the approving authority is the government whose servant is under suspicion and whose own members may be implicated in the same decision chain, and a three-month clock policed by nobody in particular is a weak discipline on a government minded to delay. Which argument prevails is a constitutional question that, as the closing sections record, the Supreme Court has taken up and has not finished answering. A separate and narrower question about Section 17A, whether it applies at all to offences alleged before its insertion on 26 July 2018, itself produced a divided Supreme Court in N. Chandrababu Naidu v. State of Andhra Pradesh in 2024, where two judges differed and the question was referred for a larger bench, which is the posture in which much of this provision's law remains: textually clear, constitutionally and temporally unsettled.

## The prosecution gate: Section 19 sanction, its jurisprudence, and the 2018 extension

If Section 17A guards the door of the investigation, Section 19 guards the door of the court. No court shall take cognizance of an offence punishable under Sections 7, 11, 13 and 15 alleged to have been committed by a public servant, except with the previous sanction of the Central Government for a person employed in connection with the affairs of the Union and removable only by or with its sanction, of the State Government in the parallel state case, or of the authority competent to remove him from office in any other case, save as otherwise provided in the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, whose own inquiry and referral machinery for the officials within its jurisdiction is set out in [the account of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013](/2011/11/15/lokpal-lokayuktas-act-2013-explained/), and to which a case may be referred for prosecution through the Lokpal's own channel. The distinction between the two gates is the one most often blurred in commentary, and the brief for this article requires it to be kept sharp. Section 17A approval is a permission to investigate, given before evidence is gathered. Section 19 sanction is a permission to prosecute, given after investigation, on the material the investigation has produced, and it conditions the court's power to take cognizance rather than the police power to enquire. A case can clear the first gate and fail at the second; a case outside Section 17A, because it is a trap case or alleges no official decision, still needs sanction before any court may act on it.

The rationale for sanction, and its limits, were worked out in a line of authority the reader should know by name. In R. S. Nayak v. A. R. Antulay, the Supreme Court tied the sanctioning authority to the power of removal: the authority competent to grant sanction is the authority competent to remove the public servant from his office, since the design entrusts the decision to the body answerable for the servant's continuance in service. In Mansukhlal Vithaldas Chauhan v. State of Gujarat, the Court held that sanction is not an empty formality: the sanctioning authority must apply its mind to the material, and a sanction granted mechanically, or under the dictation of another authority, is invalid, because the provision exists to protect the honest official from vexatious prosecution and not to be a rubber stamp in either direction. Delay was the third front. In Subramanian Swamy v. Dr Manmohan Singh, decided in 2012, the Court, dealing with a sanction request that had waited without decision, held that the competent authority should decide a sanction request within three months, with a further month where the advice of the Attorney General or Advocate General was required, and recognised the right of a private citizen to file a complaint and seek sanction, while commending to Parliament the framing of guidelines such as those the amendment later gestured towards. The unamended section also generated a gap of a particular shape. In Parkash Singh Badal v. State of Punjab, the Supreme Court read the section's protection as attached to the office held by the accused, with the result that a person who had demitted the office in which the offence was allegedly committed could be proceeded against without sanction, because the clause spoke of persons employed in connection with the affairs of the Union or a State and the courts reasoned from the section's protective purpose towards serving officers. Whether that reading was right was debated, but its practical consequence was plain: retirement ended the gate.

The 2018 amendment closed the retirement route and rewrote the section's working parts. Sanction is required in the case of a person who is employed, or who was at the time of the commission of the alleged offence employed, and an Explanation added to Section 19 provides that "public servant" for the purposes of the section includes a person who has ceased to hold the office during which the offence is alleged to have been committed, and a person who has ceased to hold that office and holds another. The same amendment disciplined the front of the process. A private person, as distinct from a police officer or an officer of an investigation agency or other law enforcement authority, may not even request sanction unless he has first filed a complaint in a competent court about the alleged offences and the court has not dismissed it; where a private request is made, the authority shall not accord sanction without giving the concerned public servant an opportunity of being heard. The authority receiving a proposal is to endeavour to convey its decision within three months of receipt, extendable by one further month, for reasons recorded in writing, where legal consultation is required, and the Central Government may prescribe guidelines for the grant of sanction. Section 19(3) retains its older and controversial discipline on courts: no finding, sentence or order of a special Judge is to be reversed for absence of or irregularity in sanction unless a failure of justice has in fact been occasioned, no court shall stay proceedings for a sanction irregularity that has worked no failure of justice, and no court shall stay proceedings under the Act on any other ground or revise interlocutory orders. The Supreme Court's insistence, in the litigation that reached it over trial stays, on reading that bar strictly is part of why corruption trials, once they genuinely begin, are harder to derail than trials under the general law: in Asian Resurfacing of Road Agency Pvt. Ltd. v. Central Bureau of Investigation, decided in 2018, a three-judge bench directed that in every case where a challenge to an order framing charge is entertained, there should be no stay of the trial, reading Section 19(3)(c) together with the Act's anti-delay design to deny courts the power to halt corruption trials at the interlocutory stage, a direction whose breadth has itself been debated in later litigation but whose statement of the Act's intent is unmistakable.

The consequences of the gate's operation are severe in both directions, which is why sanction litigation is so hard-fought. Cognizance taken without a valid sanction is void, and a trial founded on it falls; yet the defect is in principle curable, since a fresh sanction can support a fresh proceeding on the same material, so the accused gains time rather than absolution and the prosecution loses years rather than the case. A sanction refused is equally consequential, because the refusal is an administrative decision that closes the prosecution unless it is set aside on judicial review for failing the Mansukhlal standard of application of mind, and reviewing courts approach such refusals with the deference due to an evaluative decision while insisting that the deciding authority confronted the investigative material and not merely the accused's service record or the department's convenience. The 2018 additions to the section, the complainant's prior court complaint, the hearing to the public servant on a private request, the three-month endeavour, the power to prescribe guidelines, are best read as Parliament's attempt to make the gate behave like an adjudication rather than a filing decision, and the honest comment is that a gate behaves like an adjudication only when reasons are recorded and examinable, which the section encourages for extensions of time but does not in terms require for the decision itself. The drafting of the time limit itself deserves a practitioner's note. Where Section 17A directs in mandatory terms that the concerned authority "shall convey its decision" within three months, Section 19's corresponding proviso directs the authority to "endeavour to convey the decision" within three months of the proposal's receipt. The verb is the difference between a rule and an aspiration, and the Supreme Court's three-month discipline in the Subramanian Swamy sanction case was framed in mandatory terms a full six years before Parliament chose the weaker word. Nor does either section supply the consequence that would make its clock self-executing: no approval or sanction is deemed to have been granted when the period expires, so the remedy for a file that sleeps is a writ petition asking a court to direct a decision, which is to say that the timelines discipline the conscientious authority and merely inconvenience the other kind. Reform proposals across the years have repeatedly reached for a deemed-sanction device, under which silence after the period would count as a grant, and as repeatedly been resisted on the ground that prosecutions of senior officials ought not to begin by default. The 2018 text records that unresolved argument in its verbs.

Detection deserves a section of its own in any honest account, because the Act's offences are structured around transactions that both parties usually wish to hide. The trap case is the exception that proves the rule: most corruption surfaces not through police initiative but through paper, through procurement records, allotment lists, inspection reports and file notings that a complainant, a rival bidder, an auditor or a journalist has assembled, very often by extracting the record from the department itself under the transparency statute whose machinery is mapped in [the guide to the Right to Information Act 2005](/2011/09/01/right-to-information-act-2005-guide/). The Act's enforcement quality is therefore a function of matters outside its text: the willingness of vigilance organisations to act on documented complaints, the survival of the complainant through a long process, and the speed with which documentary suspicion can be converted into an investigation before the decision chain disperses into retirement and transfer, the very point at which the Section 19 extension to former servants does its work.

### What happens to property that corruption has produced?

The Act reaches the proceeds through attachment rather than a confiscation code. Section 18A, inserted in 2018, applies the Criminal Law Amendment Ordinance, 1944 to attachment and administration of money or property procured by an offence under the Act, with references to the District Judge read as the special Judge, and expressly subject to the money-laundering statute.

Attachment pending trial is one of the Act's underreported strengths, because it operates on the asset while the prosecution is still assembling itself, and Section 18A's opening words, save as otherwise provided under the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002, mark the junction with the proceeds-of-crime regime whose attachment and confiscation machinery is the subject of [the guide to the PMLA 2002](/2013/03/01/pmla-2002-complete-guide/): corruption offences are scheduled offences under that statute, so the same money can attract the Enforcement Directorate's provisional attachment on a money-laundering theory and the special Judge's attachment under the 1944 Ordinance on the corruption charge, and the two processes run on different standards and different clocks. For the illicit enrichment offence, the property is not merely a target of attachment but the very subject matter of the charge, since the offence is complete when the disproportion is possessed and cannot be accounted for.

## Two jurisdictions over the same offences: this Act and the Lokpal

No account of the Act's gates is complete without the second statutory channel that the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 opened beside them. The saving words in Section 19(1), "save as otherwise provided in the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013", acknowledge that for the classes of public servants within the Lokpal's jurisdiction, complaints may travel a different route: a preliminary inquiry by the Lokpal's inquiry wing or by an agency to which the Lokpal refers the matter, including the Central Bureau of Investigation, followed, where the Lokpal finds a prima facie case, by investigation and prosecution before the special courts under this Act, with the Lokpal's own sanction arrangements displacing the departmental route the section describes. The offences remain this Act's offences, tried by this Act's special Judges under this Act's punishments; what the Lokpal changes is who sets the machinery in motion and who answers for its movement. The same 2013 statute was also, as recorded in the punishment history in this article, the vehicle by which Parliament raised the Act's minimum sentences in January 2014, so the Lokpal legislation touches this Act at three points: jurisdiction over complaints, sanction for the cases it carries, and the severity of the penalties awaiting conviction. The full treatment of the Lokpal's composition, jurisdiction and record belongs to the linked article on that statute; for the reader of this Act, the operative point is narrower and purely structural. A complaint of corruption against a senior central official may enter the system through the police and vigilance route, with its Section 17A approval and Section 19 sanction gates, or through the Lokpal route, and the choice of door, which the complainant does not fully control, can determine which gatekeepers the case must pass. Justice Viswanathan's proposed reading of Section 17A in the constitutional challenge, under which the Lokpal or Lokayukta would decide approvals generally, would fuse the two routes at the gate itself, which is why that opinion, although delivered in a divided decision whose question awaits a larger bench, is best understood as a proposal about the system's architecture and not only about one section's validity.

## The trial's special rules: pardon, presumption and the accused as witness

The trial before the special Judge is an ordinary criminal trial modified at points that reveal the statute's theory of corruption evidence. A special Judge may tender a pardon to a person supposed to have been directly or indirectly concerned in an offence, on condition of a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within his knowledge and of every other person concerned, a power Section 5(2) imports from the pardon provisions of the procedure code. The pardon is the second of the Act's two informant devices, and the contrast with the first is worth drawing: Section 24 once protected the giver's statements in proceedings generally, where the pardon protects a participant who turns approver on terms the court sets and can withdraw if the disclosure proves false or incomplete. The 2018 removal of Section 24 makes the pardon route carry more of the system's need for insider evidence, and an approver's evidence carries the corroboration cautions that accomplice testimony always attracts.

The accused, for his part, is made a competent witness for the defence by Section 21, may give evidence on oath only at his own request, and is protected from comment on his silence and from questions about other offences or bad character except within the limits the section states. Section 22 then writes the statute's impatience with delay into the procedure code itself: the accused must be required to give in writing, at once or within the time the court allows, his list of defence witnesses and documents, so that the defence case cannot be spun out by instalments; proceedings shall not be adjourned or postponed merely because a party has applied to a higher court in revision; the Judge may, for recorded reasons, proceed with the trial in the absence of the accused or his pleader and record evidence subject to recall for cross-examination; and a revisional court shall not ordinarily call for the record without hearing the other side or where certified copies suffice for examination. Section 6 adds a niche power to try summarily certain offences connected with contraventions of control orders under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, with a sentence ceiling of one year, a survival from the era when corruption prosecutions were entangled with the enforcement of rationing and price control. Fines are governed by Section 16, which requires the court, in fixing the amount, to take into consideration the amount or value of the property the convicted person obtained by the offence, or, in an illicit enrichment conviction, the pecuniary resources or property he could not satisfactorily account for, tying the financial penalty to the measure of the corruption rather than to a tariff. Appeals and revisions lie to the High Court under Section 27, the special Judge's court being treated as a court of session for those purposes. Taken together, these provisions explain the statute's procedural personality: the trial is designed to be documentary, continuous and resistant to interlocutory interruption, and its recurring practical failures, which the timeline discussion takes up, are located less in the trial's own rules than in the years of sanction, approval and investigation litigation that precede the first witness.

## Trial before the special Judge and the two-year endeavour

Every offence under the Act is tried by a special Judge alone, appointed by the Central or State Government from among sessions judges, additional sessions judges and assistant sessions judges, and the special Judge takes cognizance without committal, follows the warrant-case procedure of the criminal procedure code, can tender pardon to an accomplice who makes a full and true disclosure, and wields, for attachment purposes, the powers of a District Judge under the 1944 Ordinance. Section 4(4), as substituted in 2018, directs that the trial be held, as far as practicable, on a day-to-day basis and that an endeavour be made to ensure the trial is concluded within two years; where it is not, the special Judge must record the reasons, and the period may be extended by further periods, for reasons recorded in writing, not exceeding six months at a time, so however that the period together with extensions shall not exceed ordinarily four years in aggregate. The word "ordinarily" carries the provision's realism: the ceiling is a discipline, not a guillotine, and no consequence is prescribed for a trial that crosses it, neither acquittal nor discharge following automatically. The honest description of the timeline provision is therefore that it creates a recorded-reasons regime around a two-year target inside a four-year ordinary outer limit, in a system where the causes of delay, sanction litigation before cognizance, interlocutory challenges that Section 19(3) was written to suppress, witness hostility, accused persons of advanced age and health, and the sheer documentary bulk of decision-based prosecutions, mostly operate before or around the trial rather than inside it.

That distribution of delay explains the shape of the enforcement record better than any single failure does. Cases end invisibly at the sanction stage, where a refusal produces no trial and no acquittal statistic, only a closed file. Cases that reach trial end disproportionately in the fact-specific acquittals the demand doctrine produces, where the complainant has died, resiled or been disbelieved, and the circumstantial route Neeraj Dutta preserves is available only where the trap or the documents were well built. Trap cases against junior officials dominate the visible record because they are the cases the machinery can complete: the evidence is created at a moment, the witnesses are officials, and the sanctioning authority faces a file with currency and a chemical test in it. Decision cases against senior officials, which require the approval gate to be passed, documents to be assembled across departments, and a theory of advantage to be constructed from files, are the cases most likely to die at a gate or to arrive at trial aged. Any conviction-rate figure quoted for this Act without that structural account is decoration, and the figures in circulation vary too much by source, period and counting method to bear the argumentative weight placed on them; the pattern, petty cases completing and grand cases stalling, is the durable finding, and it follows from the statute's own design rather than from any single government's practice. The seed question this article is asked, how long a corruption trial can take, has no truthful single-figure answer; it has a statutory design answer, which is the two and four years, and a practical answer, which is that cases measured in decades exist and the design's endeavour language is Parliament's concession to that record.

## An illustration: one file through every gate

The architecture is easier to hold in mind as a sequence, so consider an expressly illustrative file, a composite drawn from the Act's own categories and not from any real case. A regional transport office has before it a fleet operator's application for route permits. An official lets it be known that the permits will move when a payment moves. If the operator pays silently and the permits issue, no case exists in practice, because both parties are satisfied and the transaction leaves no complainant; this is the silent majority of bribery, and Section 8's criminalisation of the giver matters here only as a deterrent communicated in advance. If the operator instead complains before paying, the agency verifies the complaint and lays a trap: the official is arrested on the spot accepting the money, Section 17A's approval is unnecessary because the first proviso excepts on-the-spot arrests, the chemical test and the recorded demand do the evidential work, and the remaining gate is Section 19 sanction, which the department must grant or refuse on the investigation papers before the special Judge can take cognizance. If, instead, there is no demand for cash at all and the allegation is that the official steered the permits to a favoured operator because the favoured operator is his relative's partner, the case is decision-based from the first line: Section 17A approval must be sought from the government before an enquiry may begin, the allegation fits no offence unless an undue advantage to the official can be shown or entrusted property or illicit enrichment can be made out, and under the law before 2018 the same facts might have been charged as criminal misconduct under the deleted clause for obtaining a pecuniary advantage for another person without public interest. The same office, the same permits and the same suspicion therefore produce three legally distinct files, with different gates, different offences and different probabilities of a charge, and the distribution of outcomes across the three is the amended Act in operation.

A fourth file completes the illustration. Suppose the same official, over a decade of postings, accumulates a house, deposits and land in his spouse's name worth several times his total salary for the period, with no documented independent source. No complainant, no trap and no single decision is needed: a vigilance enquiry assembles the check-period arithmetic, an officer of Superintendent of Police rank orders the investigation as Section 17 requires for this offence, and the charge is illicit enrichment, in which the prosecution proves the disproportion and the official is called to account for it. Section 17A's application to such a file is itself contested ground, since illicit enrichment may be alleged without impugning any particular decision, and the argument over whether approval was needed has become one of the standard preliminary battles in assets cases. The illustration shows the Act's spread: a counter bribe provable in an afternoon, a decision favour provable, if at all, through documents and inference, and a fortune provable through arithmetic, all within one statute whose gates were not designed with equal care for the three.

## Reading the Act as a practitioner: charge decisions and defence lines

For the advocate, the Act's practical life is a series of choices made under its constraints, and a pillar article should record the recurring ones. The first choice is the charge. Where a demand and acceptance can be proved, Section 7 is the natural charge and carries the Section 20 presumption in support. Where the money trail is diffuse but the official's wealth is unexplained, the prosecution may frame the case as illicit enrichment under Section 13(1)(b), exchanging the difficulty of proving a transaction for the difficulty of an audit, and the two charges are not exclusive, since a course of bribe-taking and the assets it produced can be charged together. Where the official's gain was routed through a spouse, a relative or a company, the charging decision must handle the "on his behalf" language of the enrichment Explanation and the abetment provision, Section 12, under which the intermediary who knowingly assists an offence under the Act faces the same three-to-seven band as the middleman. Where a company paid, the decision is whether to proceed against the organisation for the fine, against consenting officers under Section 10, or against the individual giver under Section 8, and the three proceedings have different proof structures and different settlement dynamics, a point compliance advisers weigh from the other side.

The defence lines recur with equal regularity, and they map precisely onto the statute's design features. Absence of demand is the first, after P. Satyanarayana Murthy and Neeraj Dutta the most productive line in transaction cases, attacked through the complainant's credit, the absence of verification before the trap, and any innocent explanation for the recovery. Absence of nexus is the second: the advantage, if any, was unconnected with a public duty of the accused, being a private loan, a gift between relatives, or a payment to a person other than the official for purposes the official did not share. The gates supply the third and fourth lines: sanction granted without application of mind, sanction granted by an authority not competent to remove, sanction sought and granted for the wrong office, and, since 2018, investigation begun without the Section 17A approval the allegation required, on which last ground High Courts have quashed proceedings founded on enquiries that proceeded without the approval, treating the section's bar as a condition of the investigation's validity rather than a courtesy. The fifth line is arithmetic: in assets cases, the defence is a documentary reconstruction of the family's lawful finances, attacking the check period, the valuation of assets, the credit given for a spouse's income and the prosecution's expenditure estimates, and aiming to bring the disproportion within the margin the courts tolerate. A reader who sees that every standard defence corresponds to a deliberate design choice, the demand requirement, the nexus requirement, the two gates, the burden structure of the enrichment offence, understands the Act as its litigators do: not as a list of prohibitions but as a machine whose tolerances decide cases.

## The constitutional challenge to Section 17A, and where discretion sits

Section 17A was challenged promptly. The Centre for Public Interest Litigation filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court in 2018, argued for the petitioner by advocate Prashant Bhushan, contending that the provision resurrected the prior-permission protections invalidated in Vineet Narain and Subramanian Swamy, and that it entrusted the decision whether a public servant may be investigated to the executive, including ministers who might themselves be part of the decision chain under scrutiny. The Union, through the Solicitor General, answered that the earlier cases turned on classification by rank under Article 14, which Section 17A does not employ, that filters to screen complaints against public servants are constitutionally familiar, Matajog Dobey v. H. C. Bhari being pressed into service for the permissibility of such screens, and that the section was narrowly framed to protect decision-making and to prevent the policy paralysis that follows when every disappointed complainant can set an investigation in motion. Judgment was reserved in August 2025. When a two-judge bench of Justices B. V. Nagarathna and K. V. Viswanathan delivered its decision in January 2026, the Court divided. Justice Nagarathna held Section 17A unconstitutional and fit to be struck down, reasoning that it is an attempt to resurrect what Vineet Narain and Subramanian Swamy had invalidated, that it forecloses inquiry and protects the corrupt rather than the honest, who need no such protection. Justice Viswanathan upheld the provision but read it down, holding it constitutionally valid subject to the condition that the approval decision be taken by the Lokpal or, in a State, the Lokayukta, an authority independent of the executive, reasoning that Section 17A contains no vice of invalid classification, that the possibility of abuse is no ground to strike a provision down, and that striking it would leave complaints routed through the Lokpal screened while complaints routed through the police were unscreened, a structural imbalance. In view of the divergence, the matter was placed before the Chief Justice of India for the constitution of a larger bench. The constitutional position is therefore genuinely open: the provision operates, it has been condemned as unconstitutional by one judge of the Supreme Court and saved by reading down by another, and its final shape awaits a bench that has not been constituted at the time these lines describe.

### Where does discretion sit in the Act, and why does it matter?

Discretion sits at the two gates and inside the investigation. The government of the day decides Section 17A approval for its own servants; the removing authority decides Section 19 sanction; senior officers control the opening and ranking of investigations. That geography, rather than the text of the offences, is where allegations of selective application acquire or lose their force.

The allegation that anti-corruption law in India is applied selectively, against opponents and away from allies, is a political claim this article does not adjudicate, because the evidence for it case by case lies outside any statute's text. What the text does supply is the map of decision points at which selectivity, if it operates, must operate: an approval authority that is the suspect's own government and that may sit on a file for three months and a further month before the Act's discipline is exhausted, with no deemed approval when the clock runs out; a sanction authority of the same character at the prosecution stage; investigation that must be opened by officers of stipulated rank within organisations answerable to governments; and prosecutorial choices about which limbs to charge, in particular whether to frame a case as bribery, which needs a demand, or as illicit enrichment, which needs an arithmetic gap. A reader who wants to evaluate any particular claim of selective enforcement should look at those points, in that case, rather than at the offences, which are even-handed on their face.

## The international frame, and the offences the Act does not contain

The 2018 amendment was sold partly as a treaty-alignment measure, and the alignment is real but partial, which a pillar article should record exactly. The United Nations Convention against Corruption, which India signed in 2005 and ratified in 2011, obliges states parties to criminalise active and passive bribery of national public officials, the giving side and the taking side alike. Before 2018 India had the passive side and an abetment route to the active side; Section 8 supplies the missing direct offence, and Sections 9 and 10 go towards the Convention's expectation that legal persons be liable for participation in corruption offences. The Convention also addresses bribery of foreign public officials and officials of public international organisations, and there the Act stops short: its offences are built around the Indian public servant, and an Indian company that bribes a foreign official to win business abroad commits no offence under this Act by that payment alone, a gap that separates Indian law from the United States Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 and the United Kingdom Bribery Act 2010, both of which reach outward. Commercial commentary on the amendment noted the comparison at the time, and it remains the largest single omission against the treaty standard the amendment invoked.

The second omission is domestic. The Act does not criminalise bribery between private parties: a purchasing manager who takes a secret commission from a supplier, in a transaction no public servant touches, commits no offence under this Act, whatever his liability under the law of contract, employment or the Companies Act's fraud provisions. Several reform proposals over the years have urged a private-sector bribery offence, and the Convention treats private-sector bribery as a matter states shall consider criminalising rather than one they must. Parliament has so far kept this Act a public-office statute, and the choice is defensible: the Act's distinctive machinery, sanction, prior approval, special judges, a presumption against the accused, is justified, where it is justified, by the public character of the office abused, and exporting that machinery into ordinary commerce would multiply its coercive features in the setting where they are hardest to defend. The third omission is smaller but telling: India has no general offence of a public servant failing to report a bribe offered to him, so the official who is offered, refuses, and says nothing breaches a service rule but commits no crime, a silence the Act tolerates because its offences all run on acceptance, agreement and enrichment rather than on omission. Naming these three absences completes the pillar's map: the Act criminalises the taker, the giver, the middleman, the company and the enriched official, and it does not reach foreign bribery, purely private bribery, or silent refusal.

## The honest assessment: what the amended Act does well, what it does badly, and a verdict

Begin with what the 2018 amendment genuinely achieved, because the case for it is stronger than its critics usually allow. Criminalising the collusive giver closed a real gap. A statute that prosecuted only officials taught every contractor that the rational strategy was to pay quietly and, if caught, pose as a victim; Section 8 changes the incentive on the supply side of every transaction, and it brings Indian law into line with the active-bribery standard of the United Nations Convention against Corruption, which India ratified in 2011 and whose absence from domestic law had been an anomaly noted in every serious review, including the Law Commission's examination of the Bill. Corporate liability with an adequate procedures defence is the second genuine gain: it converts anti-corruption from a lottery of individual prosecution into a compliance discipline, giving a company a legal reason to build the systems, training, gift registers, intermediary controls, that actually prevent bribery, and it does so by the proportionate route of fining the organisation and imprisoning only those officers who consented or connived. The punishment architecture was also rationalised upward: minimum sentences of three years for bribery and four for criminal misconduct are serious without being savage. And on the other side of the ledger, the deletion of Section 13(1)(d)(iii) cured a defect that was real and not manufactured by the bureaucracy for its own comfort. A provision under which an official could be imprisoned for conferring a benefit "without any public interest", with the public interest judged retrospectively by investigators, made every allocation decision a deferred criminal risk, and no legal system that understood the distinction between crime and error should have retained it in that form.

Against that, the assessment must record where the design is weak, and the weakness is concentrated exactly where the statute's defenders say its strength lies. The Section 19 reforms are defensible in kind: timelines, a hearing for the official, a filter on private complainants, and the overdue closure of the retirement gap are all measures a careful draftsman might choose. Section 17A is a different order of intervention. It does not filter prosecutions after evidence exists; it filters enquiries before evidence can be gathered, and it gives the filter to the executive employer of the suspect. Subramanian Swamy struck down Section 6A for classifying by rank, and Section 17A's draftsmen plainly learned that lesson, since the new gate classifies by function and catches all ranks. But the equality holding was not the whole of the earlier jurisprudence: Vineet Narain and Subramanian Swamy both rest on the premise that corruption investigation must be insulated from the executive whose members and servants are its natural subjects, and a gate operated by that executive reproduces the conflict in procedure although it escapes the classification defect in form. Justice Viswanathan's reading down, transferring the approval to the Lokpal or Lokayukta, is an acknowledgement from within the defence of the provision that its decision-maker is its flaw. The short reporting window for the compelled giver is the smaller but more human defect: seven days is a period calculated for a complainant who is literate, advised and unafraid, and the citizenry that pays petty bribes under compulsion is mostly none of those things, so the provision's protection will systematically miss the cases it exists for, and the omission of the old Section 24 removed the fallback shelter that had caught such witnesses when they eventually spoke. The trial timeline, finally, is an endeavour dressed as a deadline, and its presence in the statute book will not shorten a single trial unless the sanction and interlocutory stages that precede the trial are disciplined with it.

The repair routes have all been named inside the litigation itself, which is convenient for the reader who wants the menu without slogans. The first route is transfer of the decision: Justice Viswanathan's reading down of Section 17A, under which approval would be decided by the Lokpal or the state Lokayukta, bodies designed to stand outside the executive whose servants are in question; its cost is institutional, since it loads a screening function onto bodies built for inquiry and prosecution, and its merit is that it answers the conflict objection directly rather than by denial. The second route is substitution of the filter: the petitioners in the challenge proposed that instead of executive approval, the screening of decision-based allegations be done through a preliminary inquiry of the kind the Constitution Bench contemplated in Lalita Kumari v. State of Uttar Pradesh for sensitive categories of cases, conducted by the investigator under judicial or Lokpal oversight, so that weak complaints die early without the suspect's employer holding the key. The third route is discipline of the existing gate: time limits with consequences, recorded reasons that a court can examine, and a decision-maker insulated by rule, which is the direction of the standard operating procedure the Union described and of the guidelines power Parliament wrote into Section 19 for sanction, a power whose equivalent for Section 17A approvals the statute does not contain in terms. A legislature revisiting the section would be choosing among these three, and the choice would reveal, more honestly than any speech, whether it regards the gate's purpose as protecting decisions or as protecting governments.

The verdict this article reaches is therefore a divided one, and it is the division, not indecision, that the evidence supports. The 2018 amendment was reform on the demand-supply structure of the offences: on bribe-giving, on corporate liability, on definitions and punishments, and on the worst excess of the old criminal misconduct clause, Parliament legislated well, and the Act is a better anti-corruption instrument for those changes. It was retreat at the gateway: Section 17A transfers the effective decision on whether decision-linked corruption may even be looked into from the investigator to the investigated person's own government, and no three-month clock answers that objection, because the objection is not about delay but about who decides. Whether the trade was worth making depends, as the brief for this article framed it, on which failure mode a reader believes more common, corrupt decisions or harassed officials; but the statute's own architecture suggests the two halves of the trade were separable, and a legislature confident in its giver-side reforms did not need to buy them with an executive-held gate. The article's remaining service to the reader is its map of the cluster: the institution built to receive what this Act uncovers is treated in the Lokpal article already linked, the amendment history of this statute belongs to its pillar treatment here and the enforcement record to the series' wider reading of the anti-corruption framework, and the citizen who has met the Act at the counter should carry away the two operational facts that matter most: a compelled payment reported within seven days is protected, and a demand unaccompanied by payment or proof is the beginning of a case, not the case itself. A third fact belongs beside them, for the reader who follows corruption cases in the courts and the press: the decision to investigate a decision is no longer the investigator's alone, and the first question to ask of any corruption story, any charge-sheet and any quiet burial of a complaint is which gate the case is standing at, the Section 17A approval, the Section 19 sanction, or the trial itself, because under the amended Act the gates, more often than the offences, are where cases are decided, and a report that does not say at which gate a case has stopped has not yet explained the case at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 criminalise?

The Act criminalises corruption by and around public servants. On the taking side, it punishes a public servant who obtains or accepts an undue advantage as a motive or reward for performing a public duty improperly, the middleman who takes an advantage to influence a public servant, and the public servant who takes an advantage without adequate consideration from a person concerned in his official business. It punishes criminal misconduct, meaning misappropriation of entrusted property and illicit enrichment. Since 2018 it also punishes the giver of a bribe and commercial organisations whose associated persons bribe, with abetment and attempt covered as well.

### Q: What conduct counts as criminal misconduct under the Prevention of Corruption Act?

After the 2018 amendment, criminal misconduct under Section 13 has two categories. The first is dishonest or fraudulent misappropriation, or conversion to the public servant's own use, of property entrusted to him or under his control as a public servant, including allowing another person to do so. The second is intentional illicit enrichment during the period of office, which a statutory presumption connects to possession of pecuniary resources or property disproportionate to known sources of income that the public servant cannot satisfactorily account for. Conviction carries imprisonment of four to ten years with fine. The pre-2018 definition, with five limbs including abuse of position, no longer applies.

### Q: Is giving a bribe an offence in India?

Yes, since 26 July 2018. Section 8 of the Act makes it an offence, punishable with imprisonment up to seven years or fine or both, to give or promise an undue advantage intending to induce a public servant to perform a public duty improperly, or to reward improper performance. Before 2018 the giver could be reached only as an abettor of the taker's offence. Two protections qualify the offence: a person compelled to give is outside the section if he reports the matter to a law enforcement authority or investigating agency within seven days, and a person who gives under police direction to assist a trap investigation commits no offence at all.

### Q: Why is prior sanction needed to prosecute a public servant?

Section 19 bars a court from taking cognizance of the principal offences under the Act against a public servant without the previous sanction of the government or authority competent to remove him. The stated purpose is protective: officials must take decisions and exercise discretion without the threat that every disappointed person can set a prosecution in motion, so a responsible authority screens the material before a prosecution begins. The counter-consideration is that the screen is operated by the official's own employer. The Supreme Court has held that sanction is not a formality, that the authority must apply its mind to the material, and that decisions should be taken within about three months.

### Q: What did the 2018 amendment to the Prevention of Corruption Act change?

The amendment, Act 16 of 2018, effective 26 July 2018, moved the Act in two directions. It expanded the statute by making bribe-giving a substantive offence under Section 8, introducing liability of commercial organisations with an adequate procedures defence under Sections 9 and 10, defining "undue advantage" in Section 2(d), and raising the punishment for criminal misconduct to four to ten years. It contracted the statute by reducing criminal misconduct to two categories and deleting the abuse-of-position limbs, by inserting Section 17A's prior-approval gate before investigations into official decisions, and by extending Section 19 sanction to former public servants. It also set a two-year trial endeavour in Section 4(4).

### Q: What is Section 17A and why is it controversial?

Section 17A, inserted in 2018, provides that no police officer may conduct any enquiry, inquiry or investigation into an offence under the Act relatable to a recommendation made or decision taken by a public servant in the discharge of official functions, without the previous approval of the government employing him or the authority competent to remove him. No approval is needed where a person is arrested on the spot accepting an undue advantage, and the authority must decide within three months, extendable by one. It is controversial because the approving authority is the executive whose own servant is under suspicion, and because an earlier, rank-based approval requirement in Section 6A of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2014.

### Q: Are companies liable for bribery in India?

Yes. Section 9 of the Act makes a commercial organisation punishable with fine where a person associated with it gives or promises an undue advantage to a public servant intending to obtain or retain business, or a business advantage, for the organisation. The associated person need not himself be prosecuted, and employees are presumed to act for the organisation unless the contrary is proved. The organisation has a defence if it proves it had in place adequate procedures, complying with prescribed guidelines, to prevent associated persons from bribing. Directors, managers and other officers who consented to or connived in the offence are personally liable under Section 10, with imprisonment of three to seven years.

### Q: How long can a corruption trial take under the Act?

Section 4(4) requires the trial before the special Judge to be held, as far as practicable, on a day-to-day basis, with an endeavour to conclude it within two years. If the trial is not concluded in that period, the special Judge must record reasons, and the period may be extended in writing in instalments of up to six months at a time, provided the total does not exceed ordinarily four years. The ceiling is a discipline rather than an automatic consequence, since the section prescribes no result, such as discharge, for crossing it, and the long delays in practice usually accumulate at the sanction and interlocutory stages before the trial itself begins.

### Q: What does "undue advantage" mean under the Prevention of Corruption Act?

Section 2(d), inserted by the 2018 amendment, defines "undue advantage" as any gratification whatever, other than legal remuneration. An Explanation adds that "gratification" is not limited to pecuniary gratifications or to gratifications estimable in money, and that "legal remuneration" covers all remuneration the public servant is permitted by the government or the organisation he serves to receive. The definition therefore reaches beyond cash to favours, hospitality, employment promised to a relative, and advantages routed to third parties, while excluding the salary, fees and perquisites the official is lawfully entitled to receive for the work.

### Q: What is the punishment for a public servant who takes a bribe under Section 7?

A public servant convicted under Section 7 faces imprisonment for a term not less than three years and up to seven years, and is also liable to fine. The same band applies to the middleman offence in Section 7A and to abetment under Section 12. Section 16 directs the court, when fixing the fine, to take into consideration the amount or value of the property obtained by the offence. The three-year floor dates from amendments made by the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, which raised the punishments across the Act from the lower bands enacted in 1988.

### Q: Is a person protected if they pay a bribe only to help the police trap the official?

Yes. Section 8(2) provides that the bribe-giving offence does not apply to a person who, after informing a law enforcement authority or investigating agency, gives or promises an undue advantage in order to assist that authority in its investigation of the offence alleged against the public servant. This is the statutory basis of the trap proceeding, in which the complainant hands over currency, often marked or treated, under police supervision so that the demand can be proved at the moment of acceptance. The protection depends on informing the authority first; a payment made privately and explained afterwards as an attempt to expose the official is not within the sub-section.

### Q: Can a retired public servant be prosecuted for corruption committed while in office?

Yes, and the 2018 amendment made the position more demanding rather than less. Earlier authority had read Section 19's sanction requirement as tied to the office the accused held, so an official who had retired or demitted the office could in some situations be prosecuted without sanction. The amendment rewrote Section 19 to cover a person who is employed, or was at the time of the offence employed, and an Explanation expressly includes a person who has ceased to hold the office during which the offence is alleged to have been committed. Sanction from the authority competent in respect of that earlier office is therefore required even after retirement.

### Q: Who is personally liable when a commercial organisation pays a bribe?

Section 10 makes the individual officer liable alongside the organisation. Where an offence under Section 9 is committed by a commercial organisation and is proved to have been committed with the consent or connivance of any director, manager, secretary or other officer of the organisation, that officer is guilty of the offence and punishable with imprisonment of three to seven years and fine; for a firm, "director" means a partner. Liability is not automatic on holding the office: the prosecution must prove consent or connivance. The organisation itself is punished with fine, subject to its adequate procedures defence under Section 9.

### Q: What is the presumption under Section 20 of the Act?

Section 20 provides that where, in a trial for an offence under Section 7 or Section 11, it is proved that the accused public servant accepted, obtained or attempted to obtain an undue advantage, the court shall presume, unless the contrary is proved, that he accepted it as a motive or reward for improper performance of a public duty, or, under Section 11, without consideration or for inadequate consideration. The presumption is rebuttable and arises only after the prosecution proves the foundational facts, including a demand; a Constitution Bench in Neeraj Dutta held that demand and acceptance may be proved by circumstantial evidence as well as by a complainant's testimony.

### Q: Who is allowed to investigate offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act?

Section 17 restricts investigation to senior officers: an Inspector of Police in the Delhi Special Police Establishment, an Assistant Commissioner of Police in the metropolitan areas, and a Deputy Superintendent of Police or an officer of equivalent rank elsewhere, acting without a magistrate's order; a State Government may authorise inspectors by general or special order. An offence of illicit enrichment under Section 13(1)(b) requires investigation under the order of an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police. In practice the Central Bureau of Investigation investigates central government servants, and state anti-corruption bureaux and vigilance organisations investigate state servants, subject to the Section 17A approval gate for decision-based allegations.

### Q: Can assets standing in a family member's name count in a disproportionate assets case?

Yes. The first Explanation to Section 13(1) speaks of the public servant "or any person on his behalf" being in possession of pecuniary resources or property disproportionate to his known sources of income, which brings assets held through a spouse, relative or other name-lender into the computation where the prosecution establishes that they are held on the servant's behalf. The prosecution must prove the disproportion as part of its foundational case, as settled in P. Nallammal v. State, and the servant may then account for the assets by showing a lawful source, such as the spouse's independent income, which is why such cases turn heavily on documentation of family earnings.

### Q: What is the difference between Section 17A approval and Section 19 sanction?

They operate at different stages and answer different questions. Section 17A approval is a permission to investigate: it is required before a police officer begins an enquiry, inquiry or investigation into an offence under the Act relatable to a public servant's official recommendation or decision, and it is given by the employing government or the removing authority within three months, extendable by one. Section 19 sanction is a permission to prosecute: it is required after investigation, when the material exists, before a court may take cognizance of offences under Sections 7, 11, 13 and 15. A case can receive approval and later be refused sanction, and trap cases outside Section 17A still need sanction.

### Q: Can a public servant be prosecuted for a decision that benefited someone if no bribe was paid?

Under the Act as amended in 2018, not on that fact alone. The earlier clause of criminal misconduct that punished obtaining a valuable thing or pecuniary advantage for any person without any public interest, Section 13(1)(d) of the unamended Act, was deleted, and the abuse-of-position route survives only inside Section 7, which requires an undue advantage to be obtained, accepted or attempted. A wrong or even a favouring decision with no advantage to the official, no misappropriation of entrusted property and no unexplained enrichment is therefore not an offence under this Act, though it may attract departmental proceedings, vigilance action or audit objections.

### Q: What counts as "known sources of income" in a disproportionate assets case?

The second Explanation to Section 13(1) defines known sources of income as income received from any lawful source. Case law under the predecessor clause adds the working content: the sources must be those known to the prosecution after proper investigation, the prosecution must establish the servant's income from those sources and the extent of the assets, and the comparison is between the two over the check period. In Krishnanand Agnihotri v. State of Madhya Pradesh the Supreme Court allowed a margin of tolerance, treating a disproportion in the region of ten per cent as insufficient by itself, a judicial allowance that keeps small accounting gaps from becoming criminal charges.

### Q: How are offences under the Act tried, and who tries them?

Offences under the Act are tried exclusively by special Judges appointed by the Central or State Government under Section 3, chosen from among sessions judges, additional sessions judges and assistant sessions judges. A special Judge takes cognizance without the accused being committed for trial, follows the procedure for trial of warrant cases, may also try connected offences under other laws charged at the same trial, may tender pardon to an accomplice who makes full and true disclosure, and exercises the powers of a District Judge for attachment under the Criminal Law Amendment Ordinance, 1944. Appeals and revisions lie to the High Court.
