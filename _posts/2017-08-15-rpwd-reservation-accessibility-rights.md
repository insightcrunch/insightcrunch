A candidate with a disability certificate clears a competitive examination, finds her name missing from the list of those appointed against the reserved quota, and discovers that the recruiting body counted her against the general seats while leaving the reserved seat unfilled. An employee of twenty years develops a degenerative condition and is handed a notice retiring him on medical grounds, as if the statute had never spoken. A college building sanctioned and completed years after the accessibility rules were notified has no ramp, no accessible toilet and no tactile path, and the completion certificate was issued anyway. RPwD reservation and accessibility rights exist to govern exactly these three encounters, and each of them is lost or won on a technical point that the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 answers in terms most summaries flatten: how the four per cent quota is divided and counted, what horizontal reservation does to a merit list, what protection an employee carries into service, and which instrument makes a building standard binding rather than advisory.

This article is written for the person holding the entitlement and for the institution carrying the duty. It states the reservation in government employment and higher education as the Act enacts it, works through the horizontal principle that decides real selection disputes, follows the grievance route from the establishment's own officer to the Commissioners and the Special Court, and then gives the accessibility litigation its full length, because the centre of gravity of this field moved in November 2024. On that date the Supreme Court held in Rajive Raturi v Union of India that the rule prescribing accessibility standards under the Act was itself unlawful, being written in the language of recommendation where the Act had commanded. The statute made accessibility mandatory, time-bound and backed by a penalty; the rule made under it converted that command into guidance; and for seven years the weaker instrument was the one that duty-holders actually read. That inversion, the rule undoing the Act, is the thread that runs through everything below.

## Who holds these entitlements, and on what proof

The reservation and accessibility entitlements in this article run on two different keys, and confusing them is the first professional error in the field. Reservation in employment and in higher education is keyed to the benchmark threshold. A person with benchmark disability, as Section 2(r) of the Act defines the term, is a person with not less than forty per cent of a specified disability, certified by the certifying authority under the certification provisions of the Act. The specified disabilities are those in the Schedule to the Act, which the statute pillar in this cluster examines in full in [the complete guide to the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act](/2017/08/01/rpwd-act-2016-explained/). The certificate, and the Unique Disability ID record built on it, is the document on which a claim to a reserved post or a reserved seat is made, and a candidate who cannot produce certification at the stage the recruiting body lawfully requires will ordinarily lose the seat to the next candidate in the category, however strong the underlying medical position.

Accessibility and reasonable accommodation run on the wider key. The Act's definition of a person with disability in Section 2(s) contains no percentage at all: it speaks of a person with a long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairment which, in interaction with barriers, hinders full and effective participation in society on an equal basis with others. The duty to provide reasonable accommodation, defined in Section 2(y) as necessary and appropriate modification and adjustment not imposing a disproportionate or undue burden, is owed to persons with disabilities in that wider sense. Section 3(5) obliges the appropriate Government to take necessary steps to ensure reasonable accommodation, and the definition of discrimination in Section 2(h) expressly includes denial of reasonable accommodation. The practical consequence, which the examination cases discussed later in this article establish beyond argument, is that a facility such as a scribe is not a creature of the forty per cent line: it follows the functional need, while a reserved seat or post follows the certificate.

Between the two keys sits the child. The education entitlements begin before either reservation question arises, because the Act gives every child with a benchmark disability a free education right of its own, and it is the provision parents reach for first.

The wider key has its own statutory root, and it is Section 3. Section 3(1) obliges the appropriate Government to ensure that persons with disabilities enjoy the right to equality, life with dignity and respect for their integrity equally with others. Section 3(2) requires the Government to take steps to utilise the capacity of persons with disabilities by providing an appropriate environment. Section 3(3) provides that no person with disability shall be discriminated against on the ground of disability, unless it is shown that the impugned act or omission is a proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim. Section 3(4) protects personal liberty: no person with disability shall be deprived of his or her personal liberty only on the ground of disability. Section 3(5) requires the appropriate Government to take necessary steps to ensure reasonable accommodation for persons with disabilities. The definition that feeds the section is the wide one in Section 2(s), a person with long term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairment which, in interaction with barriers, hinders full and effective participation in society equally with others. Nothing in Section 3 mentions forty per cent. The discrimination claim, the reasonable accommodation claim and the dignity claim therefore stand available to the person whose impairment is real and long term but uncertified at the benchmark line, and the case law on scribes examined later in this article is, at bottom, Section 3 and Section 2(s) at work against administrative practice that treated the certificate as the whole statute.

The proportionate-means proviso in Section 3(3) is the point at which these claims are actually decided, because most real-world discrimination in this field arrives dressed as a neutral rule: a physical standard for a post, a format requirement for an examination, a building specification, a transfer policy. The proviso places the justification burden on the authority that maintains the rule: it must show that the act or omission pursues a legitimate aim and that the means are proportionate to it. A rule that excludes a class of candidates where an accommodation would have met the aim at no material cost fails that test, which is the analytical core of the scribe decisions and of the functional assessment doctrine of Om Rathod. A claimant framing a grievance should therefore frame it in the section's own grammar, identifying the rule, the exclusion it works, the accommodation that answers it, and the absence of any proportionate justification, rather than as a general complaint of unfairness. Commissioners and courts in this field respond to that grammar because it is the statute's.

### Does a child with a benchmark disability have a right to free education until eighteen?

Yes. Section 31 of the Act provides that every child with benchmark disability between the age of six and eighteen years shall have the right to free education in a neighbourhood school, or in a special school, of his choice.

Section 31(2) obliges the appropriate Government and local authorities to ensure access to free education in an appropriate environment until the child attains eighteen. The right is framed as a right of the child and a choice between the neighbourhood school and the special school rests with the child and family, not with the admitting institution. It operates notwithstanding the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, whose ordinary age band ends earlier.

The choice language matters in practice. A neighbourhood school cannot refuse the child on the ground that a special school exists elsewhere in the district, and a special school cannot be treated as the default destination for every child with a benchmark disability regardless of the family's preference. The wider education chapter of the Act, which the pillar article in this cluster covers, adds duties on recognised institutions to admit children with disabilities without discrimination, to provide reasonable accommodation, and to make the physical and curricular environment accessible. For the remedies reader the sequence is simple: the admission refusal is first a breach of the non-discrimination duty, then a grievance to the institution and the local authority, and then, if it persists, a complaint that can be carried to the State Commissioner, whose functions include inquiring into deprivation of rights. The free education right also interacts with the five per cent reservation in higher education discussed below only at the point of transition: the child educated under Section 31 becomes the applicant under Section 32, and the certificate that grounded the first entitlement is the document that grounds the second.

## Reservation in government employment: what Section 34 actually reserves

Section 34(1) of the Act requires every appropriate Government to appoint, in every Government establishment, not less than four per cent of the total number of vacancies in the cadre strength in each group of posts meant to be filled with persons with benchmark disabilities. Three features of that sentence carry the whole field. The base is the cadre strength, not the vacancies that happen to be advertised in a given round, a point the Supreme Court settled under the predecessor statute and which recruiting bodies resisted for years. The unit is each group of posts, so the computation is made group by group rather than across an establishment as a whole. And the beneficiaries are persons with benchmark disabilities in five statutory clauses, between whom the four per cent is divided by the section itself, leaving no discretion to a recruiting body to pool the quota in favour of whichever category happens to apply.

### Why is one share of the four per cent held jointly by two disability categories?

Because Section 34(1) distributes the four per cent as four shares of one per cent across five clauses, and four shares cannot cover five clauses one each. Clauses (a), (b) and (c) take one share apiece, for blindness and low vision, for deaf and hard of hearing, and for locomotor disability.

The joint holding of the fourth share is the provision's most litigated quiet feature. Clause (c)'s share expressly includes cerebral palsy, leprosy cured persons, dwarfism, acid attack victims and muscular dystrophy within locomotor disability, while the fourth share is reserved jointly for clause (d), autism, intellectual disability, specific learning disability and mental illness, and clause (e), multiple disabilities from amongst persons under clauses (a) to (d), including deaf-blindness, in the posts identified for each disability. A candidate with an intellectual disability and a candidate with multiple disabilities compete inside the same one per cent, and the recruiting body cannot enlarge one at the expense of the other beyond what the identification of posts for each disability permits. The closing words of clause (e), "in the posts identified for each disability", tie the whole distribution back to the identification exercise under Section 33: a category's share is real only against posts identified as suitable for that category, which is why the identification duty, examined next, is not administrative decoration but the foundation on which each of the four shares stands.

The comparison with what came before shows the scale of the change. Under Section 33 of the Persons with Disabilities Act, 1995, the reservation was three per cent, divided as one per cent each for three groupings only, and whole families of disability that the 2016 Schedule recognises, including autism, specific learning disability and mental illness, had no share at all. The route from that scheme to this one, and the drafting choices that produced the five clauses, is traced in [the account of how the PWD Act 1995 gave way to the RPwD Act 2016](/2017/07/15/pwd-act-1995-to-rpwd-2016/). What matters for a candidate reading the 2016 text is that the share is fixed by the section, that the shares are not interchangeable at the recruiting body's convenience except through the narrow carry-forward and interchange machinery the Act itself provides, and that a shortfall in one category is not a lawful reason to appoint against another category's share in the same recruitment year.

Two provisos complete the section. The first proviso to Section 34(1) provides that reservation in promotion shall be in accordance with such instructions as are issued by the appropriate Government from time to time, a formulation whose contested meaning is examined in the promotion discussion below. The second proviso empowers the appropriate Government, in consultation with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner as the case may be, and having regard to the type of work carried on in an establishment, to exempt a Government establishment from the section by notification, subject to conditions. Section 34(2) then supplies the machinery for the vacancy that cannot be filled: it is carried forward to the succeeding recruitment year; if in that year also no suitable person with benchmark disability is available, the vacancy may first be filled by interchange among the five categories; and only when there is no person with disability available for the post in that year may the employer fill it by appointing a person other than a person with disability. A further proviso permits interchange where the nature of the vacancies is such that a given category cannot be employed, with the prior approval of the appropriate Government. Section 34(3) empowers the appropriate Government to relax the upper age limit for employment of persons with benchmark disabilities by notification.

## The certificate in practice: assessment, issue and appeal

Every claim in this article passes through a document, and the document's law sits in Sections 56 to 59 of the Act. Section 56 directs the Central Government to notify guidelines for assessing the extent of a specified disability in a person, and the guidelines notified in January 2018 supply the percentage tables the medical boards apply, disability by disability. Section 57 requires the appropriate Government to designate persons having the prescribed qualifications as certifying authorities, who operate within the areas the notification specifies. Section 58 provides the procedure: a person with a specified disability may apply for a disability certificate in the prescribed manner to a certifying authority within whose jurisdiction the applicant lives, or to any other notified authority; the certifying authority assesses the disability in accordance with the Section 56 guidelines and issues the certificate in the prescribed form, recording the percentage; the certificate, once issued, is valid throughout India, so that a candidate certified in one State does not begin again on crossing a border for a central recruitment. Where the certifying authority concludes that the applicant is not a person with disability, it must record its reasons and inform the applicant. Section 59 then gives the applicant an appeal against the certifying authority's decision to the appellate authority the State Government notifies for the purpose, to be filed within the period the Rules prescribe, and the appellate authority decides after giving the applicant an opportunity of being heard.

Three practical features of this machinery decide more cases than any courtroom doctrine. The first is quantification error. Percentages are assessed on tables, by boards of varying experience, and a two-point error at the forty per cent line is the difference between the benchmark key and no key at all. The appeal under Section 59 exists for exactly this error, and a candidate who treats an adverse assessment as final has surrendered the one remedy designed for the commonest mistake in the system. The second is the distinction the Supreme Court drew in Om Rathod between certification and admission-stage assessment: the quantification recorded at certification, and reflected in the Unique Disability ID record generated under the national project the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities administers, governs eligibility for reservation; a recruiting body or an admission board that re-quantifies the disability at the point of selection, and rejects a certificate holder on its own fresh percentage, acts outside the division of labour the Court laid down. The board at the gate may assess functional capacity for the post or the course; it may not rewrite the certificate. The third feature is fraud, which runs in the opposite direction and which the Act treats seriously: Section 91 punishes fraudulently availing a benefit meant for persons with benchmark disabilities with imprisonment up to two years, or a fine up to one lakh rupees, or both. The provision matters to honest candidates as much as to the State, because every fraudulent certificate consumes a seat the quota created and corrodes the credibility of the certificates that remain, which is one reason boards and recruiting bodies verify, and why a candidate whose certificate is genuine should welcome the verification and insist that it stop at authenticity rather than extend to re-measurement.

The certificate's limits should be kept in view alongside its power. It certifies a percentage against a specified disability in the Schedule. Conditions that fluctuate, conditions the Schedule captures imperfectly, and disabilities whose functional effect far exceeds their measured percentage all press against the instrument, and the litigation on scribes discussed later in this article is one product of that pressure. The Act's own answer at the remedy level is layered: certification and the benchmark line for the reserved seats, the wider definition of disability in Section 2(s) for discrimination and reasonable accommodation, and the appeal in Section 59 for the measurement itself. A claimant who knows which layer a grievance belongs to has already done half the legal work.

## The identification of posts: the step the quota stands on

Section 33 obliges the appropriate Government to identify the posts in establishments which can be held by the respective categories of persons with benchmark disabilities against the vacancies reserved under Section 34, to constitute an expert committee with representation of persons with benchmark disabilities for that identification, and to undertake a periodic review of the identified posts at an interval not exceeding three years. Every element of that duty has been fought over. Identification decides which posts a category may compete for at all. The expert committee requirement decides whether the exercise is done with the participation of the people it governs or by administrators reasoning from assumption about what a blind clerk or a deaf technician cannot do. The three-year review decides whether the list grows as assistive technology changes what a post demands, or freezes at the state of assumption in the year it was drawn.

The courts have refused to let identification become a pocket veto on the quota. In Government of India v Ravi Prakash Gupta, decided in 2010 under the predecessor Act, the Supreme Court rejected the position that reservation could be withheld because posts had not been identified, holding that the identification exercise could not be deployed to defeat the reservation the statute granted. In Union of India v National Federation of the Blind, decided on 8 October 2013 and reported at (2013) 10 SCC 772, the Court directed that the three per cent reservation under the 1995 Act be computed on the total number of vacancies in the cadre strength identically across Groups A, B, C and D, striking down the contrary clauses of the office memorandum of 29 December 2005. The same judgment carries the horizontal holding examined below. The pattern across both decisions is consistent: the administrative steps around the quota, identification and computation, are duties owed by the government, and their non-performance is the government's default, not the candidate's disqualification.

The revision exercises show what the three-year review duty is for. In 2021 the Union Government published a revised and expanded list of posts identified for reservation in central establishments, prepared through the expert committee process Section 33 prescribes, and the additions ran in a consistent direction: posts from which categories of disability had been excluded on assumptions about the work were brought in as assistive technology, screen readers, adapted workstations and redesigned workflows changed what the work in fact required. A list that does not move in that direction across successive reviews is not evidence that technology has stood still; it is evidence that the review has not been done with the seriousness the section demands, and the composition of the committee is the first place to look when a list stagnates, because the Act put representatives of persons with benchmark disabilities on it precisely so that the question asked of each post is what its holder must do, and not what its holders have conventionally looked like.

The honest counterweight is that identification is not a fiction. Some posts carry functional demands that a particular disability genuinely cannot meet even with reasonable accommodation, and the exemption proviso in Section 34 exists for establishments whose type of work makes the full quota inapplicable. The lawful use of that proviso is narrow in three ways: it requires consultation with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner, it must be exercised by notification with any conditions stated, and it must rest on the type of work carried on, not on a general preference for an unreserved workforce. An establishment that simply does not identify posts, or identifies a token handful, has not used the exemption; it has defaulted on Section 33, and the default is exactly the material a complaint to the Commissioner is built from. Under-identification is the documented cause of the backlog problem taken up in the questions at the end of this article: posts never identified generate vacancies never reserved, and drives announced years later are then presented as generosity rather than as the discharge of an arrears.

### What share of their workforce are private employers encouraged to draw from persons with benchmark disabilities?

Section 35 of the Act directs the appropriate Government and the local authorities, within the limit of their economic capacity and development, to provide incentives to employers in the private sector to ensure that at least five per cent of their workforce is composed of persons with benchmark disability.

The five per cent figure is a target the incentive structure is meant to reach, not a quota the private employer must fill. No provision of the Act reserves posts in a private establishment, and a private employer who employs no person with a benchmark disability commits no contravention of Section 34, which binds appointments in Government establishments alone.

The distinction is routinely blurred in both directions: by candidates who write to private employers claiming a statutory quota that does not exist, and by private employers who dismiss the Act entirely although its non-discrimination and accessibility provisions do reach them. Section 35 is an instruction to governments to build incentives, wage support, tax treatment or procurement preference being the obvious instruments, and its softness is a deliberate legislative choice carried over from the structure of the earlier law. What the private employee does hold is the protection against discrimination in the provisions of general application, the accessibility duties owed by service providers whether Government or private under Section 46, and the reasonable accommodation duty as the courts have expounded it. Those are real rights; they are simply not a four per cent claim on a private cadre.

## Computing the four per cent: cadre strength, groups and the carry-forward

The arithmetic of Section 34 is simple to state and persistently misapplied, so it deserves a section of its own. The percentage is computed on the total number of vacancies in the cadre strength in each group of posts. Cadre strength means the sanctioned strength of the cadre, the whole body of posts of a given grade in the establishment, and not the number of vacancies that happen to be advertised in a particular year. The distinction was the core of the National Federation of the Blind judgment: the office memorandum under challenge had confined the computation for Groups A and B to the vacancies arising in direct recruitment in the year, which shrank the base to a fraction of the cadre, and the Supreme Court struck those clauses down and directed computation on the total vacancies in the cadre strength identically for Groups A, B, C and D. An establishment that advertises three vacancies in a cadre of two hundred and reserves none of them, reasoning that four per cent of three rounds to nothing, has repeated the struck-down error in miniature. The four per cent is a property of the cadre, worked through a roster register maintained over successive recruitment cycles, so that the reserved point is reached and filled as vacancies arise across years rather than within a single advertisement.

The group-wise structure matters for a second reason. Reservation operates group by group, and a surplus of appointments of persons with benchmark disabilities in Group C does not discharge a shortfall in Group A. Senior cadres were the historical site of the deepest exclusion, because they were filled substantially by promotion and because the identification lists for them were thinnest, and the group-wise computation is what prevents an establishment from presenting aggregate figures that conceal the position at the levels where policy is made. Section 34(2) then governs the fate of the unfilled share. Where in any recruitment year a vacancy reserved under sub-section (1) cannot be filled for want of a person with benchmark disability or for any other sufficient reason, the vacancy is carried forward to the succeeding recruitment year. In that succeeding year, if a suitable person with benchmark disability is not available, the vacancy may first be filled by interchange among the five categories of sub-section (1), so that the seat moves across the disability groups rather than out of the quota. Only where no person with disability is available at all may the establishment appoint a person who is not a person with disability. A further proviso permits the interchange, with the prior approval of the appropriate Government, where a category cannot in the nature of the vacancies be employed at all. The sequence is the quota's memory: a shortfall is recorded, carried, offered across the categories, and only then released, and an establishment that fills a reserved vacancy with a general candidate in the first cycle, without the carry-forward and the interchange having run, has acted against the text however respectable its recruitment calendar looks.

Two consequences follow for anyone auditing a recruitment. The first is documentary: the roster register and the carry-forward record are the establishment's own account of its compliance, they are maintained under the record-keeping duty discussed below, and they are the first documents a Commissioner requisitions and the first documents a candidate's representation should demand. An establishment that cannot produce its register has not merely mislaid a file; it has failed the only verifiable form of the duty Section 34 imposes. The second is temporal: because the quota works across cycles, a candidate's remedy often lies not in disturbing a completed selection but in compelling the carry-forward, the interchange and the eventual filling of the backlog, which is the relief the special recruitment drives periodically announce and the relief a Commissioner's recommendation is well shaped to extract. Backlog is therefore best understood not as a statistic but as a ledger of specific unfilled reserved points, each traceable to a cadre, a group and a recruitment year, and each individually enforceable.

## Horizontal, not vertical: the doctrine that decides merit lists

The single most consequential technical point in this field, and the one recruiting bodies most often get wrong, is that reservation for persons with disabilities is horizontal rather than vertical. Vertical reservation, the Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe and Other Backward Classes compartments of the Article 16(4) scheme, divides the cadre into compartments that stand in addition to one another and are governed by the ceiling doctrine examined in [the analysis of Indra Sawhney and the fifty per cent ceiling](/2017/05/15/indra-sawhney-reservation-ceiling/). Horizontal reservation cuts across those compartments. A disability-reserved appointment is not a separate channel added to the vertical compartments; it is an adjustment made inside whichever vertical compartment the selected candidate belongs to. A candidate with a benchmark disability who is selected against the disability quota and who belongs to a Scheduled Caste is accommodated within the Scheduled Caste compartment; one who belongs to no reserved class is accommodated within the unreserved compartment. The total of vertical reservation is not increased by a single seat, which is why the Supreme Court held in National Federation of the Blind that the fifty per cent ceiling associated with Indra Sawhney has no application to disability reservation, and that the roster logic of R.K. Sabharwal, a case about vertical compartments, does not govern it either.

The working rule for preparing a merit list was stated by the Supreme Court in Rajesh Kumar Daria v Rajasthan Public Service Commission, reported at (2007) 8 SCC 785, and applied to later recruitment in Saurav Yadav v State of Uttar Pradesh, reported at (2021) 4 SCC 542. A candidate entitled to horizontal reservation who qualifies in the open category on merit, without the benefit of any relaxation attached to the quota, is adjusted against the open category and does not consume a horizontally reserved seat; the reserved seat then passes to the next eligible candidate of that horizontal category within the compartment. The error that generates litigation is the mirror image of that rule: the recruiting body adjusts the meritorious candidate against the quota, declares the quota exhausted, and leaves the next candidate with a disability unappointed although a seat the statute reserved was in substance given to a selection that never needed it. Saurav Yadav corrected precisely that adjustment in the context of women's horizontal reservation in a police recruitment, and its reasoning is the reasoning any tribunal will apply to the disability quota, because the mechanism is identical.

One development since the Act has widened the compartments across which the horizontal cut runs. The Constitution (One Hundred Third Amendment) Act added a vertical compartment of its own, the ten per cent reservation for economically weaker sections in the unreserved pool, and the disability quota operates within that compartment exactly as it operates within the others: a candidate with a benchmark disability selected against the quota who belongs to the economically weaker sections is accommodated inside that compartment, and one selected on open merit is adjusted against open merit. The mechanical rule does not change, but recruiting bodies must apply it across one more compartment than the National Federation of the Blind Court had before it, and merit lists prepared on templates that predate the amendment are a recurring source of the misadjustment errors the doctrine forbids.

Worked through a notional cadre, the mechanism is easier to see than in the abstract. Take a cadre of two hundred posts in a single group, filled in one recruitment, of which the four per cent yields eight reserved points, two for each of the first three clause shares and two for the joint share. The recruiting body prepares one merit list for the cadre. Suppose three candidates with benchmark disabilities appear in the list high enough to be selected against the unreserved compartment on their own marks, one of them belonging to a Scheduled Caste and two to no reserved class. Each of the three is adjusted against open merit within his or her vertical compartment, and none of the eight reserved points is consumed. The body then fills the eight points from the remaining candidates with benchmark disabilities in merit order: a candidate of the hearing impairment category who belongs to the Other Backward Classes takes a point but is accommodated inside the Other Backward Classes compartment; a candidate with locomotor disability from the unreserved class takes a point inside the unreserved compartment; and so on until the eight points are placed or the eligible candidates are exhausted. If only six eligible candidates remain after the open-merit selections, two points stay unfilled in that cycle: they are not surrendered to the general pool, they enter the carry-forward of Section 34(2), are offered by interchange across the categories in the succeeding cycle, and only then, if no person with disability is available, may be filled otherwise. Every figure in the vertical reservation of the cadre, meanwhile, is exactly what it would have been had Section 34 never applied, because horizontal reservation redistributes selections within compartments and adds nothing to their totals. That invariance is the whole doctrine, and a merit list that changes the vertical totals, or that consumes reserved points for open-merit selections, announces its own error on its face.

The horizontal adjustment is worked through a small notional cadre below so that the movement of each seat can be checked, step by step. The table that follows is the findable artifact of this article: it lists each accessibility obligation under the Act with its duty-holder, its time limit and the consequence of default, the computation professionals in this field are asked for repeatedly.

Take a cadre of one hundred posts, of which four are reserved for persons with benchmark disabilities. The recruiting body draws a single open merit list, ranking the candidates by merit across all vertical compartments; the one hundred posts are in play, and four of them must go to candidates with benchmark disabilities. If a candidate with a benchmark disability ranks within the open cut-off on merit alone, he is adjusted against the open category, inside his own vertical compartment; no disability seat is consumed by that adjustment, and all four reserved seats remain. The next four candidates with benchmark disabilities, taken in merit order, fall below the open cut-off; each is accommodated within the vertical compartment to which he or she belongs, the four disability seats are consumed, and the totals of the vertical compartments are unchanged.

The last movement in the list fixes the quota's character. If a fifth candidate with a benchmark disability outranks the fourth in merit, the seats go in merit order: he takes the fourth reserved seat, and the candidate displaced has no claim to a fifth seat. The quota is a floor of four, not a channel for all who qualify.

## Reservation in promotion: the proviso and the judgments

### On what does reservation in promotion depend under the 2016 Act?

Reservation in promotion under the 2016 Act depends, on the face of the statute, on instructions: the first proviso to Section 34(1) states that reservation in promotion shall be in accordance with such instructions as are issued by the appropriate Government from time to time.

Section 20(3) adds that no promotion shall be denied to a person merely on the ground of disability. Read together, the Act gives the employee with a benchmark disability a right not to be refused promotion for the disability itself, and leaves the reserved share in promotional posts to be worked out through the instructions each government issues for its establishments, against identified posts.

The proviso was a retreat from the position the courts had already reached under the 1995 Act, and the litigation since 2016 has been about how far that retreat goes. Under the predecessor statute the Supreme Court held in Rajeev Kumar Gupta v Union of India, reported at (2016) 13 SCC 153, that the bar on reservation in promotion associated with Indra Sawhney did not apply to persons with disabilities, because that bar rests on the Article 16(4) scheme for backward classes and disability reservation is sourced differently. In Siddaraju v State of Karnataka, decided on 14 January 2020, the Court confirmed that reservation in promotion for persons with disabilities follows once a post stands identified for the purpose: once a post is identified, it is to be reserved irrespective of the mode of recruitment by which it is filled. In State of Kerala v Leesamma Joseph, decided in 2021 under the 2016 Act itself, the Court rejected the argument that the proviso had extinguished the entitlement and directed the State to complete the identification of posts for promotion within three months, treating the employee's claim as enforceable rather than as a matter of administrative grace.

The administrative follow-through illustrates both the reach and the limits of the judge-made position. Acting on the line of authority from Rajeev Kumar Gupta through Siddaraju and Leesamma Joseph, the Union's Department of Personnel and Training issued instructions in 2022 providing reservation in promotion for persons with benchmark disabilities in Central Government services, extending the benefit in promotions up to the lowest rung of Group A. State governments have moved at uneven pace, some issuing corresponding instructions for their services and others leaving the field to be cleared case by case before tribunals. The unevenness is itself legally significant: because the proviso to Section 34(1) routes promotion reservation through instructions, the candidate's first inquiry in any promotion dispute is documentary, namely which instructions govern the establishment, whether the post stands identified for promotion, and whether the cadre's roster register records the reserved points that earlier promotions should have carried. Where instructions exist and are not applied, the case is an ordinary enforcement case. Where instructions do not exist, the Leesamma Joseph route, a direction compelling the government to complete identification and issue the machinery, is the realistic first relief, and the reported cases show courts granting it with deadlines rather than accepting administrative silence as an answer.

The position a candidate or an establishment should work from is therefore neither of the two slogans in circulation. Promotion reservation is not an unqualified statutory percentage that a tribunal can apply to any cadre without more, because the Act's own text routes it through government instructions and identified posts. Nor is it a dead letter awaiting instructions, because the courts have treated the entitlement as live, have read the proviso as a machinery provision rather than a negation, and have set deadlines for the identification on which it depends. An establishment that has issued no instructions and identified no posts is in the same posture as the establishment that under-identifies under Section 33: in default of a duty, and exposed to a Commissioner's inquiry and to directions in proceedings of the Leesamma Joseph kind.

## Higher education: the five per cent and the assessment that guards it

Section 32(1) requires all Government institutions of higher education and all other higher education institutions receiving aid from the Government to reserve not less than five per cent of seats for persons with benchmark disabilities, and Section 32(2) gives such persons an upper age relaxation of five years for admission. The reservation is again horizontal in character, and the regulatory framework within which universities and affiliated colleges operate it is the subject of [the guide to the UGC Act and the regulation of universities](/2015/02/15/ugc-act-1956-explained/). Two contrasts with the employment quota are worth fixing. The percentage is five, not four. And the section carries no internal division into category shares: the five per cent is a single pool for persons with benchmark disabilities, so the clause-by-clause litigation of Section 34 has no counterpart here, and disputes concentrate instead on the certificate, on whether the institution is within the section at all, and on the assessment of the candidate.

The five per cent was itself an enlargement. Section 39 of the 1995 Act had required all Government educational institutions and other educational institutions receiving aid from the Government to reserve not less than three per cent of seats for persons with disabilities. The 2016 Act raised the share to five per cent and fastened it to the benchmark definition, which the earlier statute did not possess in that form, so the claimant class is narrower and better defined while the share is larger. The coverage words deserve the same attention as the percentage: the section reaches institutions receiving aid from the Government, not government institutions alone, and an aided institution that treats the reservation as a condition of state universities only has misread its own obligation. Unaided private institutions stand outside Section 32, a boundary that admissions litigation periodically tests and that the section's text maintains; the entitlements of a student in such an institution run through the non-discrimination provisions, the accessibility duties owed by service providers and the inclusive education framework rather than through a reserved seat.

It is at the assessment stage that the entitlement is most often lost in practice. Admission to professional courses, medicine above all, is mediated by disability assessment boards applying guidelines that translate a percentage into an eligibility verdict. In Om Rathod v Director General of Health Services, decided on 25 October 2024 and reported at 2024 INSC 836, the Supreme Court set aside the rejection of a medical aspirant whose disability had been assessed at a high percentage, holding that the quantification recorded for the disability certificate could not by itself decide whether the candidate could pursue the course. The Court directed that the role of assessment boards be confined to a functional competency approach tailored to the course the candidate seeks to pursue, that boards positively record whether the disability will or will not come in the way of pursuing that course, and that the eligibility for reservation be evaluated on the quantification in the candidate's Unique Disability ID record rather than by re-quantification at the point of admission. Omkar Ramchandra Gond v Union of India, decided in the same period, applied the same functional approach. A companion decision in the medical admissions line, arising on a prescription that a candidate must have both hands intact, described that prescription as ableist and without place in a statutory regulation, and directed fresh guidelines.

The remedial lesson generalises beyond medicine. An assessment that states a percentage and a conclusion, without reasons connecting the candidate's functional capacity to the demands of the identified post or course, is vulnerable in exactly the way the Om Rathod board's assessment was vulnerable. A candidate refused at this stage should ask for the board's recorded reasons, should test them against the functional demands of the specific course or post rather than against disability in the abstract, and should use the appellate route against the certificate where the quantification itself is wrong. The board is an instrument for matching capacity to a defined activity; the moment it operates as a gate against a class, it exceeds the function the case law assigns it.

## Free education to eighteen: the right that runs beside the quota

Before the higher education quota is reached, Section 31 confers the school-level right in terms that admit no percentage at all: notwithstanding anything contained in the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009, every child with benchmark disability between the ages of six and eighteen shall have the right to free education in a neighbourhood school, or in a special school, of his choice. Each element of the sentence does work. The non-obstante clause lifts the right out of the 2009 Act's framework, which is why the entitlement runs to eighteen rather than stopping at fourteen where the general statute stops. The choice between a neighbourhood school and a special school belongs to the child and the family, not to the admitting authority, so a neighbourhood school that redirects a child to a distant special school on the ground that it is better equipped has exercised a choice the Act gives to someone else. And the right is to free education in that school, which reaches fees and the charges that operate as fees under another name.

The supporting duties stand in Sections 16 and 17. Section 16 obliges the appropriate Government and local authorities to endeavour that educational institutions funded or recognised by them provide inclusive education, through measures that include admission without discrimination, accessible buildings and reasonable accommodation, individualised support, education in appropriate languages and modes of communication, early detection of learning disabilities, and monitoring of participation and progress. Section 17 lists the specific measures governments and local authorities are to take: periodic surveys to identify children with disabilities, teacher training institutions and the training of professionals, resource centres, the promotion of Braille, sign language and other modes and formats of communication, the supply of free books, learning materials and assistive devices to children with benchmark disabilities until they complete eighteen years, scholarships, curricular and examination modifications including extra time and scribes, and promotion of research. The remedial significance of the list is that it converts the school-level complaint from a plea into an audit. A parent complaining to a State Commissioner about a school that provides no assistive device, no trained teacher and no examination accommodation is not asking for charity; the parent is reading Section 17 back to the authority that bears it, item by item, and asking which items have been discharged.

Enforcement at this level runs principally through the State Commissioner, whose inquiry jurisdiction covers the State's education authorities, and through the writ courts for the individual admission refused in breach of Section 31. The documented failure pattern is not usually an outright refusal of admission, which the Act makes indefensible, but the slow version: admission granted and support withheld, so that the child is enrolled on paper and excluded in the classroom. That pattern is precisely what the Section 16 and 17 duties are drafted to catch, and a complaint framed around those sections, with the school's own position recorded, is materially stronger than a complaint framed as a general grievance about attitude.

## Protection inside service: the employee who acquires a disability

The entitlements so far described belong to candidates at the gate. Section 20 protects the person already inside. Section 20(1) forbids a Government establishment to discriminate against any person with disability in any matter relating to employment, subject to an exemption the appropriate Government may notify having regard to the type of work. Section 20(2) obliges every Government establishment to provide reasonable accommodation and an appropriate barrier-free and conducive environment to employees with disability. Section 20(3), already noticed, forbids denial of promotion merely on the ground of disability. Section 20(4) then states the protection that employees consistently say they did not know existed: no Government establishment shall dispense with, or reduce in rank, an employee who acquires a disability during his or her service. The first proviso requires that an employee who, after acquiring the disability, is not suitable for the post held be shifted to some other post with the same pay scale and service benefits. The second proviso requires that if it is not possible to adjust the employee against any post, the employee be kept on a supernumerary post until a suitable post is available or the age of superannuation is reached, whichever is earlier. Section 20(5) empowers the appropriate Government to frame policies for the posting and transfer of employees with disabilities.

The architecture of Section 20(4) is a sequence, and each step must be exhausted before the next is reached. Retention in the same post, with reasonable accommodation under Section 20(2), is the starting point, because unsuitability must be shown against the post as accommodated, not against the post as the establishment happens to have organised it. Shifting follows, at protected pay and benefits. Supernumerary status is the last resort, and it is a status with salary, not a corridor to exit: the employee remains in service, paid, until a suitable post is found or superannuation arrives. Termination on medical grounds that skips the sequence is the standard fact pattern in the successful challenges. In Ravinder Kumar Dhariwal v Union of India, decided in December 2022, the Supreme Court dealt with a Central Reserve Police Force constable whose services had been terminated after he developed a mental illness, and set the termination aside, holding that the employer's duty was to assess and accommodate rather than to treat the onset of disability as the end of the employment. The case is the modern restatement of a protection that has stood in Indian disability law since Section 47 of the 1995 Act, and its facts show why the protection matters most in uniformed and industrial employment, where medical boards are part of the administrative furniture and their verdicts are too easily treated as self-executing.

The supernumerary device deserves one further word, because establishments misdescribe it to employees as a concession. It is not a favour and it is not a suspension of the employment; it is a statutory holding position that preserves the employee's service, pay and seniority prospects while the establishment does the work of finding or creating the suitable post it was obliged to find. An employee offered retirement benefits in place of the sequence should recognise the substitution for what it is: a request to surrender Section 20(4) for a settlement, to be accepted, if at all, with open eyes and independent advice.

## The equal opportunity policy: the establishment's own promises, on record

Two provisions that rarely headline any account of the Act do much of its quiet work. Section 21(1) requires every establishment to notify an equal opportunity policy detailing the measures proposed to be taken by it in pursuance of the provisions of the chapter on skill development and employment. Section 21(2) requires every Government establishment to register a copy of its policy with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner. Section 22(1) requires every establishment to maintain records of the persons with disabilities employed, the facilities provided to them and the schemes implemented for their benefit, and Section 22(2) opens those records to inspection by the Commissioners. Read together, the two provisions make the establishment write down, in advance and in a document the Commissioner holds, what it does for employees with disabilities: the posts it has identified, the facilities it provides, the arrangements it makes. In a dispute, that document is the standard against which the establishment's conduct is measured, and a departure from the establishment's own notified policy is far harder to defend than a departure from a complainant's expectations.

The policy is also the point at which several other duties become administrable. The identification of posts under Section 33, the facilities and amenities the establishment provides, the preference and arrangements it makes in posting and transfer under Section 20(5), the training it offers and the grievance machinery it maintains all belong in the one document a new employee with a disability can demand on joining and a Commissioner can demand at any time. Establishments that have notified a policy and ignore it, and establishments that have notified none at all, are in the same practical position once a complaint is filed: the Commissioner inquires into the gap between the document and the conduct, or into the absence of the document, and the recommendation that follows writes itself. Employees and candidates rarely ask for the equal opportunity policy; the professionals who act in this field ask for it first, because it converts an argument about what the law requires into an examination of what the establishment has already conceded.

## Reasonable accommodation: the duty that does the daily work

If reservation is the Act's arithmetic, reasonable accommodation is its daily practice, and it is the concept on which most individual victories in this field are won. Section 2(y) defines it as necessary and appropriate modification and adjustments, not imposing a disproportionate or undue burden in a particular case, to ensure that persons with disabilities can enjoy or exercise their rights equally with others. The definition carries its own limit inside it: the adjustment must be necessary and appropriate to the case, and it must not impose a disproportionate or undue burden on the duty-holder. Within those bounds the duty is not discretionary. Section 3(5) obliges the appropriate Government to take necessary steps to ensure reasonable accommodation, Section 16 carries it into education, Section 20(2) carries it into the government workplace, and the Supreme Court in Vikash Kumar held that reasonable accommodation is a substantive component of the equality guarantee, so that its denial, without the justification the statute allows, is discrimination itself rather than a lesser species of unfairness.

The concept repays precision because both sides misuse it. Claimants sometimes present it as an entitlement to the outcome, a pass, a posting, a promotion, whereas what it entitles them to is the adjustment that lets them compete or work on an equal footing: the scribe, the assistive device, the accessible format, the adjusted workstation, the modified duty roster that keeps the essential functions and sheds the incidental ones. Duty-holders misuse it in the mirror image, treating any adjustment as a concession to be weighed against convenience, whereas the burden the definition speaks of is disproportionate or undue burden, a threshold of real cost or real disruption, not of administrative habit. The case-by-case structure of the definition is deliberate: what is reasonable for a clerk's workstation differs from what is reasonable for a train driver's cab, and the inquiry is always into the particular post, the particular impairment and the particular adjustment proposed. That is why blanket rules fare so badly in this field when they reach the courts. A rule that no candidate below the benchmark line may have a scribe decides thousands of cases without inquiring into any of them; a rule that every post in a cadre is unsuitable for a whole category of disability does the same. The statute's design pushes in the opposite direction, toward the individualised assessment, and the litigation record from Vikash Kumar to Om Rathod is the record of that design prevailing over administrative preference for the blanket.

For the practitioner the consequence is a discipline of specification. A request for reasonable accommodation should name the adjustment, connect it to the impairment, show that it leaves the essential functions of the post or the integrity of the examination intact, and answer the burden question before it is raised. A refusal should be tested for the opposite qualities: a refusal that engages with the specific adjustment and demonstrates a disproportionate burden is the kind the statute contemplates; a refusal that recites a rule, a threshold or a practice is the kind the courts have repeatedly set aside. The scribe litigation, which follows, is the most fully worked example of that discipline in action.

## The scribe question: accommodation is not confined to the benchmark line

Examinations are where the two keys described at the start of this article collide in public. A reserved seat in an examination-based recruitment follows the benchmark certificate. The facilities that make the examination itself accessible follow need. In Vikash Kumar v Union Public Service Commission, reported at (2021) 5 SCC 370, the Supreme Court considered a candidate with writer's cramp, a neurological condition that makes sustained writing painful and slow, who had been refused a scribe for the Civil Services Examination because he was not a person with benchmark disability. The Court held that the facility of a scribe is an instance of reasonable accommodation under the Act, that reasonable accommodation is a component of the guarantee against discrimination, and that confining the facility to candidates with benchmark disabilities was inconsistent with the Act. In Avni Prakash v National Testing Agency, decided in 2021, the Court applied the same reasoning to compensatory time in a national entrance examination. In Gulshan Kumar v Institute of Banking Personnel Selection, decided on 3 February 2025 and reported at 2025 INSC 142, a bench of Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan JJ. returned to the scribe guidelines governing banking examinations, found the restrictions placed on candidates below the benchmark line discriminatory, and directed the revision of the governing guidelines.

The Gulshan Kumar litigation shows the next layer of the problem, which is not the threshold but the conditions. Examining bodies that accepted the scribe in principle surrounded the facility with restrictions on who the scribe could be, tying the scribe's educational qualifications and stream to the candidate's and pressing candidates toward scribes arranged through the examining body rather than a scribe of the candidate's own choosing. Restrictions of that kind recreate, inside the accommodation, the exclusion the accommodation exists to remove: a candidate who must find, from an approved pool and at examination notice, a stranger literate enough to write a banking paper and unqualified enough to satisfy the rule has been given a facility in form and a barrier in substance. The bench's direction that the governing guidelines be revised proceeds from the Vikash Kumar premise, that the facility is reasonable accommodation, from which it follows that conditions on its exercise must themselves be justified as necessary for the integrity of the examination rather than inherited from administrative caution. Examining bodies retain a real interest in preventing impersonation and collusion, and verification of the scribe's identity and a declaration of the relationship answer it; a rule about the scribe's marks in an unrelated examination does not.

The practical rule for candidates is settled. A candidate whose disability falls below forty per cent, or whose condition is not captured by a certificate in the form the examining body expects, may still claim a scribe, compensatory time and other examination accommodations as reasonable accommodation, and a refusal that rests only on the benchmark threshold applies a test the Supreme Court has disapproved three times. The claim should be made in writing, in advance, with the medical material attached and the specific facility identified, because examining bodies are entitled to organise the accommodation and a request first raised in the examination hall will usually fail on logistics rather than on law. The written request and the written refusal, where one is issued, are also the record on which any later challenge stands, which is a theme the grievance discussion below develops in full.

## The other reservations: land, housing and poverty alleviation schemes

Section 34 is the famous reservation, but it is not the only one the Act creates, and the claimant who stops reading at the employment quota leaves entitlements unclaimed. Section 37 directs the appropriate Government and the local authorities, subject to such rules as they may make in that behalf, to make provisions for five per cent reservation in the allotment of agricultural land and housing in all relevant governmental schemes and development programmes, with appropriate priority to women with benchmark disabilities; for five per cent reservation in all poverty alleviation and development schemes, again with appropriate priority to women with benchmark disabilities; and for five per cent reservation in the allotment of land at a concessional rate where the land is used for promoting the education or livelihood of persons with benchmark disabilities. The structure of the section repays attention. The share, five per cent, is larger than the employment share. The fields it covers, land, housing and anti-poverty programmes, are the fields in which a household's material base is decided, particularly in rural India where a housing plot or an agricultural allotment is the family's principal asset. And the priority direction for women with benchmark disabilities recognises, within the text of the reservation itself, that disability and gender compound one another in the queue for public goods.

The qualifying phrase, subject to rules the government may make, is where the entitlement softens, and the honest account is that Section 37 operates at the speed of the schemes it rides on. Where a housing or allotment scheme incorporates the reservation in its guidelines, the claim is administrative and strong: the applicant produces the certificate, the scheme's own reservation applies, and a denial is a complaint the State Commissioner can pursue with the scheme authority. Where the scheme is silent, the claimant must first establish that the section's direction has been carried into the scheme, which is a slower argument about the government's duty to make the provision rather than about the applicant's place in a queue. The practical lesson mirrors the one Section 33 teaches in employment: the reservation that exists on paper becomes real at the point where it is written into the operative instrument, the scheme guidelines here corresponding to the identification list there, and the audit a claimant or a Commissioner performs is the same audit in both fields, comparing the Act's percentage with the instrument's text before comparing the instrument's text with the allotment list. Welfare reservations generate less litigation than employment reservations because the sums per claimant are smaller and the claimants are poorer, not because the legal structure is weaker, and the Commissioners' scheme-monitoring function under Sections 75 and 80 is the forum designed for exactly this class of quiet shortfall.

## The special recruitment drive: the backlog's political life

Every few years the backlog acquires a political season, and a government announces a special recruitment drive to fill the vacancies reserved for persons with disabilities that ordinary cycles have left unfilled. The Union Government mounted a widely publicised drive of this kind in the middle of the last decade across its departments and public sector undertakings, and State governments have run their own versions, usually after a Commissioner's report or a court direction has put the arrears on record. The drive is an administrative instrument, not a statutory one: the Act nowhere provides for it, and its legal content is exhausted by Section 34(2). What the drive does is concentrate the ordinary duty in time, directing establishments to compute their reserved shortfall from the roster registers, advertise against the backlog specifically, and report fills against a deadline. Done properly, it is the carry-forward machinery operated at scale. Done as announcement without computation, it produces the familiar cycle in which the drive reports appointments against a backlog figure that was never audited, the unaudited remainder rolls forward, and the next drive begins from a number nobody can reconstruct.

The claimant's interest in the drive is concrete rather than ceremonial. A candidate whose category point stands in the backlog should ensure that the claim is on record before the drive's computation closes, because drives work from registers, and a reserved point omitted from the register at computation stage tends to stay omitted. The Commissioners' role in the cycle is the standing corrective: their inquiries generate the verified arrears figures that drives then adopt, and a complaint that establishes a specific unfilled point, in a specific cadre, carried through specific recruitment years, is the raw material from which both drives and directions are made. Courts have likewise treated the accumulated backlog as enforceable arrears rather than as a policy aspiration, directing governments in several States to complete identification and fill the reserved shortfall within stated periods. The honest assessment of the instrument is that it works when it is the enforcement of a ledger and fails when it is a substitute for one, and the difference is visible in whether the drive publishes its computation establishment by establishment or only its total.

## The grievance route: from the establishment's officer to the Special Court

The Act builds the complaint route in ascending order, and using it in that order is usually the fastest way to a remedy, because each level creates the record the next level acts on.

The first level is inside the establishment. Section 23 requires every Government establishment to appoint a Grievance Redressal Officer and to inform the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner of the appointment. Any person aggrieved by non-compliance with the provisions of Section 20 may file a complaint with that officer, who must investigate it and take the matter up with the establishment for corrective action. The officer must maintain a register of complaints in the prescribed manner, and every complaint must be inquired into within two weeks of its registration. The two-week figure is the only hard timeline at this level, and it is worth invoking expressly in the complaint itself, because a complaint that cites the section and the timeline is harder to leave in a tray. The Grievance Redressal Officer route is framed around Section 20 complaints, which cover discrimination in employment, denial of reasonable accommodation at work, denial of promotion on the ground of disability, and the acquired disability protections. Reservation disputes about a recruitment are ordinarily taken up through the recruiting body's own representation channels and then to the Commissioner, and a candidate should use both without waiting for one to exhaust before starting the other where selection timelines are short.

The second level is the Commissioner. The Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities, appointed under Section 74, and the State Commissioners, appointed under Section 79, hold the inquiry functions in Sections 75 and 80 respectively. Each may inquire, on complaint or of his own motion, into deprivation of the rights of persons with disabilities and the safeguards available to them, in matters for which the Central Government or the State Government, as the case may be, is the appropriate Government, and take the matter up with the authorities for corrective action. Each is armed by Sections 77 and 82 with the powers of a civil court while trying a suit: summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, requiring the discovery and production of documents, requisitioning public records, receiving evidence on affidavit and issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses. Proceedings before them are judicial proceedings within the meaning of the penal provisions the Act names. The recommendation that follows is not a bare request. Sections 76 and 81 oblige the authority to which a recommendation is made to take necessary action on it and to inform the Commissioner of the action taken within three months of receiving it, or, if the authority does not accept the recommendation, to convey its reasons for non-acceptance to the Commissioner within the same three months and to inform the aggrieved person. A Commissioner cannot execute a decree, and a determined authority can answer with reasons; but an authority that ignores a recommendation entirely is in breach of a statutory duty with a date on it, and that breach is powerful material in writ proceedings.

The third level is prosecution and the courts. Offences under the Act are tried by a Special Court: Section 84 requires the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to specify a Court of Session for each district as the Special Court for offences under the Act, and Section 85 provides for a Special Public Prosecutor. The penal provisions that matter to this article are Sections 89 to 93. Section 89 punishes contravention of any provision of the Act or of any rule made under it with a fine that may extend to ten thousand rupees for a first contravention, and for a subsequent contravention with a fine that shall not be less than fifty thousand rupees and may extend to five lakh rupees. Section 91 punishes fraudulently availing a benefit meant for persons with benchmark disabilities with imprisonment up to two years, or a fine up to one lakh rupees, or both. Section 92 punishes the listed offences of atrocity, including intentionally insulting or intimidating with intent to humiliate a person with disability in public view, with imprisonment for a term not less than six months and up to five years, and fine. Section 93 punishes failure to furnish information or documents required under the Act, with fine up to twenty-five thousand rupees for each offence and a further daily fine for a continuing default. Section 94 bars cognizance of an offence by an employee of the appropriate Government without previous sanction or an authorised complaint, a gate that complainants against official default should know exists. Alongside prosecution, the constitutional route remains: recruitment and service grievances of government employees travel to the administrative tribunals and the High Courts, and the writ jurisdiction is where the merit-list errors, identification defaults and unlawful terminations in the cases discussed in this article were in fact corrected.

Three working rules emerge from the structure. Keep the paper. The complaint a Commissioner can act on is the one supported by the certificate, the advertisement, the merit list, the refusal and the correspondence, kept in order and in readable form, and a reader assembling that file for a complaint does the assembly once and uses it at every level; the schedules, lists and replies that offices exchange in document form can be opened and checked as they arrive through [a document viewer that renders office files without the originating software](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which matters when the establishment's answer arrives as a spreadsheet of cadre calculations rather than as a letter. Mind the sequence without worshipping it: the Act does not make the Grievance Redressal Officer a jurisdictional bar to the Commissioner or the writ court, and where a selection is about to close or a termination has already taken effect, parallel remedies are the norm in this field. And treat every timeline in the Act as a lever: the two weeks of Section 23, the three months of Sections 76 and 81, and the three-year review of Section 33 are the dates against which default is measured, and a complaint written around those dates frames itself. The position stated in this article is general information about the law and its remedies; it is not advice on an individual case, and a person whose selection, promotion or service is immediately at stake should take advice on the facts and the limitation periods that govern the specific forum, because those periods are unforgiving.

Forum choice within the court route follows the ordinary map of Indian service law, and getting it right at the start saves the year that a wrong forum costs. A Central Government employee's service grievance, a denied promotion, a termination in breach of Section 20(4), belongs in the first instance to the Central Administrative Tribunal, whose benches decide service matters for the Union's establishments, with the High Court's writ jurisdiction above it. State Government employees go to the State Administrative Tribunal where one exists, or directly to the High Court. A candidate who is not yet an employee, challenging a merit list or a refusal of the quota, is not in service at all and moves the High Court under its writ jurisdiction, which is where the merit-list and identification cases discussed in this article were decided. Admission grievances against universities and professional regulators travel the same writ route. The Commissioner's jurisdiction overlaps all of these without displacing any, which is by design: the Act intends the cheap, fast, recommendatory forum to run alongside the expensive, slow, binding one, and a claimant with a live selection or a live termination is usually best served by setting both in motion, the Commissioner for the institutional record and the court for the enforceable order.

The common mistakes in this field recur with a regularity that suggests they are structural rather than accidental. Candidates claim the quota without the certificate, or with a certificate for a disability the Schedule does not specify. Recruiting bodies compute the four per cent on advertised vacancies instead of cadre strength, adjust meritorious candidates against the quota instead of against open merit, and treat unidentification of posts as an answer rather than a default. Employees accept medical retirement without demanding the Section 20(4) sequence in writing. Institutions treat the accessibility guidelines as the standard and the completion certificate as a formality, which was an understandable error before November 2024 and is not one after it. That judgment, and the twenty years of proceedings behind it, is where this article turns next.

## The Commissioners in action: reports, suo motu inquiries and the institutional record

The Commissioner offices are the Act's standing memory, and their wider functions matter to individual claimants because those functions generate the record on which individual cases are later won. Beyond complaint inquiry, Sections 75 and 80 charge the Chief Commissioner and the State Commissioners with reviewing the safeguards provided under the Act and the laws for the time being in force for the protection of the rights of persons with disabilities, and recommending measures for their effective implementation; monitoring the implementation of the Act and of schemes and programmes meant for persons with disabilities; reviewing the factors that inhibit the enjoyment of rights and recommending remedial action; and undertaking and promoting research in the field. Each Commissioner submits annual and special reports to the government that appointed him, and the reports are laid before the legislature with a memorandum of the action taken on the recommendations and the reasons for any non-acceptance. The annual report cycle is the one forum in which a department must answer, in public and in writing, for the state of its roster registers, its identification lists, its accessibility action plan and its complaint disposal, and practitioners in this field read those reports the way litigators read a weak opponent's affidavit: the admissions are already in them.

The suo motu power deserves emphasis because it changes the economics of enforcement. A Commissioner need not wait for a complainant with the stamina to litigate; the office may inquire of its own motion into deprivation of rights, and has used that power on patterns that individual complaints reveal only in fragments, such as a recruiting body's systematic misadjustment of horizontal reservation, a State's failure to appoint Commissioners' own counterparts at the district interface, or a transport undertaking's inaccessible fleet procurement. Media reports and representations from disability organisations feed this jurisdiction, and the July 2026 direction noticed later in this article, compelling States to fill vacant State Commissioner posts under Section 79, shows the Supreme Court treating the offices themselves as infrastructure whose vacancy is a rights violation in waiting. The vacancy point is not pedantry. A State that leaves its Commissioner post empty, or fills it as an additional charge for an officer with a full portfolio elsewhere, has disabled the inquiry, the recommendation and the reporting machinery at a stroke, and the pattern of long vacancies in several States is one of the documented reasons the Act's remedies underperform its text. The claimant in such a State is pushed prematurely to the writ court, at a cost in time and money that the Act's design was meant to spare him.

## What relief actually looks like, and where it stops

A remedies article owes the reader a plain account of outcomes, so this section states what successful claimants in this field in fact obtain, and what they do not. Before a Commissioner, the characteristic relief is a recommendation that the authority appoint the complainant against the backlog, complete the identification of posts, restore the employee to the supernumerary protection of Section 20(4), or carry out the accessibility work within a stated period, coupled with the three-month report the Act requires. Authorities comply with such recommendations in a substantial run of cases, for a practical reason: the recommendation is on record, the Commissioner's office follows it up, and defiance has to be explained in writing to a statutory authority and to the complainant. Before a tribunal or a High Court, the relief is harder edged: a direction to prepare the merit list afresh on the correct horizontal method, to consider the candidate against the carried-forward vacancy, to set aside a termination that skipped the Section 20(4) sequence, or to complete identification within a deadline of the Leesamma Joseph kind. Courts in the reported cases have been readier to direct the machinery than to appoint by judgment, and a claimant who asks for the direction the court habitually grants is usually better placed than one who asks the court to perform the administration itself.

The limits deserve equal plainness. A Commissioner cannot execute, cannot attach property, and cannot punish contempt; a recommendation ignored with reasons given within three months is lawful defiance, and the complainant's next step is the writ court, with the correspondence as the record. Prosecution under Sections 89 to 93 requires the Special Court route and, against government employees, the previous sanction Section 94 prescribes, and prosecutions for the general contravention penalty have been rare enough that the fine in Section 89 functions in practice more as a measure of the duty's seriousness in negotiation and in the Rajive Raturi supervision than as a regularly imposed sentence. Delay is the standing tax on every forum in this field: a recruitment concluded while a representation pends will rarely be unwound for one candidate, which is why the interim protection of the claim, a representation filed before the selection closes and a challenge brought while the list is alive, matters more than the elegance of the final argument. Money enters the remedial picture principally through service-law restoration rather than through damages in the abstract. An employee whose termination is set aside for breach of Section 20(4) is restored to service, and restoration carries the service consequences that follow the finding: continuity, the pay of the post to which the employee is shifted or restored, and the arrears question decided on the ordinary principles the service courts apply, in which a termination held unlawful from the outset places the employee materially better than one merely softened in discretion. A candidate wrongly denied a reserved appointment who succeeds after the selection has closed faces the harder road the cases acknowledge, and courts have met it with a spectrum of orders, from appointment against the carried-forward vacancy in the next cycle to notional seniority where an appointment is directed against a subsisting vacancy, the choice turning on how much of the delay the administration's own default explains. The through-line is that relief in this field is restorative and machinery-directed rather than compensatory in the tort sense, and expectations set on that understanding are rarely disappointed by the forums that deliver it.

None of this diminishes the statutory scheme; it locates where its strength lies, in dated duties, written records and authorities obliged to answer, and it tells the claimant to build the case the scheme is designed to reward.

## Accessibility as a duty with dates attached

The accessibility scheme of the Act is built as a chain, and the 2024 judgment is unintelligible until the chain is laid out link by link. Section 40 obliges the Central Government, in consultation with the Chief Commissioner, to formulate rules for persons with disabilities laying down the standards of accessibility for the physical environment, transportation, information and communication, including appropriate technologies and systems, and other facilities and services provided to the public in urban and rural areas. Sections 41, 42 and 43 add the programme duties: facilities at bus stops, railway stations and airports conforming to the accessibility standards; accessible content and electronic media with audio description, sign language interpretation and close captioning; universally designed consumer goods. Section 44 then gives the standards their teeth at the point of construction: no establishment shall be granted permission to build any structure if the building plan does not adhere to the rules formulated under Section 40, and no establishment shall be issued a certificate of completion or be allowed to take occupation of a building unless it has adhered to those rules.

Sections 45 and 46 attach the dates. Section 45(1) requires all existing public buildings to be made accessible in accordance with the rules within a period not exceeding five years from the date of notification of the rules, with a proviso empowering the Central Government to grant extension of time to the States on a case to case basis depending on preparedness and related parameters. Section 45(2) requires the appropriate Government and local authorities to formulate and publish an action plan based on prioritisation, covering buildings and spaces providing essential services, with primary health centres, civil hospitals, schools, railway stations and bus stops named. Section 46 requires service providers, whether Government or private, to provide services in accordance with the accessibility rules within two years from the date of notification of the rules, with a proviso for extension of time for a category of services in consultation with the Chief Commissioner. The definition of a public building in Section 2(w) is deliberately wide: a Government or private building used or accessed by the public at large, including buildings used for education, workplaces, commerce, health services, judicial forums, railway stations, bus termini, airports and waterways. A private mall, a private hospital and a private college are inside the scheme, not at its edge.

The rules under Section 40 were notified on 15 June 2017 as the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules, 2017, so the five-year period for existing public buildings expired in June 2022 and the two-year period for service providers in June 2019, subject to any extension granted under the provisos. Rule 15 of those Rules was the operative standard-setting provision, and its method was incorporation: it prescribed, for each sector, the guidelines issued by the responsible ministry or authority, including the Harmonised Guidelines for universal accessibility issued for the built environment. Those guidelines were written, in large part, in the language of recommendation. Standards "should" be followed; features were "desirable" or "recommended". The Accessible India Campaign, launched on 3 December 2015, ran alongside as a programme of targets for buildings, transport and the information ecosystem. The combined effect, for seven years, was that the document a building authority, an airport operator or a website owner actually consulted told them what good practice looked like, while the Act that stood behind the document had said, in mandatory terms and with a penalty in Section 89, what the law required. Nobody reading only the guidelines would have known that a completion certificate could lawfully be withheld.

## Transport, information and goods: the duties beyond buildings

Buildings dominate the accessibility debate because Section 44 gives them a gate, but the Act's scheme is wider, and Sections 41 to 43 state the parts of it that govern movement and information. Section 41 requires the appropriate Government to take suitable measures to provide facilities for persons with disabilities at bus stops, railway stations and airports conforming to the accessibility standards for parking spaces, toilets, ticketing counters and ticketing machines; to provide access to all modes of transport conforming to design standards, including retrofitting old modes of transport wherever technically feasible and within the limits of economic capacity; and to provide accessible roads to address the mobility needs of persons with disabilities. Section 42 requires measures to ensure that content available in audio, print and electronic media is in accessible format, that persons with disabilities have access to electronic media through audio description, sign language interpretation and close captioning, and that electronic goods and equipment meant for everyday use are available in universal design. Section 43 requires measures to promote the development, production and distribution of universally designed consumer products and accessories for general use. The duties are framed as obligations on governments rather than as direct commands to operators, which is why their enforcement has run through the standards under Section 40, through procurement and operating conditions, and through the supervision in the Rajive Raturi proceedings, where transport carriers, railway stations and airports were among the eleven action points of the 2017 judgment.

The digital limb of the scheme has become its most active front. Government websites, examination portals, ticketing systems and the application forms through which every entitlement in this article is claimed are services within Section 46 and information systems within Section 40, and an inaccessible portal is not a neutral inconvenience: it is the gate to the quota, the certificate and the complaint, closed to the person the Act exists to serve. It is consistent with that logic that the first of the sectoral mandatory-rule exercises taken up after the 2024 judgment, through the public consultation of June 2025, concerned accessibility standards for information and communication technology products and services. The older programme architecture points the same way: the Accessible India Campaign organised its targets under three verticals, the built environment, the transportation ecosystem and the information and communication technology ecosystem, treating the digital estate as a co-equal field of the duty rather than an afterthought. A service provider defending an inaccessible website by pointing to its ramped office has answered a question the Act does not ask; the Act asks about the service, and the service is the portal.

## Rajive Raturi: the rule that undid the Act

The proceedings that exposed this began in 2005, when Rajive Raturi, a visually impaired person working with a human rights organisation, filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court seeking directions for meaningful access to public spaces, roads, public transport and other facilities. The petition was filed under the 1995 Act; by the time it produced its first major judgment, the 2016 Act had replaced that statute. On 15 December 2017, in a judgment delivered through A.K. Sikri J., the Court identified eleven action points for compliance, drawn from the Act and the Accessible India Campaign and covering government buildings, railways, airports, the information and communication ecosystem and transport carriers, and directed the Union, the States and the Union territories to file compliance affidavits, with the case to be listed again after three months. Most States and Union territories did not file within the period. Orders of 25 July 2018 and 15 January 2019 record affidavits that were missing, out of format or incomplete, and a Court moving from direction to exasperation. In the course of that supervision the Court commissioned the Centre for Disability Studies at the NALSAR University of Law to assess the position on the ground and recommend steps, and the Centre's report, titled "Finding Sizes for All: A Report on the Status of the Right to Accessibility in India", was submitted to the Court in 2024.

On 8 November 2024, a bench of the Chief Justice, Dr D.Y. Chandrachud, sitting with J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ., delivered the judgment that reorganised the field. The Court held that several of the guidelines prescribed through Rule 15 were recommendatory guidelines under the garb of mandatory rules, and that Rule 15(1) was accordingly ultra vires the scheme and legislative intent of the Act, which creates a mechanism for mandatory compliance: a minimum floor of accessibility, the Court said, cannot be left to the altar of progressive realisation. The reasoning is a straight application of the delegated legislation doctrine: a rule must conform to its parent Act, and where the Act imposes a duty reinforced by time limits in Sections 45 and 46 and by the certificate consequences in Section 44 and the penalty in Section 89, a rule that restates the duty as advice has not implemented the Act but contradicted it. The Court directed the Union Government to delineate mandatory rules under Section 40 within three months of the judgment, an exercise that could involve segregating the non-negotiable rules from the expansive guidelines already prescribed, to be carried out in consultation with all stakeholders and with the Centre for Disability Studies involved in the process. It clarified that progressive compliance with the existing standards and the targets of the Accessible India Campaign must continue in the meantime, and directed that once the mandatory rules were prescribed, the Union, the States and the Union territories ensure that the consequences in Sections 44, 45, 46 and 89, including withholding completion certificates and imposing fines, are implemented for non-compliance. The petitions were adjourned for compliance reporting, and the proceedings remain under the Court's supervision, in the manner of the continuing mandamus that the environmental docket made familiar and that [the survey of environmental jurisprudence in the Supreme Court](/2014/12/15/environmental-jurisprudence-supreme-court/) traces in its own field.

Two features of the road from 2017 to 2024 explain why the eventual holding took the form it did. The first is the character of the 2017 judgment itself. The Sikri bench did not content itself with a declaration; it translated the statute and the campaign into eleven enumerated action points and set the governments to filing compliance affidavits against them, which converted an abstract duty into a series of answerable questions about named buildings, named railway stations, named airports, the information and communication ecosystem and road transport. When the affidavits came in late, incomplete or not at all, the Court's orders of July 2018 and January 2019 recorded the default government by government. Supervision of that kind does something litigation rarely manages: it builds, order by order, an official record of non-compliance that no party later has to prove. The second feature is the evidentiary turn the NALSAR assessment represented. Rather than argue about the standard in the abstract, the Court commissioned a university centre to go and look: to examine public buildings, transport and digital services against the norms and report what a wheelchair user, a blind traveller or a deaf citizen in fact encounters. The report's title, "Finding Sizes for All", carried the argument in miniature, that a built environment sized for only some of its users is not a neutral inheritance but a continuing act of exclusion. When the matter returned to the bench in 2024, the gap the report documented was not between the governments and an ideal; it was between the governments and the text of Rule 15's own incorporated guidelines, let alone the Act behind them. A court confronted with that record, in proceedings it had supervised for seven years, was never likely to stop at another round of directions to comply with guidelines. It went to the root, which was that the guidelines' legal status was the defect: so long as the operative standards were recommendatory, every default was arguable, every certificate a matter of grace, and every affidavit a progress report on a voluntary programme. The ultra vires holding is the sound of that argument ending.

### Why did Rule 15 of the 2017 Rules fail the Act it was made under?

Rule 15 failed because of its verbs. The Act commands: standards are to be laid down, buildings are not to be permitted or certified or occupied except in compliance, existing buildings are to be made accessible within five years, services within two, and contravention carries a fine.

Rule 15 answered that command by pointing to ministerial guidelines written to persuade rather than to bind, so the instrument that Section 40 required to create an enforceable floor instead created an aspirational ceiling. Delegated legislation that softens a statutory duty into a recommendation exceeds the power the parent section confers, and the Court held Rule 15(1) ultra vires the Act for exactly that reason, directing that non-negotiable mandatory rules be framed within three months.

The compliance position since the judgment must be stated with care, because it is still moving and because this article does not deal in invented progress. The three-month period the Court allowed expired on 8 February 2025 without the mandatory rules having been notified. In June 2025 the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities, working with the Centre for Disability Studies, released draft rules on accessibility standards for information and communication technology products and services for public consultation, with thirty days for comment; sectoral drafts for other domains were to follow the same route. The proceedings have continued under the supervision the 2024 judgment retained, and the supervision has reached beyond the rules themselves: on 29 July 2026, a bench of Pardiwala and K.V. Viswanathan JJ., hearing the Rajive Raturi proceedings among a batch of petitions, directed the States and Union territories that had not appointed State Commissioners under Section 79 to make the appointments within four weeks, in independent charge, warning that laxity would be viewed strictly. The direction is a reminder that the enforcement machinery of the Act and the standards machinery fail or hold together: a State without a Commissioner is a State in which the complaint route of Sections 80 to 82 does not run at all.

The counter-reading deserves its full strength, because the Court heard it and answered it rather than ignoring it. Retrofitting the existing stock of Indian public infrastructure is a fiscal and engineering undertaking of the first order. The buildings Section 45 covers include every primary health centre, school, railway station and bus stop in the country, much of it built before accessibility was a design consideration at all, and transport fleets turn over on cycles that budgets do not control. A uniform mandatory standard, enforced by withheld certificates and fines, falls hardest on the smallest municipalities and the weakest State budgets, which are also the ones serving the populations with the least alternative provision. That, the Union's position in substance ran, is why the standards were framed as progressive realisation under guidelines: the obligation was accepted, and the pace was to be set by capacity. Disability organisations, and the petitioner, answered that the Act had already made the calibration the argument asks for: universal design at the point of new construction, where accessibility costs least; phased retrofitting of the existing stock within a stated period, with a proviso for case-by-case extension where a State's preparedness requires it; and a penalty that begins at ten thousand rupees. What Parliament had not authorised was the conversion of the whole structure into advice. The Court's holding adopts that answer, and it is difficult to see what else a court faithful to the text could have done: the choice between command and guidance was made in 2016, by the legislature, and a rule-making power is not a power to remake it.

## After the judgment: what compliance requires of the duty-holder

For the establishment on the receiving end, the 2024 judgment changes the compliance question from one of aspiration to one of sequence. A new building is the easy case: the plan must adhere to the accessibility rules to earn permission, and the finished building must adhere to them to earn its completion certificate and lawful occupation, so accessibility is designed in at drawing stage, where its cost is at its lowest and its absence is inexcusable. The existing building is the harder case the Act itself anticipates: Section 45(2) requires an action plan built on prioritisation, and a duty-holder that has surveyed its stock, graded its buildings by public use, costed the retrofitting of the essential services first, and recorded the programme in writing is in a fundamentally different position, before a Commissioner or a court, from one that has done nothing and pleads the size of the task. The plea of expense, to carry any weight at all, must be documented rather than asserted, and the extension proviso in Section 45(1) is the lawful channel for it: case by case, on preparedness and related parameters, granted by the Central Government. A State that neither retrofits nor seeks the extension has chosen default over the two lawful courses the section offers.

Service providers stand in the same structure. The two-year period of Section 46 has long expired for the generality of services, extensions being available for a category of services in consultation with the Chief Commissioner, and the working question for a provider is therefore evidentiary: which of its services conform, which do not, and what is the recorded programme for the remainder. Audits are the instrument the system has settled on for producing that record. The Accessible India Campaign built its built-environment vertical around access audits of identified buildings in identified cities, conducted with empanelled auditors, and audit-based compliance has the double virtue of telling the duty-holder what to fix and telling the complainant what to ask for. A person with a disability confronting an inaccessible public building is correspondingly better armed than the rhetoric of awareness suggests: the building either stands in the establishment's action plan with a place in the sequence or it does not; the completion certificate either records compliance or was issued in breach of Section 44; and the State Commissioner's inquiry can be pointed at those documents rather than at the intentions of the officers who maintain the building. That is the practical legacy of the Rajive Raturi litigation for the individual complainant: it converted accessibility from a standard to be praised into a file to be produced.

## The verdict a rights-holder should take away

The entitlements in this article divide into those that are complete on the face of the Act and those that depend on machinery the State must operate, and the practical wisdom of the field is knowing which is which before choosing a forum. The four per cent and its category shares, the five per cent in higher education, the five years of age relaxation, the free education right and the Section 20(4) sequence are complete: a tribunal or a Commissioner can apply them to a file without waiting for anything. The identification of posts, the instructions for promotion, and the mandatory accessibility rules are machinery-dependent, and in each case the litigation of the last decade has converted official inaction from an answer into a default that can itself be complained of, inquired into and directed against. The thread the series has followed through other statutes, of rules quietly carrying substance the Act omitted, runs in this field in reverse: here the rule removed a compulsion the Act contained, and it took a petition filed in 2005, a judgment with eleven action points in 2017 and a university centre's report in 2024 to restore the text Parliament had enacted in 2016. A reader who keeps the certificate in order, writes the complaint around the Act's own dates, and refuses to accept the guideline as the measure of the duty has understood the statute as its courts read it, which is the only understanding worth having.

One further word belongs to the examination candidate, because the examination hall is where the largest number of rights-holders meet the Act in a single season. The claims that arise there are mostly mapped: the reserved seat under Section 34 or Section 32 on the benchmark certificate; the scribe and compensatory time as reasonable accommodation whether or not the certificate crosses the benchmark line, on the authority of Vikash Kumar and Gulshan Kumar; the accessible centre and accessible question formats under Sections 40 and 46; and the functional, course-specific assessment where a professional regulator stands at the gate, on the authority of Om Rathod. What decides outcomes is rarely the novelty of the claim and almost always the timing and the paper: the accommodation requested in writing when the application is filed, the certificate and the Unique Disability ID record kept consistent with each other, the refusal answered on the record while the examination calendar still allows a court or a Commissioner to intervene. The candidate who assembles that file has done what this article has argued throughout, which is to treat the Act not as a promise to be believed but as a set of dated, documented duties to be invoked, in order, with the proofs attached.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How much reservation in government jobs does the RPwD Act provide?

Section 34(1) of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 requires every appropriate Government to appoint, in every Government establishment, not less than four per cent of the total number of vacancies in the cadre strength in each group of posts, for persons with benchmark disabilities. The four per cent is divided by the section itself: one per cent each for blindness and low vision, for deaf and hard of hearing, and for locomotor disability including cerebral palsy, leprosy cured persons, dwarfism, acid attack victims and muscular dystrophy, and a further one per cent held jointly for autism, intellectual disability, specific learning disability and mental illness, together with multiple disabilities including deaf-blindness, in the posts identified for each disability. The base is cadre strength, not advertised vacancies.

### Q: Is disability reservation horizontal or vertical?

It is horizontal. Vertical reservation divides a cadre into compartments, the Scheduled Castes, the Scheduled Tribes, the Other Backward Classes and the unreserved category, which stand alongside one another. Disability reservation cuts across those compartments: a candidate selected against the disability quota is accommodated inside whichever vertical compartment he or she belongs to, so the vertical totals do not change and the fifty per cent ceiling on vertical reservation is not engaged. The Supreme Court stated the position in Union of India v National Federation of the Blind (2013), holding that the roster logic governing vertical reservation does not apply, and the working rule for merit lists, from Rajesh Kumar Daria and Saurav Yadav, is that a candidate who qualifies on open merit is adjusted against open merit and does not consume a reserved seat.

### Q: Does disability reservation apply to promotions?

Yes, although the route is indirect and has been settled by litigation rather than by a single clean provision. Section 20(3) provides that no promotion shall be denied to a person merely on the ground of disability, and the first proviso to Section 34(1) states that reservation in promotion shall be in accordance with such instructions as the appropriate Government issues from time to time. Under the predecessor Act the Supreme Court held in Rajeev Kumar Gupta (2016) that the bar on promotion reservation for backward classes did not govern disability, and in Siddaraju (2020) that reservation follows once a post is identified, whatever the mode of filling it. In Leesamma Joseph (2021) the Court applied the entitlement under the 2016 Act and directed the State to identify posts for promotion within three months.

### Q: What reservation applies in higher education for disability?

Section 32 of the Act requires every Government institution of higher education, and every other higher education institution receiving aid from the Government, to reserve not less than five per cent of seats for persons with benchmark disabilities. Section 32(2) adds an upper age relaxation of five years for admission. Unlike the employment quota in Section 34, the five per cent is a single pool without an internal division into category shares, so disputes concern the certificate, the coverage of the institution and the assessment of the candidate rather than the distribution between categories. The reservation is horizontal in character, operating within the vertical compartments of the institution's admission scheme, and it sits alongside the free education right in Section 31, which covers children with benchmark disabilities from six to eighteen years.

### Q: What accessibility standards must buildings and services meet?

The standards are those laid down in rules the Central Government must formulate under Section 40 of the Act for the physical environment, transport and information and communication technology. The rules notified in 2017 prescribed standards by incorporating ministerial guidelines, prominently the Harmonised Guidelines for the built environment, but much of that material was written in recommendatory language. In Rajive Raturi, on 8 November 2024, the Supreme Court held Rule 15(1) of the 2017 Rules ultra vires the Act for that reason and directed the Union to frame mandatory, non-negotiable rules within three months, segregating binding requirements from guidance. Until mandatory rules are notified for a sector, establishments remain bound by the Act itself: no building permission, completion certificate or occupation without compliance under Section 44, existing public buildings to be made accessible under Section 45, and services to conform under Section 46.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about accessibility rules in 2024?

In Rajive Raturi v Union of India, on 8 November 2024, a bench of Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud with J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ. held that Rule 15(1) of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules, 2017 was ultra vires the Act. The rule had prescribed accessibility standards by incorporating guidelines expressed in recommendatory terms, while Sections 40, 44, 45, 46 and 89 of the Act create a mandatory, time-bound compliance mechanism backed by withheld certificates and fines. The Court directed the Union Government to delineate mandatory rules within three months, segregating the non-negotiable requirements from the expansive guidelines, in consultation with stakeholders and with the Centre for Disability Studies at NALSAR involved. It directed that the consequences in Sections 44, 45, 46 and 89 be implemented once the mandatory rules were in place, and kept the proceedings under its supervision for compliance reporting.

### Q: Can a scribe be allowed without a benchmark disability?

Yes. In Vikash Kumar v Union Public Service Commission, reported at (2021) 5 SCC 370, the Supreme Court held that the facility of a scribe is an instance of reasonable accommodation under the Act, and that reasonable accommodation is owed to persons with disabilities as defined by Section 2(s), which contains no percentage threshold. Confining the facility to candidates with benchmark disabilities, meaning a certified disability of forty per cent or more, was held inconsistent with the Act. The same reasoning was applied to compensatory time in Avni Prakash v National Testing Agency (2021) and to the scribe guidelines for banking examinations in Gulshan Kumar (2025). A candidate refused a scribe only for falling below the benchmark line has been refused on a ground the Court has disapproved repeatedly; the claim should be made in writing, in advance, with the medical material attached.

### Q: What is a backlog vacancy and why does it persist?

A backlog vacancy is a post that was required to be filled from the reserved quota in an earlier recruitment cycle, was not filled by a person with a benchmark disability, and is carried forward as an arrears to be filled in a later cycle. Section 34(2) supplies the mechanism: an unfilled reserved vacancy is carried to the succeeding recruitment year, may then be filled by interchange among the five categories, and may be filled by a person without disability only if no person with disability is available in that year. Backlogs persist for structural reasons the courts and the Commissioners have documented: posts are identified narrowly or late under Section 33, so the quota is computed against a shrunken base; certificates and suitable candidates fail to meet because recruitment is episodic; and governments announce special recruitment drives against the accumulated arrears which fill a part of the backlog while a fresh one accumulates behind them.

### Q: What age relaxation do persons with benchmark disabilities get in admissions and employment?

For admissions, the relaxation is in the statute: Section 32(2) gives persons with benchmark disabilities an upper age relaxation of five years for admission to institutions of higher education covered by Section 32. For employment, Section 34(3) takes a different route and empowers the appropriate Government, by notification, to provide for such relaxation of the upper age limit for employment of persons with benchmark disabilities as it thinks fit. The consequence is that the employment relaxation varies by government and by recruitment, and is found in the recruiting notification read with the government's standing instructions, rather than in a single figure in the Act. A candidate should check the notification for the specific recruitment, because a relaxation that exists in the standing instructions but is omitted from the advertisement has repeatedly had to be restored by tribunals.

### Q: Why does reservation operate only against identified posts, and who identifies them?

Reservation under Section 34 attaches to posts, and a category's share can be filled only against a post that can in fact be held by a person of that category, which is why Section 33 makes identification the first administrative step. The appropriate Government must identify the posts in each establishment which can be held by the respective categories of persons with benchmark disabilities, must constitute an expert committee with representation of persons with benchmark disabilities for the exercise, and must review the identified posts at intervals not exceeding three years. The courts have held, in Ravi Prakash Gupta (2010) and in the National Federation of the Blind litigation, that identification is a duty and not a precondition the government can hide behind: a government that has not identified posts cannot use its own omission to deny the quota. Under-identification remains the most common structural cause of unfilled reservation.

### Q: Can a government establishment be exempted from the reservation requirement?

Yes, but only through the route the Act prescribes. The second proviso to Section 34(1) empowers the appropriate Government, having regard to the type of work carried on in a Government establishment, and in consultation with the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner as the case may be, to exempt that establishment from Section 34 by notification, subject to any conditions the notification specifies. Three features limit the power: it must rest on the type of work, it requires prior consultation with the Commissioner, and it operates by a public notification rather than by internal decision. A parallel exemption exists for the non-discrimination duty in Section 20(1). An establishment that has obtained no exemption notification is bound in full, and an establishment that has one is bound by its conditions; a candidate or employee faced with a claim of exemption is entitled to ask for the notification itself.

### Q: What protection does an employee have who acquires a disability during service?

Section 20(4) provides that no Government establishment shall dispense with, or reduce in rank, an employee who acquires a disability during his or her service. If the employee is not suitable for the post held after acquiring the disability, the establishment must shift the employee to another post with the same pay scale and service benefits. If no post is available for adjustment, the employee must be kept on a supernumerary post until a suitable post becomes available or the employee attains the age of superannuation, whichever is earlier. The protection is a sequence, retention with reasonable accommodation first, and it is reinforced by Section 20(2), which obliges the establishment to provide reasonable accommodation and a barrier-free environment. In Ravinder Kumar Dhariwal (2022) the Supreme Court set aside the termination of a paramilitary constable who had developed a mental illness, applying this protective scheme.

### Q: Where should an employee complain first about a denial of rights at work, and how quickly must the complaint be inquired into?

The first port of call inside a Government establishment is its Grievance Redressal Officer, whom every Government establishment must appoint under Section 23 and whose appointment must be reported to the Chief Commissioner or the State Commissioner. Any person aggrieved by non-compliance with Section 20, which covers discrimination in employment, denial of reasonable accommodation, denial of promotion on the ground of disability and the acquired disability protections, may file a complaint with that officer. The officer must investigate the complaint and take the matter up with the establishment for corrective action, must maintain a register of complaints, and must complete the inquiry within two weeks of the complaint's registration. The two-week period is in the section itself and is worth citing in the complaint. A complainant is not barred from approaching the Commissioner or a tribunal in parallel where the timeline of a selection or a termination requires it.

### Q: What can a Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities do on a complaint, and must the authority respond?

The Chief Commissioner under Sections 74 and 75, and each State Commissioner under Sections 79 and 80, may inquire, on complaint or of his own motion, into deprivation of the rights of persons with disabilities in matters within the corresponding government's sphere, and take the matter up with the authority concerned for corrective action. For the inquiry, Sections 77 and 82 give each Commissioner the powers of a civil court: summoning witnesses, requiring production of documents, requisitioning public records, receiving evidence on affidavit and issuing commissions. The Commissioner then makes a recommendation, and Sections 76 and 81 oblige the authority to take necessary action and report the action taken within three months, or, if it does not accept the recommendation, to convey its reasons for non-acceptance to the Commissioner within three months and inform the aggrieved person. A Commissioner cannot execute the recommendation like a decree, but silence in the face of one is a breach of a dated statutory duty.

### Q: What happens if a building is built or completed without complying with the accessibility rules?

Section 44 stops the building at three gates. No establishment shall be granted permission to build any structure if the building plan does not adhere to the rules formulated by the Central Government under Section 40. No establishment shall be issued a certificate of completion, or be allowed to take occupation of a building, unless it has adhered to those rules. A completion certificate issued for a non-compliant building is therefore issued in breach of the Act, and occupation of the building is unauthorised in the same measure. The contravention also attracts Section 89: a fine up to ten thousand rupees for a first contravention, and for a subsequent contravention a fine of not less than fifty thousand rupees extending to five lakh rupees. In the Rajive Raturi judgment of 8 November 2024 the Supreme Court directed that, once mandatory rules are prescribed, the withholding of completion certificates and the imposition of fines actually be implemented.

### Q: What time limits does the Act prescribe for existing public buildings and for service providers?

Section 45(1) requires all existing public buildings to be made accessible in accordance with the rules formulated by the Central Government within a period not exceeding five years from the date of notification of the rules. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Rules were notified on 15 June 2017, so that period ran to June 2022. The proviso to Section 45(1) empowers the Central Government to grant a State an extension of time on a case to case basis, depending on preparedness and related parameters. Section 46 gives service providers, Government or private, two years from the notification of the rules to provide services in accordance with them, which ran to June 2019, with a proviso for extension for a category of services in consultation with the Chief Commissioner. Section 45(2) also requires an action plan prioritising essential services such as health centres, hospitals, schools, railway stations and bus stops.

### Q: What is the penalty for contravening the Act or its rules?

The general penalty is in Section 89. A person who contravenes any provision of the Act, or of any rule made under it, is punishable for a first contravention with a fine which may extend to ten thousand rupees, and for any subsequent contravention with a fine which shall not be less than fifty thousand rupees but which may extend to five lakh rupees. Offences by companies are dealt with in Section 90, which fastens liability on the persons in charge of the company's business, subject to a due diligence defence. The Act's specific penalties sit beside the general one: fraudulently availing a benefit meant for persons with benchmark disabilities carries imprisonment up to two years or a fine up to one lakh rupees or both under Section 91; the offences of atrocity listed in Section 92 carry imprisonment from six months to five years and a fine; and failure to furnish required information carries a fine up to twenty-five thousand rupees for each offence under Section 93, with a daily fine for a continuing default. Offences are tried by the Special Court designated under Section 84.

### Q: Does the four per cent reservation apply to private sector employment?

No. Section 34 binds appointments in Government establishments, and no provision of the Act imposes a reservation quota on a private employer. What the Act does for the private sector is different in kind. Section 35 directs the appropriate Government and local authorities, within the limit of their economic capacity and development, to provide incentives to private employers to ensure that at least five per cent of their workforce is composed of persons with benchmark disability, a target to be pursued through incentives rather than through a mandate. Private establishments remain inside other parts of the Act: the definition of establishment includes private establishments, service providers whether Government or private must conform to the accessibility rules under Section 46, public buildings include privately owned buildings used by the public, and the Supreme Court's reasonable accommodation jurisprudence, including Vikash Kumar, treats denial of reasonable accommodation as discrimination without confining the principle to government employment.

### Q: If a candidate with a disability qualifies on open merit, is a reserved seat consumed?

No. That is the working rule of horizontal reservation stated in Rajesh Kumar Daria v Rajasthan Public Service Commission (2007) and applied in Saurav Yadav v State of Uttar Pradesh (2021). A candidate entitled to horizontal reservation who secures a place in the open category on merit, without relying on a relaxation attached to the quota, is adjusted against the open category within his or her vertical compartment, and the reserved seat remains available for the next eligible candidate of the horizontal category. The converse adjustment, counting the meritorious candidate against the quota and declaring the quota full, is the error that recurs in recruitment litigation, because it gives the reserved seat to a selection that did not need it and leaves the candidate the quota existed for unappointed. Recruiting bodies are required to prepare the list in the order the rule prescribes: open merit first, horizontal adjustment within compartments after.

### Q: Can an assessment board reject a candidate for a professional course only because the disability percentage is high?

No. In Om Rathod v Director General of Health Services, decided on 25 October 2024 and reported at 2024 INSC 836, the Supreme Court set aside the rejection of a medical aspirant by disability assessment boards that had proceeded on the quantified percentage of his disability. The Court held that the quantification of disability belongs to the certification stage and the candidate's Unique Disability ID record, and that the role of an assessment board at the admission stage is a functional competency assessment tailored to the course in question: the board must record positively whether the disability will or will not come in the way of pursuing that course, with reasonable accommodation in view. A board that reports a percentage and a conclusion, without reasons connecting functional capacity to the demands of the course, acts on the approach the Court disapproved. Omkar Ramchandra Gond v Union of India applied the same functional approach in the same period.
