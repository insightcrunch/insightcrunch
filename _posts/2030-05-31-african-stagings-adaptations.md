Romeo and Juliet reached Africa as an examination text, a missionary tale, and a statesman's translation project long before it became an African stage play, and that order of arrival is the whole argument of this article. The verdict is that the continent's claim on the tragedy is appropriation rather than inheritance, but only in the places where African hands chose the language, the venue, and the use. Where the play arrived as an imposed English set text, the charge of colonial inheritance still holds. Where African translators, directors, and schoolroom audiences remade it in Sesotho, Afrikaans, Chichewa, Kinyarwanda, and a dozen stage languages, the play became African property by the only test that matters, which is use. The proof runs through a correction that has to come first, because the most famous African name attached to the play does not belong to it at all.

![African stagings and adaptations of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-49.webp)

The correction is plain, and it is the article's hinge. Julius Nyerere, the first president of Tanzania, translated Shakespeare into Swahili, and his translations are the foundation stone of East African Shakespeare. He did not translate Romeo and Juliet. His published Shakespeare translations are Julius Caesar and The Merchant of Venice, and the record of his work is exact on the point. Treating him as the Swahili translator of the lovers' tragedy flatters a tidy story at the expense of the real one, and the real one is better: Nyerere's two translations established the precedent that a head of state could put Shakespeare into an African language as an act of national self-respect, and later generations applied that precedent to this play in languages and venues Nyerere never touched. The association attaches to him because he made the act thinkable, not because he performed it for this tragedy. Quotations from the play in this article follow the Folger Shakespeare Library text, and the play's own frame for everything that follows is its opening sentence, "Two households, both alike in dignity" (Prologue), a feud stated as a fact of the city's constitution before any lover appears.

The survey that follows is organized as a proof rather than a tour. It begins with the correction, because a map that starts from a false attribution will misgrade everything downstream of it. It then follows the play's channels of arrival in the order the article's verdict needs them: the mission press that first carried the story into an African language, the colonial schoolroom that made the text compulsory, the translators who chose other plays and thereby defined the field this play entered late, and the stage records of South Africa, Malawi, Rwanda, Uganda, and Nigeria, where the play was localized, contested, and argued with. It closes with the scholarly quarrel between the inheritance charge and the appropriation claim, a census of the play's documented African languages, and a graded map. Every production, translation, and date in the survey is stated at the strength of the record that documents it, and where the record thins, the survey says so in words rather than filling the silence with plausible scenery. That discipline is not pedantry. It is the same discipline the article applies to the brief that commissioned it, whose single false premise, once corrected, turns out to contain the whole argument: Africa's claim on this play was never going to rest on a famous name. It rests on the documented uses, and the uses are enough.

One preliminary belongs to the play itself. The African makers in this survey were not working on a story about love in the abstract. They were working on a story about a feud, a structured, inherited, publicly enforced hatred between two houses, [the Capulet and Montague feud and its unexplained origins](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) being the engine that kills the lovers and reconciles the city at the price of its children. The feud is the part of the play that travels, because the feud is the part that every divided society recognizes as its own portrait, and the map's central column, how the feud is carried, is therefore the column that decides the verdict entry by entry. Where a production or translation found the local feud, Thembu and Hlubi, Hutu and Tutsi, the categories of apartheid, the rival houses became a diagnosis. Where it merely reproduced the lovers, the play stayed a European romance on tour. The distinction runs through the whole record, and the strongest African work in this survey is, without exception, the work that staged the feud rather than the balcony.

## The Play That Is Not Nyerere's: Settling the Swahili Record

### Q: Did Julius Nyerere translate Romeo and Juliet into Swahili?

No. Nyerere's published Shakespeare translations are Julius Caesar, issued in 1963 and revised in 1969, and The Merchant of Venice, issued in 1969, both from Oxford University Press in Dar es Salaam, and no Romeo and Juliet stands in his bibliography.

The two translations matter precisely because of what they chose. Julius Caesar is a play about conspiracy, republican virtue, and the murder of a ruler, and The Merchant of Venice is a play about contract, mercy, and the outsider at the city's gate. Both gave a young nation vocabulary for its own arguments. Nyerere's own preface to the Caesar translation is modest to the point of apology, warning readers that he was neither a Swahili scholar nor an English scholar, only a man who loved both languages, and describing the work as something taken up for relief from the strain of office. Scholarship on the two editions, notably the work of Esther J. Terry on the two Caesar texts, shows something more deliberate than relaxation: the 1969 revision reworked the verse so that the Shakespearean form was colonized, in Terry's phrase, by Swahili culture, making the text more amenable to Swahili readers and to performance in Swahili. The technical device was a new blank verse built on a sixteen-syllable line, an attempt to give Swahili poetry a measure that could carry Shakespeare's line without violating the norms of Swahili verse. By 1970 Nyerere had abandoned the translation project, and the two plays stand as a completed, bounded achievement rather than the start of a complete Shakespeare.

Why the lovers' tragedy is nonetheless attached to his name is itself a piece of evidence. Alamin M. Mazrui, in his 1996 study of Shakespeare between English and Swahili literature, argues that translation in East Africa was never a purely literary act: it was a claim about which language could carry serious thought, and therefore about which people could govern themselves. Nyerere's translations performed that claim at the highest level of the state. When later writers and teachers speak of a Swahili Shakespeare, they mean the tradition Nyerere founded, and the lovers' tragedy rides on the tradition's prestige even though it was never part of its text. The article therefore treats the brief's premise as an understandable compression and corrects it in the open, because a survey that begins by crediting Nyerere with a translation he never made cannot be trusted on the translations he did make.

The second consequence of the correction is that East Africa's Romeo and Juliet has to be found elsewhere, and the search is instructive. The play's East African life is overwhelmingly a schoolroom and stage life in English, with a parallel life in prose tale form, rather than a literary translation life in Swahili. That distribution is the opposite of the Schlegel and Pasternak cases, where a single translator's text defined the national reception, and it is the reason the African story is a story of stagings more than of translations. The translators who matter most for this play on the continent worked in other languages, in other regions, and often for other plays, and the play itself reached African audiences through performance, adaptation, and analogy long before it reached them as a translated text.

Nyerere's own account of his project and the scholars' account of it disagree, and the disagreement is worth adjudicating because it decides what kind of act translation was. In his foreword the president presents the work as recreation, a relaxation taken up when the usual work tired him, hedged with a warning that he was no scholar of either language. Terry's study of the two Caesar editions reads the same books as statecraft: the translations were made across the very years in which Nyerere was developing ujamaa, Tanzanian socialism, and its doctrine of cultural self-reliance announced in the Arusha Declaration of 1967, and the 1969 revision systematically remade the verse to serve Swahili poetic norms rather than English ones. Mazrui's analysis supports the statecraft reading at the level of motive, since a translation programme that tests a national language against the world's most prestigious dramatist is a policy instrument whether or not its author calls it one. The adjudication favours the scholars, on the evidence of the revision itself: a man translating for relaxation does not return six years later to re-engineer his verse line, rework his text toward the norms of his own language's poetry, and issue the result as a second edition from the same university press. The modesty of the foreword is a politician's modesty, and the books are a founder's books. The translations even travelled as teaching texts abroad: the American scholar Ben Lindfors has described reading Nyerere's Caesar in a university Kiswahili class in California and corresponding with the president about renderings he believed could be amended, an exchange that shows the Dar es Salaam volumes functioning as the standard by which Swahili Shakespeare was judged far beyond Tanzania.

Translation remained Nyerere's second vocation after the presidency, and the retirement work confirms the statecraft reading by its sheer persistence. Having left office, he rendered the Gospels and the Acts of the Apostles into Swahili verse in the tenzi form, issued in five volumes by a Benedictine publishing house in Tanzania, a project studied by translation scholars as the capstone of a life spent proving what Swahili could carry. A hobbyist does not spend retirement putting scripture into national verse. The through-line from the 1963 Caesar to the Gospel volumes is a single conviction, that a people's language is built by loading it with the heaviest texts available, and Shakespeare was simply the heaviest secular load at hand. That conviction, more than any single book, is what East Africans mean when they attach Nyerere's name to Swahili Shakespeare, and it is why the correction with which this article opened must be stated as a correction of attribution, not of significance.

## Shakespeare in Swahili Print Before Nyerere: Zanzibar 1867

Shakespeare entered Swahili print almost a century before Nyerere, and the vehicle was not a play at all. In 1867 the Universities' Mission to Central Africa press at Kiungani, near Stone Town on Zanzibar, printed Hadithi za Kiingereza, English Tales, a slim volume of stories drawn from Charles and Mary Lamb's Tales from Shakespeare, translated by the missionary bishop Edward Steere and printed with the labour of former slave boys at the mission press. Edward Wilson-Lee, whose Shakespeare in Swahililand (2016) recovered this history, treats the volume as one of the first books ever printed in Swahili, and the detail matters for the argument: the first Shakespeare that Swahili readers could hold was prose narrative, a retelling for young readers, not a verse drama. The lovers' story, if it travelled in that volume at all, travelled as a tale stripped of its verse, its puns, and its stage machinery. The play's African career began, in other words, as story before it became drama, and that priority has never quite left it: on much of the continent the plot has always been more portable than the poetry.

The mission press also sets the terms of the inheritance charge. Steere's project was evangelical and educational, aimed at building a shared culture between missionaries and the people they taught, and the press's products carried the assumptions of their makers. Yet the press was worked by African hands, and the volume's existence gave Swahili a printed literary object at a moment when the language's written life was being built. The tension is the article's tension in miniature: a European instrument, operated partly by African labour, producing an African-language book. The appropriation verdict does not require denying the missionary frame. It requires showing where African agents took the instrument and pointed it elsewhere, and the record shows that happening repeatedly, in Mombasa, in Dar es Salaam, in Kampala, and in Lilongwe.

The print history around the 1867 volume deepens the point. Printing on Zanzibar had begun only in 1865, when Steere founded the Universities' Mission press at Kiungani and issued the first sections of his Handbook of the Swahili Language, and the first Swahili printed text of any kind had appeared two decades earlier, in 1844, when the German missionary Johann Ludwig Krapf printed portions of Genesis at Mombasa. The Lamb volume of 1867, with its four Shakespearean tales, therefore stands at the very start of Swahili print culture, cheek by jowl with the grammar and the Bible portions. Shakespeare was not imported into a mature Swahili book trade and ranked within it. He was present at the trade's creation, in a volume whose translators' purpose was to show what Swahili prose could carry. Wilson-Lee's chapter title for the episode, Shakespeare and the Slaveboy Printworks, holds the whole paradox in five words: the press hands were boys the mission had taken in from the slave trade, printing the stories of England's national poet in the language of the coast that the trade had raided. Whatever else that is, it is not a simple story of a gift handed down, and the article's later cases are best read as its continuations.

The arrival story has an even earlier chapter, and it belongs to the sea. The Folger Shakespeare Library's survey of Shakespeare in Africa opens with the journal account of the East India Company ship Dragon, anchored off Sierra Leone in 1607, whose crew performed Hamlet and Richard II for their own entertainment, the first Shakespeare performances on the African coast that any written record preserves. No African audience is recorded, and no African hand touched those performances. They are the inheritance story at its purest: the plays as cargo, carried for the carriers. Everything else in this article is measured against that baseline, because the distance between a crew performing Hamlet to itself off Sierra Leone in 1607 and a Kigali company arguing over wages in Kinyarwanda in 2013 is the distance the verdict has to travel, and the distance is the argument.

One further feature of the 1867 volume predicts the play's whole African career. Lamb's Tales is a children's book, a retelling designed to give young readers the stories before they meet the plays, and Shakespeare therefore entered Swahili print as schoolroom material from the first hour. The channel that would dominate the play's continental life, the classroom, was not a later colonial imposition laid over an earlier popular stage tradition. It was the original channel, present at the creation of Swahili print, and the stages of this article are best understood as the classroom's graduates at work: Makerere's performing students, Alliance's schoolboy actor turned abolitionist, the PACT tour's debating school audiences, the Luwinga schoolchildren in Bonsall's front rows, and the Kigali high school that hosted both the 2013 auditions and, two years later, a student production in a mosquito-net veil. Steere's own reported aim, to establish a shared culture through the volume, was achieved in a sense he could not have intended, since the culture that came to be shared was shared on terms his successors' students rewrote. At Kiungani the press and the school were a single institution, housed together, staffed by the same mission, and aimed at the same boys, so that the first Swahili Shakespeare was a schoolbook produced by schoolboys in the most exact sense. The article's two channels, print and classroom, were never separate at the source, and the later history, in which school syllabuses carried the play further than any stage until the stages answered back, is the Kiungani arrangement written at continental scale.

Mombasa supplies the next chapter. In the early twentieth century the Indian labourers brought to build the Uganda Railway carried a vigorous Shakespeare performance culture with them, and the port became a centre of Shakespearean performance in Indian languages, with plots freely altered and a flamboyant acting style that startled European observers. Colonial licences for eight Shakespeare performances or adaptations in Mombasa in February and March 1915 survive in the archive Wilson-Lee consulted, a small administrative trace of a large popular habit. The point for this article is that the continent's Shakespeare was already plural before African-language productions of this play existed: Indian, Goan, and Arab troupes, school stages, and mission halls all carried versions of the plays, and the lovers' tragedy moved through that mixed traffic rather than arriving by a single authorized route.

The Mombasa material also corrects a lazy assumption about how the plays travelled. The standard picture has Shakespeare carried into the interior by explorers as a talisman of civilization, and Wilson-Lee confirms that picture for men such as Richard Burton, who carried a volume across bog and savanna, and Henry Morton Stanley, who recounted burning his copy to calm local fears that the books were witchcraft. In that picture the book is a European's private comfort, pointedly not shared. Mombasa shows the other traffic: a port city where performance was public, commercial, multilingual, and open to anyone who bought a ticket, and where the colonial state's licensing files, not the explorers' memoirs, are the surviving record. The play reached African audiences through both channels, the talisman and the ticket, but only the second channel ever made it theirs, because a licensed public performance in a port city belongs to its audience in a way a book in a safari kitbag never can. The Mombasa performances even left sound behind: Wilson-Lee recovered two gramophone recordings on shellac of music from the port's Shakespeare productions, among the earliest traces of the African Shakespeare story that can still be heard rather than merely read about. A licensing file and a shellac disc are humble archives, and their humility is the point. The continent's grand Shakespeare narratives rest on paper trails laid down by clerks, missionaries, teachers, and theatre managers, and the survey that respects those trails, rather than the prestige of the names floating above them, is the survey that gets the story right.

## The Schoolroom Door: How the Play Entered African Education

The dominant route into African life was the classroom. Across British Africa the plays were set as prescribed reading for the Cambridge Overseas School Certificate and its successors, and Romeo and Juliet was among the most frequently set texts, because its plot is teachable, its moral is legible, and its language, though difficult, rewards the teacher who can gloss it. At Makerere University in Kampala, where generations of East Africa's post-independence political and cultural leadership studied English literature in the 1940s and after, reading and performing Shakespeare was central to the degree, and the young James Ngugi, later Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, encountered Shakespeare as a schoolboy at Alliance High School in Kenya in the 1950s and then at Makerere. The classroom made the play familiar to the very people who would later argue about whether it belonged in Africa at all, and that familiarity is why the argument had force: it was an argument about a text the arguers knew intimately, not about a distant import.

The West African schoolroom ran on the same machinery with its own institutions, and it produced the quarrel's first cartographer. Fourah Bay College in Sierra Leone, the oldest university institution in West Africa, taught the plays inside the same examination culture, and it was from Sierra Leone that Eldred Jones, the country's leading Shakespeare scholar, wrote Othello's Countrymen in 1965, the first book-length survey to treat the African presence in Shakespeare and Shakespeare's presence in Africa as a single subject. The sequence is worth noticing: the survey of African Shakespeare was itself an African product, written a year before the Dar es Salaam Merchant and three years before the Nairobi abolition paper, by a scholar working inside the very syllabus the paper would attack. The classroom, in other words, had already generated its own criticism before the politicians and the professors began theirs, and the criticism's founding question was Jones's, not Ngũgĩ's: not whether the plays should be in Africa, but what Africa had already made of them and they of Africa.

### Q: What happened to the play in Kenyan schools after independence?

After independence Kenya's Africanization drive removed nearly all Western literature from the school syllabus, and Romeo and Juliet went with it, until the play was restored to the syllabus in 1992, as Alamin Mazrui records, the expulsion and the return together forming the cleanest single proof that the play's African status changed with the choosing.

The Kenyan sequence is the article's best single exhibit, because it contains both halves of the argument in one national story. The expulsion was the inheritance charge made policy: a newly independent state decided that a curriculum built on English literature was a colonial residue, and it cleared the shelves. The purge was indiscriminate in a way that proves its target was the system rather than the books: Mazrui notes the irony that the Africanization drive caught even Nyerere's Swahili translations in its sweep, since the objection was to Western literature's place in the syllabus, not to the language a given copy happened to be printed in. A drive that expels the independence hero's own Swahili Caesar is not conducting literary criticism. It is dismantling an apparatus, and the play went out with the apparatus. The restoration was the appropriation verdict made policy: the same state, a generation later, brought this particular play back, not as a colonial relic but as a text Kenyan teachers and students wanted. The play that returned was not the play that had been expelled, because the readers had changed, and the syllabus that received it was a Kenyan syllabus rather than an imperial one. Mazrui's account is careful on the mechanism: the restoration was a decision about what Kenyan education should contain, taken by Kenyans, and the play's return is evidence that a text can outlive the system that imposed it when a new generation finds a use for it.

Makerere's role completes the schoolroom picture. Wilson-Lee stresses that the plays at Makerere were not merely dispensed as rote learning and then discarded: the generation that went on to lead the region's political, social, and cultural life studied English literature there with a heavy emphasis on reading and performing Shakespeare, and the performance habit mattered. A syllabus that is only read can be abolished by a ministry circular. A syllabus that has been acted, in school halls and university theatres, leaves behind companies, audiences, and habits of staging that survive the circular. That is one reason the East African story, after the purges of the independence years, is a story of stages filling again rather than of a text forgotten: the classroom had accidentally trained its own successors. The young Ngũgĩ is the emblematic case, a schoolboy actor and reader at Alliance who became the adult author of the abolition paper, and whose later career, writing fiction and drama in Gikuyu, enacted the paper's remedy more faithfully than any ministry managed.

The Kenyan story also frames the most famous African attack on the colonial curriculum, the 1968 paper On the Abolition of the English Department, written at University College, Nairobi, by Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Henry Owuor-Anyumba, and Taban Lo Liyong. The paper argued that the English department, with its canon of English literature, should be abolished and replaced by a department of African literature and languages, with English literature taking its place among other literatures rather than at the centre. Ngũgĩ's later book Decolonising the Mind (1986) extended the argument to language itself, urging African writers to work in African languages. The paper is sometimes misread as a call to burn Shakespeare. It was a call to de-centre him, and its authors knew the plays well enough to argue with them rather than about them. The article's adjudication, developed in the critics' section below, is that Ngũgĩ's diagnosis of the imposed syllabus was right and his remedy, where it meant translation and re-centring rather than erasure, is exactly what the translators and stage-makers of this play went on to practise.

The schoolroom channel did not close when the colonial flags came down, and its later history complicates the inheritance charge in a useful direction. Wilson-Lee's itinerary records that western oil companies pushed Shakespeare in East Africa during the Cold War, a soft-power use of the plays in which the text's prestige was rented for corporate and diplomatic purposes. That chapter belongs to the inheritance column, since the choosing was done in boardrooms far from the stages. Set against it is the plain persistence of the play in African classrooms on African terms: Kenya's 1992 restoration, the Rwandan school production of 2015 with its mosquito-net veil, and the Malawian school audience at Luwinga in 2016 all show the schoolroom still functioning as the play's largest African venue, but with the authority relocated. A set text chosen by a national examination board, taught by national teachers, and argued with by national students is not the same institution as a set text imposed by a colonial office, even when the words on the page are identical. The article's grading follows that difference throughout. The African schoolroom is one province of a worldwide institution, and the play's classroom settlement on other continents, from examination boards to national curricula, is surveyed in [the play in global classrooms](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/); the African province is distinguished within it by the completeness of its argument, since no other continent expelled the play by policy and then readmitted it by policy within a single generation.

## Translation as Language-Building: Plaatje, Decker, Mahloane, Masinga

The southern and western translation record shows a pattern that explains why the lovers' tragedy waited so long for its African-language texts. The great African translators of Shakespeare chose plays of statecraft, identity, and law. Sol Plaatje, the South African writer and political leader, rendered A Comedy of Errors into Setswana as Diphosho-phosho, published in 1930, and Julius Caesar into Setswana, published in 1937, while his Setswana versions of The Merchant of Venice, Othello, and Much Ado About Nothing were lost. Plaatje's motive, as the scholarship of David Schalkwyk and Lentswe Lapula has shown, was to extend the range of Setswana and to prove, against the colonial dismissal of African languages, that Setswana could carry the most demanding European text with style and cultural deftness. The plays he chose were plays about mistaken identity, conspiracy, and the city, and his translations were acts of linguistic nation-building in a country where his own political movement was fighting for the franchise.

The same logic governed the other early translators. In Sierra Leone, Thomas Decker's translations into Krio began in 1964 with Julius Caesar, and the choice was again a play of statecraft, translated to promote Krio itself as a language fit for the most serious themes and to reach audiences who had not received a French or English education. In South Africa, King Edward Masinga produced seven translations into Zulu in the early 1950s, part of the rich heritage of some sixty Shakespeare translations into South African languages, about half into Afrikaans and the remainder into African languages, many of them surviving only in fragments. The translators' shared motive, as the Internet Shakespeare Editions overview of Shakespeare in South Africa puts it, was a mixture of fascination with Shakespeare and a desire to build the cultural authority of the target language by testing it against an international standard. Translation, in this tradition, is a test the language passes, and the test is harder, and therefore more valuable, when the play is a play of state rather than a play of love.

Plaatje deserves a fuller paragraph, because his case shows the translator's motive at its most political. He was a founding figure of the movement that became the African National Congress, the author of the novel Mhudi, and a journalist who had watched his people's land rights dismantled by the Natives Land Act of 1913. A man in that position does not translate Shakespeare as a hobby. The Setswana Shakespeare project was a demonstration, mounted in print, that the language of a people being legislated out of full citizenship could carry the Comedy of Errors' wordplay and Caesar's rhetoric without loss, and Schalkwyk and Lapula's reading of the translations stresses their cultural deftness: Plaatje did not merely find Setswana equivalents, he naturalized the plays, letting Tswana idiom and social form do work that a school crib would have left undone. The loss of three of his translations, the Merchant, the Othello, and the Much Ado, is therefore a double loss, of texts and of evidence, and it repeats the archive's bias: the African-language Shakespeare of the early twentieth century survives unevenly, in fragments and references, because the institutions that might have preserved it were not built for that purpose. The article's map grades accordingly, and the uneven survival is one more reason the continental survey must be written at the record's strength rather than at the story's convenience.

Against that background, the actual printed translations of Romeo and Juliet into African languages are modest, late, and instructive. The earliest the record documents is Southern Sotho: Romeo le Juliet by I. Mahloane, published by Mazenod in 1964. The mission press matters here as it did in Zanzibar: Mazenod was a Catholic mission press in Lesotho, and the translation reached Sesotho readers through the church's publishing network rather than through a commercial or state publisher. The Afrikaans record is fuller. André P. Brink, the novelist and critic, published Die Tragedie van Romeo en Juliet with Human and Rousseau in 1975, and the Performing Arts Council of the Orange Free State staged it in 1977 and again in 1981, directed by Sandra Kotzé with Gerben Kamper and Rina Nienaber as the lovers. A school production of another Afrikaans version, Romeo en Juliet by D'Arcy du Toit, opened at Groote Schuur High School in Rondebosch, Cape Town, on 30 July 1971, directed by Cilliers Delport. The pattern is consistent: where the play was translated, it was translated by writers and institutions building a language's literary range, and the stage followed the book.

The date of Brink's translation carries a charge that its first audiences cannot have missed. An Afrikaans Romeo and Juliet published in 1975 entered a country where Afrikaans itself was the most contested language in public life, celebrated by its literary culture as a young language that had fought for recognition and imposed by the state as a compulsory medium in African schools, the imposition that lit the Soweto uprising of June 1976. Brink belonged to the generation of Afrikaans writers who turned the language against the regime that claimed to own it, and his tragedy translation sits inside that literary project: the state's language made to speak the world's most famous story of young people destroyed by their elders' arrangements, in the year before schoolchildren rose against the state's language policy in the streets. The article does not claim that Brink timed the book as a protest, and no evidence consulted says he did. The claim is narrower and stronger: an Afrikaans Romeo and Juliet in 1975 could not be a neutral object, because no Afrikaans book was, and the translation's existence inside the arts council system, performed by a state council in 1977 and 1981, shows the play working simultaneously as the establishment's culture and as its writers' instrument. South Africa's record is dense because every faction in the country's long argument found a use for this play, and a text used by all sides of an argument has been appropriated several times over.

### Q: What is the earliest printed translation of the play into an African language that the record documents?

The Southern Sotho Romeo le Juliet by I. Mahloane, published by the Mazenod mission press in 1964, is the earliest printed translation into an African language that the record consulted for this article documents, with earlier school or mission versions possible but unrecorded.

The qualification is not evasiveness. It is the article's method stated plainly: a survey that claims a continental first on the basis of an incomplete archive would be inventing the very record it claims to report. The Mahloane volume is documented in the Stellenbosch University theatre encyclopedia, which is the most systematic record of South African performance and translation, and no earlier African-language Romeo and Juliet appears in the sources consulted. The gap itself is evidence. The play's African life was carried by English-language school editions and by stage adaptation long before it was carried by literary translation, and the languages that did receive it first, Sesotho and Afrikaans, received it through mission and commercial publishing networks that were already in place for other purposes.

## South Africa: The Densest Record on the Continent

South Africa holds the fullest African record for the play, and the fullness is itself an argument. The country has performed the tragedy in English since the nineteenth century, in Afrikaans since the 1970s, in translation, in dance, in deaf theatre, and in school touring productions that used the text as a tool for debate under apartheid. The density reflects a theatre culture in which Shakespeare was simultaneously the colonizer's text and the opposition's weapon, and the two roles were often played by the same companies in the same season. The record begins early and socially: as early as 1851, and possibly 1853, excerpts from the play formed part of Shakespeare readings given by the performer Mrs Greig, which places the lovers' scenes in South African public culture as recitation before they were staged as drama, in the drawing-room and hall culture of a settler society. That origin is worth marking because it repeats the continental pattern in miniature: the play arrived as respectable recitation, a marker of cultivation, and only over the following century was it seized for other purposes by other hands.

The apartheid-era schools record is the most distinctive part of it. In the 1970s the Performing Arts Council of the Transvaal's Playwork programme, directed by Peter Terry, toured a theatre-in-education Romeo and Juliet that used the text as a springboard for audience debate about prejudice in South Africa and in Northern Ireland. The choice of the play for that purpose is revealing: the feud, a conflict inherited by children who did not start it, was a usable image for a society in which children inherited racial categories they had not chosen, and the touring format, with debate built into the performance, made the audience part of the argument rather than spectators of it. The programme did not stage the play as a love story with a sad ending. It staged the feud as a question, and it asked the audience to answer. It also chose its text well, because the play contains its own protest against the feud, spoken by a dying man with nothing left to lose: Mercutio's curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), is the moment the tragedy stops admiring its young lovers and starts indicting the households, and a schools production built for debate could build the whole argument out of that line and the children's replies to it.

### Q: How did a schools tour use the play to debate prejudice under apartheid?

The PACT Playwork tour of the 1970s, directed by Peter Terry, used the tragedy as a springboard for structured audience debate about prejudice in South Africa and Northern Ireland, making the inherited feud the question rather than the backdrop, and recruiting school audiences as participants in the argument rather than as witnesses to a finished performance.

The novelistic record runs parallel. R. L. Peteni's Hill of Fools, published in 1976, reworks the lovers' story across the tension between the Thembu and the Hlubi peoples of the Eastern Cape, the Hlubi being a group that had retreated into the region in the early nineteenth century to escape Shaka's wars. The novel was translated into Xhosa in 1980 and televised by the South African Broadcasting Corporation as Aba Kwazidenge in January 1996. Peteni's book is not a translation of Shakespeare and not a stage adaptation. It is a novel that takes the plot's engine, two young people destroyed by a group hatred neither chose, and runs it on Eastern Cape fuel. Its place in this article is as proof that the story's African life did not need Shakespeare's words at all: the structure travelled, and the structure did the work.

The post-apartheid stage record is deliberately varied. Dada Masilo, the choreographer, made Romeo and Juliette as a dance work, performed at the National Arts Festival in Grahamstown in July 2009 and at the Baxter Theatre in Cape Town in October 2009, removing the verse and letting the feud live in bodies. Masilo's version is the strongest South African test of the play's portability, because it discards the very thing the translators laboured over, the words, and keeps only the structure: attraction across a line, a community's prohibition, a public fight, and a double death. That the structure still reads as this play, to audiences who know it from school syllabuses and from the culture at large, tells the survey something important about where the tragedy's African life resides. It resides in the plot's machinery, which is why the story could travel as Lamb's prose in 1867, as Peteni's novel in 1976, and as Masilo's wordless dance in 2009, while the verse waited for its translators. The two imported adaptations of the festival years make the complementary point from the other direction. Calarco's Shakespeare's R&J confines the play to four schoolboys in a repressive boarding school who act the tragedy out in secret, and its Grahamstown staging in 2011, with Marcel Meyer as Romeo and the women's parts taken inside the all-male company, turned the text into a play about prohibition itself, about what a forbidden book does to the boys forbidden to read it innocently. In a country whose own school history includes both the colonial boarding school and the Bantu Education classroom, the frame needed no programme note. The Stellenbosch clinic production of the same year pushed the furthest: a Verona rebuilt as a psychiatric clinic, the feud restaged as a contest between rival regimes of treatment, and the lovers' course charted between them. Between Masilo's bodies, Calarco's schoolboys, and Olwagen's clinic, the South African record of a single festival era shows the play being used to argue about desire, schooling, and care in turn, which is what a national classic looks like when a country has finished inheriting it and started spending it. The Western Cape Theatre of the Deaf staged the play in 1999, a production whose existence alone refutes any notion that the tragedy belongs to a single sensory channel. Set the deaf production beside Masilo's dance version and the South African record contains two productions that discard the spoken word in opposite ways, one replacing it with sign, the other with choreography, and both still legibly this play. The pairing is the country's whole Shakespeare argument in miniature: a text so thoroughly naturalized that its custodians felt free to remove its most celebrated feature, the language, twice, in two different media, within a decade. Custodianship of that confidence is not inherited. It is earned by use, and South Africa's century of readings, tours, translations, and festival arguments is the earning of it. Joe Calarco's Shakespeare's R&J, an adaptation set in a repressed boys' school, was produced in South Africa at the 2011 Grahamstown Festival by Abrahamse and Meyer Productions, directed and designed by Fred Abrahamse, with Marcel Meyer as Romeo in an all-male four-hander. In the same festival year, the University of Stellenbosch drama department staged Juliet plus Romeo plus Romeo plus Juliet, an adaptation by Christiaan Olwagen from a workshop process by Marthinus Basson, set in the Verona Clinic for the insane as a metaphor for modern South Africa, with the love story charting a course between rival approaches to the treatment of psychological distress. The clinic setting is the post-apartheid turn in one image: the feud is no longer a tribal war but a contest of institutions, and the lovers must survive the systems built to cure them.

Martin Orkin's Shakespeare Against Apartheid (1987) is the classic account of the oppositional use of Shakespeare in South Africa, arguing that the plays were turned against the regime that had imported them, in classrooms, in township halls, and in prison cells. Natasha Distiller's Shakespeare and the Coconuts (2012) answers with a caution: the redemptive story, in which Shakespeare saves or civilizes his African readers, is itself a colonial narrative, and post-apartheid culture should be suspicious of any account in which the English playwright arrives as a gift. The adjudication between them is not a draw. Orkin wins on the documented uses, because the PACT tour, the township performances, and the prison readings are on the record. Distiller wins on the framing, because the uses were powerful precisely when Africans controlled them, and weak whenever the story was told as a gift from outside. The article's verdict follows that split: appropriation is the right word for the uses, and inheritance is the right word for the framing whenever the uses are narrated as a benefaction.

## Malawi 2016: Romio ndi Julieti and the Evidence of Audiences

Malawi gives the article its most carefully documented single production, and the documentation is of a rare kind: an audience study. In 2016 the director Amy Bonsall worked with students at Mzuzu University and actors from Nanzikambe Arts to stage Romio ndi Julieti, a Chichewa version of the play translated by the writer Onjezani Kenani, described by its makers as the first poetic, published translation of the play into Chichewa. The production played at Luwinga Secondary School, at Mzuzu University, and in Chingalire village near Lilongwe, where the audience numbered well over a thousand. Bonsall's Leeds doctoral thesis (2017) and her study in the journal African Theatre (2019) record the experiment in detail, and her later book, Shakespeare in Malawi: Power and Performance, consolidated a timeline of Shakespeare performance in the country from the colonial period onward.

The findings are the strongest single piece of evidence for the appropriation verdict. Bonsall reports that the three audiences, schoolchildren, university students, and rural community residents, reacted in more or less the same way as each other, and not far from the way a European audience would react to the same events, while the Chichewa text changed the performance from inside: actors working in the language for the first time on a production of this scale were freed to use Malawian performance forms, including physical comedy and dance, inside a production that carried the prestige of an elite literary form. The play in Chichewa was simultaneously more local and more legible than the play in English. The translation did not dilute the tragedy. It removed the tax that a foreign-language examination text levies on its audience, and it let the performers' own comic and choreographic skills into a text that English-language Malawian productions had kept at arm's length.

The sameness of the audiences' reactions, which Bonsall calls striking, needs a careful reading, because it can be made to serve the wrong conclusion. It does not show that the play is universal in the sense the imperial story required, a text whose meaning is fixed in London and merely received elsewhere. It shows that the events of the plot, a forbidden union, a street killing, a failed message, a double funeral, land with the same force on audiences who share the human materials the plot works with, and Bonsall's method, interviews, surveys, and observation across three publics, is what entitles the claim to be made at all. The same study records the differences that matter: the laughter lived in different places, the comedy broadened in Malawian forms, and the performers' command of the evening grew with the language. Universality of response and locality of performance are not rivals. The Malawian evidence holds them together, and it is the only African production of the play in this survey whose evidence is strong enough to hold them, which is why the article rests so much of its verdict on a village audience outside Lilongwe.

The production also sits inside a longer Malawian history that Bonsall's book reconstructs, a timeline of Shakespeare performance running from the colonial period to the present, including local adaptations of As You Like It and Macbeth and sustained attention to the role of women as performers and artists in Malawian Shakespeare. That context matters for grading the 2016 production's novelty. Malawi was not a Shakespeare vacuum into which a British director carried the first production. It was a country with its own performance traditions, its own university theatre culture, and its own prior adaptations, and Romio ndi Julieti was distinctive within that history for a narrower and more durable achievement: a complete, published, poetic translation of this play into Chichewa, tested before three different publics and measured by a researcher who published the measurements. Few African productions of any Shakespeare play carry evidence of that quality, and the map grades the entry accordingly.

The production's working method is part of its evidence. The Chichewa text was built over three years, and the staging was prepared in a three-week rehearsal period in which Mzuzu University students carried the full production, with the Chingalire village performance, on 9 April 2016, standing as the experiment's public test before an audience of well over a thousand people who had not bought tickets to an elite form but had gathered for a village event. The sequence matters: school hall, university, village green, in ascending order of distance from the colonial classroom where the play began its African life. A production that can hold the third audience has answered the only question that the appropriation debate finally asks, which is whether the play, in the local language and the local performance grammar, still works on the people it is said to belong to. At Chingalire, on the recorded evidence, it worked.

The Malawian case also carries the intercultural complication honestly. The production was made by a British director working inside a Malawian institution, and Bonsall's own writings are alert to the ethics of that arrangement: who chooses the play, who translates it, who owns the result. Her answer, in practice, was to build the production around a Malawian translator and Malawian performers and to let the audiences judge it. The audiences' verdict, as recorded, was enthusiasm. The article treats the case as appropriation with a foreign midwife rather than as a gift, because the Chichewa text, the performance forms, and the audience response were Malawian products even where the initiative was shared.

## Rwanda 2013: The Feud After Genocide

If Malawi is the article's best-documented production, Rwanda is its most charged. In the summer of 2013 the American nonprofit Youth Bridge Global worked with the Ishyo Arts Centre in Kigali to stage Romeo and Juliet with a company of thirty-two Rwandan actors aged seventeen to twenty-five, co-directed by Andrew Garrod and James G. Rice. The production was performed sixty percent in Kinyarwanda, twenty percent in French, and twenty percent in Shakespeare's English, with traditional Rwandan dance and music built into the staging, and it played seven free performances: at the SOS Technical School in Kigali, at Kimisagara in Kigali, at Gitarama, and at Butare. The organizers reported that the play had never been performed in Rwanda before. Juliet was played by Tete Divine, who had lost her father and siblings in the genocide of 1994, and Romeo was double-cast between Muhamed Nshimiyimana and Eric Shema. The project had been urged on Garrod by Rwanda's Minister of Health, Dr Agnes Binagwaho, who told him that the play he must put on was, of course, Romeo and Juliet.

The reasoning was explicit and, on its face, irresistible. A play in which two communities are reconciled only after their children die seemed to offer a theatrical metaphor for a country in which Hutu and Tutsi neighbours had to live together after the genocide, and in which, as the organizers noted, the production's young cast members from the two communities were in many cases interacting closely for the first time. The choice of minister matters to the record: the request came from the Minister of Health, the official whose portfolio held the genocide's longest aftermath, and her judgment that this play, and not another, was the one Rwanda needed was a judgment from inside the state's own work of repair. The production was, in that precise sense, commissioned by its subject. The play's own closing movement gave the framing its warrant: the Prince's judgment at the end falls on the city, not on the lovers, and the fathers' reconciliation is purchased with the children's lives. A production that asked Kigali audiences to watch that arithmetic in Kinyarwanda was staging an argument about the price of inherited hatred, in a city that knew the price. The tragedy's own arithmetic is stated by its judge in three words, "All are punished" (5.3), and the line fell, in Kigali, on a city that had conducted its own trials. The project had a pedigree, too, and the pedigree cuts both ways: Youth Bridge Global had previously mounted youth productions in Bosnia designed to bring together the children of the former warring factions, the Bosniak, Croat, and Serb communities, and the Rwanda plan applied the same template to a different aftermath. The template's portability is its strength as an organizing idea and its weakness as a reading of any particular country, because a method that fits every post-conflict society risks fitting none of them exactly, and the Kigali cast's own interventions, described below, were in part a correction of the template by the people inside it.

The production also left a trace in Rwandan education. A visiting researcher with the University of Rwanda drama programme in 2015, two years after the Kigali staging, was treated at a Rwandan high school to a student Romeo and Juliet that featured a bride in a mosquito-netting veil, a small, exact image of the play settling into local school culture on local materials. The detail is recorded in a scholarly account of drama teaching in Rwanda, and it matters to the map for the same reason the Kenyan syllabus story matters: the play's durable African homes have repeatedly been schools, and a school production improvised from a mosquito net is the appropriation verdict at its most granular, a text remade out of whatever a schoolroom has to hand.

### Q: How many free performances did the Kigali production give, and where?

Seven, all free: at the SOS Technical School and at Kimisagara in Kigali, then at Gitarama and at Butare, in August 2013, with a company drawn from different communities across the capital, in a tour designed to carry the production to its audiences rather than wait for the audiences to come to it.

The documentary film Rwanda and Juliet (2016), directed by Ben Proudfoot, complicated the story in the most useful way possible, and the article gives the complication full weight. The film records that when the cast learned they would not be paid, Tete Divine led a challenge to the visiting producers, arguing that Rwanda had already achieved reconciliation and that wealthy outsiders had no business arriving to manufacture it, while the cast's harder struggle was with poverty and the cost of education. The point is not that the production failed. The point is that the reconciliation frame, which read as obvious in Dartmouth and in the press release, read differently to the young Rwandans asked to embody it, and they renegotiated the production's meaning toward wages, schooling, and craft. That renegotiation is appropriation in its most honest form: the play was not received as a lesson but used as a workplace, a stage, and an argument, and the company's own priorities displaced the visitors' programme. A reception history that omitted the pay dispute would be telling the inheritance story, in which the gift arrives and is gratefully received. The dispute is in the record, and the record is better than the story.

The casting process had already announced the production's real subject before rehearsals began. The company of thirty-two was drawn from more than sixty auditioners at a Kigali high school, and the distinguished Rwandan actress Carole Karemera, ushering the visitors into the audition room, warned them of the panel's error before it happened: the young people in front of them were all Rwandans, whatever the visitors' categories said. Garrod and Rice's published account of the production preserves the warning because it governed everything that followed. A reconciliation production needs its cast sorted into the communities being reconciled, and the warning refused the sorting, insisting that the company's identity was national before it was ethnic. The play's own machinery made the same refusal in reverse: Verona's households are alike in dignity, and the tragedy consists of what the sorting costs. The trilingual design carried the argument into the text itself, with Kinyarwanda holding sixty percent of the performance, the language of the audience and of the company's daily life carrying the majority of the words, and the colonial and metropolitan languages, French and Shakespeare's English, reduced to minority shares. Ratios are arguments. A production in which English held sixty percent would have been an inheritance staged; at twenty percent, the English text had become one voice among three in a Rwandan room.

## Kampala and the Workshop Model; Nigeria and the Analogue

Uganda's documented staging history for the play is dominated by Makerere's teaching tradition and by collaborative productions of a newer kind. The German company fred, the Freies Ensemble Düsseldorf, mounted Romeo and Juliet in Kampala as a co-production with the House of Talent Cultural Performers and Bridgeworks, directed by Simon Eifeler of Germany with the Ugandan theatre poet Kagayi Ngobi supporting the acting work, breakdance and hip hop dance coached by Abrams Tekya of Breakdance Project Uganda, and percussion led by Ejuku Hitter Mark. The producer Ssebaggala Andrew Lwanga's casting account records more than seventy Ugandan performers showing interest in the two leading roles alone, and the auditions were run as a test of how the play sounds in Ugandan languages: Luganda, Runyoro, Rutooro, Rukiga, and Runyankole, with Nigeria's Igbo also heard in the room. His observation from the panel is the workshop model in one sentence: people act, come alive, and flow with feeling most naturally in their native language. The account does not give the production a year, and this article does not invent one. What it documents is a method, in which the translation question is not which single language will carry the play but which of many languages will carry which scene, a method that treats Uganda's multilingualism as a production resource rather than a problem.

The Kampala method and the Malawi method are the two poles of the play's African-language future, and the contrast is worth drawing exactly. Kenani's Chichewa Romio ndi Julieti is the translator model at its most classical: one writer, one language, one complete text, published and testable, in the line of Plaatje, Decker, Mahloane, and Nyerere. The Kampala auditions embody the workshop model: no single authorized translation, a company assembled across five or more languages, and the text distributed among the tongues its performers actually dream in, with dance and percussion carrying what the languages divide. The Kigali production sits between the poles, with its fixed ratio of three languages assigned in advance. Each model answers the inheritance charge differently. The translator model answers it with a book, permanent and citable. The workshop model answers it with an event, unrepeatable and owned by its room. The article's verdict accommodates both, because the test it applies is control rather than form: in Kampala as in Lilongwe, the languages were chosen by the makers, and the choosing is the appropriation.

Nigeria presents the opposite evidentiary situation: a strong tradition of plays that work the same ground, and a scholarly argument about what to call them. Zulu Sofola's Wedlock of the Gods (1972) sets lovers against family and ritual obligation in a Yoruba world, with a double death that reviewers and scholars have long compared to the Veronese ending. Femi Osofisan's Farewell to a Cannibal Rage (1986) returns to the same constellation of young love, elders, and communal violence. Survey writing on Nigerian drama has sometimes filed Sofola's play flatly among works adapted from Romeo and Juliet, alongside the same surveys' treatment of Ola Rotimi's Sophoclean borrowings and other transpositions. The Nigerian scholar Lekan Balogun, whose research programme is devoted to African recastings of Shakespeare, insists on precision: the two plays are echoes of the tragedy rather than adaptations of it, built on Yoruba narrative tradition and ethos, and a criticism that files them as versions mistakes their sources and their purposes. The article accepts Balogun's distinction and builds its map on it, marking the Nigerian entries as analogues, because the distinction is the same one the whole article observes between a play that arrives and a story that a culture tells in its own grammar.

The echo category deserves a defence, because it is the point where the survey's taxonomy does real critical work. An adaptation descends from its source: it takes the text, or the plot under instruction from the text, and reworks it, as Brink reworked the words and as Peteni reworked the plot. An echo shares a constellation, lovers, elders, lineage, a double death, without descent, because the constellation is one that Yoruba narrative tradition generates from its own materials, and the resemblance says more about the shape of the human story than about any borrowing. Collapsing the two classes, as the survey literature sometimes does when it files Wedlock of the Gods as an adaptation, flatters Shakespeare with other traditions' inventions and simultaneously demotes Sofola and Osofisan from authors to adaptors. Balogun's correction therefore protects both sides of the comparison: the Veronese play keeps its documented descendants, and the Nigerian plays keep their Yoruba parentage. For the article's verdict the analogues carry a distinctive weight, because a culture that tells the lovers' story from its own grammar has no need to inherit it, and the existence of the analogues is the standing proof that the feud-and-lovers pattern was never Europe's gift to Africa in the first place. What Europe's text added, where it was chosen, was a particular script, a set of scenes and speeches that African makers could spend. What it could never add was the story, which Yoruba tradition, like the Thembu and Hlubi history behind Peteni, already possessed. Nigeria's Shakespeare-stage record also continues to produce direct recastings of the play alongside the analogues, including the tradaptation Soyinka in the Eye of Shakespeare, directed by Balogun at the Lagos Theatre Festival in 2019, which wove the lovers and Macbeth into a collage with Soyinka's own Sidi, Lakunle, and Elesin Oba.

## Ethiopia, Sudan, and the Thin Places on the Map

An honest continental survey must spend a section on the places where the record for this play is thin, because the thinness is information. Ethiopia has one of the strongest Shakespeare traditions in Africa and a weak Romeo and Juliet file. Its central figure is Tsegaye Gabre-Medhin, the poet laureate and author of the imperial anthem's words, who translated Hamlet into Amharic and whose production of Othello won the favour of the Emperor Haile Selassie, a sequence Wilson-Lee retraces on his Ethiopian travels. Ethiopia also breaks the article's frame in an instructive way, and the break deserves a paragraph. The inheritance charge presumes a colonial power imposing its text on a colonized school system. Ethiopia, occupied by Italy for five years but never colonized, took its Shakespeare at an African imperial court, where the choosing agent was the emperor's own cultural establishment and the translator a national poet building Amharic drama. Whatever that channel is, it is not colonial inheritance, and its existence at the continent's north-east corner warns against any single-story account of how the plays travelled. The Ethiopian case grades as appropriation by a court rather than by a classroom or a company, and its thinness for this particular play is a fact about the court's taste, which ran to kings and generals, rather than about any barrier the lovers failed to cross. The distinction matters beyond Ethiopia, because it shows the article's test working on a case with no colonial school system in it at all: where the choosing was African from the start, the inheritance charge has nothing to attach to, and the only question left is the local one of which plays a culture wanted, which Ethiopia answered, as Tanzania and South Africa answered, in favour of the statecraft canon. The pattern is the translator pattern of the whole article in another region: the plays of state, jealousy, and kingship were translated and staged at the highest level of national culture, while the lovers' tragedy generated no comparably documented Ethiopian landmark in the sources consulted. Sudan appears in Wilson-Lee's itinerary at the other extreme of circumstance, with Shakespeare read even by boy soldiers in the Sudanese civil wars, a datum about the book's penetration rather than about any staging of this play. The francophone and lusophone records consulted were likewise thin for this specific tragedy, and the Arabic-language record belongs, by this series' division of labour, to the Arabic adaptations survey. The map therefore shows a continent whose documented Romeo and Juliet clusters in anglophone East and southern Africa, and the clustering reflects the history the article has told: the play travelled on the English syllabus, and where the syllabus went, the school stages, the translators, and the production records followed. Reporting the cluster as a cluster, rather than sprinkling undated claims across the regions the record leaves dark, is what distinguishes a map from a mural.

## The Critics' Quarrel: Inheritance or Appropriation

The scholarly argument about Shakespeare in Africa has a clear shape, and the play sits at its centre. On one side stands the inheritance charge, at its strongest in Ngũgĩ's Decolonising the Mind and in the Nairobi paper of 1968: the plays entered African life through a colonial school system designed to produce clerks and intermediaries, they arrived ranked above African literatures in the very syllabus that taught them, and their presence was an instrument of rule. On the other side stands the translators' and stage-makers' record, assembled by Mazrui for East Africa, by Wilson-Lee in his travels through Zanzibar, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Ethiopia, and Sudan, and by the South African scholarship of Orkin and his successors: heads of state translated the plays to build national languages, mission-press translators rendered them into Sesotho, school tours used them to argue with apartheid, and post-genocide companies used them to argue about reconciliation. Between the two sides stands Eldred D. Jones's early survey Othello's Countrymen (1965), written from Sierra Leone, which first mapped the African presence in the plays and the plays' presence in Africa as a single connected subject.

The francophone tradition gives the quarrel its most famous single exhibit, and it belongs to another play, which is itself a datum. The Folger survey notes that the rise of African Shakespeare adaptation followed decolonization's intellectual currents, the work of Frantz Fanon in Algeria, Léopold Sédar Senghor in Senegal, and Aimé Césaire in Martinique, and that of these only Césaire attempted a Shakespeare adaptation, remaking The Tempest as Une Tempête. The francophone landmark is a Tempest, a play about a colonizer and his island, chosen by a Martinican poet for a quarrel about colonialism itself. No comparably canonical francophone Romeo and Juliet stands in the record consulted, and the absence fits the article's pattern from the other side of the language line: where writers reached for Shakespeare as a weapon in the argument about empire, they reached for the plays of power, Prospero's island and Caesar's Rome, and the lovers were left to the schoolrooms, the translators of nation-building projects, and the companies with a feud of their own to stage.

### Q: What did Ngũgĩ argue in Decolonising the Mind about the language of African literature?

Ngũgĩ argued in Decolonising the Mind (1986) that African writers should write in African languages rather than in the colonial languages, since language carries a people's culture, memory, and world view, and his own fiction moved into Gikuyu on that principle.

The adjudication turns on control, and it is worth stating the test precisely. A text is inherited when its language, its venue, and its purpose are fixed by someone else and the local role is reception. A text is appropriated when local agents choose the language, the venue, and the purpose, and the text's role is use. Tested against the record, the colonial schoolroom fails the test and the translators pass it. Nyerere choosing blank verse for Caesar, Mahloane's Sesotho volume from a mission press, Brink's Afrikaans text staged by a provincial arts council, Kenani's Chichewa verse in a Lilongwe village, and the Kigali company arguing about wages inside a reconciliation production are all cases in which the language, the venue, or the purpose was locally chosen, and usually all three. The school syllabus case fails because the choices were made in London and Cambridge, and Kenya's own expulsion of the play after independence proves that Kenyans experienced it as imposed. Kenya's restoration of the play in 1992 then completes the test: the same state that had expelled the text brought it back when the choosing was its own. The verdict is therefore appropriation, with the inheritance charge retained as the accurate description of the schoolroom channel alone.

The hardest case for the test is the first entry on the map, and the article does not dodge it. Steere's Zanzibar volume of 1867 was a missionary's project, chosen in a mission house, printed for a mission's purposes, and on a strict application of the test it grades as inheritance. Yet the hands on the press were African hands, the language was the coast's own, and the book's afterlife belonged to Swahili readers rather than to the mission that issued it, which is why the map grades the entry by its documented print record and lets the article's argument carry the nuance in prose. Mixed cases are the rule in this history rather than the exception: Brink worked inside a state arts council, Bonsall inside a British university's research programme, the Kigali company inside an American nonprofit's template. The test survives the mixtures because it asks about the direction of control within each arrangement, not about the purity of the arrangement. In each mixed case the record shows the local partner bending the project toward local language, local venue, or local purpose, and the bending, documented case by case, is what the word appropriation is doing in this article. A survey that demanded purity would have to discard most of its own map. A survey that ignored control would have to call the colonial syllabus an African achievement. The graded verdict is the path between those two failures, and the grades on the map are its working form.

Wilson-Lee adds a second, quieter correction to the inheritance story. His East African research found Shakespeare at every stage of the region's passage from colony to independence: printed by freed slaves in Zanzibar, performed by Indian railway workers in Mombasa, read by the first generation of African revolutionaries, and translated by a president. His podcast conversation with Ngũgĩ himself, recorded for the Folger Shakespeare Library's Shakespeare Unlimited series, is a fitting coda to the quarrel: the foremost African critic of the colonial curriculum discussing, with evident knowledge and no submission, the plays his schoolboy self had read at Alliance. The quarrel was never between knowledge and rejection. It was between the schoolroom that imposed the play and the stages that chose it, and the stages have won on the evidence.

Mazrui's contribution deserves its own precision, because his title states the article's whole problem: Shakespeare in Africa lives between English and Swahili literature, between the language of the syllabus and the language of the nation, and the same play can sit on both sides of that line in the same decade. Kenya read the play in English because the syllabus said so, expelled it because the syllabus had said so, and readmitted it when the syllabus became Kenyan. Tanzania's president translated two other plays into Swahili to prove the national language's capacity, and the prestige of that proof attached itself to this play in the popular imagination, which is how a survey brief can come to credit Nyerere with a Swahili Romeo and Juliet that does not exist. The error is diagnostic. It happens because the two channels, English inheritance and Swahili appropriation, are constantly confused in summary writing, and the correction of the error is the cleanest possible demonstration of why the channels must be kept apart. Jones's early survey belongs in the same paragraph of honours: writing from Sierra Leone in 1965, before the abolition paper and before most of the translations in this article existed, he mapped the African presence inside the plays and the plays' presence in Africa as one connected subject, and the scholarship since has been filling in his map. Distiller's title makes the South African version of the same point from the receiving end: the coconut, in South African slang the person deemed black outside and white inside, is the figure the redemptive Shakespeare story quietly produces, the assimilated reader grateful for the gift, and her book's suspicion falls on any production history that needs that figure. The PACT school audiences arguing about prejudice, and the Kigali company arguing about wages, are the recorded refutation of the figure, because grateful recipients do not argue.

The scholarly infrastructure behind the quarrel is itself African in a way the inheritance story would not predict. The standard survey chapter on Shakespeare and Africa in the Cambridge Companion to Shakespeare on Stage is the work of Martin Banham, Roshni Mooneeram, and Jane Plastow, scholars of African theatre whose account treats the continent's Shakespeare as a branch of African performance studies rather than as an outpost of English studies. Plastow's hand reaches into this article's best-documented production as well, since she supervised Amy Bonsall's doctoral research on the Malawian staging at the University of Leeds. The line from the Cambridge survey chapter to the Chingalire village performance runs through African theatre scholarship training its own researchers, and it is one more channel in which the choosing, the framing, and the judging of African Shakespeare are done by the field's Africanists rather than delivered from outside it.

## The Languages of the Play: A Census of the Record

A census of the languages in which the record documents this play on the continent is the article's argument in tabular form, and it is worth stating in prose before the map grades it. The play exists in print in Southern Sotho (Mahloane, 1964) and in Afrikaans (Brink, 1975, with Du Toit's school version beside it), in a published poetic translation in Chichewa (Kenani, staged 2016), as a majority-Kinyarwanda stage text in Rwanda (2013), in Xhosa at one remove through the translation of Peteni's novel (1980), in Swahili only as prose tale from 1867, and in English and French as the schoolroom and minority stage languages of its own productions. Around that core stands the wider field of African Shakespeare in which the play's translators and producers were formed: Setswana (Plaatje), Krio (Decker), Zulu (Masinga's seven versions), Amharic (Tsegaye), and the five Ugandan languages tested at the Kampala auditions. The census explains the article's central correction from a new angle. The languages with complete, famous, named-author translations of Shakespeare are the languages of the statecraft plays, because translation was a state-building act and the state reached for the plays about the state. The lovers' tragedy, whose subject is the household rather than the republic, accumulated its African languages by a different route, through schools, mission presses, provincial arts councils, village greens, and festival stages. Its language list is shorter, later, and more theatrical, and every entry on it was earned by use rather than conferred by prestige. A reader who understands why Nyerere translated Caesar and not the lovers has understood the whole map, because the map is the working out of that single choice across a century and a half.

The negative census is as instructive as the positive one. In the sources consulted, no complete published translation of this play into Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, isiZulu, isiXhosa, or Setswana is documented, although several of those languages hold distinguished translations of other plays and although Yoruba tradition generated the continent's most discussed analogues. The absences cluster where the play's life was theatrical or analogical rather than bibliographic, and they warn against a flattering completionism: a survey that listed a translation for every major language would be decorating the record, not reporting it. The same caution applies to the map's geography. The documented cluster sits in anglophone East and southern Africa because that is where the syllabus, the mission presses, and the theatre archives jointly operated, and the survey's confidence is therefore a confidence about the documented record, openly bounded by where documentation exists. South Africa's density in the map makes the archival point from inside the evidence: the country's record is the fullest partly because its theatre culture is rich and partly because the Encyclopedia of South African Theatre, Film, Media and Performance at Stellenbosch University has systematically gathered what other countries' records leave scattered. A map is only ever as full as its archives, and the honest survey says which of its fullness is the continent's and which is the archivists'.

The Kampala row is the method in action, and it is worth a sentence of its own. ## Verdict: Appropriation, Graded

Africa's Romeo and Juliet is not a European bequest gratefully received. It is a text that arrived by imposition and survived by seizure, and the seizures are the documented core of its African life. The statesman who defines its East African prestige translated two other plays, and his prestige nonetheless carried this one, because the act he modelled, putting Shakespeare into an African language as a claim about that language's worth, is the act that the continent's makers repeated in Sesotho in 1964, in Afrikaans in 1975, in Chichewa in 2016, and in a Kinyarwanda-majority staging in 2013. The school syllabus that imposed the play was dismantled by the independent states that inherited it, and one of them, Kenya, readmitted this play alone on its own terms a generation after expelling it. The feud has been mapped onto Thembu and Hlubi grazing land, onto apartheid's categories in a school hall, onto the aftermath of genocide in Kigali, and onto a psychiatric clinic in Stellenbosch, and in each case the mapping was done by the people who lived with the division being mapped. That is the definition of appropriation the record supports, and the article's ruling is that the African reception of Romeo and Juliet is an appropriation graded by control: complete where Africans chose the language, the venue, and the use, and incomplete only where the play still travels as an unexamined examination text, in which channel the inheritance charge stands unanswered.

The exhibits can be ranked, and ranking them is the honest close. Strongest stand the cases where all three choices, language, venue, and purpose, were local at once: Kenani's Chichewa text played to a Lilongwe village, and the Kigali company's trilingual staging, contested from inside by its own cast. Next stand the cases where two of the three were local: Brink's Afrikaans text inside a state arts council, Mahloane's Sesotho volume inside a mission press, Peteni's novel inside a commercial list and a state broadcaster. Weakest, and accurately described by the inheritance charge, stand the channels where none of the choices was local: the colonial syllabus in its imposed decades, and the Cold War decades in which oil companies rented the plays' prestige. The ranking is the verdict's final form, because a reception history that cannot distinguish Chingalire from a licensing file in Mombasa has not yet begun to describe its subject. The play that a ship's crew performed to itself off Sierra Leone in 1607 is, by the record assembled here, a play that African translators, teachers, directors, choreographers, novelists, schoolchildren, and one arguing Juliet in Kigali have since taken into their own hands, in their own languages, for their own arguments. The taking is the story, and the story is documented.

A verdict that cannot say what would change it is a slogan, so the article ends with its own falsification conditions. The appropriation ruling would weaken if the archive produced evidence that the translations and localizations were commissioned, censored, or received as impositions by the audiences they addressed: a Sesotho Romeo le Juliet remaindered unread, a Chichewa production booed by the village it was built for, a Kigali company that experienced the staging as an occupying exercise. The record consulted contains the opposite at each tested point, a revision undertaken voluntarily in Dar es Salaam, a village audience of a thousand outside Lilongwe, and a Rwandan cast confident enough to argue about wages and meaning in front of a documentary camera. Arguments are evidence of ownership, and the archive of African Romeo and Juliet is, on inspection, an archive of arguments. That is the last reason the verdict stands as graded appropriation: the continent did not merely receive the play and did not merely refuse it. It argued with it, in print, in syllabuses, in rehearsal rooms, and in one case in front of the cameras, and a text that a continent argues with in its own languages is a text that continent owns.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which two plays did Julius Nyerere translate into Swahili, and when were they published?

Nyerere translated Julius Caesar, published by Oxford University Press in Dar es Salaam in 1963 and issued in his own revision in 1969, and The Merchant of Venice, published in 1969. No Romeo and Juliet appears in his bibliography. The two plays, one about the killing of a ruler and one about mercy argued in a courtroom, gave the young Tanzanian state a vocabulary for its own founding questions, and their prestige is what later attached his name, inaccurately, to every Swahili Shakespeare including this tragedy.

### Q: What verse form did Nyerere devise for his Swahili Julius Caesar?

Nyerere devised a new blank verse for Swahili built on a sixteen-syllable line, an attempt to give the language a measure that could carry Shakespeare's dramatic line without importing English stress patterns or breaking the norms of Swahili poetry. His preface presents the device modestly, as one law of traditional poetry adapted to a new purpose, and his 1969 revision of the Caesar translation reworked the text to make it more amenable to Swahili readers and to performance. The device mattered beyond the two books, because it showed that the question of Shakespeare in an African language was a technical question about verse, not a question about whether the language was adequate.

### Q: When did Shakespeare first appear in print in Swahili, and in what form?

Shakespeare first appeared in print in Swahili in 1867, in Hadithi za Kiingereza, English Tales, a slim volume of prose stories drawn from Charles and Mary Lamb's Tales from Shakespeare, printed on Zanzibar. The form is the finding: the first Swahili Shakespeare was narrative prose retold for young readers, not verse drama, and one of the first books ever printed in the language was a Shakespearean one. The playtexts, and this play in particular, reached Swahili speakers mainly through English school editions and the stage, a distribution that held for more than a century after Steere's volume.

### Q: Who printed the first Swahili Shakespeare, and who worked his press?

The volume was printed at the Universities' Mission to Central Africa press at Kiungani, near Stone Town on Zanzibar, under Edward Steere, the missionary bishop who translated the tales. The press was worked by former slave boys whom the mission had educated, a fact Edward Wilson-Lee's research recovered and one that complicates any simple account of the mission imprint: the book was a European project physically produced by African labour, in an African language, for African readers. The article treats that mixed origin as the African Shakespeare story in miniature, with control, not origin, deciding the verdict.

### Q: Why did post-independence Kenya remove the play from its schools, and when did it return?

Kenya removed the play as part of the Africanization drive that swept nearly all Western literature out of the school syllabus after independence, on the reasoning that a curriculum centred on English literature was a colonial residue unsuited to a new nation. The play returned in 1992, when Romeo and Juliet was restored to the syllabus, a restoration Alamin Mazrui records in his study of Shakespeare between English and Swahili literature. The two decisions, expulsion and readmission, are the article's cleanest evidence that the play's status changed with the choosing: imposed, it was rejected; chosen, it was kept.

### Q: Which Shakespeare plays did Sol Plaatje translate into Setswana, and which of his translations are lost?

Plaatje translated A Comedy of Errors, published as Diphosho-phosho in 1930, and Julius Caesar, published in Setswana in 1937. His Setswana versions of The Merchant of Venice, Othello, and Much Ado About Nothing were lost, and survive, if at all, only beyond recovery. The pattern of his choices mirrors Nyerere's a generation later: identity, conspiracy, and the city, the plays of statecraft and law, chosen to prove that an African language could carry the most demanding European texts with style. Plaatje translated no Romeo and Juliet, and the lovers' tragedy waited decades longer for its African-language texts than the statecraft plays waited for theirs.

### Q: Who translated the play into Southern Sotho, and when was that version published?

I. Mahloane translated the play into Southern Sotho as Romeo le Juliet, published by the Mazenod mission press in 1964. The volume is the earliest printed translation of Romeo and Juliet into an African language that the record consulted for this survey documents, and its route to readers is part of its meaning: a Catholic mission press in Lesotho, not a commercial publisher or a state house, carried the tragedy into Sesotho. Mission publishing had carried Shakespeare into Swahili print as prose tales in 1867, and the Sesotho verse tragedy of 1964 belongs to the same long mission-press tradition.

### Q: Who wrote the Afrikaans Romeo and Juliet that PACOFS staged?

André P. Brink, the novelist and critic, wrote the Afrikaans translation Die Tragedie van Romeo en Juliet, published by Human and Rousseau in 1975. The Performing Arts Council of the Orange Free State staged it in 1977 and again in 1981, the later production directed by Sandra Kotzé with Gerben Kamper and Rina Nienaber as the lovers. Brink's version matters because it was a literary translation by a major Afrikaans writer, made inside the apartheid state's own arts council system and yet part of the Afrikaans literary culture that also produced the era's dissenting fiction, a reminder that the language of the regime was also a language its critics used.

### Q: How did R. L. Peteni rebuild the feud between two peoples of the Eastern Cape?

Peteni's novel Hill of Fools (1976) rebuilds the feud as the tension between the Thembu and the Hlubi peoples of the Eastern Cape, with lovers caught across the line as Shakespeare's are. The Hlubi had retreated into the region in the early nineteenth century to escape Shaka's wars, so the novel's divided ground carries a real history of displacement rather than a stage quarrel. The book was translated into Xhosa in 1980 and televised by the SABC as Aba Kwazidenge in January 1996. Peteni used none of Shakespeare's words, and the novel's success shows the plot's structure, two young people destroyed by an inherited group hatred, travelling on its own.

### Q: Which company ran the theatre-in-education Romeo and Juliet, and who directed it?

The Performing Arts Council of the Transvaal's Playwork programme ran the tour, and Peter Terry directed it, in the 1970s. The production used the tragedy as a springboard for audience debate about prejudice in South Africa and in Northern Ireland, pairing two societies that taught children to inherit an enemy. The format put the play's central mechanism, a feud children did not start, in front of school audiences as a question to argue rather than a story to absorb. Under a regime that policed assembly and speech, a state-funded tour inviting debate on prejudice was a use of Shakespeare that the inheritance account cannot explain.

### Q: Who choreographed the dance version performed at Grahamstown in 2009?

Dada Masilo choreographed Romeo and Juliette, performed at the National Arts Festival in Grahamstown in July 2009 and at the Baxter Theatre in Cape Town in October 2009. The dance version discards the verse altogether and carries the feud in bodies, partnering, and refusal, which makes it a test of how much of the tragedy survives the loss of its language. The article's answer is that the structure survives entire: two houses, a public brawl, a secret union, and a double death need no words to be recognized by an audience that knows the story, and Masilo's audiences knew it from school if from nowhere else.

### Q: Who translated the play into Chichewa for the 2016 Malawian production?

The writer Onjezani Kenani translated the play into Chichewa, in the version staged as Romio ndi Julieti in 2016 and described by its makers as the first poetic, published translation of the play into the language. The production was directed by Amy Bonsall with students from Mzuzu University and actors from Nanzikambe Arts, and played at Luwinga Secondary School, at Mzuzu University, and in Chingalire village. Kenani's translation is the article's clearest modern case of the translators' tradition: a national writer putting the play into the national language so that the language, and not only the play, is enlarged by the act.

### Q: What did the three Malawian audiences show about performing the play in Chichewa?

Amy Bonsall's audience study, published in African Theatre in 2019, found that the school, university, and village audiences reacted in more or less the same way as each other, and not far from the way European audiences react to the same events, while the Chichewa text changed the performance from within. Actors working in the language could bring Malawian performance forms, including physical comedy and dance, into a production carrying elite literary prestige, and audiences met the tragedy without the tax of a foreign-language school text. The finding supports the article's verdict: what localization changes is not the story's effect but the performers' and audience's ownership of it.

### Q: Who urged the Rwandan production into being, and what did she ask for?

Dr Agnes Binagwaho, Rwanda's Minister of Health, urged the Dartmouth professor Andrew Garrod to bring a production to Rwanda and told him that the play he must put on was Romeo and Juliet. The request came from inside the Rwandan state, not from the visiting company, and it specified the play for its story of communities reconciled only after their children's deaths. The 2013 production followed, staged with the Ishyo Arts Centre in Kigali in Kinyarwanda, French, and English. The minister's instruction is a useful corrective to accounts in which reconciliation Shakespeare arrives in Africa as a foreign idea: in this case the choice of play was a Rwandan official's.

### Q: What did the Kampala auditions test about the play's languages?

The Kampala auditions for the Düsseldorf co-production tested how the play sounds in Uganda's own languages, with aspirants heard in Luganda, Runyoro, Rutooro, Rukiga, and Runyankole, alongside Nigeria's Igbo, as more than seventy performers sought the two leading roles. The producer Ssebaggala Andrew Lwanga's account of the panel's work records the discovery that performers came alive, and flowed with feeling, most naturally in their native languages, and the casting process treated Uganda's multilingualism as the production's central resource rather than as an obstacle to a single authorized text.

### Q: When did Thomas Decker begin translating Shakespeare into Krio, and with which play?

Decker began his Krio translations in 1964 with Julius Caesar, the play of conspiracy and republican virtue, following the same choice of statecraft over romance that Plaatje and Nyerere made in Setswana and Swahili. The Folger Shakespeare Library's survey of Shakespeare in Africa explains the motive: the Krio versions promoted the language itself as one that could handle the most serious themes, and they carried Shakespeare to Sierra Leonean audiences who had not received a French or English education. The lovers' tragedy was not Decker's vehicle, and Sierra Leone's place in this article is as part of the translators' pattern rather than as a home of the play.

### Q: What challenge did the Rwandan cast raise against the production's reconciliation frame?

The cast, led in the dispute by Juliet's actor Tete Divine, challenged the frame when they learned they would not be paid, arguing, as the documentary Rwanda and Juliet (2016) records, that Rwanda had already achieved reconciliation and that wealthy outsiders had no business manufacturing it, while their own pressing problems were poverty and the cost of education. The challenge did not wreck the production; it corrected its meaning. The article treats the dispute as the production's most honest moment, because the young Rwandans refused the role of grateful recipients and restated the play as their workplace and their argument.

### Q: Why does the Nigerian scholar Lekan Balogun call Sofola's and Osofisan's plays echoes rather than adaptations?

Balogun argues that Zulu Sofola's Wedlock of the Gods (1972) and Femi Osofisan's Farewell to a Cannibal Rage (1986) are built on Yoruba narrative tradition and ethos, and that their resemblance to the Veronese tragedy is an echo rather than a descent, because their sources, obligations, and resolutions are Yoruba rather than Shakespearean. Filing them as adaptations would credit Shakespeare with plays whose machinery belongs to another tradition. The article accepts the ruling and marks the Nigerian entries in its map as analogues, a class that matters to the argument: a culture telling its own story of lovers and lineages is not receiving the play but rivalling it.

The InsightCrunch Africa map records the documented translations, stagings, adaptations, and analogues of the play across the continent, from Steere's Swahili prose tales of 1867 to the Kigali staging of 2013, and grades each entry by the strength of its record: documented print, performance, programme, syllabus, or scholarly evidence, with analogues marked as analogues and uncertain dates left unstated. The grading is the point. A map that promoted echoes into adaptations or guesses into dates would repeat the error this article corrects in the Nyerere case, and the map holds its entries to the standard the correction sets.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch Africa map to argue that the African reception was an appropriation rather than an inheritance?

An essay should work from the map's entries in which the language, the venue, and the purpose were locally chosen, Mahloane's Sesotho volume, Brink's Afrikaans stage text, Kenani's Chichewa translation, and the Kigali company's trilingual staging, and set them against the map's schoolroom entries, where the choices were made elsewhere. The contrast is the argument: inheritance names the channel of arrival, while appropriation names what African translators, directors, teachers, and audiences did with the arrival. The map's grades let the essay weight its evidence, resting the claim on documented productions and translations rather than on the prestige of famous names.
