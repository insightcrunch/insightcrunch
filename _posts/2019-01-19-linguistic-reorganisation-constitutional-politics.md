On 1 November 1956 the States Reorganisation Act redrew the internal map of India, and the standard account of the states reorganisation of 1956 treats that redrawing as an act of rational administration: a commission studied the problem, a report recommended a solution, and Parliament tidied a messy inheritance of princely states and British provinces into fourteen states and six union territories. That account is accurate in every particular and false in its overall impression, because the tidying was the least consequential part of what happened. What the Act of 1956 actually did was constitutional. It settled, after a decade of official refusal, that language, and with language the cultural identity of a region, was a legitimate ground on which the Indian Union could be divided into states. A federation whose founders had designed its units for administrative convenience acquired, by statute, units defined by who their people understood themselves to be. Every later demand for a new state, from the Punjabi Suba of 1966 to the Telangana movement that ended in bifurcation in 2014, argued from the precedent that 1956 created, and the reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir in 2019 was executed with the same parliamentary instrument the 1956 settlement had normalised.

## The Administrative Story and What It Hides

The conventional narrative runs through three documents. On 29 December 1953 the Government of India appointed the States Reorganisation Commission, chaired by Syed Fazl Ali, a former judge of the Supreme Court, with Hriday Nath Kunzru and Sardar K. M. Panikkar as members. The Commission toured the country, received memoranda numbering in the thousands, and submitted its report on 30 September 1955. Parliament then passed the States Reorganisation Act of 1956, which took effect on 1 November 1956 alongside the Seventh Amendment to the Constitution, abolishing the classification of states into Part A, Part B and Part C categories and producing a union of fourteen states and six union territories. Told this way, the episode reads as the republic's most successful exercise in federal housekeeping, and generations of textbooks have told it exactly this way.

The narrative hides its own beginning. The Commission of December 1953 was not appointed because administrators in Delhi had concluded that the map was untidy. It was appointed eleven weeks after the creation of Andhra State on 1 October 1953, and Andhra State had been conceded because a Gandhian named Potti Sriramulu fasted to death in Madras in December 1952 and the Telugu districts rose in disorder behind his funeral. The sequence matters more than the paperwork. A political mobilisation, conducted in the idiom of linguistic identity and paid for with a life, forced the first breach in the founding settlement on state boundaries; the Commission was the instrument by which the Union government converted an emergency concession into a general policy it could control. Administration followed politics at every step, and the form the administration took, a commission of eminent persons hearing evidence, was designed to slow the politics down and spread its costs. None of this appears in the housekeeping version, which begins with the Commission as though the Commission had been the cause rather than the consequence.

The second thing the narrative hides is the constitutional character of the change. Reorganising states in India is not an ordinary legislative act that happens to concern geography. Articles 2, 3 and 4 of the Constitution give Parliament the power to form new states, alter boundaries and rename states by ordinary law, and Article 4 declares that such a law is not to be deemed a constitutional amendment for the purposes of Article 368. The founders placed the map inside Parliament's ordinary competence because they expected the map to be provisional. What they did not decide, because they could not agree, was the principle by which Parliament should use that power. Between 1947 and 1953 the executive's answer, given by committee after committee, was that language must not be that principle. In 1956 the same Parliament, exercising the same Articles, answered the opposite way. A reversal of the operative principle of federal design, carried out through the Constitution's own machinery and then applied for sixty years, is constitutional politics in the strictest sense, and it is the argument of this article that the 1956 Act should be read that way: the Act was not federal housekeeping; it was the constitutional politics of identity-based federalism whose consequences ran for sixty years.

The federal architecture the Act transformed deserves a moment of precision, because the transformation is invisible without it. The Constitution that commenced on 26 January 1950 organised India into states that were largely the cartographic residue of conquest and treaty: nine governor's provinces, the princely unions and states absorbed after 1947, and centrally administered fragments, graded by their route into the Union rather than by any shared characteristic of their populations. Madras Presidency's successor state contained Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam and Kannada speakers; Bombay State contained Marathi and Gujarati speakers; the Punjab contained Punjabi and Hindi speakers whose census returns had become a communal battleground. The map of 1950 was a map of how the territory had been acquired. The map of 1956 was, in large though not complete measure, a map of who lived on the territory and what language they spoke at home. Readers who want the full architecture of the founding design should begin with [the asymmetric federal architecture that 1956 constitutionalised in identity-based form](/2019/01/03/federalism-indian-constitution-explained/), because the asymmetry the founders built for exceptional cases became, after 1956, the ordinary grammar of Indian federalism.

## An Unfinished Map: The Deferral the Founders Chose

The Constituent Assembly knew the linguistic question was coming, because the Indian National Congress had carried it for a generation. At its Nagpur session of 1920 the Congress reorganised its own provincial committees on linguistic lines, creating units such as Andhra, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Gujarat within the party, and the Nehru Report of 1928 endorsed the redistribution of provinces by language as a desirable principle for a free India. The demand also had an administrative precedent under British rule: Orissa, constituted on 1 April 1936 out of Oriya-speaking tracts of Bihar, Madras and the Central Provinces, was the first province formed substantially on the linguistic principle, the product of the Utkal Sammilani movement led by Madhusudan Das from 1903 onward. A generation of nationalist politicians had therefore promised linguistic provinces to their followers, in the party's own organisational practice, for thirty years before independence. The promise was part of the movement's inheritance.

Partition broke the promise's spell. The division of 1947, executed on religious lines in Punjab and Bengal, taught the founding generation to fear any principle that sorted Indians into states by an identity marker, because the sorting of 1947 had arrived with mass killing and the largest forced migration of the century. Language was not religion, and the founders knew the difference; Gandhi himself had supported linguistic redistribution. But the political psychology of 1947 and 1948 treated every identity-based claim on territory as a spark near the same powder. This was [the Partition-induced caution on community-based federalism that the founding period had imposed and 1956 began relaxing](/2019/01/08/partition-constitutional-consequences-india/), and it explains why men who had organised Congress provinces by language in the 1920s refused, as constitution-makers in the late 1940s, to write language into the federal design.

The Assembly's institutional answer was deferral by design. Articles 2 to 4 left the map to Parliament, which meant the question could be reopened without amending the Constitution's federal chapter, and the Assembly then declined to conduct the reopening itself. The linguistic question sat inside [the founding's deliberate deferral of the linguistic-states question that 1956 partially resolved](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/): one of a set of founding questions, alongside the uniform civil code and the precise balance of the directive principles against enforceable rights, that the founders consciously left to their successors because settlement in 1949 would have fractured the coalition that made the Constitution itself possible. Deferral was not indecision. It was a technique, and the technique had a price that fell due within six years.

What the Assembly did settle was the official-language question, and the contrast between the two settlements is instructive. On 14 September 1949 the Assembly adopted the Munshi-Ayyangar formula: Hindi in the Devanagari script as the official language of the Union, English continuing for fifteen years, and the Eighth Schedule listing the major languages of India for purposes of representation on language bodies. The founders could compromise on the language of administration because administration has a centre that can hold a compromise in place. They could not compromise on the language of territory, because a territorial compromise would have required drawing lines, and every line created winners who would demand execution and losers who would organise against it. The map stayed unfinished because finishing it was the one task the founding coalition could not survive performing. That judgement, defensible in 1949, hardened into doctrine in the two reports to which this article turns next, and doctrine is harder to reverse than a judgement: reversing it required a death.

## Two Warnings: The Dhar Commission and the JVP Committee

On 17 June 1948 the President of the Constituent Assembly, Rajendra Prasad, appointed the Linguistic Provinces Commission under S. K. Dhar, a retired judge of the Allahabad High Court, with Jagat Narain Lal and Panna Lal as members. The Dhar Commission reported on 10 December 1948, and its recommendation was unambiguous: linguistic provinces should not be formed. The Commission argued that the administrative convenience of the existing units, the needs of national unity in a fragile moment, and the dangers of provincial sub-nationalism all pointed the same way, and it suggested that if reorganisation ever became unavoidable it should proceed on grounds of geography, finance and administration rather than language. The report's deeper move was to redefine the question. The Congress tradition had treated linguistic provinces as the democratic completion of the national movement, the alignment of political boundaries with the cultural communities that composed the nation. Dhar treated them as a concession to sentiment that a responsible state should resist. The first official word on the subject after independence was therefore not a postponement but a rejection on principle.

The political leadership ratified the rejection within four months. At the Jaipur session of the Congress in December 1948 the party appointed a committee of Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya, remembered by its initials as the JVP Committee, to examine the Dhar recommendations in the light of the changed conditions after Partition. The JVP report, submitted on 1 April 1949, accepted the substance of Dhar's position while adjusting its tone. The committee held that the time was not ripe for linguistic provinces, that the security and consolidation of the new state came first, and that the question could be revisited after a passage of years, with any eventual reorganisation requiring the consent of the people affected and the subordination of linguistic claims to national unity. The adjustment mattered. Where Dhar had rejected the principle, JVP deferred its application, and a deferral by [the Patel-Nehru caution on linguistic states that the JVP Committee documented](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/) kept the door technically open while ensuring that no one in authority intended to walk through it.

Read together, the two reports constructed the doctrine that governed until 1953: language was an illegitimate basis for state formation in the official reasoning of the Indian state, whatever the Congress had promised before 1947 and whatever the Constitution's silence permitted. The doctrine had respectable arguments behind it. India in 1949 contained hundreds of princely accessions still being digested, a war in Kashmir, a police action in Hyderabad concluded only in September 1948, and refugee populations in two great cities. The men who signed the JVP report had integrated the princely states and fought the first Kashmir war; their caution was the caution of administrators who had watched one identity-based partition nearly destroy the state they were building. Granville Austin, in the first volume of his constitutional history, treated this founding caution as part of the Assembly's broader achievement of holding a diverse polity together by refusing premature settlements. The caution deserves that respect. What it could not do was survive contact with democratic politics, because the doctrine denied the legitimacy of the very demand that electoral competition in the Telugu, Marathi, Malayalam and Kannada regions was about to make irresistible. A doctrine that requires voters to stop wanting what they manifestly want has a limited life in a democracy holding elections, and the first general election of 1951 and 1952 returned a Parliament in which the demand for Andhra was pressed by elected representatives rather than by petitioners. The doctrine's collapse, when it came, came through the oldest instrument in the Gandhian repertoire.

## The Fast: Potti Sriramulu and the Making of Andhra State

Potti Sriramulu was a Gandhian worker from Nellore district who had participated in the salt satyagraha of 1930 and had fasted before, in 1946, for the opening of temples in Madras Presidency to Harijan worshippers. On 19 October 1952, in the house of Bulusu Sambamurthy in Madras city, he began a fast unto death for a separate Andhra province carved out of the Telugu-speaking districts of Madras State, with Madras city claimed for Andhra. The demand he carried was old: the Andhra Mahajana Sangam and successive Andhra provincial Congress committees had pressed it since 1913, and the Andhra legislators in the Madras Assembly had made it a standing grievance. What Sriramulu added was a deadline written on his own body. Nehru, whose correspondence in those weeks shows a Prime Minister determined not to concede statehood under the pressure of a fast, held the government's line while the fast lengthened. Sriramulu died on the night of 15 December 1952, and the Telugu districts answered with strikes, arson against government property and deaths in police firing. On 19 December 1952 Nehru announced to Parliament that a separate Andhra State would be formed. The first linguistic state in independent India was conceded within four days of one man's death, after four years in which two commissions of the state itself had explained why it should not exist.

The correspondence between Nehru and C. Rajagopalachari, the Chief Minister of Madras, in those months is the under-cited record of how the concession actually happened, and it survives in the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library archives. Rajagopalachari, who had returned to office in April 1952 to manage a Madras State whose Assembly arithmetic had failed to produce a stable ministry, wrote to Delhi in the idiom of an administrator reporting a province becoming ungovernable on the language question; Nehru replied in the idiom of a Prime Minister guarding a principle against a method. The exchange shows the doctrine of 1948 and 1949 dying of administrative causes. Once Madras's own Chief Minister treated the Andhra demand as a fact of governance rather than an error of sentiment, the JVP position had no provincial government left to enforce it, and the Union's refusal had become a refusal to hear its own appointee. Ramachandra Guha's narrative in India After Gandhi (2007) reconstructs this trigger sequence with the fullest archival texture available, and its evidentiary weight is one reason the political reading of 1956 commands the field: the documents show pressure, not analysis, moving the state.

Andhra State came into being on 1 October 1953, with Tanguturi Prakasam as Chief Minister and Kurnool as capital, after the Andhra State Act of 1953 carved the Telugu districts out of Madras. The concession's logic was immediately general. If Telugu speakers had won a state by mobilisation, the Malayalam speakers of Travancore-Cochin and Malabar, the Kannada speakers scattered across Madras, Bombay, Hyderabad and Mysore, the Marathi speakers of Bombay State and the Punjabi speakers of PEPSU and Punjab all held a promissory note signed in December 1952. Nehru understood the generalisation problem at once, and the government's answer was to appoint a commission whose remit covered the whole country, so that the Andhra precedent would be processed as policy rather than repeated as agitation. The States Reorganisation Commission, announced in Parliament on 22 December 1953 and constituted days later, was thus born from the Andhra emergency. Its appointment is unintelligible as administrative planning; it is perfectly intelligible as the state's attempt to regain control of a constitutional question that a faster in Madras had taken out of its hands.

## The Commission: Fazl Ali and the Grammar of Compromise

The States Reorganisation Commission worked for twenty-one months. Its members, Fazl Ali, Kunzru and Panikkar, between them carried a judicial temperament, a liberal parliamentarian's suspicion of majoritarian sentiment and a historian-administrator's feel for the Deccan's layered sovereignties; the report they signed on 30 September 1955 bears all three marks. The Commission accepted language as a legitimate and weighty factor in reorganisation, thereby reversing the Dhar doctrine, and then hedged the acceptance with counterweights: national unity, administrative viability, financial capacity, the wishes of the people affected and the protection of linguistic minorities within any new unit. It recommended sixteen states and three union territories, against the fourteen and six that the Act ultimately produced, and it declined several demands outright, most consequentially Punjabi Suba and a unilingual Maharashtra including Bombay city.

### What did the Fazl Ali Commission actually recommend in September 1955?

The Commission's report of 30 September 1955 recommended sixteen states and three union territories, accepting language as a factor in redrawing boundaries while subordinating it to national unity, administrative viability and financial capacity. It proposed a bilingual Bombay, a separate Maharashtra without Bombay city settled as proposed, the merger of Hyderabad's Telugu districts with Andhra, and no Punjabi Suba.

The report's hedges were not evasions; they were the grammar in which a linguistic settlement could be spoken by a state still afraid of its own concession. By making language one factor among several, the Commission preserved Delhi's discretion in every contested case, and the contested cases of 1955, Bombay city and Punjab above all, were precisely the cases where language and community bled into each other. The Commission proposed that Bombay city be constituted separately, a recommendation the government abandoned under pressure; it proposed that Maharashtra and Gujarat remain joined in a bilingual Bombay State, a recommendation the government accepted and lived to regret; it refused Punjab's reorganisation on the ground that the Punjabi-Hindi census dispute made the linguistic claim unverifiable, a refusal that held for a decade. Historians who consult the report's evidentiary volumes as scanned documents rather than summaries will find that a tool for reading office files in the browser, such as [the Office File Viewer for Excel, DOCX and PPTX documents at ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), makes the Commission's district tables and memorandum annexures far easier to work through, and the tables repay the effort: village-level language data, not editorial generalisation, is what the Commission's compromises were built on.

The government accepted the framework and adjusted the arithmetic. The States Reorganisation Bill was debated through 1956, and the Act that emerged differed from the report in ways that confirmed the report's political rather than administrative function: the Commission had supplied the reasoning, and the Cabinet supplied the exceptions. Bombay stayed bilingual because the Congress organisation in Gujarat and the party's Bombay city interests could block separation; Vidarbha, which the Commission had proposed as a separate state, was folded into Bombay because Maharashtra's claim to it was politically weightier than the Commission's tidy map. Each exception was a political fact wearing an administrative costume. The Act received assent and took effect on 1 November 1956, and on that date the Indian Union quietly changed the principle of its own composition, while describing the change, in the Statement of Objects and Reasons, as the implementation of a commission's recommendations.

## The Settlement of 1 November 1956

The States Reorganisation Act of 1956, Act 37 of 1956, redrew India in a single commencement. The fourteen states of the new union were Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Bombay, Jammu and Kashmir, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Mysore, Orissa, Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. The six union territories were the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, the Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands, Manipur and Tripura. The princely unions that had survived the accessions disappeared into the new geography: PEPSU was merged into Punjab, Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh and Bhopal into the enlarged Madhya Pradesh, Saurashtra and Kutch into Bombay, and Travancore-Cochin, joined with the Malabar district of Madras, became Kerala. Hyderabad State, the largest of the princely survivals, was dismembered three ways among Andhra Pradesh, Bombay and Mysore. The settlement reached well beyond the linguistic question; it completed the territorial integration of the princely order as a by-product of solving the language question, which is one more reason the housekeeping description fails: housekeeping does not abolish a category of the federation.

The constitutional machinery mattered as much as the map. Because reorganisation proceeded under Articles 2 to 4 by ordinary law, the Seventh Amendment, passed alongside the Act, performed the deeper surgery: it abolished the Part A, Part B, Part C and Part D classification, rewrote the First Schedule, recast the provisions on High Courts and state legislatures for the new units, and ended the institution of Rajpramukhs, the former rulers who had headed the Part B states. The pairing of a reorganisation statute with a constitutional amendment was a pattern the republic had already seen in [the executive-driven amendment pattern that the 1956 Act's Seventh Amendment continued](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/): from 1951 onward, the Union executive treated the Constitution's text as an instrument to be adjusted when a governing project required it, and 1956 extended that treatment to the federal map itself. The significance is easily missed. A federation whose constituent units can be redrawn by the central legislature's ordinary majority, with the state legislatures' views solicited under Article 3 but not binding, has placed the identity of its own provinces inside national politics. After 1956, forming a state was a campaign a movement could plausibly win, in Delhi, by pressure. The Act taught every regional movement in India that lesson, and the movements kept the receipt.

### How was Jammu and Kashmir treated differently in the 1956 settlement?

Jammu and Kashmir entered the 1956 settlement as a listed state whose constitutional relationship with the Union remained governed by Article 370 and its own Constituent Assembly, which adopted a state constitution in November 1956. The Reorganisation Act scheduled the state among the fourteen without dissolving its special position, making the settlement an exercise in asymmetry as well as language.

The Jammu and Kashmir case is the clearest evidence that 1956 constitutionalised a method rather than a single principle. Language supplied the general rule; history, treaty and security supplied exceptions that the same settlement carried without embarrassment. The reorganisation did not impose uniformity on the federation. It demonstrated that the federation's units could be constituted on different grounds, linguistic, historical, strategic, tribal, provided the ground could be argued in Delhi and carried in Parliament. That pluralism of grounds, inherited from the founding's asymmetric design, is what later movements exploited: the Naga claim argued ethnicity and a unique political history, the Jharkhand claim argued tribal identity and regional neglect, the Telangana claim argued a breached compact within a linguistic state. Each ground was different; the route, Article 3 and a parliamentary majority persuaded by mobilisation, was the route 1956 had paved.

### What changed for Mysore when the Kannada-speaking districts were united in 1956?

On 1 November 1956 the enlarged Mysore State united the old Mysore princely territory with Kannada-speaking districts drawn from Bombay, Madras, Hyderabad and Coorg, fulfilling the Ekikarana unification movement's demand of four decades. The unification made Karnataka's linguistic state a reality in substance; the state took the name Karnataka only in 1973, under Chief Minister Devaraj Urs.

The Mysore unification illustrates the settlement's quiet radicalism. The districts transferred to Mysore were taken from four different administrations, including the Nizam's Hyderabad, whose Kannada-speaking Marathwada and Hyderabad-Karnataka districts had never shared a government with Mysore city. No administrative logic required their union; the Kannada language, and the Ekikarana movement's long campaign in literature, journalism and Congress politics, required it. A reader comparing the 1950 and 1956 maps of the Deccan watches a linguistic idea overrule the accumulated boundaries of three centuries of states. The same pattern repeated in Kerala, where the Aikya Kerala movement's demand joined Travancore-Cochin to Malabar across the old line between princely and British India, and in Andhra Pradesh, where the Telugu districts of Hyderabad State joined Andhra State to form the enlarged Andhra Pradesh of 1 November 1956, a union whose internal compact, the Gentlemen's Agreement of February 1956, carries its own section later in this article. The settlement of 1956 was therefore not one decision but a class of decisions, each legitimating the next, and classes of decisions are what constitutional politics produces.

## The Safeguards: Linguistic Minorities Inside the New States

A settlement that constituted states by majority language created, by arithmetic, a new class of citizen: the linguistic minority, the Kannada speaker in Solapur, the Telugu speaker in Madras city, the Urdu speaker everywhere, whose home district had been assigned to a state of another tongue. The founders' text had anticipated the problem in general terms, and the settlement of 1956 answered it with machinery that deserves notice because it shows the Act's authors understood the principle's dangers and tried to constitutionalise the protections alongside the principle. The Seventh Amendment inserted two provisions into the Constitution's language chapter. Article 350A obliged every state to endeavour to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage to children belonging to linguistic minority groups. Article 350B created a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities, appointed by the President, charged with investigating all matters relating to the safeguards provided for linguistic minorities and reporting to the President at intervals, the reports to be laid before Parliament and sent to the governments concerned. The first Commissioner for Linguistic Minorities took office in 1957, and the office's annual reports became a standing audit of the settlement's internal justice, documenting, decade after decade, the gap between the constitutional promise of mother-tongue schooling and the practice of state education departments.

The safeguards matter to this article's argument because they reveal the settlement's self-consciousness. A merely administrative reorganisation does not amend the Constitution to protect the citizens its boundaries strand; the 1956 operation did, because its authors knew they were allocating peoples to states and not merely offices to districts. The protection regime also marked the settlement's limit. The safeguards guarded language in the classroom and in the citizen's representations to authority under Article 350, which secured the right to submit grievances in any language used in the Union or the state; they did not guard the minority's share of state employment, its place in the legislature, or its claim on the capital city's future. The minorities who fell outside the safeguards' reach, the Marathi speakers of Belgaum assigned to Mysore, the Bengali speakers of Assam's Barak valley assigned to a state whose official language politics turned Assamese, organised their own movements in the decades that followed, using the majority's own grammar. The settlement had taught every community in India the same lesson, majorities and minorities alike, and the minority movements of the following generation, from Belgaum to Silchar, were its aptest pupils.

## Counting a People: The Census and the Politics of Language Data

Every argument in this article rests on a prior transformation that is easy to overlook: language in India had become countable. The decennial census, conducted since 1881, asked every enumerated person for a mother tongue and published the answers district by district, and the Linguistic Survey of India directed by George Grierson, published between 1903 and 1928, had mapped the subcontinent's tongues in nineteen volumes. The consequence was that a linguistic claim arrived in Delhi with numbers attached. When the Andhra movement demanded the Telugu districts, it could cite the census majorities of those districts; when the Fazl Ali Commission weighed a boundary, it weighed taluk-level language returns. Identity politics in the twentieth century usually runs on assertion. In India after 1951 it ran, unusually, on statistics compiled by the state itself, which made the claims harder to dismiss as sentiment and easier to process as administration. The Dhar doctrine could declare language an illegitimate principle, but it could not unpublish the census volumes in which the Indian people appeared, district by district, sorted by the language they spoke at home.

The census also taught the movements their sharpest lesson, that numbers are made by the counting. The Punjab dispute turned on exactly this discovery. In the censuses of 1951 and 1961, organised campaigns among Punjabi Hindus urged the return of Hindi as mother tongue, and among Sikhs the return of Punjabi in Gurmukhi, so that the published language map of Punjab recorded a communal mobilisation wearing a linguistic mask. The Fazl Ali Commission cited the corruption of the data as a ground for refusing the Suba, and the Shah Commission of 1966, instructed to use the 1961 returns at tehsil level, drew the boundary from the same contested numbers because no other numbers existed. The episode generalises uncomfortably. A federation that constitutes its units by census language hands every community an incentive to organise the census itself, and the incentive has operated in Assam's language returns and in the politics of the Eighth Schedule ever since. The 1956 settlement did not merely use language data; it raised the political value of language data permanently, and that elevation is part of its constitutional legacy, visible in every subsequent decade's quarrels over what the census should ask and what its answers should build.

## Hyderabad: The State Dismembered Three Ways

The largest single operation of 1956 was the disappearance of Hyderabad. The Nizam's dominion, absorbed into the Union after the police action of September 1948 and governed thereafter as a Part B state under an elected ministry from 1952, contained three linguistic regions under one Urdu-administered roof: the Telugu districts of Telangana, the Marathi districts of Marathwada and the Kannada districts of the west. The States Reorganisation Commission recommended the state's dismemberment, and the Act executed it: Telangana joined Andhra State to form Andhra Pradesh, Marathwada joined Bombay, and the Kannada districts joined Mysore, while Hyderabad city passed to Andhra Pradesh as its capital. A state of more than eighteen million people, older as a polity than most of the federation's units and possessed of its own university, railway and administrative services, ceased to exist because its internal language map said it should. No administrative argument could have dissolved Hyderabad; its finances were sound and its services experienced. Only the identity principle, operating at full strength, sufficed, and the men who executed the dissolution knew it, which is why the Telangana safeguards were negotiated before the merger rather than after: the Gentlemen's Agreement was the price of persuading Telangana's leadership to accept a linguistic logic that dissolved their state in the name of a larger Telugu one.

Hyderabad's dismemberment also exposes the selectivity that politics imposed on the principle. Visalandhra, the greater Andhra of the Telugu imagination, was achieved; the Marathi claim absorbed Marathwada into a bilingual Bombay its people had not chosen; the Kannada districts of Hyderabad entered Mysore under the Ekikarana settlement their movement had sought since the 1920s. Three regions of one dissolved state received three different qualities of consent, and the differences tracked the strength of each region's organisation in Delhi rather than any variation in the principle itself. The pattern recurs through the whole reorganisation and explains its otherwise puzzling unevenness: the principle was general, the applications were negotiated, and the negotiations were won by movements with parties, newspapers and parliamentary leverage. Constitutional politics, in 1956 as in every other year, was principle multiplied by power, and a reading of the Act that sees only the principle mistakes the statute book for the event.

| Jammu and Kashmir reorganised, 31 October 2019 | Executive initiative of the Union government, without a mass statehood movement | Presidential Orders of August 2019 with the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 |

The timeline also exposes the one genuine novelty in its final row. Every event from 1953 to 2014 was driven from below, by movements that forced a reluctant centre to move; the event of 2019 was driven from above, by a centre using the machinery the movements had normalised, for a purpose no statehood movement had demanded. Whether that reversal is read as the machinery's maturation or its capture is a dispute this article takes up below; the procedural continuity is not in dispute, and the continuity is the legacy of 1956.

## Capitals: The Geography of the New Politics

States require capitals, and the settlement's capital questions carried its politics in concentrated form, because a capital is where a people's state becomes visible to itself. The Andhra claim to Madras city had been the emotional core of the fast of 1952; the Andhra leadership surrendered the claim in negotiation, accepting Kurnool in 1953 and then Hyderabad in 1956, and the surrender was remembered in Andhra politics for a generation as the price exacted by Tamil resistance and Delhi's arbitration. Bombay city consumed the bilingual state's four years and emerged in 1960 as Maharashtra's capital, the single most valuable asset transferred by any reorganisation of the century, its municipal revenues and port and film industry passing to the state whose language its working class substantially spoke. Chandigarh, the new city Le Corbusier's team was building as Punjab's capital from 1951, became in 1966 a union territory and joint capital, a city held in trust between two claimant states because neither could be allowed to lose it, and the trust has never been wound up. Bhopal, a princely capital of modest size, became the seat of the enlarged Madhya Pradesh over the rival claims of Jabalpur and Indore, chosen in part because it belonged fully to neither of the state's great regions.

The pattern that unites these cases is that the capital question was always settled last and argued hardest, because the capital embodied what the reorganisation was really distributing: not administrative convenience but the seat of a people's sovereignty, with its secretariat, its university, its high court and its jobs. The disputes outlived the settlements that created them with remarkable fidelity. Chandigarh's status remained a live clause of Punjab-Haryana politics into the following century; Hyderabad's designation as a common capital for ten years in the Act of 2014 replayed, in reverse, the anxieties of 1956, with the successor Andhra Pradesh driven to build a new capital at Amaravati from green fields, as Punjab had built Chandigarh; and Belgaum, a district town awarded to Mysore in 1956 against the Marathi claim, acquired a second legislative house of Karnataka built in the following century expressly to perform sovereignty over the disputed ground. A housekeeping operation does not generate sixty years of capital litigation and competitive architecture. A constitutional settlement does, because its stakes are the visible seats of peoples, and peoples do not stop caring where their sovereignty sits.

## The Electoral Test: Kerala 1957 and the Verdict of the Ballot

Within five months of the new map taking effect, it faced the test that distinguishes a constitutional settlement from a paper one: a general election. The election of February and March 1957, the first conducted on the reorganised map, returned the Congress to power at the centre and in most states, and in the new state of Kerala it produced the result that travelled around the world. The Communist Party of India won sixty of the one hundred and twenty-six seats in the Kerala Assembly, and with the support of five independents E. M. S. Namboodiripad formed a ministry on 5 April 1957, among the first instances in any country of a communist party taking office through a free election. Kerala existed as an arena for that verdict because 1956 had assembled the Malayalam-speaking districts of Travancore-Cochin and Malabar into one political unit with a shared public sphere of newspapers, unions and libraries; the communist movement's strength had been built separately in the princely south and in Malabar's tenancy struggles, and the linguistic state fused the two constituencies into a single electorate that could return a government. The map had made the majority.

The sequel tested the settlement from the other side. The Kerala ministry's education bill and agrarian relations bill mobilised the church, the Nair Service Society and the Congress and Praja Socialist opposition into the Vimochana Samaram, the liberation struggle of 1958 and 1959, and on 31 July 1959 the Union government dismissed the ministry under Article 356, the first dismissal of an elected state government of an opposition party in the republic's history. The linguistic state had created an electorate that could install a communist government; the federation's emergency machinery then removed that government for the politics it pursued. Both halves of the episode belong to the constitutional history of 1956. The settlement redistributed democratic opportunity downward to language communities, and the centre retained, in Article 356, an instrument for overruling the use a community made of the opportunity. The tension between those two facts, statehood conceded and statehood supervised, runs through the entire later history of Indian federalism, from the dismissal of the DMK ministry in 1976 to the reorganisation of 2019, and it begins, in its recognisably modern form, in Kerala in the first electoral cycle of the linguistic map.

## The Purulia Exception and the Merger That Failed

The settlement of 1956 was not a one-way ratchet toward smaller, purer units, and its two forgotten counter-examples prove the rule by showing where politics overruled even the government's own cartography. The States Reorganisation Commission and the Cabinet had contemplated joining Bihar and West Bengal into a single eastern state, a proposal associated with the West Bengal Chief Minister Bidhan Chandra Roy and the Bihar Chief Minister Sri Krishna Sinha, which would have run directly against the linguistic logic of the hour in the name of economic planning for the Damodar valley and the coal belt. Bengal's public answered in 1956 with a hartal and a political storm in Calcutta, the proposal was abandoned, and the only territorial fruit of the episode was the transfer of the Purulia subdivision from Bihar's Manbhum district to West Bengal under the Bihar and West Bengal (Transfer of Territories) Act of 1956, a Bengali-speaking tract awarded to Bengal on the purest linguistic reasoning in the whole settlement. A merger of two great states, sponsored by two of the most powerful chief ministers in the country, died of public resistance within weeks, while a subdivision changed states on language grounds without controversy. The lesson the political class drew was precise: after 1956, boundaries moved with identity or not at all, and any proposal that asked a language community to dissolve into a larger administrative unit for its own economic good would be treated as an attack. Every subsequent attempt to argue a state out of existence, or to resist a bifurcation on grounds of viability, has had to overcome the presumption Purulia and the failed merger installed.

## The Deferred Questions: Bombay and Punjab

The settlement of 1956 was candid about its own incompleteness in exactly two places, and both places exploded on schedule. Bombay State survived the settlement as a bilingual unit of some forty-five million people because the Commission's proposal for separating Maharashtra and Gujarat, with Bombay city constituted apart, had been abandoned between the report and the Act. The Marathi claim to Bombay city was demographic and emotional: the city lay in Maharashtra's geography, its working class was substantially Marathi, and the Samyukta Maharashtra Samiti, formed in February 1956 under leaders including S. M. Joshi, Acharya Atre and the communist trade-union leadership of the city's mills, organised for a Maharashtra with Bombay as its capital. The Gujarati claim rested on the city's commercial capital and its Gujarati-speaking merchant houses, and the Mahagujarat movement under Indulal Yagnik organised for a Gujarat that would not be severed from the metropolis. In January 1956, when Nehru announced the government's intention to make Bombay a centrally administered city, Bombay's streets answered with days of rioting and police firing; the memorial at Hutatma Chowk in the city's Flora Fountain square commemorates 106 martyrs of the movement. A settlement that requires a martyrs' memorial within its own first season has not settled the question it deferred.

### Why did Bombay State survive 1956 as a bilingual state?

Bombay survived bilingual because the Congress command would not surrender Bombay city to either claimant and the Commission's proposal to constitute the city separately collapsed against Marathi insistence that the city was Maharashtra's capital and Gujarati insistence that its commerce was Gujarat's creation. The bilingual state was a postponement dressed as a solution, undone by agitation within four years.

The postponement lasted until 1960. The Congress lost ground in both regions at the election of 1957, the Samiti's campaign kept Maharashtra's claim at boiling point, and on 1 May 1960 the Bombay Reorganisation Act divided the bilingual state into Maharashtra, with Bombay as capital, and Gujarat, with its capital first at Ahmedabad. The outcome vindicated the linguistic principle against the leadership that had hedged it, and it did so through the same sequence as Andhra: mobilisation, electoral cost, concession, statute. Granville Austin's institutional-resolution reading of the period, developed in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), emphasises the system's capacity to absorb such corrections without breaking, and on Bombay the emphasis is earned: the federation corrected a bad settlement through its ordinary politics. The constitutional-politics reading adds the observation Austin's frame understates. Each correction enlarged the precedent. After Bombay, no Indian government could again tell a linguistic movement that its demand was illegitimate in principle; the argument had been lost in 1956 and the loss ratified in 1960.

Punjab's deferral ran on a darker fuse because in Punjab language and religion were census categories in combat. The Punjabi Suba demand, pressed by the Shiromani Akali Dal under Master Tara Singh, sought a state in which Punjabi in the Gurmukhi script would be the majority language; the demand was opposed by Hindu organisations in the state, including the Arya Samaj's Hindi campaign, whose supporters returned Hindi as their mother tongue in the censuses of 1951 and 1961 in numbers that made the linguistic map of Punjab unreadable as a neutral fact. The Fazl Ali Commission refused the Suba substantially on this ground, that the demand was communal in linguistic dress, and the refusal held through the States Reorganisation Act. Sant Fateh Singh, who succeeded to the movement's leadership, fasted for the Suba in 1960 and 1961 and threatened self-immolation in 1965, suspending the threat as the war with Pakistan began in September of that year, a patriotic suspension the government acknowledged. The Shah Commission of 1966 then drew the line the Commission of 1955 had declined to draw, and the Punjab Reorganisation Act, effective 1 November 1966, ten years to the day after the great settlement, created a Punjabi-speaking Punjab, a Hindi-speaking Haryana, and transferred the hill areas to Himachal Pradesh, with Chandigarh as a union territory and shared capital. The deferral had purchased a decade and charged interest on it: the Suba's eventual concession arrived entangled with the communal politics the refusal had hoped to starve, and the Punjab crisis of the following decades grew in soil the deferred settlement had prepared. Deferral, in the linguistic history of the republic, is never free; it is a loan against a future crisis at a rate the founders did not live to see quoted.

## The Village Line: Boundary Disputes the Settlement Left Behind

A reorganisation drawn at the level of states must eventually be drawn at the level of villages, and the village line is where the linguistic principle met its evidentiary limits. The Fazl Ali Commission had worked from district and taluk majorities, but along every new boundary ran a band of mixed country in which the majority changed from village to village and the census returns could be read two ways. The settlement's authors knew the bands would litigate themselves for decades, and they did. The Maharashtra-Karnataka dispute over Belgaum, a district with a Marathi-speaking plurality in its city and a Kannada-speaking countryside, administered from 1956 by Mysore, produced the Mahajan Commission of 1966, whose report of 1967 awarded most of the disputed territory to Mysore and satisfied neither state; the dispute was carried by Maharashtra to the Supreme Court in a suit filed in 2004, and it remained part of the two states' politics two generations after the boundary was drawn. Kasaragod, the Malayalam-speaking northern tip of the old Malabar, went to Kerala in 1956 over the claims of Kannada organisations that have renewed the demand for its transfer at intervals ever since. On the Punjab-Haryana line, the Shah Commission's tehsil arithmetic left the Abohar and Fazilka tracts in Punjab against Haryana's claim, a grievance written into the Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal dispute that poisoned the two states' relations for the rest of the century.

These disputes are usually filed as the settlement's failures, the untidy residue of a rushed operation. The constitutional-politics reading files them differently, as evidence of the settlement's nature. Administrative boundaries generate jurisdictional quarrels that officials settle; identity boundaries generate disputes that peoples inherit, because the boundary decides which people's state a village's children will be educated by, employed by and policed by. The Belgaum dispute's longevity, its martyrs' memorials and border agitations and legislative resolutions, is unintelligible as a quarrel over administration and perfectly intelligible as a quarrel over which linguistic nation a mixed district belongs to. The 1956 settlement had made that question the most consequential question a boundary could pose, and the districts where the answer was genuinely mixed became the permanent exhibits of the settlement's logic. Their unfinished business is not an exception to the constitutional politics of 1956. It is the constitutional politics of 1956, still in session at the village line.

## The Tamil Thread: Language as a Political Inheritance

The most consequential linguistic mobilisation of the century ran through Tamil country, and its thread connects 1956 to the party system of the south in ways the administrative narrative cannot reach. The Dravidian movement, whose ideological charter E. V. Ramasamy, called Periyar, had supplied through the Self-Respect Movement from 1925 and the Dravidar Kazhagam from 1944, treated language as the carrier of a civilisational claim: Tamil antiquity against Sanskritic hierarchy, the non-Brahmin majority against a Brahmin administrative elite. M. S. S. Pandian's Brahmin and Non-Brahmin (2007) anatomised the movement's earlier genealogy, tracing how the Justice Party era and the non-Brahmin manifesto of 1916 had already joined caste, language and provincial politics into a single idiom a generation before the Constitution. Pandian's reading matters for 1956 because it shows that the linguistic demand, in the Tamil case, was never only about the language of administration. It was the territorial form of a social revolution that had been organising for forty years, and a settlement that redrew Madras on linguistic lines was, in Tamil country, ratifying that revolution's geography.

Madras State in 1956 lost Malabar to Kerala and kept its Tamil districts, shrinking to a substantially Tamil unit that the movement could plausibly call its own, though the state took the name Tamil Nadu only on 14 January 1969, under the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam ministry of C. N. Annadurai. The DMK's trajectory measures what the linguistic settlement made possible. Founded in 1949 by Annadurai after his break with Periyar, the party carried a demand for a separate Dravida Nadu into the 1950s, abandoned separatism after the war of 1962 and the Sixteenth Amendment of 1963, which criminalised secessionist advocacy, and won Madras State in the election of 1967 on a programme of Tamil pride, anti-Hindi agitation and rice prices. The anti-Hindi agitations of 1965, in which students immolated themselves against the scheduled end of English's fifteen years, showed language politics at an intensity no other state matched. A movement that began by demanding exit from India ended by governing a linguistic state within India and alternating in its government for the rest of the century. That conversion, from secession's edge to incumbency, is the strongest exhibit for the celebratory reading of 1956: the linguistic state gave the Dravidian movement a prize worth more than departure. It is equally an exhibit for the constitutional-politics reading, because the prize existed only after identity had been made a constitutional currency. The Tamil thread shows the currency circulating at its highest denomination.

## The Long Arc: The Northeast and the Creations of 2000

The settlement of 1956 barely touched the Northeast, where Assam stood as a vast composite state holding the Naga, Khasi, Garo, Jaintia, Mizo and other hill peoples under a plains administration, protected in part by the Sixth Schedule's autonomous district councils. The region's reorganisations, when they came, extended the 1956 logic from language to tribe and political history, and they came through the same parliamentary door. Nagaland led: the Naga National Council's insurgency, the Sixteen Point Agreement of July 1960 between the Government of India and the Naga People's Convention, the Thirteenth Amendment of 1962 inserting Article 371A with its protections for Naga customary law and land, and statehood on 1 December 1963 made a state of some sixteen thousand square kilometres whose ground of being was neither language nor administration but a people's refusal to be governed from the Assam plains. The North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971 then redrew the whole region at one stroke, effective 21 January 1972: Manipur and Tripura became states, Meghalaya, assembled from Assam's Khasi, Jaintia and Garo hills, became a state, and Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh began as union territories on their way to statehood in 1987. The Northeast's map is the 1956 principle generalised past language into identity as such, negotiated with movements that held arms as well as memoranda. No one drafting the Fazl Ali report imagined its grammar would be used to settle insurgencies; grammars escape their authors.

The creations of November 2000 carried the logic back to the Hindi heartland and stripped it of insurgency altogether. Chhattisgarh, formed from Madhya Pradesh on 1 November 2000, Uttarakhand, formed from Uttar Pradesh on 9 November 2000 under the name Uttaranchal, and Jharkhand, formed from Bihar on 15 November 2000, the birth anniversary of the tribal leader Birsa Munda, were the products of long regional movements: the Jharkhand movement's lineage ran from Jaipal Singh Munda's Adivasi Mahasabha of 1938 through the Jharkhand Party and the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha's founding in 1972, and the Uttarakhand movement had turned violent at Muzaffarnagar in October 1994, when police fired on statehood demonstrators travelling to Delhi. The three states were created by a Bharatiya Janata Party-led coalition honouring commitments its leaders had made in opposition, with the parent states' assemblies consenting, and the transactions were orderly to the point of routine. The routine is the point. By 2000, the creation of a state on an identity claim, tribal, regional, cultural, had become a normal instrument of Indian politics, usable by a governing coalition for reasons of conviction and arithmetic alike. The distance from the Dhar Commission's refusal of 1948 to the unremarked statehood bills of 2000 is the distance the constitutional politics of 1956 travelled. What had been illegitimate became administrative; the housekeeping narrative of 1956 had become true at last, half a century late, because the constitutional argument had been settled in the movements' favour.

## Telangana: The Compact Inside the Linguistic State

Andhra Pradesh, the enlarged state of 1 November 1956, was itself a linguistic settlement with a fault line built into its foundations, and the fault line's name is Telangana. The Telugu-speaking districts of the Nizam's Hyderabad, nine districts with Hyderabad city, joined Andhra State to form Andhra Pradesh on the strength of the Gentlemen's Agreement of 20 February 1956, signed in Delhi by leaders of the two regions, among them Bezawada Gopala Reddy, the Andhra Chief Minister, and Burgula Ramakrishna Rao, the Hyderabad Chief Minister. The agreement promised Telangana safeguards in public employment, education, expenditure of revenues and ministerial representation, including a regional committee of the state legislature and a deputy chief ministership or chief ministership by turns. The Andhra Pradesh that the 1956 Act created was therefore a compound: a linguistic state outside, a bilateral compact between two Telugu regions inside.

### What promise did the Gentlemen's Agreement of February 1956 make to Telangana?

The Gentlemen's Agreement of 20 February 1956 promised Telangana protected shares in public employment and educational places, expenditure of state revenues in proportion to the region's contribution, a regional committee to watch over the safeguards, and a share of the ministry's highest offices, in exchange for Telangana's consent to merge with Andhra State inside a single Telugu-speaking Andhra Pradesh.

The safeguards eroded, and the erosion is documented in the state's own instruments: the mulki rules protecting local employment, carried from the Nizam's firman into the republic's law, were diluted by administrative practice and litigated for decades; the regional committee was wound up in 1973 after the Six-Point Formula replaced the agreement's architecture; the agitations of 1969, in which students and employees of Telangana rose for a separate state and more than three hundred people died in police firing and associated violence, and the counter-agitation of Jai Andhra in 1972 and 1973, showed both regions treating the compact as broken from opposite ends. The Supreme Court's judgment of October 2009 in the free-zone employment dispute reignited the final movement, the Telangana Rashtra Samithi, founded by K. Chandrashekar Rao in 2001, carried the demand through a decade of fasts, resignations and negotiations, and the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act of 2014 divided the first linguistic state into Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, with Telangana formed on 2 June 2014 and Hyderabad held as a common capital for a period of ten years. The connection between 1956 and 2014 is not an analogy; it is a legal and political descent. The state that identity created in 1956 was divided in 2014 because an identity claim inside it, regional rather than linguistic, invoked the same Article 3 route and the same argumentative grammar: that a people's self-understanding, backed by mobilisation, is a legitimate ground for redrawing the Union's map. The 1956 settlement had made that grammar the constitution's working language, and Telangana spoke it fluently.

## The Instrument Reversed: Jammu and Kashmir in 2019

On 5 August 2019 the President of India issued Constitutional Order 272, applying the Constitution of India in full to Jammu and Kashmir and superseding the Constitutional Order of 1954, with the concurrence of the state's government obtained while the state stood under President's Rule and its legislature's powers were vested in Parliament. A second order, Constitutional Order 273 of 6 August 2019, rendered Article 370 inoperative except as reformulated. The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, passed by Parliament in the same week and effective 31 October 2019, divided the state into two union territories: Jammu and Kashmir, with a legislature, and Ladakh, without one. The state that the settlement of 1956 had listed among its fourteen, its special position carefully preserved, was dissolved as a state by the Union acting through the reorganisation power, the first time in the republic's history that a full state was demoted to union territory status. A later part of this series returns to the regional and federal questions the 2019 action opened; the concern here is the instrument, and the instrument was the one 1956 had made ordinary.

The defenders and critics of the 2019 action argued past each other in a way the long view clarifies. The Union government presented the reorganisation as integration, the completion of the national unity the founders had sought, and pointed to the text of Article 3 and to Parliament's undoubted competence. Critics presented the demotion of a state without its legislature's consent, obtained instead from a Governor during President's Rule, as a breach of the federal compact and of the assurances embedded in the accession settlement; the challenges filed in the Supreme Court pressed exactly that ground. Both arguments accept the premise that matters for this article: that the map of India is made and unmade in Delhi by constitutional instruments, and that the question is always whose claim the instruments will serve. From 1953 to 2014 the instruments served movements pressing identity claims upward against a reluctant centre. In 2019 the instruments served the centre pressing downward against a state. The machinery did not change; the hands did. A settlement that constitutionalises a method rather than a final answer leaves the method available to every future holder of a parliamentary majority, and the founders' deferral of the map, executed through Articles 2 to 4, guaranteed that availability. The 2019 reorganisation is therefore not an interruption of the 1956 story. It is the 1956 story read from the other end: identity-based federalism's instrument, forged by movements, inherited by the state.

## The Party System Under the New Map

The settlement's deepest political consequence was one its documents never mention: it rebuilt the Indian party system on linguistic foundations. The Congress organisation the founders inherited had been structured since 1920 on linguistic provincial committees, and the reorganisation of 1956 aligned the party's internal geography with the state's geography for the first time, giving each linguistic unit a Congress ministry, a Congress legislature party and a Pradesh Congress Committee whose boundaries coincided. Rajni Kothari's account of the Congress system, in his essay of 1964 and in Politics in India (1970), described a dominant party containing India's diversity through internal factions that approximated a multi-party system; the linguistic states gave that containment a territorial architecture, because a faction defeated in Madras could be consoled with office in a way that required Madras to be a prize worth holding. The chief ministers of the new states, K. Kamaraj in Madras, Y. B. Chavan in Bombay and then Maharashtra, Morarji Desai's successors in Gujarat, became a new order of political magnate, governing peoples whose language they embodied, and the high command's authority over them rested on negotiation rather than command from the moment their states acquired linguistic legitimacy. The syndicate that managed the successions of 1964 and 1966 was a college of these linguistic-state bosses, Kamaraj its presiding figure, which is to say that the party system of the 1960s was staffed at its summit by the settlement of 1956.

The opposition inherited the same architecture with interest. A regional party in the old composite provinces had been a provincial faction; in a linguistic state it could be a government in waiting, speaking the state's language against a national party obliged to speak several. The DMK's victory of 1967 was the first full demonstration, and the Akali Dal's career in Punjab, the Telugu Desam's capture of Andhra Pradesh in 1983 on the slogan of Telugu self-respect under N. T. Rama Rao, and the Asom Gana Parishad's victory in Assam in 1985 traced the demonstration's spread across the map the settlement had drawn. None of these parties is imaginable in its achieved form without a state whose boundaries matched its people's self-description. The coalition era that followed, in which national governments were assembled from linguistic-state parties, was therefore not a departure from the settlement's design but its maturity: the federation of 1956 had created the units whose parties would, forty years on, compose the government of India itself. Party politics is where constitutional settlements go to become irreversible, and by the time the creations of 2000 arrived as routine business, the routine had been rehearsed in every state capital the Act of 1956 had called into being.

## The Warnings That Failed: Foreign Observers and the Fragmentation Thesis

The constitutional significance of 1956 is sharpened by recalling what informed observers expected it to do. Selig S. Harrison, the American journalist and scholar, published India: The Most Dangerous Decades in 1960, arguing that the linguistic states had armed the fissiparous tendencies of caste, language and region, and that the odds favoured India's disintegration or authoritarian rescue within the decade. Harrison was no hostile amateur; he had reported India for years and his reading of the Dravidian movement's separatist rhetoric and of the Bombay violence was drawn from the record this article has rehearsed. His prediction failed, and its failure is the founding datum of the celebratory school: the linguistic settlement, on the evidence of the 1960s, absorbed the energies Harrison catalogued rather than detonating them. The DMK's abandonment of separatism in 1963, the peaceful bifurcation of Bombay in 1960 and the concession of the Suba in 1966 all arrived within his dangerous decade, and each was processed by legislatures and elections rather than by armies.

The constitutional-politics reading claims Harrison's failure for its own side, and the claim is sound, because the prediction rested on the administrative school's premise. Harrison treated the linguistic states as concessions that would whet appetites without limit, each new state a platform for the next secession; that is the arithmetic of a state distributing territory. What 1956 had actually distributed was standing: a recognised status, inside the federation, for peoples who had argued themselves into statehood, together with a procedure for arguing further claims. Peoples with standing defend the system that grants it, and movements with a procedure spend their energy on memoranda, fasts and elections rather than on exit. The appetite was not whetted; it was constitutionalised, which is a different fate, and the difference between the two is precisely the difference between reading the Act as a transfer of land and reading it as a change in the constitution's working principle. Harrison's India did not break because the India of 1956 was no longer the state his categories described.

## The Disciplined Counterfactual: India Without the Act

A final test of the thesis is the counterfactual, conducted with the discipline the evidence allows. Suppose the Union government had held the JVP line after 1953: Andhra refused or indefinitely postponed, the Fazl Ali Commission never appointed or its report shelved, the map of 1950 carried into the 1960s with presidential assurances. The materials for judging that world survive in the record itself. The Andhra agitation after Sriramulu's death showed a provincial Congress organisation unable to hold its own districts for a policy of refusal; the Madras ministry of Rajagopalachari fell in 1954 in substantial part under the strains the language question imposed. In Bombay, the Samiti's campaign was already organised before the Act and drew its strength from the city's mills and municipalities, institutions that did not require Delhi's permission to strike. In Punjab, the Akali Dal's mobilisation proceeded through the 1950s against the refusal that actually existed, and the refusal produced the census wars and the fasts of 1960 and 1961. Against a continuing all-India refusal, these movements would not have dissolved; they would have converged, in the electoral cycles of 1957 and 1962, into a common front against a centre that had declared their defining demands illegitimate, and the Congress, which governed most of the affected provinces, would have faced the choice its provincial units were already signalling they could not survive. The counterfactual does not yield a stable administrative India. It yields a decade in which the language question poisons every other question, and a concession extracted later, in worse temper, with less control over its terms. The men of 1956, Nehru above all, had lived the minor version of that decade between 1948 and 1953 and declined the major one. Their concession deserves to be read neither as surrender nor as housekeeping, but as the recognition, arrived at under pressure and executed with skill, that the constitution of a democratic federation is written by the claims its citizens succeed in making, and that the map was going to be redrawn by politics in any event. The Act's achievement was to make the redrawing legislative. That achievement is the thesis of this article stated from the negative side, and it is why the administrative reading, which cannot explain why the Act was necessary at all, fails even as narrative.

## Ambedkar's Pamphlet: A Founder Recants the Refusal

One voice in the debate of 1955 carries a weight no other can match, because it belonged to a founder who had watched the deferral from inside. B. R. Ambedkar, the chairman of the Drafting Committee, who had resigned from Nehru's cabinet in 1951 and lost his parliamentary seat in the election of 1952, published his Thoughts on Linguistic States in 1955, as the Fazl Ali Commission's work concluded, and the pamphlet dismantled the doctrine of 1948 from the standpoint of the Constitution's own chief draftsman. Ambedkar argued that linguistic states were not merely permissible but desirable for a democracy, because a state whose legislature and administration worked in the people's language made government intelligible to the governed; he treated the fear of fragmentation as an argument against democracy itself, since the same people trusted with the vote could be trusted with a province of their tongue. The pamphlet is the strongest single document for this article's thesis, because it shows the founding generation's most rigorous constitutional mind concluding, within five years of the Constitution's commencement, that the deferral had been an error and that the identity principle was the democratic one.

Ambedkar's support was not uncritical, and its conditions show a founder thinking in constitutional mechanics rather than slogans. He warned against the formula of one language, one state where it would create units too large for democratic government, and he proposed dividing the great Hindi-speaking provinces, suggesting that Uttar Pradesh be split so that no single state would dominate the federation by size; he insisted that minorities inside linguistic states required protection and that the official language of the Union must not become an instrument by which one linguistic people governed the rest. His pamphlet even addressed Maharashtra and Bombay with a draftsman's specificity, proposing arrangements for the city that the politics of 1955 could not carry. The significance of the document for the adjudication below is its provenance. When the constitutional-politics reading claims that 1956 changed the republic's working principle, the claim is not a retrospective construction by historians: it is the position the Drafting Committee's chairman published while the change was being made, against the position the Constituent Assembly's own committees had taken in 1948 and 1949. The founders were on both sides of the question, and the side that won in 1956 had a founder's authority behind it.

## The Parallel Settlement: Official Language and the Kher Commission

The reorganisation cannot be separated from the settlement running beside it in the same years, the official language of the Union, because the two together fixed the place of language in the republic's design. Under Article 344, the President appointed the Official Language Commission on 7 June 1955, chaired by Balasaheb Gangadhar Kher, the former premier of Bombay, and its report of July 1956 recommended the progressive replacement of English by Hindi as the Union's official language according to the Constitution's fifteen-year timetable, with safeguards for the non-Hindi regions. The report's reception in the south and in West Bengal, where it was read as the administrative counterpart of a majoritarian map, forced the qualifications that followed: Nehru's assurance in the Lok Sabha in August 1959 that English would continue as an associate language for as long as the non-Hindi peoples wished it, and the Official Languages Act of 1963 that wrote a version of the assurance into statute on the timetable's eve. The crisis of 1965 in Madras, when the fifteen years expired amid student agitation and self-immolations, belonged to this parallel settlement's history, and the anti-Hindi movement's force cannot be understood apart from the linguistic state that gave Tamil politics its arena after 1956.

The parallel settlement matters here for one structural reason. In the territorial question, the Union conceded the identity principle and survived; in the official-language question, it pressed a majoritarian programme and retreated. The contrast taught the political class the operating rule of the new constitutional politics: language claims pressed upward by peoples for their own states were legitimate and would be processed, while language programmes pressed downward by the centre on unwilling peoples were illegitimate and would be resisted. That rule, never written in any statute, governed the republic's language politics for the rest of the century, from the three-language formula's uneven career to the Eighth Schedule's expansion by amendment as further languages, Sindhi in 1967, Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali in 1992, Bodo, Dogri, Maithili and Santhali in 2003, argued their way into constitutional recognition by the same mobilisation-and-commission route the states had used. The Eighth Schedule's growth from fourteen languages to twenty-two is the linguistic settlement's quietest and most continuous legacy, a schedule amended the way the map was redrawn, by claims the constitution had taught its citizens how to file.

## The Big-State Problem: The Heartland the Settlement Left Whole

The settlement of 1956 redrew India where languages differed and left India whole where one language, Hindi, was returned across an immense arc from Bihar to Rajasthan. The decision was the Fazl Ali Commission's least examined legacy, and Ambedkar's pamphlet had named its flaw in advance: units of continental size, above all an Uttar Pradesh of more than sixty million people in 1956, larger than most nations, would dominate the federation's politics by arithmetic and govern their own interiors badly by distance. The Commission retained the great Hindi states substantially intact because no linguistic claim divided them, and the claims that did arise inside the arc, for a hill state in the north, a tribal state in the south-east of Bihar, a region's grievance in Chhattisgarh, were framed in the vocabularies of region, tribe and backwardness rather than language. The settlement of 2000, which divided all three of the arc's giants, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, was therefore the linguistic settlement's logic arriving, forty-four years late, in the one region where language could not carry it, carried instead by the identity vocabularies the post-1956 constitution of claims had legitimised. The heartland's divisions were argued in the grammar of 1956 even though not one of the three new states was a linguistic state.

The unexecuted remainder of Ambedkar's warning keeps the question open in the heartland itself. Uttar Pradesh, even after Uttarakhand's separation in 2000, remained a state of some two hundred million people by the census of 2011, and its division was proposed from within its own politics: the Bahujan Samaj Party government of Mayawati carried a resolution through the Uttar Pradesh Assembly in November 2011 proposing the state's division into four units, Purvanchal, Bundelkhand, Awadh Pradesh and Paschim Pradesh, and the demand for a separate Bundelkhand, argued from the region's drought and neglect across its districts in two states, and for Harit Pradesh in the west, argued from cultural and economic distinctness, continued to be filed in the recognised forms, memoranda, private members' resolutions, election manifestos. None has succeeded, because the claimants lack the one asset every successful movement in this article possessed, a parliamentary majority in Delhi persuaded that concession is cheaper than refusal. The point for the long argument is that the claims are filed at all, in the expectation that the machinery of 1956 stands ready. The big-state problem is the settlement's unfinished business in its purest form: a question the Fazl Ali Commission declined, Ambedkar posed, and the constitution's working method guarantees will be posed again, because the method, unlike the map, was settled permanently.

## The Adjudication: Austin's Resolution Against the Constitutional-Politics Reading

The scholarly disagreement this article must settle can be stated cleanly. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), his history of the Constitution's first half-century in operation, reads the reorganisation as an institutional resolution: the Indian state, confronted with a demand its founders had deferred, processed the demand through commissions, legislation and elections, conceded the linguistic principle with safeguards, and emerged with a federation that accommodated diversity instead of fragmenting on it. On Austin's reading, 1956 belongs to the larger story of the Constitution working, of a democratic state solving by procedure the problems that tore other post-colonial states apart. The reading is powerful because its central fact is real. India did not fragment. The linguistic states became the federation's stable building blocks, separatist movements that entered the linguistic arena, the DMK above all, were converted into governing parties, and the doomsday predictions of foreign observers in the 1950s, who expected language to do to India what religion had done to the subcontinent in 1947, were falsified by events.

The constitutional-politics reading defended in this article accepts Austin's fact and contests his frame. The resolution of 1956 resolved the immediate question, the map of that decade, and the resolution was genuine; but a resolution that proceeds by changing the operative principle of federal design does not close a subject, it opens one under new rules. After 1956 the subject of Indian federalism was no longer whether identity could ground a state but which identities, argued how, with what mobilisation behind them, would ground the next states. The documentary record assembled in this article supports the second reading at the points where the two readings diverge. The Fazl Ali Commission's hedges failed in Bombay within four years and in Punjab within ten, which is not how closed questions behave. The Telangana compact of 1956 generated the agitation of 1969 and the bifurcation of 2014 from inside the settlement's own flagship state. The Northeast's reorganisation generalised the principle past language entirely, and the creations of 2000 made identity-based statehood a routine instrument of coalition politics. At each divergence, the institutional-resolution reading must treat the sequel as a new problem, and the constitutional-politics reading treats it as the same problem continuing, which is the more parsimonious account of sixty years of evidence. The adjudication therefore favours the constitutional-politics reading, while recording that Austin's institutional success and this article's constitutional transformation are compatible descriptions of different aspects of the same settlement: the success was real, and the success itself was the mechanism that constitutionalised identity, because a failed concession would have discredited the principle and a successful one entrenched it.

The strongest counter to the constitutional-politics reading deserves its full weight before the verdict rests. The counter runs: a federation that conceded linguistic states and then enjoyed decades of stable, increasingly prosperous and democratically competitive state politics should be described by its outcome, stability, rather than by the anxiety of an endless renegotiation; on this view, the post-1956 creations were the settlement working as designed, fresh claims processed by a proven procedure, and 2019 was an exercise of a power the Constitution had always contained rather than a legacy of 1956. The counter is right about the power's antiquity and wrong about the procedure's meaning. Article 3's power existed from 1950, yet for six years the state's own commissions declared its linguistic use illegitimate, and movements, not ministries, changed that declaration. Procedure in a democracy is not a machine that runs itself; it is an argument that has been won. The argument won in 1956 was that identity is a legitimate ground for statehood. Every later use of the procedure, including the use of 2019, cites the winnings. That is the sense in which the consequences of the 1956 Act ran for sixty years, and it is the sense the housekeeping narrative, with its commissions and commencement dates, is built to miss.

## The Arithmetic of the Map: Fourteen States and Their Progeny

The settlement's progeny can be counted, and the count is itself an argument. The fourteen states of 1 November 1956 became, by the accessions and divisions this article has traced, twenty-five states by 1987, when Goa, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh completed the statehood ladder in a single year; twenty-eight after the three creations of November 2000; and twenty-nine after Telangana in June 2014. Not one of the additions after 1956 was made by annexation of foreign territory into a new unit, and only one subtraction of status, the demotion of Jammu and Kashmir in 2019, disturbed the upward pattern. Each addition was an identity claim processed by the machinery of 1956, and the intervals between additions shortened as the machinery's use became habitual: eleven years from the settlement to the Suba's concession, then a generation of northeastern and heartland claims, then three states in a single month of 2000. A federation that had been warned that one linguistic concession would dissolve it instead acquired, over sixty years, a standing procedure for peaceful self-division, and used the procedure fifteen times. The arithmetic rebuts the fragmentation thesis more completely than any single episode can, because it shows the direction of the traffic: claims entered the federation's institutions and emerged as states of the federation, their leaders as its ministers, their movements as its parties. Exit was the one outcome the procedure never produced after 1956, in a century and a subcontinent where exit was produced elsewhere by the same kinds of claims. The difference between the Indian experience and the experience of federations that broke on language, in Pakistan in 1971 and in Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union at the Cold War's end, is not that India's claims were weaker. It is that India's constitution, after 1956, had made a legitimate profession of them.

## What the Constitutional-Politics Reading Explains

The methodological claim can be put at its sharpest. Read the States Reorganisation Act as administrative reform and a series of disconnected files results: a Bombay file closed in 1960, a Punjab file closed in 1966, a Northeast file, a Telangana file, each with its own commission, its own agitation, its own statute. Read the Act as constitutional politics and the files become one archive, the record of a single renegotiation conducted across sixty years under rules the Act itself established: that the Union's units are constituted by argued identities, that mobilisation is the recognised way of filing a claim, that commissions and committees are the recognised way of processing one, and that Parliament under Article 3 is the recognised court of final decision. The second reading explains what the first cannot: why movements in regions the Fazl Ali Commission never visited framed their demands in its vocabulary; why the creation of three states in November 2000 excited no constitutional controversy whatsoever; why a government in 2019 reached for reorganisation, rather than any other instrument, to execute its Kashmir policy; and why the map of India should be expected to change again whenever a mobilisation assembles the argument, the numbers and the moment. A map understood as a settlement invites reverence; a map understood as a process invites claims. India has the second kind of map, and 1956 is when it chose it.

The reading also disciplines the questions a student of the later decades should ask of any new statehood demand, and the discipline is worth stating as method. Ask first what identity the claim names and what evidence, census, history or compact, it files in support; ask which commission, committee or negotiation the claim seeks as its processor, since a claim without a processor is a demonstration and a claim with one is a case; ask what the parent state's politics gains and loses by division, and what the governing coalition in Delhi gains and loses by concession, since every successful claim in this article succeeded at the moment concession became the cheaper option for the holders of the Article 3 power; and ask, finally, what safeguards the claim offers the minorities its own boundary will create, because the settlement of 1956 made that question part of every legitimate file. A demand that can answer all four questions in the recognised forms is, by the constitution's working practice since 1956, a state in gestation, whatever the ministry of the day says of it. A demand that cannot is a movement still assembling its case. The distinction, learned across the sixty years from Nellore's fast to the common capital at Hyderabad, is the most practical knowledge the founding pillar's closing subject has to teach, and it is knowledge the administrative narrative, with its tidy commencement dates, never thinks to impart.

This closing article of the founding pillar therefore hands the series its federal thread in a precise form. The founders deferred the map because their coalition could not draw it; their successors drew it under pressure and called the drawing administration; the drawing changed the constitution's working principle from territory to identity; and the change, once made, could not be confined to language, to the south, or to movements, because a principle, unlike a boundary, does not stay where it is put. The Constituent Assembly's three tensions run through the whole story: the federal-unitary tension supplied the question, the question of who counts as fully Indian supplied the linguistic claimants their standing as peoples entitled to states, and the contest over which past the present continues supplied the competing memories, of Nagpur in 1920 and of Partition in 1947, between which the settlement of 1956 had to steer. The States Reorganisation Act was not federal housekeeping. It was the constitutional politics of identity-based federalism whose consequences ran for sixty years, and the remaining articles of this series, turning in due course to the regional politics and federalism of the decades the settlement shaped, will keep meeting its consequences on ground the Act prepared.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the States Reorganisation Act of 1956?

The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 was the statute, Act 37 of 1956, that redrew India's internal map with effect from 1 November 1956, acting on the recommendations of the Fazl Ali Commission with Cabinet modifications. It produced fourteen states and six union territories, abolished the princely unions and residuary Part B and Part C units, and was accompanied by the Seventh Amendment, which ended the classification of states by their route into the Union. Most of the new states were drawn substantially on linguistic lines, which is why the Act is remembered as the statute that created India's linguistic states, though it also completed the territorial absorption of the princely order.

### Q: Who chaired the Fazl Ali Commission?

The States Reorganisation Commission was chaired by Syed Fazl Ali, a former Chief Justice of the Patna High Court and a former judge of the Supreme Court of India, who had also served as Governor of Assam. Appointed on 29 December 1953, the Commission's other members were Hriday Nath Kunzru, the veteran liberal parliamentarian, and Sardar K. M. Panikkar, the historian and administrator who had served the princely state of Bikaner and as India's ambassador. The Commission toured the country receiving memoranda and evidence for twenty-one months and submitted its report to the Government of India on 30 September 1955.

### Q: Why was Andhra State created in 1953?

Andhra State was created because the demand for a separate Telugu-speaking state, pressed since 1913 by Andhra organisations and legislators, became impossible for the Union government to refuse after Potti Sriramulu's fast unto death. Sriramulu began his fast in Madras on 19 October 1952 and died on 15 December 1952; the disorder that followed in the Telugu districts forced Jawaharlal Nehru to announce a separate Andhra State in Parliament on 19 December 1952. The Andhra State Act of 1953 then carved the Telugu districts out of Madras State, and Andhra State came into being on 1 October 1953 with Kurnool as its capital and Tanguturi Prakasam as Chief Minister.

### Q: Who was Potti Sriramulu?

Potti Sriramulu was a Gandhian social worker from Nellore district in the Telugu country of Madras Presidency, born in 1901, who had joined the salt satyagraha of 1930, worked for the uplift of Harijans, and fasted in 1946 for the opening of temples to worshippers of all castes. In October 1952 he undertook a fast unto death at Madras for the separation of the Telugu districts from Madras State, demanding an Andhra province with Madras city. He died on the night of 15 December 1952, and the political storm his death raised compelled the creation of Andhra State in 1953, making him the man whose martyrdom opened the linguistic reorganisation of India.

### Q: What states were created by the 1956 reorganisation?

The fourteen states of the Union from 1 November 1956 were Andhra Pradesh, Assam, Bihar, Bombay, Jammu and Kashmir, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Madras, Mysore, Orissa, Punjab, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal. Some, such as Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal, continued with adjusted boundaries; others were substantially new constructions, Kerala from Travancore-Cochin and Malabar, Andhra Pradesh from Andhra State and the Telugu districts of Hyderabad, and the enlarged Madhya Pradesh and Mysore from multiple predecessor units. Alongside the states stood six union territories: the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Delhi, Himachal Pradesh, the Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands, Manipur and Tripura.

### Q: Did the Constituent Assembly support linguistic states?

The Constituent Assembly never voted the linguistic principle into the Constitution, and the official inquiries it set in motion opposed it: the Dhar Commission of 1948 recommended against linguistic provinces, and the JVP Committee of Nehru, Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya reported in April 1949 that the time was not ripe. Individual founders were divided; many Congress veterans had supported linguistic provinces since the party's Nagpur session of 1920, when Congress provincial committees were organised by language. The Assembly's institutional answer was deferral: Articles 2 to 4 left the drawing of state boundaries to Parliament, so the question stayed open without being settled in language's favour.

### Q: How does the 1956 Act connect to the 2014 Telangana bifurcation?

The connection is direct descent. The enlarged Andhra Pradesh created on 1 November 1956 joined the Telugu districts of Hyderabad State, the Telangana region, to Andhra State under the safeguards of the Gentlemen's Agreement of February 1956. When those safeguards eroded and the Telangana movement, from the agitation of 1969 to the mobilisation led by the Telangana Rashtra Samithi after 2001, established that the compact had failed, the claim for a separate Telangana state proceeded by the same route the 1956 settlement had normalised: an identity-based demand, sustained mobilisation, and a reorganisation Act under Article 3. The Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act of 2014 produced Telangana on 2 June 2014.

### Q: What does Article 3 of the Constitution empower Parliament to do?

Article 3 empowers Parliament, by ordinary law, to form a new state by separating territory from an existing state or uniting states or parts of states, to increase or diminish the area of any state, and to alter the boundaries or the name of any state. A bill for the purpose requires the President's recommendation and must be referred to the legislature of any affected state for its views, but Parliament is not bound by those views, and Article 4 provides that such a law is not treated as a constitutional amendment under Article 368. This machinery, used for the reorganisation of 1956, is what makes India's federal map revisable by national legislation.

### Q: What did the Dhar Commission recommend in 1948?

The Linguistic Provinces Commission, appointed on 17 June 1948 under the retired Allahabad judge S. K. Dhar, reported on 10 December 1948 and recommended that linguistic provinces should not be formed. The Commission held that the reorganisation of provinces on the basis of language would encourage sub-national loyalties at a moment when the new state needed consolidation, and it argued that if boundaries were ever redrawn the governing considerations should be administrative convenience, geography and financial viability rather than the language of the inhabitants. Its report was the first official word of independent India on the subject, and it converted the Congress tradition of promising linguistic provinces into an official doctrine of refusal.

### Q: What did the JVP Committee conclude in 1949?

The JVP Committee of Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya, appointed by the Congress at its Jaipur session in December 1948 to consider the Dhar Commission's report, submitted its conclusions on 1 April 1949. The committee accepted the substance of the Dhar position: the moment was unfit for linguistic provinces, the security and unity of the new state took precedence, and the demand should wait. It softened the refusal in one respect, holding the question open for a future in which consolidation had been achieved and the people concerned desired the change, so that deferral rather than outright rejection became the official position the Andhra agitation overturned in 1952 and 1953.

### Q: When did the Indian National Congress first accept the principle of linguistic provinces?

The Congress accepted the principle organisationally at its Nagpur session of December 1920, when, as part of Gandhi's reconstruction of the party into a mass movement, it reorganised its provincial committees on linguistic lines, creating units for regions such as Andhra, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Kerala that cut across the administrative provinces of British India. The Nehru Report of 1928, the Congress-aligned attempt at a draft constitution for a free India, carried the principle into constitutional planning by endorsing the redistribution of provinces with regard to language. The founding generation of the republic had thus been formed politically inside a party whose own map was linguistic, a fact that makes the official refusal of linguistic states after 1947 a reversal, not an inheritance.

### Q: Why is Orissa's formation in 1936 treated as a precedent for the linguistic states?

Orissa, constituted on 1 April 1936, was the first province of British India formed substantially on the linguistic principle, assembled from Oriya-speaking tracts that had been scattered among Bihar, Madras and the Central Provinces. Its creation rewarded the Utkal Sammilani movement, founded in 1903 and led by Madhusudan Das, which had argued for a generation that the Oriya people's development required a province of their own language. Orissa's existence mattered after 1947 as proof of concept: a linguistic province had functioned inside the Indian administrative system for a decade before independence without the disintegration that official opinion later predicted, and the advocates of Andhra, Karnataka and other linguistic states cited the precedent whenever Delhi warned of fragmentation.

### Q: Why did the States Reorganisation Commission refuse to treat one language, one state as an absolute rule?

The Fazl Ali Commission treated language as a major factor in reorganisation but refused to make it a mechanical rule, because several of the demands before it did not fit the formula. Bombay city was multilingual and commercially bound to two claimant regions; the Punjab claim was entangled with a census dispute in which language returns had been communally organised; Hyderabad's three linguistic regions pointed toward dismemberment rather than a single successor; and regions such as the Northeast were organised by tribe and history rather than language alone. The Commission therefore balanced language against national unity, administrative viability, financial capacity and the protection of linguistic minorities, preserving the Union government's discretion in every hard case while conceding the principle in the general run of the map.

### Q: What did the Seventh Amendment of 1956 change alongside the States Reorganisation Act?

The Seventh Amendment, enacted with the States Reorganisation Act and effective from 1 November 1956, performed the constitutional surgery the new map required. It abolished the classification of states into Part A, Part B, Part C and Part D categories inherited from the founding settlement, rewrote the First Schedule to list the new states and union territories, discontinued the office of Rajpramukh held by former rulers in the Part B states, and recast provisions on state legislatures, High Courts and the allocation of seats in Parliament for the reorganised units. Without the amendment, the Act would have redrawn boundaries inside a constitutional scheme that still graded states by how they had entered the Union; with it, the units of the federation stood, for the first time, in a single formal class.

### Q: What happened to the princely-state unions under the 1956 settlement?

The reorganisation of 1956 dissolved the princely unions and states that had survived the accessions of 1947 and 1948 as separate federal units. PEPSU, the Patiala and East Punjab States Union, was merged into Punjab; Madhya Bharat, Vindhya Pradesh and Bhopal were absorbed into the enlarged Madhya Pradesh; Saurashtra and Kutch went into Bombay State; Travancore-Cochin joined the Malabar district of Madras to form Kerala; and Hyderabad State, the largest princely survival, was dismembered among Andhra Pradesh, Bombay and Mysore along linguistic lines. The settlement thereby completed the territorial integration of the princely order, and the rulers' remaining constitutional office, the Rajpramukhs of the Part B states, was abolished by the Seventh Amendment in the same operation.

### Q: How did the Punjab Reorganisation Act of 1966 settle the Punjabi Suba demand?

The Punjab Reorganisation Act, effective 1 November 1966, divided the Punjab that the 1956 settlement had left unilingual in name only into a Punjabi-speaking Punjab and a Hindi-speaking Haryana, transferring the hill districts to Himachal Pradesh and constituting Chandigarh as a union territory to serve as the shared capital of both successor states. The Act conceded the Punjabi Suba, the demand the Akali Dal had pressed since the 1950s and the Fazl Ali Commission had refused, after the Shah Commission of 1966 drew the boundary on the basis of the language returns of the 1961 census at the tehsil level. The settlement gave the Suba movement its state a decade after the general reorganisation had passed Punjab by.

### Q: What did the North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971 change?

The North-Eastern Areas (Reorganisation) Act of 1971, effective 21 January 1972, redrew the map of India's Northeast in a single operation. Manipur and Tripura, union territories since 1956, became full states; Meghalaya, which had existed since 1970 as an autonomous state within Assam assembled from the Khasi, Jaintia and Garo hills, became a full state; and Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh, the latter the former North-East Frontier Agency, became union territories, both proceeding to statehood in 1987. Assam, the vast composite state of the founding period, was reduced toward its Brahmaputra-valley core. The Act extended the reorganisation method beyond language into tribal identity and political history, applying in the hills the same parliamentary machinery the settlement of 1956 had normalised.

### Q: Which three states were created in the year 2000, and what produced them?

Chhattisgarh was created from Madhya Pradesh on 1 November 2000, Uttarakhand from Uttar Pradesh on 9 November 2000 under the name Uttaranchal, and Jharkhand from Bihar on 15 November 2000. Each was the product of a long regional movement: the Jharkhand demand ran from the Adivasi Mahasabha of 1938 through the Jharkhand Party to the Jharkhand Mukti Morcha founded in 1972, arguing tribal identity and the neglect of the mineral-bearing plateau by Patna; the Uttarakhand movement argued the distinct condition of the hill districts against the government of the plains at Lucknow; and the Chhattisgarh movement argued the separateness of the old Chhattisgarh region within Madhya Pradesh. The three creations, carried by the governing coalition at the centre with the parent assemblies' consent, showed statehood on identity grounds operating as routine parliamentary business.

### Q: How does Granville Austin read the 1956 reorganisation in Working a Democratic Constitution?

In Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), Granville Austin reads the reorganisation as an institutional resolution: a democratic state confronted with a demand its founders had deferred, processed that demand through a commission, parliamentary legislation and elections, conceded the linguistic principle with safeguards, and emerged with its federation strengthened rather than fragmented. Austin's emphasis falls on the Constitution working as intended in operation, absorbing a potentially explosive question by procedure. The reading captures the settlement's genuine success, since India did not fragment and the linguistic states became stable units of the federation, though the constitutional-politics interpretation holds that the procedure's success entrenched the identity principle and opened six decades of further renegotiation rather than closing the subject.

### Q: What is meant by identity-based federalism?

Identity-based federalism describes a federation whose constituent units are constituted by the self-understanding of the peoples who inhabit them, by language, tribe, region or political history, rather than by administrative convenience or the accidents of conquest and treaty. The term marks the contrast between the Indian map of 1950, which largely recorded how territory had been acquired through British provinces and princely accessions, and the map produced by the reorganisation of 1956, whose units were argued for and conceded as the homes of linguistic peoples. Under identity-based federalism, a state is a people's political address as well as a tier of administration, which is why its boundaries carry emotional and constitutional weight that a merely administrative boundary never acquires.
