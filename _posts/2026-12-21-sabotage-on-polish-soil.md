A warehouse fire that investigators cannot immediately explain. A severed cable on a rail line. A blaze at a facility connected, however loosely, to the movement of goods eastward. Any one of these is a police matter, handled by prosecutors and insurers and forgotten within a news cycle. Read together, and read against the question of who benefits, sabotage on Polish soil becomes a different object entirely: not a series of crimes but a campaign, conducted below the threshold that would trigger a military response, and designed from the outset to be difficult to prove.

That design is the point of this analysis. The instinct when confronting a sabotage campaign is to measure it by damage. A burned warehouse is worth so many millions; a cut cable costs so many hours of delay; a derailment carries a repair bill and a disruption cost. By that measure the campaign looks small, and a reader who stops there concludes that the whole business is a nuisance dressed up as a threat. The instinct in the other direction is equally common and equally wrong: to treat each incident as the leading edge of something imminent, a probing action that presages invasion, evidence that the shooting has already started in some quiet form. Neither reading survives contact with what a sabotage campaign is actually built to do.

![Sabotage on Polish soil, a gray-zone threat analysis of deniable proxies and the attribution problem - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-59.webp)

The argument developed here is that the campaign should be measured by the deniability it purchases rather than the damage it inflicts. A sponsor who wants to impose costs on Poland has many ways to do so, most of them attributable and therefore answerable. What physical interference on Polish territory offers, and what no other tool offers in quite the same combination, is the ability to impose a cost while retaining a plausible claim that the cost was imposed by someone else, or by no one, or by circumstance. That claim does not need to convince the Polish government. It needs only to be sufficiently unresolved that a response becomes contested, delayed, or scaled down. The damage is the receipt. The deniability is the product.

This article holds itself to the threat rather than the answer to it. The countermeasures, the hardening of facilities, the societal preparedness, and the institutional reforms that reduce exposure are the subject of a separate treatment, and this one defers to it: the resilience question belongs to [the building of resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), and the protection of specific infrastructure categories belongs to [the wider treatment of protecting critical infrastructure](/2028/06/21/protecting-critical-infrastructure/). What follows is the threat side: what the campaign is trying to buy, how it is organized so that the buyer stays hidden, which categories of target serve those aims, and why attribution, not damage limitation, is the contest that decides whether the campaign works.

A word on what this analysis will not do. It describes the shape of a threat so that the people who have to think about it can think about it accurately. It does not describe methods, does not enumerate specific vulnerable sites, and does not walk through how any act would be carried out. Where the argument requires reference to what has been targeted, it stays at the level of categories, because a category is enough for a defender to reason with and not enough for anyone else to act on. That restraint is not decorative. A sabotage campaign that relies on cheaply recruited participants is a campaign in which published operational detail is genuinely useful to the other side, and no analytical gain is worth that.

## What Sabotage on Polish Soil Actually Means

The word covers more ground in ordinary speech than it should in an assessment. In the loosest usage, sabotage means any hostile act short of open force, which collapses it into the whole gray-zone toolkit and makes it useless as a category. The more disciplined definition, and the one used throughout this article, is narrower: the deliberate physical damage, destruction, or degradation of property, infrastructure, or materiel, carried out covertly by or on behalf of a foreign state, with the intent of producing an effect on that state's adversary while concealing the sponsor's hand.

Three elements in that definition do real work. The act is physical, which separates it from network intrusion and from information operations. The act is covert as to sponsorship, which separates it from an overt military strike that a state acknowledges. And the sponsorship is foreign and deliberate, which separates it from vandalism, from industrial accident, and from ordinary crime. Strip any one of those three away and the category dissolves into something else, which is exactly what happens in most public discussion of the subject.

The distinction from adjacent tools matters because each tool has a different logic and a different countermeasure. Network operations against Polish systems belong to a different domain with different forensics and a different attribution timeline. Information operations aimed at Polish audiences work on belief rather than on matter, and they are treated in [the analysis of Russian disinformation aimed at Poland](/2027/02/01/russian-disinformation-aimed-at-poland/). Airspace incursions raise a classification problem that is genuinely distinct, because an object in the air may be an accident in a way that a burned building cannot be, which is why that question gets its own treatment in [the assessment of drones over Poland](/2027/01/01/drones-over-poland-accident-or-probe/). Physical interference on the ground sits apart from all three: it produces a scene, it produces forensic evidence, it produces human participants who can be arrested, and it produces a criminal case rather than a purely intelligence one.

That last feature is more consequential than it first appears. Because the acts are physical and occur on Polish territory, they fall to Polish law enforcement and Polish prosecutors before they fall to anyone else. Poland has investigated incidents in this category and has brought charges arising from them, which is a fact of the open record rather than an inference. The immediate institutional response is therefore judicial rather than military or diplomatic, which shapes everything that follows: evidentiary standards, timelines, the burden of proof, and the difference between what a government believes and what a court can find.

### What counts as sabotage on Polish soil?

Sabotage on Polish soil means deliberate physical damage to property or infrastructure, carried out covertly on behalf of a foreign state, with the sponsor's hand concealed. It excludes network intrusion, information operations, and airspace incidents. The physical, covert, and foreign-sponsored elements must all be present for the category to hold.

Working from that definition, the honest analytical position is that the boundary is often unclear in real time. An arson may be commercial fraud, personal grievance, or hostile action, and the distinguishing evidence may not surface for months or at all. A rail incident may be maintenance failure. A cable break may be an excavator operator working from an outdated plan. The category is clean in the abstract and messy in practice, and any assessment that pretends otherwise has already made the mistake this article is written to prevent.

The messiness is not a flaw in the analysis. It is the operating environment the sponsor selected, and it is worth stating plainly that a sponsor choosing this tool is choosing it partly because the boundary is unclear. If every hostile act announced itself, the tool would lose most of its value. The ambiguity is a feature purchased at the design stage, and the defender inherits it as a condition rather than as an accident.

## Why This Tool Keeps Reappearing

A reader encountering this subject for the first time often assumes the technique is novel, a product of a particular technological moment or a particular adversary's inventiveness. It is not. Covert physical interference conducted through deniable intermediaries is one of the oldest instruments available to a state that wants to impose costs on another without accepting the consequences of doing so openly, and it recurs whenever a specific set of conditions holds. Understanding those conditions explains both why the tool is in use and, more usefully, what would cause it to stop being used.

The first condition is a large asymmetry between what a state wants to affect and what it can affect openly. A state that could interdict a flow of goods militarily would do so. A state that cannot, because the flow runs through territory whose violation would trigger a response it does not want, needs an alternative that produces some fraction of the effect without the trigger. Physical interference through intermediaries produces a small fraction of the effect at a very small fraction of the risk, and when the direct option is closed, a small fraction of an effect is better than none.

The second condition is a permissive target environment, meaning an environment where the things worth affecting are extensive, distributed, commercially operated, and impossible to protect completely. Modern logistics and infrastructure systems are optimized for throughput and cost, not for resistance to deliberate interference, and that optimization is rational under ordinary conditions. The result is a target surface of enormous length maintained by actors whose security investment answers to insurance premiums rather than to national threat assessments. No policy decision created this condition and no policy decision can remove it; it is a structural property of how developed economies work.

The third condition is a population from which participants can be recruited at low cost, with no requirement of ideological alignment. This is the condition that has changed most, because the mechanics of reaching strangers at a distance and paying them for tasks have become vastly easier and cheaper than they were in earlier eras. What used to require a physical network of contacts, cultivated over time, now requires far less. The novelty in the current campaign is therefore not the concept but the recruitment economics, which have made the tool available at a volume that would previously have been unattainable.

The fourth condition is an attribution environment where a state's suspicion cannot easily be converted into a shared and actionable finding. Where attribution is fast, credible, and collectively endorsed, the tool loses most of its value, because the deniability it purchases becomes worthless and the acts revert to being attributable state actions with attributable consequences. Where attribution is slow and contested, the tool works. This is the only one of the four conditions that a defender can meaningfully change, which is why the earlier argument about compressing the attribution timeline is not a marginal recommendation but the central one.

What the historical pattern teaches, cautiously and with the limits of any analogy acknowledged, is that this tool tends to be a supplement rather than a substitute. It appears alongside other instruments, it rarely produces decisive results on its own, and it persists as long as the underlying confrontation persists, because it is cheap enough that there is no point at which its expected value drops below its cost. The corollary is not encouraging for anyone hoping this will pass: absent a change in the confrontation itself or in the attribution environment, a campaign of this type has no natural endpoint. It is a standing condition to be managed rather than a phase to be waited out.

The limits of that historical reading should be stated as clearly as the reading itself. Earlier campaigns of covert interference occurred in different legal environments, against different infrastructure, with different recruitment mechanics and different forensic capabilities on the defending side. Anyone drawing tight operational lessons from earlier periods is overreaching. What survives the transfer is the structural logic: the conditions that make the tool attractive, the trade of effectiveness for deniability, and the observation that attribution capacity is the variable that determines whether the trade pays off.

## The Aims: What the Campaign Is Trying to Buy

An assessment that lists what has been damaged without asking what the damage is for produces a chronicle rather than an analysis. The question that organizes everything else is what a sponsor gains that it could not gain by other means, and the answer separates into four aims that overlap but are not the same.

The first aim is disruption of support to Ukraine. Poland occupies a structural position in the war next door that has no substitute: it is the principal land route through which Western assistance reaches Ukraine, and the logistics function that role implies is not a matter of opinion but of geography. That position is examined at length in [the treatment of how Poland became Ukraine's lifeline](/2026/10/11/poland-as-ukraines-lifeline/), and the relevant point here is narrower. A state that cannot stop the flow at its source and cannot interdict it militarily without crossing into open war against a NATO member has an obvious interest in raising the friction of the transit itself. Friction does not need to stop the flow to be worth generating. It needs only to make the flow slower, more expensive, more heavily guarded, and more politically visible as a burden.

The second aim is the imposition of cost without attribution. This is the aim most often overlooked, because the costs in question are largely invisible in the damage figures. Every incident in this category consumes investigative capacity, security resources, insurance capacity, executive attention, and parliamentary time. A state that must treat a certain fraction of its industrial accidents as potentially hostile is a state paying a permanent tax on its own uncertainty. The tax is paid whether or not any particular incident turns out to be hostile, which is a remarkably efficient return for the sponsor, who pays only for the incidents actually commissioned but collects on the ones merely suspected.

The third aim is psychological, and it is the aim most easily overstated. The intent is to produce a sense among a population and among a political class that ordinary life is exposed, that the war next door has reached across the border in some form, and that the state cannot fully protect what it is responsible for protecting. Whether this aim succeeds is a genuinely contested question and one this article returns to, because Polish public reaction to hostile pressure has historically run toward hardening rather than accommodation, which suggests the psychological return may be negative for the sponsor. The aim is nonetheless real and should be named as an aim even where its success is doubted.

The fourth aim is signaling, and it is the most strategic of the four. A campaign of physical interference communicates capability and reach: it says that the sponsor can act inside the target's territory, that the target's counterintelligence has gaps, and that escalation has rungs below the ones everyone is watching. This signal is directed at multiple audiences at once, which is part of its efficiency. It tells the Polish government something, it tells allied capitals something slightly different, and it tells domestic audiences in the sponsoring state something different again.

### What is sabotage in Poland trying to achieve?

The campaign pursues four aims at once: raising friction on the transit of support to Ukraine, imposing costs that consume Polish investigative and security capacity, producing a sense of exposure among the public, and signaling reach into NATO territory. Damage is the mechanism rather than the objective in each case.

These aims are not equally weighted and they are not pursued through separate campaigns. A single act can serve all four, which is part of what makes the tool attractive and part of what makes it hard to counter analytically. If a defender asks which aim a given incident served, the honest answer is usually that the question is malformed, because the sponsor did not need to choose. The efficiency of a low-cost act that advances four objectives simultaneously is the economic logic underneath the whole enterprise.

It is worth being precise about what is not among the aims. Physical interference of this kind is not preparation of the battlefield in the operational sense, or at least it is not primarily that, and the assumption that it is has led analysts astray. Preparation for a conventional attack would look different in scale, in timing, in target selection, and in the relationship between the acts and other observable military activity. Reading every incident as a precursor to invasion imports a conclusion the evidence does not support and, more damagingly, wastes the analytic attention that genuine warning indicators require. The relationship between below-threshold pressure and the transition to open force is a distinct question with its own treatment in [the analysis of when gray-zone activity becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/), and the discipline of keeping the two questions separate is itself part of the answer.

Nor is the aim territorial or coercive in the ordinary sense. A sabotage campaign does not present demands. There is no negotiation it is trying to force, no concession it is priced to extract, no ultimatum it accompanies. This absence confuses observers who expect coercion to look like coercion, with a stated price and a stated deadline. What the campaign generates instead is a diffuse pressure with no address, which is harder to answer precisely because there is nothing to answer to. A demand can be refused. A general condition of friction cannot be refused; it can only be absorbed or reduced.

## The Means: Deniable Proxies and Why They Are Chosen

The organizational question is where most public discussion goes wrong, because the mental model most readers bring is drawn from fiction: a trained officer, inserted, working to a plan, extracted. That model is not how a campaign of this type is generally understood to work in the open record, and the difference is not a detail. It is the whole reason the tool functions.

The pattern that open reporting and prosecutions across Europe have described involves a different structure: individuals with no prior connection to any intelligence service, recruited at a distance, often through commonplace channels, offered payment for tasks framed vaguely or presented as something other than what they are, and used once or a small number of times before being discarded. They are frequently not nationals of the sponsoring state. They are frequently not ideologically committed. They are frequently not told, in any complete way, who they are working for or what the act is meant to accomplish. The relationship is transactional and shallow by design.

That shallowness is the engineering. A recruited individual who does not know who commissioned the task cannot reveal it under interrogation, because the information is not in their possession. A participant who was told the target was a commercial dispute cannot testify to a state's intent. A chain of intermediaries, each of whom knows only the link on either side, degrades gracefully: an arrest at the bottom yields a person, and the person yields at most the next link up, and that link is itself designed to be terminal from the investigator's perspective. Nothing about this requires the sponsor to be sophisticated. It requires only that the sponsor accept a much lower success rate in exchange for a much lower exposure.

The acceptance of a low success rate is the point that surprises readers most, and it deserves emphasis. An intelligence service running its own officers has every incentive to ensure each operation succeeds, because failure is costly and officers are scarce. A sponsor running recruited strangers has almost the opposite incentive structure. Failures are cheap. A recruited participant who is caught before acting costs the sponsor essentially nothing beyond the small sum already paid. A participant who acts and is caught afterward costs the sponsor a prosecution that produces a conviction of a private individual, which is precisely the outcome the structure was designed to produce. A participant who acts and is not caught produces the intended effect. Every branch of that tree is acceptable to the sponsor, and only one branch is genuinely bad for the target.

### Why does a sponsor use proxies instead of its own officers?

Proxies convert a state action into a criminal case. A recruited stranger who does not know the sponsor cannot expose it, failures cost the sponsor almost nothing, and a conviction produces a private individual rather than a state. Officers would deliver higher reliability but would carry the sponsor's fingerprints into every investigation.

There is a further consequence of the proxy structure that is rarely drawn out. Because participants are cheap and interchangeable, the campaign can operate at a tempo and geographic spread that a service running its own personnel could not sustain. This is why the pattern appears across multiple European states rather than concentrating in one, and why the incidents do not cluster in the way a resource-constrained operation would. The distribution itself is evidence of the organizational model: a campaign that shows up in many places at low intensity is more consistent with cheap distributed tasking than with a small number of skilled teams.

The proxy structure also explains a feature of the incident record that otherwise looks like incompetence. Some acts in this category are crude, poorly executed, or fail outright. Observers reading for a professional intelligence signature find this puzzling and sometimes conclude that the incidents cannot be state-linked at all, since a capable service would do better. That inference runs backward. Crudeness is what the model predicts, because the model deliberately trades execution quality for deniability. Judging the campaign by the craftsmanship of individual acts is like judging a mass-mailing fraud by the elegance of any single letter. The letters are meant to be cheap, and enough of them work.

None of this means the sponsor is uninvolved in a meaningful sense. Recruitment, tasking, payment, and the selection of what categories of thing to interfere with are all directed activity requiring organization, funds, and intent. The deniability is not an absence of state involvement. It is a structure that places layers of expendable material between the state and the act, so that everything an investigator can reach is expendable and everything that is not expendable is out of reach. That is a very different thing from innocence, and the distinction is the one a serious assessment has to hold onto.

## Why the Supply of Participants Is Hard to Cut Off

If the campaign depends on recruited individuals, an obvious question is why the supply cannot simply be closed. The answer is instructive about the whole problem, and it is not a story about insufficient effort.

The first difficulty is that the recruitment pool has no distinguishing characteristic. Ideological commitment, the traditional marker that counterintelligence services are organized to detect, is not required and is often absent. A participant motivated by a modest payment and an incomplete understanding of what they are doing presents none of the patterns that screening is designed to catch. There is no profile because the model does not need a profile, and a defense built around profiles is looking for something the sponsor deliberately does not use.

The second difficulty is legal and, properly understood, a feature of the system being defended rather than a weakness in it. A person who has been contacted, or who has expressed willingness, or who has accepted a payment for a task not yet performed, occupies a legally ambiguous position in a rule-of-law state. Preemption requires evidence sufficient to justify intervention, and the earlier in the sequence a state intervenes, the thinner that evidence is. A state willing to detain on suspicion alone would find this easier and would have given up something considerably more valuable than the incidents it prevented. The constraint is real and should not be treated as an obstacle to be engineered around.

The third difficulty is scale and turnover. Because participants are used once or a small number of times and discarded, closing off any particular set of individuals does not close the pipeline. The sponsor is not maintaining a network that can be rolled up; it is drawing repeatedly from a large, shifting population. Disrupting a recruitment channel produces a genuine effect that decays as the channel is replaced, which means the work is continuous and its successes are temporary. This is unsatisfying and it is the actual shape of the task.

The fourth difficulty is that most of the relevant contact happens outside the reach of any single state's authorities, through channels that are lawful in themselves and used overwhelmingly for ordinary purposes. Any measure broad enough to interdict the small hostile fraction would sweep in an enormous lawful majority, which is both a rights problem and a practical one, since a measure that generates that volume of noise stops functioning as a detection mechanism.

What does work, on the evidence, is narrower and less satisfying than closure. Raising awareness among the populations most likely to be approached reduces the yield of a recruitment attempt without any coercive apparatus. Rapid identification after the fact, and prosecution that is publicized, changes the expected value of accepting a task for people who were weighing it as ordinary casual work. Cooperation across states shortens the time between a channel appearing in one jurisdiction and being recognized in another. None of these closes the supply. All of them raise the sponsor's cost per successful act, which is the same objective as reducing the deniability discount, arrived at from the input side rather than the output side.

The honest summary is that the participant supply is a condition to be managed rather than a vulnerability to be eliminated, and that a defense promising elimination is promising something it cannot deliver. This is worth saying publicly rather than privately, because a public that has been told the supply can be closed will read every incident as a failure, which returns the psychological effect to the sponsor by a route the sponsor did not have to build.

## The European Pattern: Poland Is Not a Special Case

An assessment confined to Poland would misread the campaign, because the campaign is not confined to Poland. Incidents fitting the same description, involving similar target categories and similar recruitment patterns, have been investigated across a number of European states, and prosecutions have followed in several of them. The distribution is itself analytically informative and deserves treatment rather than a passing mention.

The first inference from the distribution is organizational. A campaign appearing thinly across many countries rather than intensively in one is consistent with cheap distributed tasking and inconsistent with a small number of capable teams operating under resource constraints. Skilled personnel are scarce and would be concentrated where they produce most effect. Recruited strangers are abundant and can be tasked anywhere reachable, so the campaign spreads to fill the available space. The geography of the incident record therefore corroborates the organizational model inferred from the incidents themselves, which is a useful independent check on an inference that would otherwise rest on the character of individual cases.

The second inference concerns aims. Several of the affected states have no comparable role in the transit of support eastward, which means the disruption aim cannot explain their inclusion. Their inclusion is better explained by the cost and signaling aims, which apply to any state within reach and do not require the target to occupy a particular logistical position. This supports the earlier argument that the four aims are pursued together and that the campaign is not narrowly instrumental to any single one of them. It also cautions against reading Polish incidents purely through the Ukraine-support lens, which is the frame most readily available and therefore the one most likely to be overapplied.

The third inference is about Poland's particular exposure, which is real but should be stated precisely rather than dramatically. Poland's exposure is higher than that of most affected states for three structural reasons: its position on the principal land route eastward, the length and density of the relevant infrastructure within its territory, and its border with the states from which pressure originates. Higher exposure means a larger volume of incidents and a larger investigative burden. It does not mean qualitatively different incidents, and the evidence does not suggest that Poland faces a distinct or more dangerous variant of the campaign. The difference is one of quantity and of consequence, not of kind.

The fourth inference concerns the defender's opportunity, and it is the most actionable. A campaign distributed across many states creates a shared evidentiary base that no single state possesses alone. Patterns invisible within one national caseload become visible when caseloads are compared: recurring recruitment channels, common intermediary structures, similar payment patterns, and similar target-selection logic. Comparative case knowledge is the single most effective attribution accelerator available, and it is available only through cooperation that no adversary can prevent. This is the clearest instance of the campaign's structure creating a vulnerability for its sponsor, since the same volume and spread that make the campaign cheap also make it comparable across jurisdictions.

There is a countervailing consideration worth stating fairly. Cooperation of this kind is slow, requires legal frameworks that differ across states, and depends on political willingness that varies with each government's assessment of the confrontation. The mechanism exists; the friction in using it is real. An assessment that treats comparative attribution as easy because it is theoretically available would repeat exactly the error this article criticizes elsewhere, which is confusing what is possible in principle with what happens in practice.

## Target Categories and Why They Are Chosen

This section stays at the level of categories throughout, and it does so deliberately. Naming specific facilities would serve no analytical purpose that categories do not serve and would carry a real cost. Readers who need site-level detail have it through professional channels; readers who do not need it should not receive it from a public analysis. What follows is therefore a taxonomy of target types and the logic that makes each attractive, presented so that a defender can reason about exposure without anyone else gaining a shopping list.

The first category is logistics: the physical means by which materiel moves. This category is attractive for the obvious reason that it connects directly to the first aim, and for a less obvious reason that matters more. Logistics infrastructure is extensive, distributed, largely unguarded by necessity, and operated by commercial actors rather than by the state. A rail network cannot be perimeter-secured along its length. A warehouse operated by a private firm answers to commercial security economics, not to national security requirements. The asymmetry between the length of the target surface and the finite supply of protective resources is permanent and cannot be engineered away, only managed.

The second category is general infrastructure: utilities, communications, and the physical substrate of ordinary economic life. The logic here is different from the logistics logic. Interference with this category does not principally serve the disruption aim, since the effect on the flow of support is indirect at best. It serves the cost and psychological aims. Damage to something a population uses daily converts an abstract security concern into a lived inconvenience, and it does so with a very small input. This category is also where the risk of unintended harm is highest, a point taken up later.

The third category is industrial and commercial property connected, sometimes loosely, to defense production or to the support relationship with Ukraine. The connection need not be tight for the target to be attractive, and the looseness is itself useful to the sponsor, because a facility with a partial or ambiguous connection produces an incident whose significance is contestable. A fire at a plainly military facility is an act with an obvious reading. A fire at a commercial site with a marginal defense contract is an act that can be argued about, and arguing about it is exactly the response the sponsor wants to provoke.

The fourth category is symbolic and public: sites whose damage carries meaning disproportionate to their function. This category is the smallest in volume and the largest in effect per act, and it is the category where the psychological aim dominates entirely. The analytical point is that this category should be read differently from the others, because an act here is a message rather than a disruption, and treating it as a disruption misreads the intent.

A fifth pattern cuts across the taxonomy and is worth stating separately because it is frequently mistaken for a target category in its own right. Some incidents appear to have been selected for their timing rather than for what they touch, occurring in proximity to a political event, an allied decision, or a visible moment in the wider confrontation. Timing selection is a different logic from target selection, and an incident chosen for when it happens may fall into any of the four categories above. An assessor who sorts only by what was damaged will miss this, and will read a timed incident as an ordinary one in whichever category it happens to occupy. The corrective is to record the surrounding calendar alongside the physical particulars, which costs nothing at the time and is unrecoverable later.

Two observations cut across all four categories. The first is that target selection appears driven more by accessibility than by importance. This is what the proxy model predicts, since a recruited stranger can reach what is reachable and cannot reach what is genuinely protected. The consequence is that the target set skews toward the soft end of the distribution rather than the valuable end, which is one of the strongest arguments against reading the campaign as decisive. The second observation is that the categories are not equally deniable, and the deniability gradient is a better organizing principle than the target-type gradient, which is the frame the next section builds.

## The Private-Sector Layer

Most of what a campaign of this kind touches is not owned by the state. Warehouses, rail operators, logistics firms, utility providers, and industrial sites are overwhelmingly commercial entities making commercial decisions, and this fact shapes the threat and the defense more than almost any other structural feature.

The immediate consequence is a mismatch of incentives. A commercial operator sets its security investment against expected commercial loss, which is a calculation about theft, fire, and liability, discounted by insurance. A state assesses the same facility against a different loss function that includes strategic consequence, alliance credibility, and the cost of an incident it cannot attribute. These two calculations produce different answers about how much protection is warranted, and the commercial answer is almost always lower. Nobody is behaving irrationally. The operator is not underinvesting relative to its own risk; it is underinvesting relative to a risk it does not bear.

Insurance deepens this rather than correcting it. An operator whose losses are covered has limited financial exposure to an incident, which further reduces the incentive to invest in prevention. Insurance also introduces a subtler distortion into the evidentiary picture. Insurers investigate incidents to determine cause and liability, and their investigations are competent and thorough within their own frame, but that frame asks whether a claim is payable, not whether a state directed the event. A cause established to an insurer's satisfaction may close a case administratively long before any question about sponsorship has been asked, and the evidence may not survive the closure in a form useful to a later inquiry.

The reporting problem compounds both. Commercial operators have real disincentives to publicize incidents that suggest their facilities are exposed, since disclosure affects contracts, premiums, share prices, and customer confidence. An operator experiencing an ambiguous event has every reason to record it as an accident, resolve it quietly, and move on. This is not misconduct; it is what commercial pressure produces. The aggregate effect, though, is that the incident record available to any national assessment is systematically incomplete, and incomplete in a direction that understates the pattern. Any figure anyone cites for how many incidents have occurred should be understood as a floor rather than a count, and readers encountering such figures should confirm what definition and reporting basis they rest on.

The workforce dimension is the least discussed and among the more consequential. Logistics and infrastructure operations rely on large, mobile, frequently subcontracted workforces with high turnover and, necessarily, physical access to facilities. This is an operational requirement, not a lapse, and it cannot be engineered away without making the operations unworkable. It does, however, mean that the recruitment pool a sponsor is drawing from and the workforce operating the target infrastructure are not entirely separate populations. Addressing this without producing a suspicion regime that damages a workforce which is overwhelmingly uninvolved is a genuine policy problem with no clean answer, and it is worth naming as such rather than resolving glibly.

What follows for the defense is that a purely state-centered response cannot work, because most of the relevant surface is not the state's to protect and most of the relevant information is not the state's to see. The alignment of commercial and national incentives, through requirements, information sharing, liability structures, or public support for costs a commercial actor would not rationally bear, is a policy question belonging to the resilience and infrastructure-protection treatments. The threat-side point is narrower and firmer: the campaign is aimed at a surface the state does not own, which is a substantial part of why it is difficult.

## The Aims-Means-Targets Sabotage Frame

The framework below is the findable artifact of this analysis. It separates the campaign into its three constituent layers, aims, means, and target categories, and pairs each with the specific attribution difficulty that layer creates. The frame is defensive in orientation: its purpose is to let an analyst or an official locate where in the structure a given incident sits and, from that, understand why the response problem takes the shape it does. It is not a targeting guide and contains nothing that is not already implicit in the open record.

The frame also makes visible something that a chronological incident list obscures. The attribution difficulties in the right-hand column are not incidental byproducts of each layer. They are the reason each layer was chosen. Read the column on its own and it becomes clear that the campaign is, at every level, optimized for a single output, and that output is not damage.

## The Deniability Discount

The claim this article advances, and the one it asks a reader to carry away and use, can be stated as a rule. Every act of physical interference imposes a cost on the target, and every act also generates a response entitlement: a right, in effect, to answer. Deniability applies a discount to that entitlement. The size of the discount is set not by how much damage was done but by how confidently and how quickly the sponsorship can be established. The strategic problem is therefore not the damage, which is generally modest, but the discount, which is generally large.

Call it the deniability discount. Its properties are worth spelling out, because the rule is only useful if a reader can apply it.

The discount is applied at the moment of the act, not at the moment of resolution. A response is needed when the incident occurs, and the evidence sufficient to justify a strong response arrives, if it arrives, months or years later. By then the political moment for a proportionate answer has passed, the incident has been absorbed into the background, and a response would appear disproportionate precisely because the incident is no longer live. This timing mismatch is the discount's principal mechanism, and it is the reason a sponsor gains from delay even when the eventual attribution is complete and public.

The discount compounds across allies. A single state that has concluded, on its own assessment, that a sponsor is responsible must nonetheless bring allies along before a collective response becomes available. Each ally applies its own evidentiary standard, its own political calculation, and its own appetite for escalation. The result is that the effective threshold for a collective answer is set not by the best-informed member but by something closer to the least willing, which multiplies the discount rather than adding to it. This dynamic is not unique to sabotage, but sabotage exploits it more efficiently than any other tool because the underlying evidence is the most contestable.

The discount is asymmetric in cost. Establishing attribution requires investigative resources, forensic work, intelligence collection, and often international legal cooperation, all of which are expensive and slow. Maintaining deniability requires the sponsor to do nothing further, because the structure already built does the work. The defender pays continuously to reduce the discount; the sponsor pays once, at the design stage, to create it. Over any extended period this asymmetry favors the sponsor decisively, which is the strongest argument for treating attribution capacity as an investment rather than as a case-by-case expense.

The discount has a floor, and the floor is where the defender's leverage lives. Deniability is not binary and it is not permanent. A pattern that repeats becomes harder to attribute to coincidence, prosecutions that accumulate produce a public evidentiary record, and a government that has documented a sequence has something to work with that a government responding to a single incident does not. The sponsor's deniability erodes as the campaign continues, which sets up a genuine tension inside the sponsor's own calculation: the volume that makes the campaign worth running is the same volume that eventually makes it undeniable. A defender who understands this stops trying to win each incident and starts trying to compress the timeline from pattern to public record, which is a different and more achievable objective.

That reframing is the operational value of the rule. If the strategic problem is the discount rather than the damage, then the defender's central task is not preventing every act, which is impossible given the length of the target surface, but shrinking the discount, which is possible. Shrinking it means faster attribution, more durable evidentiary records, better allied alignment on evidentiary standards before an incident rather than after, and a declared response posture that does not require case-by-case renegotiation. The relationship between this campaign and the broader below-threshold contest, of which it is one instrument among several, is set out in the parent assessment of [Russia's gray-zone war on Poland](/2026/12/11/russias-gray-zone-war-on-poland/), which owns the top-line judgment about the campaign as a whole.

## The Volume Paradox

There is a tension inside the sponsor's own calculation that a defender should understand, because it is one of the few places where the structure of the campaign works against the party running it.

Deniability of an individual act is high. Deniability of a pattern is not. A single warehouse fire has an innocent explanation available and always will. Forty incidents across several countries, involving comparable target categories, comparable recruitment channels, and comparable intermediary structures, do not have an innocent explanation available in any serious sense. The explanatory burden shifts as the number rises, and it shifts nonlinearly: each additional incident does more damage to the coincidence hypothesis than the one before, because coincidence has to account for all of them simultaneously.

This creates the paradox. The campaign's value derives from volume, since each act is individually small and only accumulation produces effect. But volume is precisely what destroys the deniability that gives each act its value. A sponsor running the campaign at a level low enough to preserve individual-act deniability produces almost no effect. A sponsor running it at a level high enough to produce effect generates a pattern that becomes progressively harder to deny. There is no setting that optimizes both, and the sponsor must accept erosion on one side or futility on the other.

Two implications follow, and the second is more useful than the first.

The first implication is that the campaign has a self-limiting quality. It is not a tool that can be scaled indefinitely without cost, and the cost of scaling is paid in the currency the tool exists to accumulate. This is a modest piece of good news and should not be oversold, since erosion of deniability is slow and the sponsor may well judge the trade acceptable for a long time.

The second implication is that the erosion rate is not fixed. It depends on how quickly the defender can assemble the pattern, document it durably, and make it credible to the audiences that matter. A pattern that exists in the world but has not been assembled in any usable form does not erode anything. The same forty incidents, comparatively analyzed, publicly documented, and endorsed by multiple states, erode a great deal. The defender therefore has direct influence over the rate at which the sponsor's deniability decays, which converts a passive structural feature into an active line of effort. This is the same conclusion the deniability discount produces from a different direction, and the convergence is a reason to have some confidence in it.

A fair counterpoint deserves acknowledgment. The sponsor may not particularly mind the erosion, and there is an argument that a partially eroded deniability serves the signaling aim better than a complete one. A campaign that everyone suspects but nobody can prove communicates reach and impunity simultaneously, which is a more useful message than either alone. On this reading, deniability's purpose is not to prevent belief but to prevent action, and it continues to do the second long after it has stopped doing the first. This is a serious argument. It does not overturn the conclusion, since a documented pattern still expands the response options available and still constrains the sponsor more than an undocumented one, but it does suggest the returns to attribution work diminish somewhat past the point where belief is already established, and that the remaining value lies specifically in converting belief into collectively actionable finding.

## Why Attribution Is the Real Battleground

If the discount is the strategic problem, attribution is the contested ground on which the discount is set. This section treats attribution as four distinct problems that are usually collapsed into one, because the collapse is the source of most confusion about why the process takes as long as it does.

The first is the forensic problem. Physical scenes degrade, and the acts in this category are chosen partly because they degrade evidence as they occur. Fire consumes the material that would establish its own origin. A rail incident occurs in an environment of continuous mechanical wear where a deliberate intervention and a maintenance failure can leave similar traces. Cable damage occurs in an environment where accidental damage by construction equipment is routine and frequent. In each case the baseline rate of non-hostile occurrence is high enough that a hostile instance does not stand out statistically, which is the statistical cover the target categories were selected to provide.

The second is the evidentiary problem, and it is the one most often underestimated by readers thinking about this in strategic rather than legal terms. A government may reach a confident assessment that a state directed an act. A court requires something else entirely: admissible evidence, tested by defense counsel, meeting a criminal standard, connecting a specific act to a specific person and, if the charge is to reach the sponsorship, connecting that person to a chain of direction. The proxy structure is built to break that chain, and it breaks it at the point where admissibility and intelligence sourcing collide. Intelligence that establishes sponsorship is often not usable in open court without exposing collection, and the choice between a conviction and a protected source is a real one that governments face repeatedly.

The third is the political problem. Attribution is not merely a finding; it is a decision to say something publicly, and that decision carries consequences a technical finding does not. Naming a sponsor commits a government to a posture. It invites the question of what the government intends to do about it. It exposes the government to the charge of alarmism if the evidence is later contested, and to the charge of weakness if nothing follows the naming. Governments therefore attribute more slowly than their internal assessments would allow, and the gap between what is believed and what is said is a structural feature rather than a failure of nerve.

The fourth is the collective problem. Poland can attribute on its own account. A collective response requires collective attribution, and collective attribution requires an evidentiary and political convergence among allies with differing exposures, differing intelligence pictures, and differing domestic politics around confrontation. The alliance dimension of below-threshold acts, including the question of what they trigger and what they do not, is treated in [the analysis of how Poland fights below the threshold](/2027/02/21/fighting-below-the-threshold/), which owns the response-posture question this article deliberately does not.

### How hard is it to attribute a sabotage act?

Attribution faces four separate obstacles: forensic evidence that the act itself destroys, a criminal standard the proxy chain is designed to break, a political cost to naming a sponsor publicly, and the need for allied convergence before any collective answer. Each can be overcome individually; together they impose delay measured in months or longer.

Two clarifications keep this section honest. Attribution is difficult, not impossible, and the record of European prosecutions in this area demonstrates that individuals can be identified, charged, and convicted. What remains hard is the step from the individual to the state, and that is the step the whole structure exists to obstruct. It is also true that attribution has become somewhat easier over time as investigative services across the affected states have shared patterns, built comparative case knowledge, and treated incidents as connected rather than isolated. The trend line is favorable to the defender even where any given case remains stubborn.

The second clarification is that certainty is not the standard a policymaker should be working to. Intelligence assessment does not deliver certainty and never has, and demanding it before acting is functionally equivalent to deciding not to act. The relevant question for a decision-maker is whether the assessment is strong enough to support a proportionate response given the cost of being wrong, and that question has a different answer for a diplomatic measure than for anything further up the ladder. Treating attribution as a threshold to be cleared once, rather than as a confidence level that supports different responses at different points, is the error that produces paralysis.

## Measuring a Campaign That Hides Its Own Effects

A recurring difficulty in assessing this subject is that the standard metrics do not measure what matters, and the metrics that would measure what matters are largely unavailable. This section sets out how to reason about effect without pretending to a precision the evidence does not support.

Damage totals are the most available metric and the least informative. They capture the mechanism rather than the objective, they omit the costs that fall on investigative and security capacity, and they omit entirely the costs generated by incidents that were never hostile but had to be treated as potentially so. A damage total is a measure of what was destroyed, and destruction was never the point.

Incident counts are better but are compromised by the reporting problem described earlier and by definitional instability. Whether an ambiguous event enters the count depends on who is counting, under what definition, and at what stage of investigation, and these vary across states and across time within a single state. Counts are useful for detecting direction of travel and nearly useless for cross-national comparison or for absolute claims. Anyone citing an incident count should be asked what went into it.

Disruption to the flow of support eastward is the metric most directly tied to the campaign's first aim, and on this measure the honest reading is that observable disruption has been marginal. The flow has continued. This is a genuine argument for the nuisance school and should be conceded plainly rather than argued around. The counterargument is that marginal disruption at negligible cost still returns a positive value to the sponsor, and that the metric measures only one of four aims.

Resource consumption on the defending side is closer to the right measure and is not published, which is understandable and analytically inconvenient. Investigative hours, counterintelligence capacity, security spending, executive attention, and parliamentary time are all consumed by this campaign, and the consumption includes the substantial fraction spent on incidents that turn out to be ordinary. A defender who wanted to know what the campaign actually costs would measure this, and would find the number uncomfortable.

Public sentiment is measurable and, on the available evidence, is the aim where the campaign has performed worst. Polish public response to external pressure has historically run toward hardening rather than accommodation, and there is no indication that this campaign has produced the anxiety and division it was presumably intended to produce. This is a real failure of the campaign and should be counted as one.

The composite judgment that emerges from working through the metrics is that the campaign returns positively on cost and burden, marginally on disruption, negatively on psychology, and unmeasurably on signaling. Aggregated against an extremely low input cost, that is a positive return overall, which is why the campaign continues. It is also a modest return, which is why it has not become the sponsor's principal instrument. Both halves of that assessment need to be held simultaneously, and the failure to hold them together is what produces the two errors this article was written to correct.

## The Response Problem

Attribution difficulty translates into a constrained set of responses, and the constraints are worth laying out because they explain why the answer to this campaign looks weaker than observers expect it to look.

The default response channel is judicial, because the acts are crimes committed on Polish territory. This channel has real advantages. It produces a public record, it operates on established authority without requiring new decisions, and a conviction is a fact rather than an assertion, which makes it durable in a way that a government statement is not. It also has a structural limitation: it prosecutes the person who acted, which is the layer the sponsor considers expendable. A judicial response therefore imposes the cost exactly where the sponsor has arranged for the cost to fall harmlessly.

The counterintelligence channel operates differently and largely invisibly. Disrupting recruitment, identifying intermediaries, and rolling up networks before they act produces outcomes that are, by their nature, unannounced and unmeasurable from outside. This is genuinely effective work and it is also work that cannot be pointed to when a public wants to know what is being done. The invisibility problem is not trivial: a government doing well at this cannot demonstrate that it is doing well, which creates political pressure toward visible responses that may be less effective.

The diplomatic channel, expulsions and restrictions and formal protest, imposes reciprocal costs and communicates displeasure. Its limitation is that it is available in finite quantity and does not replenish quickly. A state that has already expelled substantially cannot expel substantially again without exhausting the instrument, and an instrument that is visibly nearing exhaustion loses deterrent value in advance of actually running out.

The economic channel operates on a different timescale entirely and is generally applied to state behavior in aggregate rather than to specific incidents. This makes it poorly matched to individual acts and reasonably well matched to a sustained pattern, which is another argument for treating the campaign as a pattern rather than as a sequence of separate events.

The alliance channel is the one most often assumed to be available and least often examined. Below-threshold acts do not automatically engage collective defense, and the question of what they do engage is genuinely contested among serious people. Consultations are available at a much lower threshold than collective defense, and consultations are meaningful even though they are not a response in themselves, because they align allied understanding in advance of any incident that would require more. The realistic assessment is that this channel produces alignment rather than action, and that alignment is worth more than it appears when the alternative is discovering allied disagreement in the middle of a crisis.

A sixth channel deserves mention because it is rarely counted as a response at all: the public evidentiary record itself. A documented, published, comparatively grounded account of a pattern is not merely input to some later decision. It is an action with effects of its own, since it constrains what the sponsor can claim, gives allied governments something to point at when their own domestic politics require justification, and raises the cost of the next act by shrinking the space in which it can be explained away. Governments tend to treat publication as the announcement that follows a decision rather than as an instrument in its own right, which undervalues the one response the defender can always produce without needing anyone's agreement.

What the survey of channels shows is a genuine mismatch. The campaign is cheap, distributed, continuous, and deniable. The available responses are expensive, centralized, episodic, and attributable. That mismatch is not the result of poor policy choices; it is the structural condition the sponsor selected the tool to create. Recognizing it as structural is the beginning of a realistic answer, because a defender who expects a symmetric response to be available will keep looking for one and will keep being disappointed. The productive path runs through reducing the discount and raising the cost of the input side, not through finding a proportionate blow to return.

## What the Campaign Reveals About the Sponsor

Reading a campaign backward, from its structure to the constraints that produced it, is one of the more productive analytical moves available, and it is generally more reliable than trying to read intent from statements. Four things about the sponsor can be inferred with reasonable confidence from the way this campaign is built.

The first is that the sponsor is operating under a genuine constraint about escalation. A state indifferent to the threshold would not go to the trouble of building an elaborate deniability structure, accepting a poor execution rate, and confining itself to the accessible end of the target distribution. The structure is expensive in effectiveness terms, and nobody pays that price for nothing. What it buys is the ability to act while remaining below the line, which implies the line is respected, which in turn implies that the deterrent architecture the line rests on is doing something. This is a modestly encouraging inference and one that observers focused on the incidents themselves frequently miss.

The second is that the sponsor's direct options are limited. A state with better instruments available for the same objectives would use them. The choice of a tool that produces marginal disruption and negative psychological returns suggests not preference but a narrow menu, which is consistent with the wider assessment that direct action against a NATO member carries costs the sponsor is unwilling to accept. Reading the campaign as evidence of capability is therefore partly backward; it is at least as much evidence of the constraints under which capability operates.

The third is that the sponsor's feedback loops are probably poor. A campaign whose psychological aim is failing so visibly, and which continues at similar intensity nonetheless, is either indifferent to that aim or unaware of the failure. Indifference is possible but unlikely given the effort devoted to the aim. Unawareness is more plausible and follows from the organizational structure: intermediaries paid by results report results, and a chain built to prevent information flowing downward toward participants also degrades information flowing upward toward the sponsor. This has a practical consequence for the defender, since a sponsor with poor feedback is a sponsor more likely to misjudge the effect of an escalation, which is a genuine risk factor rather than a comforting sign of incompetence.

The fourth is that the campaign is cheap enough that no cost-benefit calculation will terminate it. The inputs are small payments to expendable participants and the maintenance of intermediary structures that serve other purposes as well. Against inputs that low, even a marginal return justifies continuation indefinitely. This is why arguments that the campaign has failed and will therefore stop are unpersuasive. Failure relative to its aims is compatible with success relative to its cost, and it is the second calculation that determines whether it continues.

Together these inferences support a specific expectation about the campaign's future shape, offered as assessment rather than prediction. The most likely trajectory is continuation at broadly similar intensity, with target selection remaining at the accessible end, execution quality remaining poor, and volume responding to the temperature of the wider confrontation rather than to the campaign's own results. The lower-probability branches, a shift toward protected targets or toward persons, would each require the sponsor to abandon the trade that defines the current model, and would therefore signal a decision at a higher level than the campaign itself.

## The Escalation Risk

The most serious analytical error available in this subject is to treat below-threshold acts as safely bounded because they are below the threshold. They are bounded by intent, not by physics, and the gap between those two is where the escalation risk lives.

The first source of risk is casualties. Every category of target described earlier carries some probability of unintended human harm, and the probability is not uniformly small. Interference with utilities or transport can produce consequences the commissioning party did not price. A fire in a commercial structure can kill people who were not part of anyone's calculation. The sponsor's control over outcomes is thin precisely because the executing party is unskilled, uninformed, and unsupervised, which is the same feature that delivers deniability. Deniability and control are traded against each other, and a sponsor who has maximized deniability has minimized control over exactly the variable most likely to produce escalation.

The second source is the interpretive risk on the defending side. A state absorbing a continuous low-level campaign develops, reasonably, a heightened readiness to read events as hostile. That readiness is protective in most cases and dangerous in a specific one: the case where a genuine accident occurs during a period of elevated tension and is read as an act. The sponsor has spent the preceding period making accidents look like acts, and the defender has adjusted its priors accordingly. Neither party controls what happens when the adjusted prior meets a real accident with severe consequences.

The third source is the cumulative-threshold problem. A campaign composed of individually tolerable acts can, in aggregate, cross a line that no individual act approached. Where that line sits is not defined in advance by anyone, including the defender, and it is not defined identically across allies. A sponsor calibrating each act against the threshold is calibrating against a quantity it cannot observe, and the possibility that it has already crossed a line the defender has not yet articulated is real. This is why the transition question matters and why it belongs to a dedicated treatment rather than being handled as a footnote here.

The fourth source is the sponsor's own information problem. A campaign run through layered intermediaries and expendable participants does not report back accurately. The sponsor learns what its intermediaries choose to tell it, and intermediaries paid by results have an incentive to overstate results. A sponsor may therefore believe the campaign is producing more effect than it is, and may escalate to obtain effects it thinks are within reach. Poor feedback loops in a coercive campaign are a well-documented escalation driver in the historical record, and nothing about this structure suggests immunity.

### Could a sabotage act escalate into open conflict?

Not by design, since the sponsor's whole investment in deniability presumes staying below the response line. The realistic escalation path runs through accident rather than intent: an act with unintended casualties, or a genuine industrial failure misread during a tense period, either of which could force decisions nobody planned for.

The practical implication is that escalation risk in this domain should be assessed as an accident-rate problem rather than an intentions problem. Assessing intentions asks what the sponsor wants, and the answer is reliably that it wants to stay below the line. Assessing the accident rate asks how often a structure built on unskilled, unsupervised participants will produce an outcome nobody chose, and that answer is considerably less reassuring. A defender planning against this should therefore plan for the consequence management of a severe unintended outcome, not merely for the prevention of deliberate ones.

Set against these, one factor cuts the other way and deserves fair statement. The sponsor has a strong interest in staying below the threshold, since the entire value of the tool evaporates if it triggers the response that overt action would trigger. That interest is a real restraint and should not be dismissed. The honest assessment is that intent restrains, execution does not, and the risk therefore concentrates in the space between what is intended and what unskilled participants actually do.

## Decisive Weapon or Nuisance: The Debate Presented Fairly

Two schools contend over how much this campaign matters, and both contain serious people with serious arguments. Presenting the strongest version of each is more useful than asserting a verdict without them.

The strategic-tool school argues that the campaign is a genuine instrument of state pressure and should be assessed as such. Its case is that the aims described earlier are real strategic objectives, that the campaign is sustained rather than opportunistic, that its geographic spread indicates central direction rather than local initiative, and that dismissing it as vandalism confuses the modest scale of individual acts with the significance of the effort behind them. This school points out that a tool which imposes continuous cost, consumes defensive resources, and cannot be answered proportionately is close to the definition of an effective instrument, whatever the size of any single act. It also argues, with force, that the tool's low cost is a reason to take it more seriously rather than less, since a cheap instrument can be sustained indefinitely and scaled at will.

The nuisance school argues that the campaign's actual effects are small and that treating it as strategically significant hands the sponsor a return it has not earned. Its case is that the disruption to the flow of support eastward has been marginal, that the psychological aim has plainly failed given how Polish opinion has responded to pressure, that the target set skews toward the accessible rather than the important, and that the execution quality is poor. This school makes a further argument that deserves weight: excessive attention to the campaign is itself one of the campaign's aims, so a defender who devotes disproportionate resources and political attention to it is delivering the effect the sponsor could not deliver on its own. On this reading, calm and proportion are not complacency but the correct counter.

The verdict this analysis reaches sits between them and is closer to the strategic-tool school on the diagnosis while borrowing the nuisance school's discipline on the prescription. The campaign is a real instrument, centrally directed, sustained, and pursuing coherent aims, and it is not vandalism. It is also not decisive and shows no sign of becoming decisive, because the structure that makes it deniable also caps what it can achieve: expendable participants cannot reach protected things, and a campaign confined to accessible targets cannot produce strategic effect. The deciding consideration is the deniability discount rather than the damage, which means the campaign's significance lies in what it does to the defender's response options rather than in what it destroys. A defender who internalizes that will neither panic nor dismiss, which is the disposition the evidence supports.

There is a third position worth naming even though it is weaker than the other two: the reading that individual incidents are unconnected and that the pattern is an artifact of heightened suspicion. This deserves a hearing because base rates for accidental industrial and infrastructure failures are genuinely high, and because a security establishment primed to see a campaign will find one. The reason it does not survive is the prosecution record across multiple European states, which has produced individuals, charges, and in a number of cases convictions arising from acts of this type. That record is a fact rather than an inference, and it establishes that at least some incidents in the category are what they appear to be. The open question is proportion, not existence.

## Five Recurring Analytic Mistakes

The value of an assessment lies partly in the errors it prevents. Five mistakes appear repeatedly in public and, less often but more damagingly, in professional discussion of this subject.

The first is measuring the campaign by damage. Everything in this article follows from rejecting that measure, and the mistake is worth naming plainly because it is so intuitive. Damage is visible, quantifiable, and reportable, and the thing that actually matters, the discount applied to the response entitlement, is none of those. Analysts default to what can be counted. Here what can be counted is the byproduct.

The second is inferring absence of state involvement from poor execution. This inference feels rigorous, since it applies a professionalism test to the evidence, but it applies the wrong test. The model deliberately trades execution quality for deniability, so crude acts are what it predicts. A capable service running an expendable-proxy campaign will produce crude acts by design, and treating crudeness as exculpatory rewards exactly the feature the structure was built to create.

The third is treating every incident as a precursor. This mistake imports a conclusion the evidence does not support and does real damage beyond being wrong, because it consumes the analytic attention that genuine warning indicators require and it degrades the credibility of assessments that later need to be believed. The relationship between below-threshold pressure and a transition to open force is a real question, but it is a question about correlation with other observable activity, not one that any single incident answers.

The fourth is treating attribution as a binary that must be cleared before anything can be done. Intelligence assessment produces confidence levels, not certainty, and different confidence levels support different responses. A standard of proof appropriate to a criminal conviction, applied to a policy decision about a diplomatic measure, is not rigor but a decision not to decide. The discipline is to match the response to the confidence rather than to withhold all responses pending certainty that will not arrive.

The fifth is symmetry-seeking, meaning the search for a proportionate response in kind. The campaign is cheap, distributed, continuous, and deniable, and the responses available to a rule-of-law state are expensive, centralized, episodic, and attributable. There is no symmetric answer, and looking for one produces frustration and, occasionally, worse. The productive path runs through reducing the value of each act to the sponsor rather than matching it, which is a different objective and an achievable one.

A sixth pattern is worth mentioning although it is less an analytic mistake than a communications one. Governments frequently face a choice between describing the campaign accurately, which risks amplifying the psychological effect the sponsor is seeking, and understating it, which risks appearing evasive when incidents become public anyway. The evidence suggests that accurate description with proportionate framing performs better than either extreme, because a public that has been told what to expect does not experience each incident as a revelation. Silence is not neutral here. It cedes the interpretive ground to whoever speaks first.

## Reading a New Incident: A Discipline, Not a Verdict

The most common request an assessor faces when an incident occurs is for an immediate judgment about whether it was hostile. That request should usually be declined in the form it is made and answered in a better one. The following is a discipline for handling a new incident, derived from everything above, and it is offered because a reader who takes nothing else from this analysis can still use it.

Begin with the base rate, not with the incident. Ask how often events of this type occur in this category of facility without any hostile cause. For most of the favored target categories the answer is that they occur frequently, which means the prior probability of hostile causation for any single event is low, and it means the sponsor selected the category for exactly that reason. Starting with the incident rather than the base rate produces overreading, and overreading is the more common failure among people paying close attention.

Locate the incident in the target taxonomy next, because the category carries information the incident alone does not. An event at a facility in the loosely defense-connected category behaves differently from one in the symbolic category, which should be read as communication rather than disruption, and both behave differently from a general utility failure where the innocent explanation is strongest. Knowing which category is in play sets a realistic expectation about how contestable the case will be and how long resolution should take.

Then ask what the incident would have to be part of. A single event is not a campaign, and a campaign is the level at which this subject becomes legible. The productive question is whether this event, if hostile, fits the pattern already documented in terms of target category, execution quality, apparent participant profile, and geographic distribution. Fit strengthens the hostile reading; poor fit weakens it and should be allowed to weaken it rather than being explained away.

Hold the judgment open explicitly, and say so. The temptation is to resolve, because an unresolved assessment feels like a failure to do the job. Resolution before evidence is not analysis. What a decision-maker needs at the moment of an incident is not a verdict but an accurate account of what is known, what is not, what would resolve it, and roughly how long that is likely to take. That account is deliverable immediately and is more useful than a premature judgment that may have to be reversed in public.

Separate the response question from the attribution question. Many responses do not require attribution at all: securing the site, restoring the function, reviewing exposure in the same category elsewhere, and briefing allies on the fact of the incident are all available immediately and are not contingent on knowing who was responsible. Coupling every response to attribution is what produces paralysis, and decoupling the responses that do not need it is one of the cheapest improvements available to a defender.

Finally, record for the pattern rather than for the case. The single highest-value output of incident handling is not the resolution of that incident but the durable, comparable record it contributes to. Details that seem irrelevant to a case may be exactly what makes it comparable to an incident in another state two years later, and comparative case knowledge has been the most effective attribution accelerator available. An investigator optimizing for closing the file will discard what an analyst assembling a pattern would keep, and the institutional design question is how to prevent that loss. Analysts who want to maintain their own structured record across incidents can keep it in a private, device-local workspace and build it up over time, which is exactly the working pattern VaultBook is built for.

The discipline is deliberately unheroic. It produces slower judgments, more explicit uncertainty, and fewer confident statements in the first week after an event. What it produces in exchange is a record that supports attribution when attribution becomes possible, which is the only thing that actually reduces what the sponsor collects.

## Where Resilience Fits

This article is about the threat, and the answer to it belongs elsewhere, but the shape of the answer follows so directly from the analysis that a brief orientation is warranted before deferring.

If the strategic problem is the deniability discount, then resilience work divides into two categories that are often confused. The first reduces the effect of acts that occur: redundancy, hardening, rapid restoration, and the general capacity of systems to absorb interference without cascading. This is engineering and investment, it is expensive, and it is bounded by the length of the target surface, which is to say it can never be complete. The second reduces the discount: attribution capacity, evidentiary durability, allied pre-alignment on standards, and a declared posture that removes the need for case-by-case decisions under time pressure. This is institutional rather than physical, it is considerably cheaper, and it is not bounded by the target surface at all.

The analytical implication is that the second category deserves more relative weight than it usually receives. A defender who spends entirely on the first category is competing on a surface where the attacker has permanent advantage, since the surface cannot be shortened and the attacker chooses where on it to act. A defender who invests in the second is competing on the variable that actually determines the campaign's value to the sponsor. This does not argue for neglecting physical protection, which is necessary, but it does argue against treating physical protection as the whole of the response.

A third element belongs alongside both and is easily overlooked: public understanding. A population that expects some incidents, understands why attribution takes time, and does not read every failure as either an attack or a cover-up is a population that denies the sponsor the psychological return. That is a resilience measure with no engineering cost, and it is one of the few areas where honest public communication is directly protective rather than merely virtuous. The full treatment of how a state builds this, across the physical, institutional, and societal dimensions, is the subject of the dedicated resilience analysis, which owns that territory and should be read for it.

There is one further point about sequencing that follows from the deniability discount and is easy to miss. Institutional measures that shrink the discount are most valuable when they are in place before an incident rather than assembled after one. Agreeing evidentiary standards with allies during a crisis means negotiating under pressure with the sponsor watching the negotiation. Agreeing them in advance converts a contested judgment into a procedural one. The same applies to a declared response posture, to arrangements for sharing case detail across jurisdictions, and to the internal question of who in a government is authorized to say what and on what confidence level. None of this is expensive. All of it is far harder to do quickly, which is the argument for doing it early.

## What Would Change This Judgment

An assessment that cannot say what would falsify it is an opinion. Four developments would materially shift the judgment reached here, and each is observable.

A shift in target selection from accessible to protected categories would indicate a change in the organizational model, since expendable recruited participants cannot reach genuinely protected things. Such a shift would imply either the introduction of trained personnel or a substantial upgrade in intermediary capability, and it would mean the sponsor had accepted higher exposure in exchange for higher effect. That trade is precisely the one the current model refuses to make, and a decision to make it would signal a change in what the campaign is for.

A shift from property to persons would be a threshold change rather than an escalation in degree. Deliberate targeting of people, as distinct from unintended harm arising from property acts, would move the campaign into a different category entirely, and the response calculus would change accordingly across the alliance.

A shift in tempo tied to other observable military activity would suggest the campaign had been repurposed from standing pressure into preparation, which is the reading this article has argued against on current evidence. The correlation, not the tempo alone, is the indicator: below-threshold activity rising while conventional posture stays flat means one thing, and the two rising together means another.

A collapse in deniability, meaning attribution becoming fast, public, and allied-endorsed as a matter of routine, would reduce the tool's value to the sponsor to the point where continuing it would be irrational. Whether this happens is substantially within the defender's control, which is the most useful conclusion in this article. The other three developments are things the sponsor decides. The fourth is something the defender can build.

## The Verdict

Sabotage on Polish soil is a real campaign, centrally directed, and organized around the deliberate purchase of deniability at the cost of effectiveness. Its acts are individually small, frequently crude, and skewed toward what is accessible rather than what is important. Read as a weapon it is unimpressive. Read as a mechanism for imposing cost while blunting the response, which is what it was built to be, it is efficient, and it will remain available and cheap for as long as the underlying confrontation continues.

The analytical discipline this subject demands is to hold two things at once: the campaign is not decisive, and the campaign is not trivial. The bridge between those statements is the deniability discount. Damage measures what a tool does; deniability measures what the target is prevented from doing about it. A defender who measures only the first will underrate the campaign; a defender who measures only its own alarm will overrate it. Measuring the discount gives the accurate reading and, more usefully, points at the one variable the defender can move.

That is the practical bottom of the assessment. Prevention across the full target surface is not achievable, and a defense built on the expectation of prevention will fail visibly and repeatedly. Compression of the timeline from incident to attributed pattern is achievable, is largely institutional rather than physical, and directly reduces what the sponsor gets for each act. The contest over this campaign is not fought at the fence line. It is fought over how fast and how credibly a state and its allies can say who did it, and mean it, and be believed.

For the reader who wants one sentence to carry away, it is this: the campaign is not measured by what it burns but by what it prevents the target from doing in reply, and that quantity is the one a defender can change. Everything else in this assessment is elaboration on that point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does sabotage on Polish soil actually target?

Target selection falls into four categories rather than a list of named sites. Logistics infrastructure, meaning the rail, road, and storage capacity that carries materiel eastward, connects most directly to the disruption aim. General utility and communications infrastructure produces lived inconvenience at very low input cost. Industrial and commercial property with a loose or partial defense connection produces incidents whose significance is contestable, which is itself useful to a sponsor. Symbolic and public sites deliver message value out of proportion to their function. Across all four, selection appears driven far more by accessibility than by importance, which is exactly what an organizational model built on unskilled recruited participants would predict, and it is one of the strongest reasons the campaign has not produced strategic effect.

### Q: Why does sabotage rely on deniable proxies?

Because the proxy is the product. A sponsor using its own trained personnel would obtain better execution and would carry its own fingerprints into every investigation, which converts each incident into an attributable state action requiring an answer. A sponsor using recruited strangers obtains worse execution and a structure in which every element an investigator can reach is expendable. The recruited participant frequently does not know who commissioned the task or what it was meant to achieve, so the information an investigator needs is not in that person's possession to give. The reliance is not a shortcut or a sign of limited capability. It is a deliberate trade of effectiveness for concealment, made because concealment is what the campaign is buying.

### Q: Why is attribution the real battleground for sabotage in Poland?

Because deniability, not damage, is what the campaign produces, and attribution is what destroys deniability. Each act generates a right to respond, and uncertainty about sponsorship discounts that right, usually heavily. The discount is applied when the incident happens, while the evidence that would justify a strong answer arrives, if at all, much later, by which point the political moment has passed. Everything the defender can do to compress that timeline directly reduces what the sponsor collects per act. This is why attribution capacity, evidentiary durability, and pre-agreed allied standards matter more than they appear to: they are not administrative overhead but the actual contested ground on which the campaign is won or lost.

### Q: Is sabotage a decisive weapon or mere vandalism?

Neither, and the gap between those two options is where the accurate reading sits. It is a real instrument pursuing coherent strategic aims, sustained over time, spread across multiple states, and centrally directed, which vandalism is not. It is also not decisive and shows no sign of becoming so, because the same structure that delivers deniability caps what the campaign can achieve. Unskilled expendable participants cannot reach genuinely protected targets, so the campaign is confined to the accessible end of the distribution, where strategic effect is not available. Its significance lies in what it does to the defender's response options rather than in what it destroys, which is a real form of significance and a bounded one.

### Q: How does sabotage deniability blunt Poland's response?

Through four compounding mechanisms. Timing: a response is needed immediately, and proof arrives late or never, so the window for a proportionate answer closes before the evidence opens it. Evidentiary standards: a government assessment and a criminal conviction require different things, and the proxy chain is built to break at exactly the point where intelligence and admissibility collide. Political cost: naming a sponsor commits a government to a posture and invites the question of what follows, so states attribute more slowly than they believe. Collective alignment: a shared answer requires allied convergence, and the effective threshold ends up set closer to the least willing partner than the best informed one.

### Q: Who organizes the sabotage campaign against Poland?

The organizing layer sits above everything an investigator normally reaches. Open reporting and prosecutions across Europe describe a structure of layered intermediaries who handle recruitment, tasking, and payment, standing between the commissioning party and the person who acts. Each intermediary knows the link on either side and is designed to be terminal from an investigator's perspective, so an arrest at the bottom yields at most one step upward. The deliberate consequence is that direction, funding, and target-category selection remain organized state activity while every element that can be seized is expendable. Deniability of this kind is not an absence of state involvement; it is a structure that places disposable material between the state and the act.

### Q: What aims drive sabotage against Polish infrastructure?

Four aims, pursued simultaneously rather than in sequence. Raising friction on the transit of support eastward, which does not need to stop the flow to be worth generating. Imposing a continuous burden on investigative, security, insurance, and executive capacity, a burden that accrues from suspected incidents as well as actual ones, so the sponsor is credited with acts it never commissioned. Producing a sense of domestic exposure among the public, an aim whose success is genuinely doubtful given how Polish opinion has historically responded to pressure. And signaling reach into allied territory, which communicates to several audiences at once. A single act can serve all four, and that efficiency is the economic logic underneath the whole effort.

### Q: Why does sabotage outsource acts to expendable proxies?

Because every branch of the outcome tree is acceptable to the sponsor when the participant is expendable. A recruited person caught before acting costs almost nothing beyond a small payment already made. One caught afterward produces a prosecution of a private individual, which is the outcome the structure was designed to yield. One who acts undetected produces the intended effect. An intelligence service running its own officers faces the opposite incentives, since officers are scarce and failure is costly, so it must ensure each operation succeeds. Expendability also permits volume and geographic spread that a small number of skilled teams could never sustain, which is why the incident pattern appears thinly across many states rather than concentrated in one.

### Q: How serious is the sabotage threat inside Poland?

Serious as a persistent condition, not as an acute danger. The campaign will not damage Poland's capacity to function, will not meaningfully interrupt support to Ukraine, and has not shifted Polish opinion in the direction its sponsor intended. What it does reliably is consume defensive resources, complicate the response calculus, and demonstrate reach at negligible cost. Treating it as an emergency overstates it and hands the sponsor a psychological return the acts themselves cannot deliver. Treating it as background crime understates a directed state effort and leaves the attribution problem unaddressed until an incident with serious consequences forces the issue. The proportionate posture is sustained institutional attention without alarm.

### Q: What categories of targets does sabotage in Poland favor?

The favored categories share a common property that matters more than what they are: high baseline rates of non-hostile failure. Logistics infrastructure is extensive, distributed, and unguarded along most of its length by necessity. Utility and communications systems fail routinely without any hostile cause. Commercial property burns for ordinary reasons every day. That statistical cover is the selection criterion, because it means a hostile instance does not stand out against the background and an alternative innocent explanation is always available. Symbolic sites are the exception, chosen for message value rather than cover, which is why an incident in that category should be read as communication rather than as disruption and assessed on different terms.

### Q: How do analysts tell sabotage from an ordinary accident?

Rarely from a single incident, which is the honest answer and the uncomfortable one. Individual cases are frequently unresolvable, because fire consumes its own origin evidence and mechanical systems fail on their own often enough to supply innocent explanations. What resolves the question is pattern rather than instance: the correlation of incidents with a category of facility, their distribution across states with a common exposure, their timing against other observable activity, and the accumulating record of prosecutions. An analyst working incident by incident will be wrong in both directions. One working from the pattern, and holding individual cases open until evidence closes them, produces a more reliable picture and a slower one.

### Q: Does sabotage in Poland aim at damage or at fear?

At neither as an end in itself. Damage is the mechanism and fear is one of four intended effects, but the object underneath both is a cost imposed without an attributable author. If damage were the aim the target set would prioritize importance over accessibility, and it plainly does not. If fear were the aim the campaign would seek publicity for its acts rather than deniability, and the two pull in opposite directions, since an act loudly claimed frightens more and denies less. The reason to hold this distinction is practical: a defender who believes the aim is damage will invest entirely in physical protection, and a defender who believes the aim is fear will overreact to media coverage.

### Q: Has Poland prosecuted people for sabotage-related offenses?

Yes. Polish authorities have investigated incidents in this category and have brought charges arising from them, and comparable prosecutions have occurred in other European states facing the same pattern. This is a matter of open record rather than assessment, and it settles one question while leaving another open. It establishes that at least some incidents are genuinely what they appear to be, which disposes of the reading that the whole pattern is an artifact of heightened suspicion. What it does not establish is proportion, meaning how many incidents in the wider category are hostile. The prosecutions also illustrate the structural limit, since they reach the person who acted, which is precisely the layer the sponsor treats as disposable.

### Q: Why does sabotage carry a risk of unintended escalation?

Because deniability and control are traded against each other. A sponsor maximizing deniability uses unskilled, uninformed, unsupervised participants, and therefore has minimal control over outcomes, including the outcome that people are hurt. Interference with utilities or transport can produce consequences nobody priced. A second risk runs the other way: a defender conditioned by a long campaign to read events as hostile may misread a genuine accident during a tense period, and the sponsor has spent months making accidents look like acts. A third is cumulative, since individually tolerable incidents can in aggregate cross a line nobody defined in advance. Intent restrains this campaign; execution does not.

### Q: Does a sabotage act on Polish soil trigger NATO consultations?

Consultation is available at a far lower threshold than collective defense, and that distinction carries most of the weight here. A below-threshold incident does not automatically engage the collective defense obligation, and treating it as though it does confuses two very different mechanisms. Consultation, by contrast, is precisely the instrument designed for ambiguous pressure that falls short of armed attack. Its realistic product is alignment rather than action: allies arriving at a common reading of a pattern, agreeing on evidentiary standards before rather than during a crisis, and reducing the chance of discovering disagreement at the worst moment. That is less than observers expect and worth considerably more than it appears.

### Q: What should the Polish public understand about this covert threat?

Three things, each of which is itself protective. First, that some incidents will occur and that no state can protect a target surface of this length completely, so failure to prevent is not evidence of negligence. Second, that attribution legitimately takes time, and the gap between what a government suspects and what it can state publicly is a structural feature of evidentiary and allied processes rather than concealment. Third, that public composure is a genuine countermeasure, since one of the four aims is psychological and it is the only aim a population can defeat directly by declining to supply the reaction. A public that expects incidents and does not treat each as either an attack or a cover-up denies the sponsor a return the acts cannot produce on their own.
