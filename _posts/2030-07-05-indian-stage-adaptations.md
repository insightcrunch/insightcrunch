India's Romeo and Juliet was a stage creation before it was a screen inheritance, and the stage that created it was not the classroom or the colonial playhouse but the commercial company theatre of Bombay. Indian stage adaptations of the tragedy begin inside the Parsi theatre, the company-based entertainment that from the 1850s carried Gujarati, Urdu and Hindi drama across the subcontinent and beyond it, and that took Shakespeare into its repertoire as raw material for songs, spectacle and local feeling. The verdict of this article is plain at the outset: the Parsi companies did not transmit the play, they rebuilt it, and the rebuilding was not a failure of reverence but the founding transaction of Indian popular theatre. When an Urdu dramatist of that stage turned the lovers' tragedy into a three-act tragicomedy with a happy ending, he was not betraying a text his audience revered in English. He was honouring the only contract his audience had signed, the contract of the ticket, which promised an evening of music, marvels and feeling, and which the tragedy of Verona, suitably rebuilt, could fill better than almost any story in the world.

![A Parsi theatre company stage with painted backdrop and footlights in Bombay - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-25.webp)

The documentary base for everything that follows is the theatre history of the period, above all Somnath Gupt's The Parsi Theatre: Its Origins and Development, the Hindi study of 1981 that Kathryn Hansen translated, abridged and edited for its English editions, and that Hansen calls the best single reference for the early period of Parsi theatre history. Gupt wrote from within reach of the survivors: his coverage begins with the antecedent phase of English theatre in eighteenth and early nineteenth-century Bombay and extends through the end of the nineteenth century, and his pages preserve play titles, company rosters and performance details that exist nowhere else, because the plays from the early period of the Parsi theatre are nowhere to be found as printed texts. That evidentiary shape governs the method of this article throughout. Where the record supports a playbill, the playbill is given. Where it supports only a title in a catalogue, the title is graded as a title. Where a date is divided in the scholarship, the division is stated rather than silently resolved. The Parsi record is rich and uneven at once, and an honest profile of the pre-Bollywood Indian stage has to keep both facts in view at every step.

## Before the Companies: English Shakespeare and the First Gujarati Adaptation

The Parsi stage grew out of a city that already had playhouses. Gupt's history opens with the Bombay Theatre and the English-language stage of the colonial port, where British officers and residents had introduced Shakespeare to Indian spectators towards the last quarter of the eighteenth century, performed by and for the rulers. That English Shakespeare belonged to the elite circuit: expatriate amateurs, college dramatic clubs, the educated few. Its Indian afterlife in the classroom, where the universities founded in 1857 made the plays set texts and examinations made them instruments, is a separate history with its own record, traced in the series' account of [Romeo and Juliet in the Indian curriculum](/2030/03/22/romeo-and-juliet-indian-curriculum/). The popular line begins elsewhere, at the moment Indian performers took the plays out of English and into the languages of the street and the bazaar.

The date the scholarship gives for that moment is startlingly early. Sisir Kumar Das, in his essay "Shakespeare in Indian Languages" in the 2005 volume India's Shakespeare, records what Chandravadan Mehta's account of Shakespeare in Gujarati also reports: the earliest known adaptation of a Shakespeare play in an Indian language was Dinshah Ardeshir Talyarkhan's Gujarati version of The Taming of the Shrew, its title translatable as A Bad European Woman Brought to Her Senses, staged by Kunvarji Nazar in the Andrews Library in Surat in November 1852. The first documented Indian-language Shakespeare was therefore a comedy, in Gujarati, performed in a library hall in Surat a year before the date usually given for the first Parsi production in Bombay. The fact matters for the shape of the whole tradition. Adaptation into Indian languages was not a late corruption of an originally faithful practice. It was the original practice, prior to the professional companies, prior to the playhouses, undertaken at the very first contact between Shakespeare and an Indian audience that did not read English.

The first Parsi production is usually dated to October 1853, when the Parsee Stage Players performed at the Grant Road Theatre in Bombay. Gupt counts only two theatres in the city in that year, one on Grant Road and the Khetvadi Theatre, and the early companies played in whatever halls they could build or borrow. What they played, from 1853 onward, was increasingly in local languages, Gujarati first and then Urdu and Hindi, and the repertoire mixed Persian romance, Hindu mythology, contemporary farce and, very soon, Shakespeare. In 1858, Gupt records, the Student's Amateur Club performed Romeo and Juliet itself. The performance was in English, by students, inside the elite amateur circuit, and it would be a mistake to make it the origin of the Indian stage Romeo and Juliet in any popular sense. But it fixes a date worth holding: within five years of the first Parsi production, the tragedy was already being acted in Bombay, and the two lines of its Indian life, the collegiate English line and the popular vernacular line, were running side by side from the start. The vernacular line is the one that built an industry. The earliest printed book of play scripts from the Parsi theatre, Nanabhai Rustamji Ranina's Shekaspir Natak, Shakespeare's Plays, appeared in 1865, and its very existence announces the direction of travel: Shakespeare had become printable, saleable Parsi theatre property while the century still had a third to run.

## The Company Machine: How the Parsi Theatre Worked

The Parsi theatre was a machine for making and moving plays, and its economics explain its Shakespeare better than any theory of influence. The establishment of the long-lived Victoria Theatrical Company in 1868 marks, in Kathryn Hansen's phrase in her study of actresses and female impersonators on the Parsi stage, the moment the Parsi theatre entered a period of professionalisation. Gupt's account of the Victoria is concrete about what professionalisation meant. When the company came into the hands of Dadi Patel in 1870, the question of a permanent home was settled by construction: the Victoria Theatre rose on a plot on Grant Road, so that the largest company of its time would have no difficulty performing its plays. A company that owned its playhouse owned its repertoire's future, and the Victoria's future was long. Around it stood the other great firms whose names structure the period: the Parsee Stage Players of the first production, the Elphinstone Dramatic Club out of the college amateur tradition, the Zoroastrian Theatrical Club, the Alfred Theatrical Company and its successor the New Alfred, the Empress Victoria Theatrical Company, companies in Calcutta under the Madan interest, and dozens of smaller troupes that formed, toured, split and re-formed across the decades. By the 1890s the leading companies employed salaried dramatists and salaried actors, which is to say the Parsi theatre had invented, inside India, the professional play factory.

### What did the word Parsi name in Parsi theatre, if not the audience?

It named the owners. "Parsi" was the trade sign over the door, marking the men who founded, financed and managed the companies, not the communities who wrote for them, acted in them or filled their seats, all of which were mixed from the form's first decade.

The word named the ownership and the management, not the performers, the writers or the crowd. The companies were founded and financed largely by men of the Parsi community, the Zoroastrian mercantile community of Bombay, and they took the community's name the way a firm takes its founder's. The product, from early in the form's life, belonged to everyone the ticket could reach: the playwrights were Hindus and Muslims as well as Parsis, the actors were drawn from several communities, the languages were Gujarati, Urdu and Hindi, and the audiences of the Grant Road playhouses and the touring grounds were the mixed public of the colonial city. Parsi theatre is a trade name that outgrew a community, and reading it as an ethnic label understates the machine's reach by exactly the distance between a boardroom and a bazaar.

The machine's reach was geographical as much as social. The companies were mobile by design. Gupt's history describes a company-based entertainment that reached all corners of colonial and princely India as well as South-east Asia, and the theatre histories record the Victoria Natak Mandali and its peers touring the whole country and beyond it, to Mandalay in Burma, to Bangkok, to Java. By 1900, troupes on the Bombay model had started in Karachi, Lahore, Jodhpur, Agra, Aligarh, Meerut, Lucknow and Hyderabad. A successful play was therefore not a production but a property, rehearsed once and sold nightly for years across a subcontinental circuit, and a successful adaptation was a property of unusual durability because its story arrived pre-tested. Shakespeare's plots came to the companies with two centuries of European box office behind them and no royalties attached. The wonder is not that the Parsi stage used Shakespeare so freely. The wonder, given the economics, would have been restraint.

### Why could Urdu carry a Bombay company's play to Mandalay and Java?

Because no other language the companies owned could travel the whole route. Gujarati stopped at the region's edge; Urdu, the subcontinent's prestige lingua franca and the language of the Lucknow court tradition behind Urdu drama itself, was understood from Karachi to Calcutta and in the Indian Ocean ports beyond.

Because Urdu was the one literary language the whole circuit held in common. Gujarati anchored the companies at home in Bombay and Surat, but a touring company playing Karachi, Lucknow, Calcutta and the ports beyond India needed a medium that travelled, and the prestige lingua franca of the nineteenth-century subcontinent, the language of the Lucknow court tradition from which Urdu drama itself descended, was Urdu. Kathryn Hansen's study of Urdu on the Parsi stage makes the strategy explicit: the adoption of Urdu as the principal medium of the stage enabled the Parsi theatre to extend its audience far beyond Bombay. Urdu carried the ghazal and the courtly love lyric inside it, a ready-made poetry of longing that a love tragedy could draw on without translation of feeling, and the companies that mastered it owned the road.

Inside the machine stood a figure the English theatre had no exact equivalent for: the munshi, the salaried company playwright. The munshi wrote to order, at speed, for known actors and known machinery, and his raw material was whatever the manager put in front of him: a Persian dastan, a Sanskrit epic episode, a novel, a European play in an English edition or in somebody else's adaptation. He was paid for playable nights, not for fidelity, and the repertoire he produced was judged nightly by the only critic the company could not dismiss, the paying crowd at the Grant Road railings. Everything about the Parsi Shakespeare, its songs, its spectacle, its rewritten endings, its comic servants promoted to co-stars, follows from that employment contract. The adaptation was not an interpretation of the play. It was a manufacturing specification.

## Seventy-Five Shakespeares: The Adaptation Trade by the Numbers

The scale of the trade is measurable, and the measurement survives in the scholarship. A scholarly census quoted in the theatre histories counts at least seventy-five texts that are direct translations or adaptations of Shakespeare, written roughly between 1870 and 1910, covering twenty-three different plays from the comedies, the tragedies, the histories, the romances and the Roman plays. Almost each of the twenty-three was translated or adapted more than once and by different authors. Not all of the seventy-five were staged, and not all were stage-worthy, and some were rehashes of already existing versions by more successful hands, which is itself evidence of a market: a text gets rehashed when a title sells. The Russian scholar Anna Suvorova, lecturing on Shakespearean influence on Urdu drama, put the concentration plainly: a whole number of theatre companies, the Alfred Theatrical Company, the New Alfred Theatrical Company, the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company among them, staged almost only the adapted versions of Shakespeare's plays through the years 1890 to 1910. For two decades, in other words, the spine of the commercial Urdu stage was adapted Shakespeare, and the companies were not ashamed of the fact. They advertised it in their names.

### What did a munshi actually do inside a Parsi company?

He manufactured the repertoire to order. Given a story by the manager, he converted it into a playable evening for known actors and known machinery, at speed, for a salary, and revised it under the nightly pressure of the house. The munshi was the company's engineering department, and its product was feeling.

He manufactured the repertoire. A manager acquired or assigned a story, and the munshi converted it into an evening: a main drama built in scenes that alternated pathos with comedy, dialogue in the heightened Urdu or Gujarati the house style demanded, lyrics for the songs the audience expected at the emotional peaks, business for the company's star comedian, and spectacle cues for the machinery the playhouse owned, the painted backdrops, the trick transformations, the flying and vanishing effects the Parsi stage was famous for. He worked fast, he worked for a salary, and he revised under the pressure of the house, lengthening what the crowd applauded and cutting what it endured. The munshi was less an author in the modern sense than the engineering department of a firm whose product was feeling, manufactured nightly.

The named munshis of the Shakespeare trade map its range. Mahmud Miyan Banarasi, called Raunaq (1825 to 1886), the senior Urdu dramatist of the early companies, wrote the famous romances of the first repertoire. Munshi Vinayak Prasad Talib wrote across the forms, from Ali Baba to a Ramlila, and drew Gupt's criticism for putting Urdu dialogue into the mouths of Ram and Sita, a complaint that shows the chronicler policing taste even inside his own archive. Narayan Prasad Betab, a giant of the later stage, ran a magazine actually called Shakespeare to publish his versions, an act of branding that deserves a pause: by the early twentieth century the name Shakespeare had become, on the Indian commercial stage, a masthead under which a Hindi playwright could sell a magazine. The name had detached from the man and attached to a genre of evening.

Towering over the trade stands Agha Hashr Kashmiri (1879 to 1935), regarded by Gupt as the greatest Parsi playwright. Hashr adapted across the canon: his Murid-e-Shak reworks The Winter's Tale, his Said-e-Hawas takes King John, his Khwab-e-Hasti builds a new play on the plot machinery of Macbeth, Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking included. He founded a company and named it the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company, and he liked to be called the Indian Shakespeare. The title is usually reported as vanity, and vanity was in it, but it was also a trade description. Hashr's claim was that he stood to the Indian commercial stage as Shakespeare stood to the English one: its chief supplier of playable stories, its house poet, its brand. The claim is the Indian stage's own verdict on where Shakespeare lived inside it. He lived in the writing office, as a job.

Beside the playwrights stood the actor-managers and the stars, and the Shakespeare trade ran on their persons as much as on texts. Khurshedji Balliwala of the Victoria Company, Kavasji Khatau of the Alfred, the comic actors whose names sold the farce that followed the tragedy, the female impersonators whose Juliets are treated below: the companies were star systems a generation before the film industry inherited the arrangement entire. A new Romeo and Juliet entered the repertoire when a company had a pair of lovers the crowd would pay to watch suffer beautifully, and it stayed while they stayed. The text was the occasion. The company was the work.

## Bazm-e Fani: Ahsan Rebuilds the Tragedy

Against that background the play's own Parsi career can be told at the strength the record supports. The named adaptation at its centre is the work of Mehdi Hasan, called Ahsan, Ahsan Lucknowi, one of the most prolific Urdu playwrights of the companies. Gupt's catalogue of Ahsan's Shakespeare adaptations lists four: Bhul Bhulaiyan from Twelfth Night, Dilfarosh from The Merchant of Venice, Khun-e-Nahaq from Hamlet, and Bazm-e Fani from Romeo and Juliet. The title is the first piece of analysis the adaptation offers. Bazm-e Fani means the fatal banquet, the assembly of mortality, a gathering under the sign of death, and a play that in English ends in a tomb is retitled in Urdu for the feast that carries its poison: Paris's wedding feast that never happens, the banquet of the Capulets at which the lovers meet, the whole social machinery of eating, marrying and burying that Verona runs on. Ahsan also gave the play a second title, Gulnar Firoz, attaching the lovers' story to a heroine's name in the Persian romance manner. Both titles announce the method. The play has been re-housed inside the dastan tradition, the Persian-Urdu romance of lovers, magicians, feasts and fates that the Parsi audience already knew how to watch.

The ending is the famous fact, and it must be stated with its evidence graded. The reception scholarship records that Ahsan's version turned the tragic grandeur of the original ending into a happy resolution, and that Bazm-e Fani was reshaped as a tragicomedy in three acts. A verdict recorded in the 2005 Shakespeare-in-India literature, quoted in the later reception study, judged that the adaptation ran the original completely upside down. The scholarly record divides on the date, and the division is carried here openly: the version is dated 1890 in the account that reports the happy ending, and 1897 in the reception study that glosses the title as The Fatal Banquet and describes the three-act tragicomedy. Whether the two dates mark composition and publication, a first version and a revision, or an error propagated between catalogues, the scholarship consulted does not settle, and this article does not pretend to settle it. What is not divided is the substance. In Ahsan's hands the lovers did not die as the Prologue promises they must, "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" who "take their life" (Prologue.6). The machine that Verona builds across five acts, in which "the continuance of their parents' rage" (Prologue.8) can end only in the children's burial, was disarmed in the final act, and the audience went home with the lovers alive.

The critical instinct to call that a falsification should be resisted until its ancestry is noticed, because the happy ending for this play has an English pedigree older than the Parsi stage itself. The reception scholarship, citing Sen's 1964 study, sets Ahsan in a line that runs through the Restoration adapters: Nahum Tate, whose King Lear spared Lear and married Cordelia to Edgar and held the London stage for a century and a half; George Granville, whose Jew of Venice reworked the Merchant; Colley Cibber, whose Richard III reshaped its original; and Thomas Otway, whose Caius Marius of 1679 is built directly on Romeo and Juliet, grafting the lovers onto Roman history. London's own commercial stage, the stage of Shakespeare's successors and improvers, treated the tragedies exactly as the Parsi munshis did, as strong stories whose endings were negotiable against the audience's appetite. Ahsan was not an Indian vandal defacing a European shrine. He was a late practitioner of the oldest commercial tradition the play has, the tradition that asks what the house will bear and writes that.

What the Parsi version did with the middle of the play can be reconstructed from the system's known habits and from the one piece of visual evidence the scholarship treasures. Kathryn Hansen's study of Urdu on the Parsi stage reproduces, as its second figure, a photograph of a Parsi theatre production of Romeo and Juliet, and her caption commentary reads the image closely: the foreshortened distance between the footlights, the actors' feet and the painted backdrop, and the tableau of the so-called pointed style, the freezing of bodily movement in elongated or angular poses that suggest the conclusion of a dance-like sweep of movement. The lovers of the Parsi stage, that is, did not converse their way to the tomb scene by scene as a modern realist production converses. They swept, they posed, they held the picture while the emotion landed and the song began. The play had been re-choreographed into the grammar of the Indar Sabha, the Urdu court pageant of Agha Hasan Amanat from the Lucknow of Wajid Ali Shah, in which song, dance and tableau were the substance and dialogue the connective tissue. A Romeo and Juliet rebuilt on that grammar kept the feud, the meeting, the balcony's longing and the potion's terror, and re-expressed each as music and picture. What it could not keep was the wordplay, the verbal fencing in which Shakespeare's lovers actually live, and the loss is real and should be named as a loss. It is priced into the verdict this article reaches below.

## Gulzar and Gulnar: The Sindhi Romeo and the Audience's Veto

The play's second documented vernacular life in this period belongs not to a company but to a scholar, and the contrast between the two cases defines the tradition's range. Mirza Qalich Baig (1853 to 1929), remembered in Sindh as the Shakespeare of Sindh, was the most prolific Sindhi author of his age, credited with more than four hundred and fifty books across dozens of disciplines, and among his life's work was the introduction of Shakespeare to Sindhi letters. His adaptations renamed their originals into his own landscape: Shah Elea for King Lear, Hasne Dildar for The Merchant of Venice, Shamshad-Marjana for Cymbeline, Aziz-ain-Sharif for The Two Gentlemen of Verona. His Romeo and Juliet, recorded in the reception scholarship under the title Gulzar and Gulnar and dated 1909, gave the lovers Sindhi names that belong to the garden imagery of Persian romance, Gulzar the rose garden, Gulnar the pomegranate flower, and set the Verona story down inside the naming world of Layla and Majnun and the Sindhi folk romances.

The ending of Qalich Baig's version carries the single most instructive detail in the play's whole Indian stage history. The reception scholarship records that he transformed his translation-adaptation so that it had a happy ending under popular pressure. The phrase deserves to be weighed. This was not a company munshi writing to a manager's order; this was the most learned man in Sindh, a scholar-translator working in print, and even he bent the ending because the audience for the story would not accept the tomb. Popular pressure is the Parsi system's deepest law surfacing inside a scholar's study: the Indian public that received Romeo and Juliet in these decades received it as a love story, and a love story, in the narrative economy that public inhabited, from the dastan to the folk romance, owed its lovers a union or owed its audience a reason beyond machinery for denying one. Shakespeare's ending, in which the reconciliation of the houses is purchased with two young corpses and the Prince's audit, "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love" (5.3.291-292), is a civic verdict disguised as a love story's close. The Indian popular audience, stage and page alike, repeatedly declined the disguise and demanded the love story stand alone. The demand was not ignorance of the original. Qalich Baig demonstrably knew his originals across a shelf of adaptations. It was a genre judgment, delivered by the only electorate the popular stage recognizes, and it won everywhere it was cast.

Ahsan and Qalich Baig therefore stand as the two poles of the Indian Romeo and Juliet before film. One was a professional of the companies, the other a scholar of the study; one wrote Urdu for the touring stage, the other Sindhi for the reading public; and both arrived at the same ending by the same road, the refusal of their audiences to let the lovers die unrescued. The convergence is the strongest evidence this article has that the happy ending was structural rather than accidental, a property not of any adapter's weakness but of the receiving culture's story system. When the film tradition later inherited the play, it inherited this settled expectation along with the songs and the spectacle, and the long Bollywood argument about whether the lovers may live, an argument the films conduct title by title, begins here, in a Bombay playhouse and a Hyderabad scholar's study, a generation before the first Indian feature film.

## The Southern Circuit: The Play in Kannada and Tamil

The Parsi companies were a northern and western machine, Bombay-built and Urdu-carried, but the play's pre-film Indian stage life was wider than the machine, and the southern record, thinner in documentation, deserves its own grading. The Internet Shakespeare Editions' survey of Shakespeare in India, drawing on the regional theatre histories, reports the Karnataka pattern: at least fifteen Kannada titles had been performed by 1929, usually in prose adaptations with some verse, beginning with a Comedy of Errors in 1871, and the freer appropriations of the 1920s amounted at times to original plays with a large Shakespearean infusion. Within that pattern the survey names this play directly: Romeo and Juliet was translated, or rather reinterpreted, into Kannada by Ananda Rao, and the actor A. V. Varadachar ensured the success of an immensely popular version of the play. The phrasing of the survey is the evidence's own grading and this article keeps it. The Kannada Romeo and Juliet was a reinterpretation rather than a translation, it was carried by a star actor whose presence guaranteed the house, and it was immensely popular, which in the company-stage economy of Mysore meant it toured and it lasted. The southern companies worked as the northern ones did, on the strength of an actor-manager's name and a repertoire of adapted classics, and Varadachar's Romeo and Juliet belongs in the profile below as a documented success whose text, like most of the period's texts, does not survive to be quoted.

The Tamil stage offers the parallel case at one further remove. The same survey credits P. Sambanda Mudaliar, the founding figure of the modern Tamil theatre, with popularizing the Tamil Shakespeare as Varadachar did the Kannada, through the amateur and early professional companies of Madras. The documentation for a specific Tamil Romeo and Juliet in this period is thinner than the Kannada record, and it is written as thin here: the Tamil Shakespeare was a repertoire fact, this play's place in that repertoire is asserted in general accounts of the southern stage rather than documented to a playbill in the scholarship consulted, and the honest sentence is that the lovers were part of what the southern companies played without a surviving record of the production that would let a historian describe it. The grading matters because the profile this article builds is meant to be used, and a profile that cannot say where its evidence runs out will be believed exactly where it should not be.

Taken together, the southern cases confirm the northern pattern from outside it. In Bombay the play was rebuilt by salaried adapters inside a commercial machine; in Sindh it was rebuilt by a scholar under his readers' pressure; in Mysore it was rebuilt around a star. Three different institutions, one result: an Indian Romeo and Juliet that belonged to its performers and its public first and to its English author second, if at all. The play had become, in the strictest sense, repertoire, material a company kept because the house came for it, and repertoire is the condition from which the Indian cinema's whole relationship with the story descends.

## What the Machine Did to the Tragedy: Song, Spectacle and the Moral Frame

The transformations can be itemized, because the Parsi system's habits are documented in general even where a particular production's prompt book is lost. The first transformation was musical. Music was not decoration on the Parsi stage; it was load-bearing, the medium in which feeling became public, and the system was old enough to have a named founder for the practice.

### Why does Somnath Gupt credit Dadi Patel with changing the Parsi stage?

Because Gupt credits Dadi Patel, the proprietor who took over the Victoria Theatrical Company in 1870 and built it its Grant Road playhouse, with introducing music into the Parsi theatre's productions. The attribution makes a manager, not a playwright, the author of the form's defining feature.

A company that sang its feelings needed a different kind of play from a company that spoke them: scenes built to arrive at songs, lovers whose meetings and partings were scored, dying words set to melody. Gupt's summary of the mature Urdu stage is that even fighting heroes and dying heroines would sing, and a theatre in which the dying sing has already decided, before any adapter touches the ending, that death in it will be an occasion for beauty rather than for waste. The musical is the happy ending's advance guard.

A Romeo and Juliet passed through that machine emerged with its emotional peaks redistributed. In Shakespeare's text the peaks are verbal: the shared sonnet of the first meeting, in which the lovers build a poem between them line by line; Juliet's "O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33), a soliloquy that argues with a name; the aubade's debate about the lark and the nightingale. On the Parsi stage those peaks became songs and tableaux, the pointed style freezing the lovers into pictures the house could applaud as pictures. The gain was immediacy across every barrier of language and literacy in a mixed urban crowd. The loss was the play's intelligence, the sense that these lovers think in language faster than their world can move, and it is the loss every later Indian version inherits and negotiates, the films included, which solve the same problem with the song picturization and inherit the same price.

The second transformation was spectacular. The Parsi playhouses were famous for their machinery: painted perspective backdrops that rolled and changed, trick scenes, transformations, processions, the whole apparatus of Victorian stage wonder that the Bombay companies bought, built and outdid. A five-act tragedy entering that repertoire was restocked with occasions for the apparatus, battles and banquets and magical interventions beyond anything Verona contains, because the apparatus was a fixed cost and every play was expected to pay it off. The third transformation was structural and comic. The Parsi evening was a bill, not a single work: the main drama was followed by a farce, a short comic piece typically in Hindustani, and the comic register also lived inside the main play in promoted servants and interpolated clowns.

### How did the farce that followed the main play change what the tragedy meant?

It discharged the tragedy's wound before the audience went home. A house that wept at a death and then laughed for half an hour at a farce by the same company left with its feelings completed, and the bill's structure framed even the gravest story as one movement inside a full evening's entertainment.

It changed the tragedy's finality. An audience that wept at a heroine's death and then, after a short interval, laughed for half an hour at a farce performed by the same company left the theatre with the evening's emotions completed and discharged rather than with a wound kept open. The bill structure taught its public to treat even the gravest story as one movement inside an entertainment, bounded by comedy on the far side, and that training is a large part of why the Parsi Romeo and Juliet could be at once a tragedy of young love for its first acts and a tragicomedy by its close. The farce did not trivialise the tragedy. It framed it inside the older Indian performance logic of the full evening, in which no single rasa, no single flavour of feeling, is allowed to monopolise the night.

The fourth transformation was corporeal, and it concerns who could be Juliet. Kathryn Hansen's study of female impersonators and actresses on the Parsi stage, its period running from 1853 to the arrival of the first sound feature in 1931, establishes that the early Parsi Juliet was played by a young man, in the stri bhumika, the female role taken by a male performer trained to its gestures, its costume and its codes of desirability, and that the arrival of actresses on the commercial stage was a slow and contested transition entangled with the public image of Indian womanhood itself. The balcony scene in a Bombay playhouse of the 1880s was therefore a double performance: a romance of two young lovers, embodied by two young men, before an audience that read the impersonation as craft rather than as disguise. The fact recalibrates any easy claim about what the Indian popular stage found in this play. It found, among other things, a supreme vehicle for the feminine impersonator's art, a heroine whose speeches of longing could display a performer's mastery of the feminine register the culture most prized and most policed, and the play's Indian popularity is inseparable from that vehicle's excellence.

The fifth transformation was moral, and it is written in the titles. Bazm-e Fani frames the story as a fatal banquet; Ahsan's four Shakespeare adaptations consistently renamed their sources into the moral and romance vocabulary of Urdu letters, Dilfarosh the heart-seller for the Merchant, Khun-e-Nahaq, innocent blood, for Hamlet. The New Parsi Victoria Company's Kali Nagin of 1906, Joseph David's adaptation of Antony and Cleopatra, ended happily with Antony regaining his throne and reuniting with his family. The pattern across titles and endings is a single gesture: the imported story is re-moralized into the receiving culture's ledger, in which suffering is real but must be accounted for, virtue must be visible in the outcome, and the heavens that in Verona kill the lovers "with love" as a scourge on their parents' hate are expected, on the Indian popular stage, to balance their books in the open. Shakespeare's play is itself a moral machine, but its morality is civic and ironic, purchased by corpses. The Parsi machine kept the moralizing and declined the irony, and the decline, once again, was a choice with an audience behind it.

## The Quarrel Over the Parsi Shakespeare

The scholarship has fought over this inheritance for a century, and the fight has three positions that a verdict has to separate. The first is the custodial position, internal to the theatre's own history. Gupt the chronicler is also Gupt the judge: his pages praise and blame individual playwrights by the standards of literary taste, as when he criticises Talib for the Urdu dialogue of his Ram and Sita, and the literary histories that followed him largely treated the Parsi stage as a commercial embarrassment, a bazaar episode between the classical traditions and the modern serious drama. The second position is the fidelity verdict applied to the adaptations themselves, of which the sentence already quoted is the sharpest surviving specimen: that Ahsan's Romeo and Juliet ran the original completely upside down. On this view the songs, the spectacle and the rescued lovers are so many corruptions, and the Parsi Shakespeare is a cautionary tale about what markets do to masterpieces.

The third position is the rehabilitation, and it is the one the evidence assembled in this article supports. Kathryn Hansen's work, as Gupt's editor and as the historian of Urdu and of gender on the Parsi stage, treats the adaptations as co-creations of playwrights and actor-managers answering a paying public, a distinctive Parsi-Urdu theatrical style whose literary and musical intelligence the custodial historians were trained not to see. Vasudha Dalmia's account of the formation of modern Indian theatre supplies the structural version of the same argument: the nineteenth century's new Indian drama was made precisely through translation and adaptation, through the labour of taking English and Sanskrit models apart and rebuilding them for Indian stages, so that adaptation is not what happened to Indian theatre after its creation but the manner of its creation itself. Sisir Kumar Das's survey of Shakespeare in Indian languages gives the rehabilitation its documentary spine for this subject in particular, a record that begins with a Gujarati Shrew in a Surat library in 1852 and runs through every major language, in which the translations and adaptations are the Indian Shakespeare, not a footnote to an English one happening elsewhere.

The adjudication this article reaches accepts the rehabilitation's explanation and the fidelity school's single narrow point. The narrow point is real: the Parsi versions do not transmit Shakespeare's language, and a claim that they spread Shakespeare must be discounted to mean that they spread his stories, his situations and his name. Everything beyond that point, the fidelity verdict gets wrong, and it gets it wrong in an instructive way. It measures the adaptations against a purpose, textual custody, that nobody in the transaction had contracted for. The managers contracted for playable properties; the munshis for evenings that held a house; the audiences for feeling, music and wonder; the scholars who later rewrote the endings in print bent to the same public the managers served. Judged by the purposes actually in force, the Parsi Romeo and Juliet was not a corrupted success but an uncorrupted one, among the most successful transfers of the play anywhere in the nineteenth-century world, measured by the only metric its makers recognized, the nightly verdict of people who had paid to feel something and kept paying for two generations. The corruption story tells us about the custodians. The record tells us about the play.

## The Lovers in the Machine: The Play's Scenes Under Parsi Grammar

The transformation can be followed scene by scene, because the play's great set pieces and the Parsi system's great strengths meet at predictable points, and at each point the system's grammar decides what survives. Take first the meeting. Shakespeare stages it as a shared sonnet: Romeo and Juliet speak a fourteen-line poem into existence between them, his quatrain answered by hers, the form itself enacting two minds discovering a single rhythm, before the kiss the sonnet has argued for. It is the most verbal seduction in English drama, fourteen lines of pure form, and it is almost perfectly untranslatable into the Parsi grammar, which has no use for a duet of words. What the Parsi stage had instead was the duet itself, sung. The meeting became a song exchange, the lovers' voices joining in melody as Shakespeare's join in metre, and the equivalence is exact at the level of function and total at the level of loss: the audience received the union the scene exists to display, received it more immediately than a sonnet can deliver it to listeners following a foreign text's reputation, and received nothing of the wit that makes the sonnet a proof of the lovers' quality rather than merely their ardour. Every later Indian version replays this bargain at this exact spot. The song at the first meeting is the Indian Romeo and Juliet's founding substitution, metre traded for melody, and it was made in the playhouses of Grant Road generations before it was made in a film studio.

The balcony scene tests the system differently. Its core is a soliloquy overheard, Juliet arguing with Romeo's name in the dark: "O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33), followed by the rose argument, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44). The scene's action is thinking. A stage of tableau and song cannot photograph a thought, but it can do the next thing: it can hold the picture, the girl at the lattice in the painted garden, the lover below in the darkened orchestra of the set, and let a solo song carry the longing that the soliloquy carries in argument. Hansen's photograph of the Parsi Romeo and Juliet, with its pointed style and its compressed depth between footlights and backdrop, is a picture of exactly that solution: the scene as an icon, composed to be read at a glance from the back of a large house. The icon travelled. The balcony image, lovers stacked vertically in a frame of greenery and moonlight, is the single most reproduced picture of the play in Indian popular culture, on playbills, on film posters, in the song picturizations that descend from the stage tradition, and its dominance begins with the practical fact that a posed tableau reads at forty rows and a soliloquy does not.

The potion scene tests the system's moral grammar. Juliet alone with the vial is Shakespeare's bravest construction, a thirteen-year-old arguing herself past terror in a speech of mounting hallucination. The Parsi system had no patience for a heroine alone with her thoughts at that length, but it had a supreme resource for terror: spectacle and the supernatural machinery of the dastan stage, the world of Bazm-e Fani's own title, in which potions are real instruments and their effects can be shown. What the monologue interiorizes, the machine exteriorized, and the scene's fear moved from the girl's mind to the stage's wonders. The tomb scene completes the pattern, because the tomb is where the systems' endings divide. Shakespeare's tomb is an audit chamber: Paris dead at the door, Romeo dead by his own hand mid-speech, Juliet dead on waking to a room already full of evidence, and the Friar and the Prince arriving to conduct the inquiry the last movement becomes. A stage that has exteriorized the potion's terror can stage the tomb's horrors magnificently, the vault, the torches, the bodies discovered by lantern light, and the Parsi companies, heirs of a stage that loved a transformation scene, cannot have stinted it. What that stage could not do, by the law documented in Ahsan and Qalich Baig alike, was leave the account unsettled: the lovers' deaths in the tomb are the one expenditure the Indian popular audience repeatedly refused to authorize, and so the tomb, the best spectacle in the play, became in version after version the place where the machine performed its greatest trick, the rescue, the revival, the discovery that the death was a sleep and the sleep has ended. The happy ending was not pasted onto the play's end from outside. It grew at the exact point where the Parsi stage's spectacle was strongest and its audience's moral demand was firmest, and any account of the Indian Romeo and Juliet that treats it as a blemish has failed to locate it in the machinery that produced it.

## The Archive Problem: Writing the History of a Theatre Whose Texts Are Lost

A word about evidence is owed before the profile, because the profile's entries carry different weights and the reader is entitled to the scale. Gupt states the problem at the head of his history: the plays from the early period of the Parsi theatre are nowhere to be found; it may be that they were not published. The commercial stage did not print what it could keep earning from as manuscript property, and what it did print, in playbooks and magazines and company editions, perished with the companies. The historian of the Parsi Shakespeare therefore works from a characteristic scatter: Gupt's chronicle, written in Hindi in 1981 from catalogues, memoirs and the testimony of a world still within memory's reach; the catalogues of printed Urdu and Gujarati drama on which the census of seventy-five adaptations rests; stray playbills and advertisements; the illustrated record that Hansen's edition of Gupt doubles, thirty-one illustrations against the original's set, photographs like the Romeo and Juliet tableau discussed above; and the retrospective scholarship, Das mapping the languages, Hansen the Urdu stage and its women, the Internet Shakespeare Editions' survey gathering the regional threads. Each stratum has its failure mode. Chronicles flatter the famous companies. Catalogues record titles of plays that may never have been staged, and the census itself warns that not all its seventy-five were stage-worthy. Photographs show a pose, not a performance. Retrospective scholarship repairs the record and, in repairing, argues with it, as the quarrel rehearsed above demonstrates.

The method of this article has been to let each claim stand at the height of its stratum. Ahsan's Bazm-e Fani is a documented adaptation with a divided date, because chronicle and catalogue agree on the play and divide on the year. Qalich Baig's Gulzar and Gulnar is a documented adaptation with a documented alteration, because the reception scholarship reports both the version and the pressure that changed its ending. Varadachar's Kannada Romeo and Juliet is a documented popularity with an unrecovered text, because the regional survey reports the success and no text is quoted anywhere in the record. The Tamil case is a repertoire probability stated as a probability. The distinction is not pedantry. The global literature on this play is full of Indian firsts and Indian versions repeated from secondary source to secondary source until repetition masquerades as documentation, and a series that audits its own claims has to decline the masquerade even when the masquerade would make a better paragraph. What survives the grading is still a remarkable record: the most widely travelled popular Shakespeare of the nineteenth-century world was not in English, and a measurable part of it was this play.

## The Touring Circuit: Grant Road to Mandalay

The geography deserves its own pass, because the play's Indian meanings were made as much by the road as by the repertoire. The companies' Bombay was a corridor of playhouses along Grant Road, in the district the British called Black Town, with the prestigious houses, the Gaiety opposite Victoria Terminus among them, standing apart in the Fort. Gupt's inventory of the playhouses built by Parsis reads like a property ledger of the whole enterprise: the Victoria Theatre built for the Victoria Company from 1870; the Tivoli, standing by 1886 opposite the terminus, built for the Alfred Natak Mandali and described by Gupt as a rough and ready playhouse; houses rising into the 1920s, the Wellington constructed by Seth Rustamji Dorabji and inaugurated in 1925, another completed the same year and converted into a cinema hall within a decade, a conversion that compresses the form's whole fate into one building's biography. The companies that filled these rooms were Bombay's, but their stage was the subcontinent: the touring circuit carried the repertoire through the presidency towns and the princely states, to Karachi and Lahore, to Calcutta where the Madan interest built its theatrical and then cinematic empire, and overseas to the port cities of the Indian Ocean world, Mandalay, Bangkok, Java, where Indian merchant communities made ready audiences for a travelling company playing in Urdu and Gujarati.

For Romeo and Juliet the circuit meant that the play's Indian form was standardized by travel. A version that played Grant Road had to play, with the same scenery in the same trunks, in a Lucknow hall and a Rangoon one, before audiences whose common property was the story's outline and the Urdu lyric's prestige, not any local dialect of feeling. The system's famous features are touring features: the tableau that reads at a distance, the song that carries across a noisy hall, the spectacle packed into portable wonders, the ending adjusted to the broadest denominator of the whole route's expectation. And the circuit outlived the form's Bombay prime. The regional theatre histories note Parsi-style companies surviving into the 1940s and beyond, Fida Hussain's name attached to the Calcutta tail of the tradition, long after the talkies had taken the centre. The play that Ahsan rebuilt in the 1890s therefore had a stage life of two generations at minimum, played by companies whose grandchildren in the profession walked, many of them, straight into the film studios.

## Ahsan's Four: The Retitling System of an Urdu Shakespeare

Ahsan's Romeo and Juliet gains depth when it is read as one panel of a set of four, because the four titles together reveal a system rather than a series of improvisations. Gupt's catalogue assigns Ahsan four Shakespeare adaptations. From Twelfth Night he made Bhul Bhulaiyan, the name translatable as a labyrinth of errors, a maze in which people lose their way, which converts Shakespeare's Illyria of mistaken identities into a piece of architecture the audience is warned about at the door. From The Merchant of Venice he made Dilfarosh, the heart-seller, which takes a play whose English title names a tradesman and renames it for the emotional commodity actually being traded, the heart in the casket plot and in the pound-of-flesh bond alike. From Hamlet he made Khun-e-Nahaq, innocent blood, which strips the Danish court of its philosophy and names the play for its moral residue, the guiltless blood the tragedy spills. And from Romeo and Juliet he made Bazm-e Fani, the fatal banquet. Read in sequence, the four titles perform the same operation four times: each discards the English play's persons and places, each seizes the story's emotional or moral essence as the Urdu audience would price it, and each reissues that essence as a romance signboard. Comedy becomes a maze, the merchant play becomes a heart market, the revenge tragedy becomes innocent blood, the love tragedy becomes a deadly feast. The system is a theory of adaptation in four title pages, and its consistency across comedy, romance and tragedy is the strongest internal evidence that the Parsi transformation was a house method with principles, not the sum of individual carelessnesses.

The method extended to the architecture of the evenings. Bazm-e Fani's three acts, against Shakespeare's five, are not a truncation but a re-proportioning to the bill: the Parsi main drama shared its night with music, with the comic business inside the play and with the farce that closed the evening, and three long acts carrying song and spectacle at the system's density fill the same hours that five acts of spoken English fill at the Globe's pace. The act count is a unit of time, not of respect. Inside those acts the dastan frame did the work the English play assigns to fate. Where Shakespeare opens with a Chorus who announces the deaths in advance and frames everything that follows as the working out of a star-written sentence, the Urdu romance frame offered its own machinery of destiny, the feast, the omens, the gathering under a sign, which Ahsan's title announces before a word is spoken. The lovers' story is equally fated in both systems; only the idiom of the fate differs, astrology in one, hospitality and its poisonings in the other. Fidelity criticism that counts the changed ending and stops counting there misses the deeper equivalence the adaptation laboured to build: a world in which the lovers were always going to be crushed by the arrangements of their elders, and in which the final question is not whether the machine works but whether, this once, it can be stopped in time. The Parsi audience's answer, delivered through its pressure on scholar and munshi alike, was that it could.

## Actresses and Impersonators: Who Could Be Juliet

The question of who embodied Juliet on the Parsi stage opens the form's most consequential social history, and Kathryn Hansen's study of female impersonators and actresses, covering the period from 1853 to the first sound feature in 1931, is its governing account. Hansen's premise is that the latter half of the nineteenth century and the early twentieth were a period of transition in which the public image of Indian womanhood was being crafted not only in literature and social experiment but through the commercial media of the Parsi theatre and the silent cinema. The commercial stage was thus a factory of femininity before it was anything else, and its first workers were men. The young male specialists of the stri bhumika, the woman's role, trained into its costume, jewellery, hairstyle and gestural codes, were among the companies' most bankable artists, and a great Juliet in the 1870s and 1880s was a great impersonator, admired precisely for the completeness of an illusion nobody in the house mistook for nature. The paradigm of womanly virtue that the culture most revered was, on its most popular stage, a male construction, rehearsed and applauded as construction.

The arrival of women performers on that stage was correspondingly fraught, and Hansen's account makes the mechanism clear: the actress carried into the theatre the associations of the public woman, of courtesan culture and sexual availability, which the impersonator's art had kept at a safe formal distance, and the companies' slow adoption of actresses across the period tracks a negotiation between the box office's appetite for novelty and the audience's policing of respectability. For Romeo and Juliet the transition changed the tragedy's charge in ways the record lets us state generally even where individual Juliets' names are lost. An impersonator's Juliet made the balcony scene a display of craft, the beloved as an achieved artwork. An actress's Juliet made it a display of exposure, a woman publicly performing desire, and the scandal and the thrill of the early actresses' performances are part of why the love tragedy, of all the repertoire, sat at the centre of the transition's controversies. The play whose heroine defies her father for a lover was, in the decades of the actress debates, not a safely antique story at all. It was the argument the society was having with itself, staged nightly in a Grant Road playhouse by the very women whose right to stand there was the argument's other half. That charge, too, passed to the cinema, whose first decades re-fought the same battle over its heroines with the stage's veterans on both sides of the camera.

## The Laughter After: Parody and the Parsi Stage's Long Afterlife

The commercial Parsi theatre died into the cinema in the 1930s, but the name survived in a second, distinct institution, the amateur Parsi-language theatre of the community itself, and this play survived there in the form least expected by the fidelity school: as comedy. The distinction matters and is kept throughout this section: the community stage of the twentieth century, charity productions and club theatricals in Gujarati, is not the professional company system of Gupt's history, and its Shakespeare belongs to the afterlife rather than to the adaptation trade proper. Within that afterlife, Meher Marfatia's history of twentieth-century Parsi theatre, Laughter in the House, records a charity tradition, the Parsi Medical Amateurs, presenting outrageous spoofs of the tragedies from 1917 into the 1950s and again from the 1970s to 1995, Hamlet no Omelette and Manchoo Macbeth among them, pioneered by Dr Jehangir Wadia, who wrote, directed and acted in the pieces. The veteran playwright Adi Marzban, remembered by the actor Burjor Patel, brought his sense of the comic and the absurd even to Shakespearean tragedy, turning Romeo and Juliet and Hamlet into parodies in the 1950s and 1960s that audiences received as laugh riots, and the record of the form's heyday claims King Lear itself played as comedy and a farce billed as Hamlet No Omelette.

Parody is evidence, and it cuts in the rehabilitation's favour. A story can only be parodied for an audience that knows it cold, beat by beat, and the Parsi community's twentieth-century laughter at Romeo and Juliet is the receipt proving how completely the nineteenth-century companies had naturalized the play: the lovers had passed from repertoire into folklore, the common property of a public that no longer needed the tomb explained or the balcony introduced. The sequence across the whole Indian record stands visible end to end. The play arrives as an English collegiate exercise; the companies rebuild it as song and spectacle and rescue its lovers; the scholar of Sindh rebuilds it again under his readers' veto; the southern stars carry it as repertoire; and the community stage, two generations on, laughs at it with the intimacy of ownership. Every stage of that sequence is a form of possession, and possession, not preservation, is what a popular theatre is for. The films that inherit the play in the next century inherit a story already possessed at every level of Indian performance culture, which is why their freedom with it has never needed an apology and never will.

## The Indar Sabha Inheritance: Lucknow's Gift to the Bombay Stage

The Parsi system's grammar did not invent itself in Bombay. Kathryn Hansen's history of Urdu on the Parsi stage begins the story outside the commercial capital altogether, in Lucknow, at the court of Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh, where in the middle of the nineteenth century Urdu drama, or at least its rudimentary form, originated in the Indar Sabha, the Assembly of King Indra, penned by Agha Hasan Amanat. The Indar Sabha was a court pageant of gods, fairies and lovers, built from song, dance, spectacle and the ghazal's idiom of longing, a entertainment in which the story existed to carry the music and the music existed to carry the feeling. Hansen's abstract states the succession in one line: beginning with the popular pageant, the Indar Sabha, Parsi theatrical companies embraced the form and carried it into the commercial theatre. The companies took a court entertainment, industrialized it, and set it touring.

The inheritance explains the Shakespeare adaptations better than any influence model that starts from the English text. When a Parsi munshi received Romeo and Juliet, he was not measuring an English tragedy against a void; he was fitting new story material into a going form whose slots were already cut: the lovers' meeting as a musical exchange, the beloved's beauty catalogued in lyric, separation scored as lament, the supernatural machinery of fortune embodied in spectacle, the whole carried by performers trained to pose, sweep and sing rather than to converse. Verona's feud mapped onto the form without forcing because the form's native stories, the Persian and Arabic romances the dastan tradition supplied, were already full of divided houses, forbidden meetings and family honour. Even the play's most famous verbal instrument, the lovers' shared sonnet, had a functional cousin waiting in the ghazal tradition's antiphonal longing. The Indar Sabha inheritance is why the adaptation trade could move so fast and so far: Shakespeare did not have to create an Indian popular theatre in order to be played by one. The theatre existed, with its grammar complete, and it treated the English canon as the newest warehouse of stories tall enough for its stage. Romeo and Juliet, the tallest love story in the warehouse, was always going to be among the first taken down.

## The Other Track: The English Shakespeare That Stayed English

Against the companies' rebuilt Shakespeare stood the small, stubborn world that kept the plays in English, and the contrast between the tracks is itself a finding. The college dramatic clubs, the Elphinstone College club in Bombay conspicuous among them, kept the flag for Shakespeare in English flying while the commercial stage a few streets away was turning the same plays into Urdu song. The English track had its own virtues: it preserved the words, it trained the Indian English-speaking elite in the metropolitan canon, and it fed directly into the examination system that made the plays the property of the universities after 1857. It also had a fraction of the audience. The two tracks ran through the same cities for the same eighty years and barely touched, one playing to hundreds in college halls and drawing rooms, the other to the subcontinent in playhouses and touring grounds, and the play's Indian destiny was decided overwhelmingly on the second track.

The afterlife confirms the proportions. When, in the middle of the twentieth century, Geoffrey Kendal's Shakespeareana company carried Shakespeare in English across India by train, town by town and school by school, it was performing a heroic coda to the English track, bringing the original words to audiences the companies had never sought, and the tour's fame in Indian memory is precisely the fame of an exception. The rule had been set in the Grant Road playhouses: in India, Shakespeare would be loved in translation, in adaptation, in song, or he would be, for the vast majority, unmet. The Parsi stage did not choose that rule out of perversity. It discovered it at the box office in the 1850s and 1860s, and every Indian medium since, the cinema above all, has ratified the discovery. Fidelity was available in India from the start, in the colleges and later on tour. The public, offered both, bought the other thing, and the history of Romeo and Juliet on the Indian stage is the record of what the other thing was.

## The Playbook Trade: Print, Piracy and a Magazine Called Shakespeare

The stage's relation to print was double, and the doubleness explains both the tradition's spread and its archive's poverty. A hit play was a company's capital, and capital is not published for rivals to steal; the early Parsi repertoire lived in manuscript prompt copies, guarded in the manager's trunk, which is why Gupt could lament that the plays of the early period are nowhere to be found. Yet print was also the trade's advertising and its memory. Ranina's Shekaspir Natak of 1865, the earliest printed book of Parsi play scripts, shows Shakespeare's name doing commercial work in print within a dozen years of the first production: by 1865 the plays were not only playable but sellable as reading, and the title's promise, Shakespeare's Plays in Gujarati dress, markets the adaptation itself as the attraction rather than apologizing for it. Across the following half-century the catalogues on which the modern census of seventy-five adaptations rests accumulated, printed versions multiplying because print served the second rank of the trade even when the great companies withheld their prompt books: provincial troupes buying texts they could not commission, amateur clubs in Karachi and Hyderabad staging what Bombay had premiered, readers encountering the stories in the bazaar editions that were the period's paperbacks.

Narayan Prasad Betab's magazine Shakespeare is the print trade's most eloquent single artifact. A leading company playwright founding a periodical under the English dramatist's name, in order to publish his own Hindi versions, completes the detachment the repertoire had begun: Shakespeare had become a Hindi publishing brand, a word that sold magazines to readers who would never see an English edition. It is worth setting Betab's masthead beside Agha Hashr's company name, the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company, and his personal title, the Indian Shakespeare. Between them, the three appropriations of the name map the whole system: the name as a company's trade sign, as a playwright's professional identity, and as a magazine's promise to its subscribers. In each case the name's value is commercial and its reference is to a function, the supplier of stories that hold a house or a readership, rather than to a corpus of English texts. Print also disciplines the historian's claims in the other direction, because catalogues preserve the titles of plays that were printed without ever being staged, rehashes and speculative versions among the seventy-five, and the census's own caution, that not all its texts were staged or stage-worthy, is the print record confessing its bias. The Indian stage Romeo and Juliet that can be documented to performance, Ahsan's above all, matters more than the printed versions that may never have faced a house, and the profile below grades them accordingly.

## Why This Play: Verona Inside the Dastan Story System

It remains to ask why this tragedy, among the twenty-three plays the trade adapted, proved so durable in the Indian repertoire, and the answer lies in how completely its machinery was already native to the story system the audience carried into the playhouse. The Parsi public's narrative inheritance, Persian dastan and Arabic romance filtered through Urdu, the folk romances of the regions, Heer and Ranjha in Punjab, Laila and Majnun everywhere, the Sohni and Sassi cycles of Sindh and the west, is a literature of love obstructed by family, tribe and fate, prosecuted through separation, disguise, messages miscarried and deaths that the audience is invited to weep over as the price of a world's arrangements. Romeo and Juliet enters that system not as an exotic import but as a recognizable citizen. Its feuding houses translate without friction into the clan and biradari politics every dastan assumes; its secret marriage is the romance tradition's own device; its friar with his potion is cousin to the dastan's holy men and magicians, whose drugs and sleeps are standard instruments; its miscarried letter is the missed message on which half the folk romances turn. Even the tomb has its native analogue in the lover's grave of the legends, the place where the community gathers to read the verdict on its own arrangements.

The play also offered the system two gifts its native stories lacked. The first was compression: Shakespeare runs the whole arc, meeting to marriage to tomb, in days, where the romances wander for years, and a commercial stage that had to deliver a complete emotional evening found in that compression a ready-made intensity, a story that could be sung and posed from first meeting to final tableau without a wasted scene. The second was prestige with deniability. The play arrived as the masterpiece of the rulers' own dramatist, certified by the colleges and the examinations, so that a company adapting it could claim elevation while practising transformation, and an audience could enjoy a dastan wearing the crown of English literature. The Indian Romeo and Juliet's longevity is overdetermined, and the overdetermination is the point: at every level, story, structure, machinery and status, the play fitted the receiving system as if cut for it, because in large part the system and the play descend from the same Mediterranean and Persianate romance stock, meeting again in Bombay after centuries apart. Ahsan and Qalich Baig were not forcing a foreign body into an Indian frame. They were repatriating a story.

## What the Stage Handed to the Screen

The bequest can be itemized as precisely as the transformations, because the film industry that displaced the Parsi theatre in the 1930s was built, in conspicuous part, by the theatre's own people and money. Kathryn Hansen's summary of the transfer is the authoritative one: the Parsi theatre contributed to early Indian cinema its textual legacies of story and theme, genre and star roles, and it also supplied the new industry with technical expertise, personnel and capital. The capital line is concrete. The Madan family, whose Calcutta theatrical empire stood inside the Parsi company system, retained its grip into the 1930s partly by converting its theatres into cinemas, and the regional histories credit Parsi capital with sustaining at least three major studios, the Imperial Film Company, Minerva Movietone and Wadia Movietone, alongside the Madan distribution network. The Imperial Film Company made Alam Ara in 1931, the first Indian sound feature, and Alam Ara arrived with its songs built in, the stage's musical contract transferred to celluloid in the very first talkie. Agha Hashr Kashmiri, the Indian Shakespeare of the company stage, wrote for the screen as he had written for the companies, and his Yahudi ki Larki, a stage property of the Parsi repertoire, reached Bombay film audiences in a well-known 1955 version. The playwright, the capital, the star system and the song were all stage property before they were film property.

For this play the bequest is specific. The Bollywood tradition that the series traces in its account of [the Bollywood Romeo and Juliet tradition](/2029/02/02/bollywood-romeo-and-juliet-tradition/) conducts, across a century of films, a running negotiation with the double death: which versions keep the tomb, which redistribute the deaths, which refuse them outright. That negotiation is usually described as Bollywood's own argument with Shakespeare. The stage record shows it is older than Bollywood and older than film, a settled term of the Indian popular contract that Ahsan's tragicomedy and Qalich Baig's vetoed ending had already written into the story's Indian form before any camera turned. The song that replaces the balcony's soliloquy, the spectacle that replaces the tragedy's inwardness, the family honour repriced for an Indian audience's moral ledger, the star pair whose persons sell the story before its plot is known: every one of these, which the film articles treat as cinema's grammar, is documented in this article as the Parsi stage's grammar first. Even the late, lavish inheritor examined in the series' study of [Ram-Leela, Bhansali's Indian Romeo and Juliet](/2029/01/19/ram-leela-bhansali-adaptation/), with its songs staged as confrontations and its feud rebuilt as clan spectacle, is spending capital the Grant Road companies accumulated. The screen did not Indianize Romeo and Juliet. It inherited an Indian Romeo and Juliet, fully formed, from the stage, and mistook the inheritance for its own invention because the stage's texts had perished and its own had not.

## The Verdict on the Indian Stage Tradition

The verdict follows from the record rather than from taste. Between 1852, when a Gujarati Shrew was staged in a Surat library, and the coming of sound film in 1931, the Indian stage made Romeo and Juliet into Indian popular property by the only method that has ever made a foreign play popular anywhere: it rebuilt the play in the local grammar of feeling and sold the result nightly to people free to stay away. The method cost the play its words. Nothing in the Parsi record suggests that any audience between Bombay and Mandalay received Shakespeare's language, and the series' standard for quotation, act and scene and line from a named edition, has nothing to fasten on in Ahsan or Qalich Baig, whose versions the scholarship describes and judges without quoting at any length. What the method bought was everything else: a story naturalized so thoroughly that the Indian cinema could inherit it as a native possession, a star-crossed machinery of feuding houses and forbidden love that Indian audiences had been rehearsing for two generations before the first film, and a standing audience expectation, the demand that the lovers' story justify its suffering inside a moral ledger the audience recognizes, against which every Indian version since has had to bargain. The classroom kept another Shakespeare in the same decades, the set text of the examinations, and the two Indian Shakespeares, the examined one and the enacted one, met far less often than either tradition's historians admit; the play's later school life across countries, India included, is gathered in the series' account of [the play in global classrooms](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/). Of the two, the enacted Shakespeare is the one that made the play India's. The Parsi munshi who ran the original upside down understood his commission exactly. He was not keeping a text. He was founding a repertoire, and the repertoire is still running.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Parsi theatre, and when did it flourish?

The Parsi theatre was the commercial, company-based theatre that dominated popular entertainment in India from the 1850s until the coming of sound film in the 1930s. It took its name from the Parsi, Zoroastrian, businessmen of Bombay who founded and financed its companies, though its playwrights, actors and audiences came from every community. Its first production is usually dated to October 1853, when the Parsee Stage Players performed at Bombay's Grant Road Theatre, and its professional era opens with the Victoria Theatrical Company in 1868. The companies played in Gujarati, Urdu and Hindi, built their own playhouses, employed salaried playwrights and actors, and toured a circuit that reached across India and overseas to Mandalay, Bangkok and Java. Somnath Gupt's history, in Kathryn Hansen's edition, is the standard reference for its early period. The form's mixture of song, spectacle, melodrama and comedy made it the direct ancestor of Indian popular cinema, which inherited its capital, its personnel and its grammar when the talkies displaced it.

### Q: Who was Mehdi Hasan Ahsan, and what did he do to Romeo and Juliet?

Mehdi Hasan Ahsan, known as Ahsan Lucknowi, was one of the most prolific Urdu playwrights employed by the Parsi companies, and he rebuilt Romeo and Juliet as the Urdu tragicomedy Bazm-e Fani. Gupt's catalogue credits Ahsan with four Shakespeare adaptations: Bhul Bhulaiyan from Twelfth Night, Dilfarosh from The Merchant of Venice, Khun-e-Nahaq from Hamlet, and Bazm-e Fani from Romeo and Juliet. His method was systematic. Each English title was discarded and replaced with a signboard naming the story's emotional essence, a maze of errors, a heart-seller, innocent blood, a fatal banquet. Bazm-e Fani, which also circulated under the title Gulnar Firoz, compressed the five-act tragedy into three acts, rehoused it inside the Persian romance frame the Parsi audience knew, and replaced the tomb ending with a happy resolution in which the lovers survive. Ahsan was no marginal hack but a leading professional of the adaptation trade at its height, and his Romeo and Juliet is the best-documented Parsi version of the play, which is why this article builds its account of the system around his example.

### Q: What does Bazm-e Fani mean, and why is its date disputed?

Bazm-e Fani means the fatal banquet, or the assembly of mortality: a gathering held under the sign of death. The title reframes Shakespeare's tragedy around the feast rather than the tomb, pointing at the Capulet banquet where the lovers meet and at the wedding feast that turns into a funeral in the original's machinery. Its date is disputed because the scholarship that preserves the play divides against itself. The account that reports Bazm-e Fani's happy ending dates the version to 1890, while the reception study that glosses its title as The Fatal Banquet and describes its three-act tragicomedy form dates it to 1897. The two dates may mark composition and publication, an original and a revision, or an error copied between catalogues; the sources consulted in preparing this article do not settle the question, and the article therefore carries the division openly rather than choosing a year. The substance is not in dispute: Ahsan's authorship, the Urdu adaptation, the three-act shape and the substituted happy ending are consistently reported, and the play stands in the InsightCrunch Indian-stage profile as a documented adaptation with a divided date.

### Q: Why did Ahsan's version let the lovers live?

Ahsan's version let the lovers live because his audience's story system required the rescue, and his employment required him to satisfy that audience nightly. The Parsi companies sold feeling to a paying public whose narrative inheritance, the Persian dastan and the regional folk romances, treated love obstructed by family as a trial to be survived or mourned inside a moral order that balances its books in the open. Shakespeare's ending, in which the houses reconcile over two corpses, is a civic verdict, and the Prince states its logic outright: heaven has found means to kill the lovers' joys with love as a scourge on their parents' hate. The Indian popular audience repeatedly declined that bargain, in the playhouses and, as the Sindhi case shows, in print as well. Ahsan was also working inside an older commercial tradition than the objection assumes: London's own Restoration stage had rewritten the tragedies' endings for a century and a half, Tate sparing Lear, Otway rebuilding this very play as Caius Marius. The happy ending was a professional's answer to a documented demand, not a failure to understand the original.

### Q: Who was Mirza Qalich Baig, and what is Gulnar and Gulnar?

Mirza Qalich Baig (1853 to 1929) was the most prolific Sindhi author of his age, honoured in Sindh as its Shakespeare, credited with more than four hundred and fifty books across dozens of disciplines, and the scholar who introduced Shakespeare to Sindhi letters. His adaptations renamed their originals into his own landscape: Shah Elea for King Lear, Hasne Dildar for The Merchant of Venice, Shamshad-Marjana for Cymbeline, Aziz-ain-Sharif for The Two Gentlemen of Verona. Gulzar and Gulnar, dated 1909 in the reception scholarship, is his Romeo and Juliet. The title gives the lovers names drawn from the garden imagery of Persian romance, Gulzar the rose garden and Gulnar the pomegranate flower, setting Verona down inside the naming world of Layla and Majnun and the Sindhi folk romances. Where Ahsan was a salaried professional of the touring companies, Qalich Baig was a scholar working in print for a reading public, and the contrast between the two men defines the range of the play's Indian reception before film: the same story rebuilt at opposite ends of the literary economy, by the playhouse and by the study, with the same ending imposed on both.

### Q: What does the phrase "under popular pressure" reveal about the Sindhi version's ending?

The phrase reveals that the happy ending of the Indian Romeo and Juliet was demanded from below rather than merely supplied from above. The reception scholarship records that Mirza Qalich Baig transformed his translation-adaptation Gulzar and Gulnar so that it had a happy ending under popular pressure. Qalich Baig was the most learned man in Sindh, a scholar-translator with a shelf of Shakespeare adaptations behind him, demonstrably capable of following his originals where he chose. His ending was therefore not ignorance and not a company's commercial formula imposed on a hireling; it was a considered surrender to his readership's genre judgment, the insistence of the Sindhi reading public that a love story owed its lovers a union or an account of their loss that the tomb does not give. Placed beside Ahsan's case, the phrase completes the evidence for this article's central claim. The munshi in Bombay and the scholar in Hyderabad bent the same ending under the same force, which means the force was structural, a property of the receiving culture's story system, and not an accident of any single adapter's taste or competence.

### Q: How many Shakespeare adaptations did the Parsi stage produce between 1870 and 1910?

The scholarly census quoted in the theatre histories counts at least seventy-five texts that are direct translations or adaptations of Shakespeare, written roughly between 1870 and 1910. Those seventy-five texts cover twenty-three different plays, spanning the comedies, the tragedies, the histories, the romances and the Roman plays, and almost every one of the twenty-three was translated or adapted more than once by different hands. The census carries its own cautions, which the histories repeat: not all seventy-five were staged, not all were stage-worthy, and some were rehashes of more successful authors' versions, produced because a title that had sold once could be sold again. The rehashing is itself evidence, since texts are only worth rehashing in a working market. For two decades at the trade's height, roughly 1890 to 1910, companies including the Alfred, the New Alfred and the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company staged almost nothing but adapted Shakespeare, according to the Russian scholar Anna Suvorova's account of Urdu drama. Adapted Shakespeare was not a sideline of the Parsi stage. For a generation it was close to being the repertoire itself.

### Q: Who was Agha Hashr Kashmiri, and why was he called the Indian Shakespeare?

Agha Hashr Kashmiri (1879 to 1935) was the Parsi stage's most celebrated playwright, regarded by Somnath Gupt as the greatest dramatist of the companies, and the title Indian Shakespeare was his own professional claim, which the theatre world ratified. His adaptations ranged across the canon: Murid-e-Shak reworks The Winter's Tale, Said-e-Hawas takes King John, and Khwab-e-Hasti builds a new play from the plot machinery of Macbeth, Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking scene included. He founded a company and named it the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company, an act of branding that announced where Shakespeare lived inside the Indian commercial theatre: in the writing office, as a job of work. Hashr liked to be called the Indian Shakespeare, and the title was more than vanity. It asserted that he stood to the Indian stage as Shakespeare stood to the English one, its chief supplier of playable stories and its house poet, and the claim held because the box office kept confirming it. His career also bridges the stage and the screen: he wrote for the early cinema as he had written for the companies, and his stage property Yahudi ki Larki reached Bombay film audiences in a well-known version of 1955.

### Q: What was the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company?

The Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company was the company founded by Agha Hashr Kashmiri, the leading playwright of the Parsi stage, and named by him for the English dramatist whose works supplied so much of the commercial repertoire. Its name is the most compact piece of evidence in this article. A touring company of the early twentieth century, playing Urdu drama to popular audiences across India, chose to trade under Shakespeare's name, which means the name itself had become a box-office asset separable from any English text: it promised an evening of strong story, high feeling and spectacle, the product the company existed to sell. The company belonged to a cluster of firms for whom adapted Shakespeare was the core business in the decades around 1900. The Russian scholar Anna Suvorova's account of Urdu drama lists the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company beside the Alfred and the New Alfred among the companies that staged almost only adapted versions of Shakespeare's plays through the years 1890 to 1910. Hashr's company simply carried the trade's logic furthest, wearing on its letterhead the dependence that the whole industry practised.

### Q: What did the Student's Amateur Club perform in Bombay in 1858?

Somnath Gupt records that the Student's Amateur Club performed Romeo and Juliet in Bombay in 1858. The performance was in English, by students, inside the elite amateur circuit of college and club theatricals, and it matters to this article as a fixed point rather than as an origin. It shows that within five years of the first Parsi production in 1853, the tragedy was already being acted in Bombay, and that the two lines of the play's Indian life, the collegiate English line and the popular vernacular line, ran side by side from the beginning. The English line led toward the examination hall, where the universities founded in 1857 were making Shakespeare a set text. The vernacular line led toward the Grant Road playhouses, the touring circuit and eventually the film studios. The 1858 performance belongs to the first line, and this article cites it to mark the fork, not to claim it for the popular tradition. The popular Romeo and Juliet, the one that filled playhouses from Bombay to Mandalay and taught the Indian audience to expect songs and a rescue, was built later, by the companies, in Indian languages.

### Q: What happened to the Parsi companies when sound film arrived?

Sound film dismantled the Parsi company system within a decade, but it dismantled it by absorbing it rather than by defeating it. The first Indian sound feature, Alam Ara, was released in 1931 by the Imperial Film Company, one of the studios the regional histories credit Parsi capital with sustaining, and it arrived with songs built in, the stage's musical contract transferred directly to the screen. Kathryn Hansen's summary of the transfer is that the Parsi theatre supplied early Indian cinema with its textual legacies of story and theme, genre and star roles, and with technical expertise, personnel and capital besides. The Madan family, whose theatrical empire stood inside the company system, held its position into the 1930s partly by converting its empire of theatres into cinemas. Playwrights became screenwriters, Agha Hashr among them; actors crossed over; playhouses became picture palaces, one of Gupt's 1925 theatres converting within ten years of its construction. A few companies lingered on the road into the 1940s and beyond, Fida Hussain's name attached to the Calcutta tail of the tradition, but the centre had moved. The stage did not lose an argument with the cinema. It became the cinema, and stopped needing the road.

### Q: How did the Kannada stage take up Romeo and Juliet?

The Kannada stage took up the play as a star vehicle inside a regional company system that mirrored the Parsi machine's economics. The Internet Shakespeare Editions' survey of Shakespeare in India, drawing on the regional theatre histories, reports that at least fifteen Kannada titles had been performed by 1929, usually in prose adaptations with some verse, beginning with a Comedy of Errors as early as 1871, and that the freer appropriations of the 1920s shaded into original plays with a large Shakespearean infusion. Within that pattern the survey names this play directly: Romeo and Juliet was translated, or rather reinterpreted, into Kannada by Ananda Rao, and the actor A. V. Varadachar ensured the success of an immensely popular version. The survey's phrasing grades the evidence exactly, and this article keeps the grading. The Kannada Romeo and Juliet was a reinterpretation rather than a translation, its success was built on a star performer's presence, and its text does not survive in the record to be quoted. The Tamil stage offers the parallel at one remove, P. Sambanda Mudaliar popularizing the Tamil Shakespeare as Varadachar did the Kannada, with this play's specific Tamil record thinner than the Kannada one.

### Q: What is Kali Nagin, and what does its ending show about the adaptation system?

Kali Nagin, the Black Snake, is the Urdu adaptation of Antony and Cleopatra made by Joseph David and produced in 1906 by the New Parsi Victoria Company, and its ending is this article's control case for the adaptation system's deepest habit. Where Shakespeare's Roman tragedy ends with both lovers dead and Egypt annexed, Kali Nagin ended happily, with Antony regaining his throne and reuniting with his family. The case matters because it cannot be explained by anything specific to Romeo and Juliet: not by the lovers' youth, not by romance sentimentality, not by one adapter's weakness. A Roman political tragedy, adapted by a different hand for the Victoria's successor company a decade and a half after Bazm-e Fani, received the same operation, the tragic ending dismantled and the account settled in the audience's favour. The reception scholarship sets the pattern in a lineage that includes London's own Restoration adapters, Tate's Lear spared and Otway's Caius Marius rebuilt from this very play. Kali Nagin proves the happy ending was house policy across the system, the Parsi stage's standing answer to tragedy itself.

### Q: Why do scholars disagree about whether the Parsi adaptations corrupted Shakespeare?

The disagreement is about what an adaptation is for, and the two sides measure the Parsi versions against different contracts. The fidelity school measures them against textual custody: by that standard the songs, the spectacle, the promoted clowns and above all the rescued lovers are corruptions, and the sharpest surviving verdict, recorded in the 2005 Shakespeare-in-India literature, holds that Ahsan's Romeo and Juliet ran the original completely upside down. The custodial instinct existed inside the tradition too: Gupt himself, the form's great chronicler, judges his playwrights by literary taste and criticises Talib's Urdu dialogue for Ram and Sita. Against this stands the rehabilitation associated with Kathryn Hansen's histories of the Urdu stage and Vasudha Dalmia's account of modern Indian theatre's formation: the adaptations were co-creations answering a paying public, and adaptation through translation was the very mechanism by which a modern Indian drama was made. This article adjudicates for the rehabilitation, conceding the fidelity school one point only: the versions transmitted Shakespeare's stories and name, not his language, and claims for them must be discounted to exactly that.

### Q: What was the Indar Sabha, and how did it shape the Parsi stage's Shakespeare?

The Indar Sabha, the Assembly of King Indra, was the Urdu court pageant written by Agha Hasan Amanat for the Lucknow of Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh, in the middle of the nineteenth century, and it is the template from which the Parsi stage's grammar descends. Kathryn Hansen's history of Urdu drama begins there: Urdu drama originated in that court milieu, and the Parsi companies, beginning with the popular pageant of the Indar Sabha, embraced its form and industrialized it. The pageant was a spectacle of gods, fairies and lovers built from song, dance and tableau, its story existing to carry its music and its music to carry the ghazal's idiom of longing. When Parsi munshis later received Shakespeare's plays, they fitted them into slots the Indar Sabha had already cut: the meeting as a musical exchange, separation as lament, fortune embodied in spectacle, feeling posed in tableau rather than argued in dialogue. Romeo and Juliet fitted those slots with unusual completeness, which is why its adaptation was early, repeated and durable. The play was not Indianized against its grain. It was poured into a mould that Lucknow had cast before Bombay built its first playhouse.

### Q: Who played Juliet in the early Parsi productions?

Juliet was played, in the early Parsi productions, by young men: specialists of the stri bhumika, the woman's role, trained into its costume, jewellery, hairstyle and gestural codes. Kathryn Hansen's study of female impersonators and actresses on the Parsi stage, covering 1853 to the first sound feature in 1931, establishes the impersonator as one of the commercial stage's bankable artists, admired for the completeness of an illusion the audience read as craft rather than disguise. The paradigm of womanly virtue that the surrounding culture revered was, on its most popular stage, a male construction, applauded as construction. The arrival of actresses across the period was slow and contested, because the actress carried associations of the public woman that the impersonator's formalism had kept at a distance, and Hansen argues that the commercial media of the Parsi theatre and early cinema were among the forces crafting the period's public image of Indian womanhood itself. For this play the transition mattered concretely: an impersonator's Juliet made the balcony scene a display of achieved art, while an actress's Juliet made it a display of exposure, a woman publicly performing desire, and the love tragedy sat at the centre of the controversy.

### Q: How far did the Parsi companies tour, and where did they play?

The Parsi companies played a circuit that covered the subcontinent and reached overseas, and touring was a condition of their economics rather than an occasional venture. Somnath Gupt describes a mobile, company-based entertainment reaching all corners of colonial and princely India as well as South-east Asia; the theatre histories record the leading companies touring the whole country and beyond it, to Mandalay in Burma, to Bangkok and to Java, where Indian merchant communities made ready audiences. By 1900 troupes on the Bombay model had also taken root in Karachi, Lahore, Jodhpur, Agra, Aligarh, Meerut, Lucknow and Hyderabad, and the Calcutta companies under the Madan interest anchored the east. At home the machine's geography was a corridor of playhouses along Bombay's Grant Road, the Victoria Theatre built for the Victoria Company from 1870 among them, with grander houses standing apart in the Fort. For the repertoire the circuit was a standardizing force: a Romeo and Juliet that had to play, with the same scenery in the same trunks, in a Lucknow hall and a Rangoon one was rebuilt for the broadest common expectation of the whole route, in tableau, song and spectacle that read at any distance.

### Q: Why did a Hindi playwright publish a magazine called Shakespeare?

Narayan Prasad Betab published a magazine called Shakespeare in order to publish his own versions of the plays, and the masthead is one of the most eloquent artifacts of the whole adaptation trade. Betab was a leading playwright of the later Parsi stage, and his periodical turned the English dramatist's name into a Hindi publishing brand: a word that could sell a magazine to readers who would never open an English edition. The gesture completes a detachment visible across the system. Agha Hashr founded a company named the Indian Shakespeare Theatrical Company and took the Indian Shakespeare as his personal title; Betab put the name over a magazine. In each case the name's value is commercial and its reference is functional, pointing to the supplier of stories that hold a house or a readership rather than to a corpus of English texts. By the early twentieth century Shakespeare no longer named an author whose works might be performed faithfully or otherwise. It named a genre of evening and a standard of story, and the men who traded under it were, by the only measure their market kept, entitled to the name.
