The question of how Gatsby criticism changed by decade has a clean answer and a complicated one, and this article insists on both. The clean answer is the sequence everyone in the field can recite: contemporary reviews, mid-century revival and New Criticism, the theory decades, the identity-focused and textual turns. The complicated answer is the claim this account defends from its first sentence to its last: each era of that sequence asked different questions rather than better ones, and the novel kept answering because the questions kept changing, not because the criticism kept improving. A scholarly conversation that ran for more than a century did not converge on the book. It circled the book, approaching from whichever side each generation cared about most, and the circling is the story. Review asked whether the novel was good. Revival asked whether it was serious. New Criticism asked how it was made. Theory asked what it concealed. Identity criticism asked whom it served and whom it harmed. Textual scholarship asked which words were really Fitzgerald's. The trajectory shows something rarer than progress. It shows a book capacious enough to be reread every time the methods of literary study were reinvented, the same novel generating new questions whenever the discipline found new ones to ask, the same sentences yielding new evidence to readers trained to look for different things.

That claim needs a precise territory, because this series treats the criticism of the novel in several articles that must not blur into one another. The critical-history lens article gives the general-student version, the streamlined narrative of reputation from 1925 to the canon, and a reader who wants the short version should begin with that [critical-history account](/2028/02/28/gatsby-critical-history/). The scholarship survey gives the field map, the strands and landmarks arranged by topic rather than by time, and a reader who wants to know where a particular debate lives should consult that [survey of the scholarship](/2030/12/02/gatsby-scholarship-survey/). This article owns the decade-by-decade account: the shifting questions and methods from the 1920s to the 2020s, laid out in chronological order, with the evidence for each shift and the argument about what the shifts mean. The three accounts answer different questions. The lens article tells a student what happened to the reputation. The survey tells a researcher where the debates are. This article tells anyone who wants the full record how the questions themselves changed, era by era, and why the changes took the shape they did. The division is deliberate, and the articles are built to be read against one another rather than in place of one another.

The evidence for a trajectory like this is not a single archive but a composite, and the composite has to be assembled with discipline or the story becomes impression. The 1920s survive in the periodical reviews, named reviewers in named newspapers and magazines, preserved in the files of the periodicals and in the reception histories that anthologized them. The 1940s revival survives in essays and editions: Edmund Wilson's memorial work, Lionel Trilling's 1945 essay on Fitzgerald, the 1945 Armed Services Edition that put 155,000 copies into servicemen's hands. The 1950s survive in the institutional record: Arthur Mizener's The Far Side of Paradise in 1951, Malcolm Cowley's 1953 Viking edition, Marius Bewley's 1954 essay on Fitzgerald's criticism of America, and the syllabi that began to carry the novel into classrooms. The theory decades survive in the journals, the monographs, the dissertations, the edited collections, Matthew J. Bruccoli's Some Sort of Epic Grandeur in 1981 and his edited New Essays on The Great Gatsby in 1985 among the landmarks. The identity and textual turns survive in work like Judith Fetterley's chapter in The Resisting Reader from 1978, Toni Morrison's Playing in the Dark from 1992, Sarah Churchwell's Careless People from 2014, the Cambridge Trimalchio edition of 2000, and James L. W. West III's 2019 Cambridge variorum. Attribution in this article follows the series rule: name the scholar, the work, and the year only when the record is verified, and otherwise attribute generically, because a history of criticism that invents its evidence is worse than no history at all. Where the record is thin, as in the 1930s, the article says so and explains what the thinness means rather than filling it with conjecture.

The complication this article addresses honestly is the temptation to narrate the trajectory as simple progress, each generation seeing more clearly than the last, the criticism maturing toward a final true reading. That story is flattering and false, and it does a disservice to every era it touches. The 1925 reviewers were not simply wrong in a way the 1950s corrected; they were reading inside a frame, the daily notice, that made certain questions askable and others invisible, and the later critics read inside frames of their own that had the same structure of blindness. New Criticism saw the form with unprecedented precision and had little to say about the world that produced it. The theory decades saw the ideology with unprecedented penetration and sometimes lost the sentences in the system. Identity criticism restored the bodies and the politics the formalists had bracketed, and textual scholarship restored the words themselves, and each restoration was also a narrowing, a choice about what counts as the real object of study. Gains were real. Losses were real. The honest account records both, era by era, and argues that the right verb for what happened is not improved but changed: the criticism changed its questions, and the novel, being large enough to contain the answers to questions its first readers never thought to ask, answered.

The stakes of this history are practical, not antiquarian. A reader who knows how the questions changed reads the novel differently from one who knows only the latest answers, because the history exposes the contingency of every reading, including the latest one. The student who learns that the thinness charge was a 1925 frame rather than a fact will not repeat it unknowingly. The scholar who learns that the national-allegory reading was Trilling's invention rather than the novel's essence can use it as an instrument rather than inheriting it as a dogma. The teacher who sees the classroom's role in shaping the certified readings can distinguish the pedagogical necessity from the critical discovery. And every reader gains the same larger lesson the trajectory teaches: that the book now being read has been read, and misread, and reread, by generations of intelligent people who were certain they had it right, and that the certainty of the present deserves the same skepticism the history trains on the past. The timeline below gives the skeleton of the account; the sections that follow put flesh on each era's questions, methods, visions, and blind spots.

### What did the 1920s reviews establish?

The 1920s reviews established the founding pattern of all later Gatsby criticism: admiration for the prose paired with doubt about the substance. The reviewers praised the writing warmly, called the story thin, and filed the novel as a brilliant period piece, fixing the terms every later generation had to answer.

The corpus is unusually well preserved for a 1925 reception, and its shape matters more than any single verdict. Edwin Clark in the New York Times Book Review called it a curious book and praised the perfected form. H. L. Mencken in the Chicago Tribune granted the craftsmanship and dismissed the story as a glorified anecdote, obviously unimportant at bottom. Gilbert Seldes in The Dial celebrated the intense life of the rendering and judged that Fitzgerald had mastered his talents. The New York World's unsigned headline called the novel a dud. L. P. Hartley in the Saturday Review found the story absurd as romance, melodrama, or record of New York high life. Ruth Snyder in the New York Evening World called it painfully forced. Lilian C. Ford in the Los Angeles Times called it a work of art. Clifford Trembley in the St. Paul Daily News found the central fellow not worth so much effort. Harvey Eagleton in the Dallas Morning News finished with regret, not for the characters but for Fitzgerald. The St. Paul Pioneer Press judged it far the best of his novels though not of the greatest importance. The Springfield Republican filed it among negligible novels. Life offered brief amused approval. Taken together, the notices form a split decision with a signature gesture: praise with one hand, withdrawal with the other.

What the 1920s established, beyond the verdicts, was the critical vocabulary the novel would carry for decades. Reviewers gave later critics the thinness charge, the period-piece frame, and the style-substance split as a ready-made set of problems. The shared aesthetic behind the verdicts is worth naming: the reviewers expected the substantial novel of character and incident, the large moral canvas, and they measured Gatsby against that expectation as a matter of course. The expectation was not unreasonable for its moment; it was the standard the serious novel had met for generations. What the reviewers could not see was that Fitzgerald was writing a different kind of book, a fable rather than a chronicle, and that the narrowness they faulted was the concentration the fable required. Every later era defined itself against this baseline. The revival had to prove the substance was there. New Criticism had to show the style was the substance. Theory had to show the thinness was an ideological effect. Identity criticism had to show the unworthiness of the characters was itself a political fact worth analyzing rather than a reason to dismiss the book. The 1920s also established the market facts that conditioned everything after: the novel sold about 20,000 copies in its first year, respectable for a literary novel and a disappointment against Fitzgerald's hopes, and the modest sales meant the book entered the next decade without the momentum that protects reputations. A bestseller can survive bad reviews. A modest seller with mixed reviews enters the 1930s exposed, and the exposure shaped the criticism that followed as much as any verdict did.

### How did the 1930s read the novel through the Depression?

The 1930s read the novel through the wreckage of the economy that had produced its parties, converting Gatsby's wealth from a marvel into evidence. Depression-era critics treated the bootlegger's fortune, the careless Buchanans, and the valley of ashes as a parable the crash had verified, less a love story than a document of fraudulent prosperity.

The thinness of the 1930s record is itself the first fact a history of the criticism must report. Fitzgerald's career collapsed in the decade: the stories sold less well, the novels stalled, the Esquire essays of 1936 gathered under the title The Crack-Up confessed a personal and artistic breakdown in public. The Great Gatsby sold slowly through these years and had moved fewer than 25,000 copies in total by the time of Fitzgerald's death in 1940. There was no sustained critical conversation to chronicle, no school of Gatsby studies, no annual harvest of essays. What existed was occasional, and the occasional notice shared a tone: the book belonged to a dead era, and its interest was archaeological. The Jazz Age, which Fitzgerald had named and chronicled, now read as a preface to catastrophe, and Gatsby's parties read as the last nights before the fall. This was not close reading. It was period judgment, and it kept the novel in print without keeping it in the critical conversation.

Yet the 1930s contributed something the later revival would need: the economic frame. When critics of the Depression years looked at Gatsby, they saw the money first, and they saw it as criminal money, speculative money, money that the crash had exposed as unreal. The valley of ashes, a relatively minor element in the 1925 reviews, grew in the Depression reading into the novel's moral center, the place where the bill for the parties came due. Tom Buchanan's brutality read less as personal villainy than as class violence. The green light dimmed as a romantic symbol and brightened as an economic one, the dream of acquisition literalized. A concrete instance shows the mechanism: the parties, which the 1925 reviewers had treated as social comedy, became in Depression retrospect an image of the credit economy itself, abundance without foundation, hospitality as speculation, the whole glittering apparatus one margin call away from the ashes. None of this was theorized; the decade lacked the critical instruments for theorizing it. But the questions the 1930s asked in passing, what does the money mean, who pays for the parties, what does the crash do to the romance, became the standing questions of the materialist criticism that would arrive decades later. The decade that had the least to say about the novel gave later decades some of their most durable questions, which is the first of many instances in this history where an era's poverty of method is also its gift of problems.

### What made the 1940s the decade of the revival?

The 1940s made the revival through a convergence of advocacy, reframing, and readership. Edmund Wilson's memorial work restored Fitzgerald's serious standing, Lionel Trilling's 1945 essay reframed the novel as a figure for America itself, and the 1945 Armed Services Edition put 155,000 copies into servicemen's hands.

The sequence repays attention because each element did a different kind of work. Wilson, Fitzgerald's Princeton friend and the most authoritative critic of his generation, published the memorial essay in 1941 and edited The Crack-Up in 1945, and the two acts together replaced the obituary image of the wasted playboy with the image of the serious artist undone by his time. The Crack-Up volume did particular work: by gathering the Esquire self-examinations with letters, notebook fragments, and tributes from fellow writers, it let readers encounter Fitzgerald's mind directly, the intelligence that the legend of the playboy had hidden, and the encounter made the dismissal of the novels intellectually untenable. A rehabilitated author lifts his books; criticism cannot easily canonize a novel while dismissing its maker as a lightweight, and Wilson removed that obstacle. Trilling's 1945 essay then performed the intellectual move on which the revival turned: it changed the scale of the reading. Where the 1925 reviewers had measured Gatsby against the standards of the substantial novel of character and found him thin, Trilling read him as standing for America itself, a figure in whom the national imagination could recognize its own force and fraudulence. The thinness charge dissolved not by refutation but by reframing; a figure who embodies a nation cannot be dismissed as unworthy of attention, because the attention is owed to what he represents. The move was characteristic of the decade's criticism, which was learning to read novels as national allegories, and it gave Gatsby the interpretive key that would make it teachable for the next half century.

The third element was demographic rather than critical, and perhaps the most decisive. The Council on Books in Wartime issued its Armed Services Edition of the novel in 1945 and distributed 155,000 copies to American servicemen, readers outside every critical establishment's categories, many encountering the book for the first time. Soldiers liked it. When those readers came home, they formed a constituency for the novel that owed nothing to the 1925 verdict, an audience for whom Gatsby was a discovery rather than a disappointment. Critics change verdicts by argument; readers change them by arithmetic, and 155,000 new readers outvoted the old consensus without ever arguing against it. The revival was therefore not only a critical event but a convergence: advocacy supplied the permission, reframing supplied the method, and readership supplied the demand. Any history that credits Trilling alone mistakes the essay for the audience, and any history that credits the audience alone mistakes the demand for the argument. The 1940s matter because all three arrived together, and the criticism of every later decade inherited the settlement they produced: Gatsby as a serious book, a national book, a book with readers.

### How did the 1950s consolidate the canon?

The 1950s consolidated the canon through institutions rather than arguments: the scholarly biography, the stable classroom edition, the close-reading method, and the syllabus. Arthur Mizener's 1951 life of Fitzgerald, Malcolm Cowley's 1953 Viking edition, and the New Critical close readings of the decade together converted the 1940s revival from a critical event into a permanent institutional fact.

Mizener's The Far Side of Paradise, the first full-scale life of Fitzgerald, did for the author what Trilling had done for the novel. It replaced the legend of the wasted playboy with the documented record of the disciplined craftsman, and it ended with the famous hedge that the novel could probably be called a classic of twentieth-century American fiction. The hedge is the point: a biographer does not declare a book a classic lightly in 1951, and the sentence records the moment the consensus had hardened enough to be said aloud. Cowley's 1953 Viking edition then gave the novel the stable, trustworthy text that classrooms require, with an introduction presenting Fitzgerald as a deliberate artist rather than a lucky chronicler. Canons run on teachable texts as much as on arguments, and the edition made the novel assignable in a way the scattered earlier printings had not. Around the same time, the close-reading method associated with the New Critics began its work on the novel's form. Marius Bewley's 1954 essay on Fitzgerald's criticism of America treated the novel's formal control as the vehicle of its moral vision, the exact reunification of style and substance that the 1925 reviewers had failed to perform, and the method spread through the journals and the classrooms with the force of a professional standard.

The mid-century formalist engagement with the novel is treated at length in [the account of the New Critics and the novel](/2031/04/28/new-critics-great-gatsby/), and this history does not duplicate that account's work. What the decade-by-decade view adds is the institutional mechanism: the 1950s criticism succeeded because it built the infrastructure of permanence. Biography gave teachers a life to attach to the work. The edition gave students a text to cite. Close reading gave scholars a method that made the novel yield new results with each application. The postwar paperback boom put cheap copies in every bookstore, and the veterans filling the universities on the GI Bill filled the classrooms where the novel was taught. Each development presupposed the last, and the compounding is visible in the sequence. By the end of the decade, the question was no longer whether Gatsby was serious. The question was what the serious book meant, and that question belonged to the professionals.

### What did the 1960s add to the conversation?

The 1960s added scale, taking the novel the 1950s had certified and making it unavoidable: the most taught American novel, the default text for the American Dream unit. Its criticism industrialized the old questions rather than inventing new ones, as dissertations, monographs, and teaching editions multiplied the certified readings.

The mechanism was the classroom. The novel's brevity, its teachable symbols, its manageable cast, and the national-allegory frame Trilling had supplied made it the ideal text for the expanding postwar university, and the 1960s were the decade the American university expanded most dramatically. Every new section of American literature needed a Fitzgerald unit, and every Fitzgerald unit needed Gatsby. The criticism followed the teaching: studies of the American Dream in the novel, studies of the symbols, studies of Nick Carraway's narration, studies placing Fitzgerald among his contemporaries. The characteristic 1960s question was synthetic rather than original, asking how the established insights fit together into the complete reading the classroom required. This was not a failure of imagination. It was the work of consolidation, the conversion of a revival into a curriculum, and curricula need settled readings the way buildings need foundations.

The 1960s also began, quietly, the bibliographical and textual work that would transform the field decades later. Scholars started assembling the documentary record of the novel's composition with professional rigor: the drafts, the galleys, the correspondence with Maxwell Perkins and with Scribners, the serializations and the revisions. Matthew J. Bruccoli's long labor of Fitzgerald bibliography and biography began in these years, the patient archival work that would eventually underwrite the Cambridge Edition and the variorum. The decade's textual scholars asked a question the critics had not thought to ask: which Gatsby are we actually reading, and how did these particular words come to stand in this particular order? The question sounded antiquarian in the 1960s, a matter for footnotes. It would become, half a century later, one of the central questions of the field, when the variorum made the textual history visible and the public-domain moment made it urgent. The 1960s planted that seed without knowing what it would grow into, which is characteristic of the decade's contribution: it built the infrastructure, the courses, the bibliographies, the archives, on which later revolutions would depend. A sample research question of the era shows the consolidation at work: not whether the novel's symbols mean, but how the established symbolic readings cohere into the complete account the classroom requires, the dissertation as the demonstration that the certified insights can be extended to every corner of the text.

### How did textual scholarship change the object of study?

Textual scholarship changed the object of study by showing that the novel was itself a construction, assembled through drafts, galleys, and editorial decisions. The Cambridge Trimalchio volume of 2000 and James L. W. West III's 2019 variorum let scholars watch Fitzgerald making the choices earlier critics had interpreted as fate.

The effect on the criticism was profound because so many classic readings had treated the published text as a seamless given. The variorum made visible the labor behind the seamlessness: the rewritten ending, the adjusted chronology, the cut passages, the galley-stage revisions that tightened the prose the 1925 reviewers had praised without understanding. A critic who knows that the famous last pages went through multiple states reads them differently from one who assumes they arrived whole, not because the knowledge invalidates the reading but because it changes what the reading is about. Interpretation of a finished design becomes interpretation of a design process, and the author's choices become evidence rather than background. The textual turn also disciplined the field's arguments about intention. Where earlier critics had speculated freely about what Fitzgerald meant, the textual scholars could show what he did, revision by revision, and the showing constrained the speculation without ending it.

The textual scholarship belongs to the most recent phase of the trajectory, but its roots run through the whole history, which is why it serves the article's claim so well. The 1925 reviewers praised a prose they could not see being made. The New Critics analyzed a form whose construction they could not document. The theory decades interpreted a text whose instability they asserted but could not demonstrate. The variorum let scholars demonstrate it, and the demonstration changed the questions: not only what does the novel mean, but which novel, in which state, means it, and what do the discarded states tell us about the meanings the published state forecloses? For a researcher entering the field, the practical consequence is direct: no serious argument about the novel's language can now proceed without consulting the textual record, and the Cambridge materials have made that record accessible. The object of study is no longer simply the book. It is the book plus its becoming, and the criticism that ignores the becoming is working with half the evidence.

### How did the 1970s bring feminist and structuralist questions?

The 1970s brought feminism and structuralism to bear on the novel simultaneously, and the combination changed what counted as evidence. Feminist critics asked whose experience the narration served; narrative theorists asked how the telling worked and what Nick's voice concealed. Both questions had been unaskable in the New Critical frame, which is why the decade felt like a rupture.

The landmark that reorganized the feminist strand is Judith Fetterley's chapter on the novel in The Resisting Reader: A Feminist Approach to American Fiction, published in 1978. Fetterley's chapter, titled with the phrase Fitzgerald's droit de seigneur, argues that the novel trains its readers to see through male eyes and to accept as universal a vision that is partial: Daisy becomes the prize of male competition, Jordan the cool exception that proves the rule, and Myrtle the body on which class and gender violence converge. The argument's force lies in its address to the reader rather than only to the text. Fetterley contends that the criticism which celebrated the novel's design was complicit in its gender politics, admiring a structure built on the reduction of women. Whether or not a researcher accepts the full charge, the chapter is unavoidable because it changed the question. After Fetterley, no serious account of the novel could discuss Daisy without acknowledging that the discussion itself had a history of male-centered assumptions, and the Daisy debates on victimhood, agency, and irony that followed all move within the space her chapter opened.

The structuralist strand worked the other side of the same problem. Where the feminists asked whom the narration served, the narratologists asked how the narration worked: what kind of narrator Nick is, what his retrospective position enables and distorts, how the novel's information economy, what is shown, what is withheld, what is learned too late, produces its effects. A concrete instance shows the method's force: Nick learns the truth about Gatsby's past in stages, and the novel rations that knowledge so the reader's judgment of Gatsby is formed before the facts arrive, which means the judgment is aesthetic and emotional before it is ever moral. The 1970s were the decade when the profession learned to treat narration as a system rather than a window, and Gatsby, with its famously compromised and retrospective narrator, was an ideal test case. The two strands shared a deeper move: both treated the novel's form not as a transparent vessel for meaning but as an interested construction, shaped by positions, gendered, partial, strategic. That move was the decade's real revolution, and it made the 1970s the hinge of the whole trajectory. Before it, the criticism had largely trusted the novel's design. After it, the design itself was on trial, and every later approach, from deconstruction to identity criticism, inherited the suspicion the 1970s had legitimized.

### What did the theory decades of the 1980s do to the novel?

The 1980s subjected the novel to the full battery of high theory: deconstruction, new historicism, psychoanalysis, and Marxist criticism. Its critics asked what ideologies the text encoded and concealed, how Gatsby's self-making naturalized capital, and how the novel's symbols worked as instruments of class power rather than ornaments of meaning.

The new historicist strand put the novel back into the 1920s with unprecedented thickness, reading its parties against the decade's advertising, its wealth against the decade's economics, its anxieties against the decade's nativism. Where the 1930s had read the novel as a parable of the crash, the new historicists read it as a product of the boom, embedded in the credit economy, the public-relations industry, the commodification of personality that the decade had perfected. The deconstructive strand pressed on the novel's binaries, dream and reality, old money and new, East and West, showing how each term depended on the other and how the text's certainties undid themselves. The psychoanalytic strand read Gatsby's desire as structure rather than sentiment, the repetition compulsion of a man trying to rewrite a past that will not stay rewritten. The Marxist strand, building on the economic questions the 1930s had asked without the instruments to answer, analyzed the novel as an anatomy of capital: the criminal origins of the fortune, the labor hidden in the valley of ashes, the carelessness of the Buchanans as the structural indifference of a class.

What the 1980s shared across these methods was a hermeneutics of suspicion, the conviction that the text's surface meaning was an effect to be explained rather than a truth to be received. This was the direct inheritance of the 1970s rupture, systematized into professional method. The gains were substantial: the criticism learned to see the novel's politics, its economics, its unconscious, with a clarity no earlier era had achieved. The losses were real too, and the honest history records them. The theory decades sometimes lost the sentences in the system, treating the novel as an instance of a structure rather than as a particular achievement, and the readings could feel interchangeable, the same theoretical machinery applied to Gatsby producing results that differed only in local color from its application to other books. The decade also professionalized obscurity, writing criticism that addressed the profession rather than the reader, a narrowing the later identity criticism would both inherit and rebel against. The 1980s proved that the novel could survive the most aggressive interrogation the discipline could devise. It did not prove that the interrogation had exhausted the book, and the decades that followed would show how much remained.

### How did the 1990s turn the criticism toward identity?

The 1990s turned the criticism toward identity by asking whom the novel includes, excludes, and injures. Race, gender, sexuality, and class moved to the center of the conversation, and the novel's silences, its dismissed black characters, its caricatured gangster, its circulated women, became primary evidence rather than embarrassments.

The pivotal intervention was Toni Morrison's Playing in the Dark from 1992, which brought the novel into the argument about race in American literature with the authority of a novelist and the precision of a critic. Morrison named the novel's racial anxieties as central rather than incidental: Tom Buchanan's white-supremacist ranting, drawn from the actual doctrines of writers like Lothrop Stoddard whose 1920 volume on the rising tide of color was a real document of the era's nativism, and the novel's construction of whiteness as a fragile, policed identity. After Morrison, the criticism could no longer treat Tom's racism as character color or Wolfsheim's caricature as period detail. The questions became structural: how does the novel's dream of self-making depend on racial hierarchy, whose labor underwrites the parties, what does the text do with the black figures who cross its pages? The answers varied, and scholars disagreed sharply about whether the novel critiqued or reproduced the racism it depicted, but the disagreement was productive because it was about the right question.

The identity turn extended beyond race. Queer readings found in Nick's narration a desire for Gatsby that the text both encodes and disavows, and in the novel's intense male friendships a submerged emotional world the earlier criticism had sentimentalized rather than analyzed. Gender criticism, building on Fetterley's foundation, pursued the Daisy debates with new archival and theoretical resources, asking about victimhood, agency, and irony with a subtlety the 1970s manifestos had not always achieved. Class criticism, drawing on the Marxist work of the 1980s, read the novel's violence, Myrtle's death, Gatsby's murder, as the system's routine operations rather than as tragic accidents. The 1990s also brought the canon debates of the culture wars to the novel's doorstep, and Gatsby, as the most taught American novel, became a test case for arguments about whose stories the curriculum should center. The decade's achievement was to make the criticism answerable to readers the earlier criticism had not imagined: readers for whom the novel's exclusions were not abstractions but injuries. The cost, honestly recorded, was a sometimes reductive moralism, readings that judged the novel by the standards of a later politics without fully reckoning with the distance between the text's moment and the critic's. But the questions the 1990s asked, whom does this book serve, have not gone away, and no later criticism has been able to proceed as if they had never been asked.

### What did the 2000s contribute?

The 2000s contributed the archive, turning the documentary record of the novel's composition into a critical resource. The Cambridge Trimalchio volume of 2000 printed the earlier draft, letting scholars compare what Fitzgerald first wrote with what he published, and the comparison changed long-settled arguments.

The archival turn of the 2000s was the payoff of the bibliographical labor the 1960s had begun. With the drafts, the galleys, the correspondence, and the serial evidence increasingly available in scholarly editions, critics could ground claims about Fitzgerald's intentions in documentary fact rather than in speculation. The Trimalchio comparison was the decade's signature exercise: set the draft's cruder, more explicit handling of a scene against the published novel's control, and watch the revision process itself become the evidence for the reading. A worked example shows the method's power. Where the draft states a judgment about a character outright, the published novel often withdraws the judgment into the imagery, letting the symbol do the work the sentence had done. The critic who can show that withdrawal happening across the two states has not merely interpreted the reticence but documented its making, and the reading gains a kind of hardness that interpretation alone cannot supply. A critic arguing about the ending could now show, not merely assert, how Fitzgerald had rewritten it. A critic arguing about Daisy's voice could trace the adjustments across the states of the text. The method disciplined the field without impoverishing it; the archive constrained speculation, but the constraints made the surviving arguments stronger, because they had survived contact with the documents.

The 2000s also saw new historicism's mature harvest, the thick-context readings of the 1980s deepened by decades of accumulated research into the 1920s. Studies of the novel's advertising culture, its credit economy, its nativism, its Prohibition underworld, its changing press and celebrity culture, arrived with a density of reference the pioneering historicist work had lacked. The decade's characteristic question was connective: how does this particular textual detail open onto that particular historical formation, and what does the connection change about the reading? Consider the eyes of Dr. T. J. Eckleburg, which the 1950s had read as symbol and the 1980s as ideology: the 2000s historicist could set the billboard against the actual outdoor-advertising industry of the 1920s, its economics, its visual rhetoric, its placement along the new motor roads, and show how the novel's most famous image was also a precise registration of a commercial form. The answers were often superb, and the best of them permanently altered the understanding of passages that had seemed settled. The 2000s also began the digital transformation of the research infrastructure, with archives, concordances, and databases moving online, though the transformation would mature in the following decade. Taken together, the 2000s moved the field from assertion to documentation, from the brilliant reading to the documented reading, and the move raised the standard of evidence for everything that followed.

### How did the 2010s extend the identity and textual turns?

The 2010s extended the identity-focused and textual strands, deepening both by bringing them into contact with each other and the historical record. Sarah Churchwell's Careless People (2014) exemplified the synthesis, reconstructing the Buchanans' real-world milieu to give debates about class, gender, and violence denser factual grounding.

The identity work of the 2010s built on the 1990s foundation with greater archival depth and greater theoretical range. Critics pursued the novel's racial imagination with the documentary resources the archives now supplied, connecting Tom Buchanan's doctrines to the specific nativist literature of the era, including Stoddard's 1920 volume, and connecting the novel's policing of whiteness to the decade's immigration politics, eugenics discourse, and color-line anxieties. Gender criticism continued the Daisy debates with new attention to the historical constraints on women's agency in the 1920s, refusing both the sentimental rescue of Daisy and the easy condemnation, and finding in the historical record a more precise account of what the novel's women could and could not do. Class criticism, sharpened by the aftermath of the 2008 financial collapse, read Gatsby's speculative fortune and the Buchanans' insulated wealth against a new public awareness of how money moves and who it crushes, though the best work kept the historical distance visible rather than collapsing the 1920s into the present. The decade's identity criticism was distinguished by its refusal of the simple verdict: the question was less whether the novel was racist or sexist than how its imagination worked, what it could see and what it could not, and what that pattern of vision reveals.

The textual strand reached its culmination in the decade's final years with West's 2019 Cambridge variorum, the mapping of the novel's full textual history that the archival turn had been building toward. The variorum made the compositional record not merely available but navigable, and the criticism absorbed the consequence: arguments about the novel's language now routinely reckoned with the states of the text, and the old habit of treating the published novel as a seamless given became a marked methodological choice rather than an unthinking default. The 2010s also saw the digital humanities arrive in Gatsby studies in earnest, with computational approaches, distant reading, and digital archives offering new ways to test old claims about the novel's diction, its patterns, its place in the larger corpus of American fiction. The decade closed with the field's two great turns, identity and text, no longer separate enterprises but interwoven practices, the question of whose story it is asked together with the question of which words tell it.

### What questions define the 2020s?

The 2020s have been defined by the convergence of the textual and identity turns with a new condition of the novel: its entry into the public domain in the United States on January 1, 2021, which ended a century of exclusive control and forced the criticism to reckon with a text now existing in countless versions beyond scholarly supervision.

The public-domain moment changed the criticism's object in a way the variorum had anticipated but not completed. Where textual scholarship had shown that the novel existed in multiple states, the public domain showed that it now exists in multiple lives: new editions with new apparatus, stage and screen versions that rewrite as they adapt, retellings that center the marginal figures, pedagogical editions that frame the novel for new audiences. The criticism of the 2020s asks how to think about a classic under these conditions: what authority the scholarly text retains when the text is everywhere, how adaptation feeds back into interpretation, whether the proliferation of versions enriches or dissolves the object of study. These are new questions, without precedent in the earlier trajectory, because no earlier era had watched the novel pass out of copyright within living critical memory.

The identity turn has continued through the 2020s with undiminished energy and increasing precision. The questions the 1990s opened, whom the novel includes and injures, are now asked with the full resources of the archive, the variorum, and the digital corpus, and the answers have grown more nuanced as the evidence has grown richer. Pedagogy has become a critical question in its own right: how to teach a novel whose racial and gender politics require direct address, how to keep the aesthetic achievement and the political critique in the same classroom conversation, what the most taught American novel owes the students who now read it. The decade has also seen the criticism turn reflexive, with scholars writing the history of the criticism itself, examining how the field's questions were formed, which is the tradition this article belongs to. The reflexive turn asks questions the earlier trajectory never posed: why did the thinness charge take the form it did, what institutional conditions made the national-allegory reading persuasive in 1945, how did the classroom's needs shape the certified interpretations, which voices were excluded from the professional conversation and what did the exclusion cost. These are second-order questions, the criticism studying itself, and they represent the field's maturity rather than its exhaustion. A discipline that can historicize its own methods has acquired the self-awareness the 1925 reviewers lacked and the 1980s theorists claimed, and the histories of the criticism now being written, of which this article is one instance, are the evidence. The 2020s have not produced a single dominant method to set beside New Criticism or new historicism. They have produced something the trajectory suggests is more characteristic: the simultaneous deepening of all the live questions, the textual, the political, the pedagogical, asked together, with better evidence than any earlier era possessed.

### What did each era see that its predecessors missed?

Each era saw what its methods let it see and missed what its frame made invisible. The 1920s saw the prose and missed the architecture; the 1950s saw the form and missed the world; the 1980s saw the ideology and missed the sentences; the 1990s saw the exclusions and missed the historical distance.

The inventory of these visions is the most useful summary of the trajectory. The 1930s saw the money, and missed the form that made the money meaningful. The 1940s saw the national allegory, and missed the particularity of the lives the allegory absorbed. The 1960s saw the teachable classic, and missed the strangeness that certification smooths away. The 1970s saw the interested narration, and missed the pleasures of the design they were anatomizing. The 2000s saw the archive, and missed the synthesis the archive was meant to serve. The 2010s and 2020s see the text's multiplicity and the politics of its afterlife, and will doubtless be found, by whoever writes the next history, to have missed something equally central.

The inventory matters because it makes the article's central claim concrete. If each era saw something real and missed something real, then the trajectory cannot be simple progress, and the right description is a rotation of attention, each generation bringing its instruments to the part of the book its instruments could measure. The inventory also disciplines the researcher's use of the older criticism. A 1950s formalist reading is not obsolete because the 1990s asked different questions; it remains the best account of what it set out to explain, and a researcher who needs to understand the novel's structure still reads the formalists first. The older work is not a ladder kicked away but a set of instruments kept in the case, each suited to its task. The practical consequence is that the history of the criticism is also a map of the novel's affordances: the book contains the prose the reviewers praised, the money the Depression critics saw, the allegory Trilling found, the form the New Critics analyzed, the ideology the theorists decoded, the exclusions the identity critics named, the textual states the variorum mapped. A book that did not contain all of these could not have sustained all of these readings. The multiplicity of the criticism is evidence of the multiplicity of the novel.

### How did critical methods evolve from impression to institution?

The methods evolved from impression to institution along a visible path: the daily verdict gave way to the essay, the essay to the monograph, the monograph to the theory-driven article, the article to the archival and digital project. Each step professionalized the criticism further and changed what counted as knowing the novel.

The 1920s method was impressionistic by design and by deadline. The reviewer read quickly, judged confidently, and wrote for the general reader, and the unit of thought was the verdict. The 1940s replaced the verdict with the reframing essay, Trilling's mode, which read for the general intellectual reader and aimed to change the scale of perception. The 1950s replaced the essay with the close reading, a method with protocols, teachable moves, reproducible results, and the unit of thought became the analyzed passage. The 1960s multiplied the method across the profession, and the dissertation became the characteristic genre, the long demonstration that the certified readings could be extended. The 1970s and 1980s replaced the passage with the system, feminist, structuralist, deconstructive, historicist, psychoanalytic, Marxist, and the unit of thought became the theoretical claim, warranted by the method rather than by the text alone. The 2000s replaced the theoretical claim with the documented claim, warranted by the archive, and the 2010s added the computational warrant, the pattern demonstrated across the corpus.

The evolution was not a mere refinement. Each method brought its own standard of evidence, its own imagined reader, its own definition of what a critical statement is. The reviewer's verdict addressed the buyer. The essayist's reframing addressed the citizen. The close reader's analysis addressed the student. The theorist's claim addressed the profession. The archivist's documentation addresses the record. The honest history does not rank these readers; it notes that the criticism's audience narrowed as its methods sharpened, and that the narrowing was both a gain in rigor and a loss in reach. The 1925 reviews were read by thousands. The variorum is consulted by specialists. Between those poles lies the whole professionalization of literary study in the twentieth century, and Gatsby criticism is one of the clearest cases through which to watch it happen, because the novel was present at every stage, always available as the test case for whatever the profession, in each era, had decided that knowing a book meant.

### How did teaching the novel change the scholarship?

Teaching changed the scholarship by creating a demand the criticism had to supply: settled readings, teachable units, examinable questions. The novel's brevity, vivid symbols, and the national-allegory frame made it the ideal teaching text, and once it became the most taught American novel, the criticism reorganized itself around instruction.

The mechanism worked in both directions. Teaching created the market for criticism: the editions with introductions and notes, the casebooks gathering the classic essays, the monographs synthesizing the debates, the articles supplying the new angle for next year's lecture. A scholar who could offer a fresh reading of the green light or a new account of Nick's narration had a professional asset, because thousands of teachers needed fresh readings every term. The criticism also shaped the teaching in return: the questions the profession certified became the questions the examinations asked, and the examination questions became, for generations of students, what the novel was about. The American Dream reading, the symbol-hunting exercise, the narrator-reliability debate, these are classroom artifacts as much as critical discoveries, forms the pedagogy required and the scholarship supplied.

The teaching nexus also explains some of the trajectory's most puzzling features. The 1960s industrialization of the certified readings, the 1980s professionalization of obscurity, the 1990s moral urgency, each makes more sense when the classroom is kept in view, because each was also a change in what teachers needed. The 1960s teacher needed the settled synthesis. The 1980s teacher needed the theoretical sophistication that the graduate curriculum was producing. The 1990s teacher needed the political answerability that the students were demanding. The honest history therefore treats pedagogy not as the criticism's afterthought but as one of its engines, and it warns the researcher against mistaking a pedagogical necessity for a critical discovery. The reading that works in the classroom is not thereby false, but it is shaped by the classroom's demands, and the scholar who forgets the shaping mistakes the instrument for the object. The most durable criticism of the novel has been the work that served teaching without being deformed by it, the readings that survived the move from the lecture hall back to the study.

### How did Gatsby's standing among Fitzgerald's other books change?

Gatsby's standing among Fitzgerald's books changed from one strong novel among several to the undisputed masterpiece, a change that distorted Fitzgerald studies for decades. The classroom needed one Fitzgerald novel and chose the shortest and most teachable; the hierarchy that resulted made Gatsby the masterpiece and everything else the hinterland.

The 1920s reviewers judged Gatsby against This Side of Paradise and The Beautiful and Damned, finding it an advance in craft but not yet the summit. The 1930s complicated the ranking: Tender Is the Night, published in 1934, divided opinion, with some critics preferring its larger canvas and others finding it diffuse, and the debate over which novel was Fitzgerald's greatest ran through the revival decades. The 1940s and 1950s settled the question by fiat as much as by argument. Trilling's national frame fit Gatsby better than it fit the other novels, and Mizener's biography, for all its range, centered the narrative on the making of Gatsby. By the 1960s the hierarchy was institutional fact: Gatsby the masterpiece, Tender a flawed second, the stories a rich hinterland, The Last Tycoon an unfinished promise.

The hierarchy had costs that later criticism had to reckon with. Fitzgerald scholars argued, with justice, that the Gatsby monopoly impoverished the understanding of the whole career: the short stories, where Fitzgerald's art of the compressed scene reached its height, were treated as apprenticeship work, and Tender Is the Night was read as the failed rival rather than as the different achievement it is. The 1970s and 1980s began the rebalancing, as feminist critics found richer material in the stories' women, and as the theoretical interest in the unfinished and the fragmentary gave The Last Tycoon a new critical life. The textual scholarship of the recent decades has further leveled the field, since the Cambridge Edition's treatment of the whole corpus lets scholars compare the making of all the books with equal documentary rigor. Gatsby remains the most written-about, and the imbalance will likely persist, because the novel's teachability guarantees its centrality. But the trajectory shows the hierarchy softening from monopoly toward primacy among equals, and the softening has been good for Fitzgerald studies. The masterpiece looks larger, not smaller, when the rest of the career is allowed its full stature, and the criticism that once needed Gatsby to be the only great Fitzgerald novel has learned to let it be the greatest among several.

### How did the canon debates test the novel?

The canon debates tested the novel by asking whether the most taught American book deserved its place, and Gatsby survived changed but unseated. Challengers cited its exclusions; defenders argued its centrality made it the ideal site for critical reading. The novel stayed on the syllabus, but the way it was taught changed.

The culture wars over the curriculum, which peaked in the 1980s and 1990s, challenged the traditional canon on the grounds of its exclusions: too male, too white, too narrow a vision of American experience. As the representative text of that canon, Gatsby became the test case, and the challenges came from several directions at once. Critics asked whether a novel about rich white people in 1922 deserved its curricular centrality when so much of American experience went untaught. Teachers asked whether the book's politics could be taught honestly to the diverse classrooms of the late century. Scholars of the expanding canon asked what the novel's dominance had crowded out.

The defenses were as instructive as the attacks. Defenders argued that the novel's very centrality made it the ideal site for the critical work the canon debates demanded: because everyone read Gatsby, a political or identity-focused reading of Gatsby reached everyone, and the book became the place where the profession taught students to read canonically central texts critically. The identity criticism of the 1990s, in this light, was not an alternative to the canon but the canon's self-criticism, conducted on its most prominent text. Other defenders argued historically, that the novel's 1920s exclusions were the material of its meaning rather than an embarrassment to be managed, and that removing the book would remove the occasion for understanding the exclusions. The outcome was neither the removal the challengers sought nor the complacent retention the traditionalists wanted. Gatsby stayed on the syllabus, but the syllabus around it changed, and the way it was taught changed more: the novel that had been taught as celebration was now taught as problem, the Dream as question rather than as creed. The change is visible in the examination questions. Where the mid-century classroom asked what the green light symbolizes, the late-century classroom asked whose dream the green light serves and at whose expense, and the difference between those two questions is the whole canon debate in miniature. Teachers developed the double pedagogy the situation required: honoring the aesthetic achievement while interrogating the politics, holding the beauty of the sentences and the brutality of the world they describe in a single sustained attention. The canon debates thus repeated the trajectory's characteristic pattern, the same book generating new questions under new pressures, and proved again that canonicity is not immunity from criticism but exposure to it.

### How did the critical vocabulary change across the eras?

The critical vocabulary changed from the language of verdict to the language of system to the language of power to the language of text. The 1920s spoke of thin and substantial; the 1950s of image and symbol; the 1980s of ideology and discourse; the 1990s of identity and whiteness; the recent decades of text, variant, and archive.

Tracking the keywords is a compact way to read the whole trajectory. The 1920s spoke of good and bad, prose and story, the vocabulary of the reviewing trade. The 1940s spoke of seriousness, America, the national imagination, the vocabulary of the public intellectual. The 1950s spoke of myth, structure, irony, tension, the New Critical lexicon that made form analyzable. The 1960s added theme, the American Dream, the canon, the vocabulary of the classroom synthesis. The 1970s introduced gender, narration, the implied reader, the vocabulary of the new methodologies. The 1980s spoke of the subject, hegemony, difference, the vocabulary of high theory. The 1990s spoke of race, queerness, the body, the vocabulary of the political turn. The recent decades speak of the material, the digital, the vocabulary of the documentary and computational turns.

The vocabulary changes matter because keywords are not labels but instruments: each term made certain perceptions possible and others difficult. Symbol made the green light analyzable and made the labor behind it invisible. Ideology made the power visible and made the sentences secondary. Whiteness made the racial formation visible and made the aesthetic achievement awkward to discuss. Archive made the documents authoritative and made the speculative reading harder to defend. The honest history of the criticism therefore attends to what each vocabulary enabled and what it foreclosed, and it warns against the illusion that the latest vocabulary is the transparent one. Every keyword in the list above once felt like the final adequate term, and every one was succeeded. The researcher who learns the vocabularies as a repertoire rather than as a succession, who can move between symbol and ideology, between text and identity, has the full range of the field's instruments. The vocabulary will keep changing, and the history told here suggests the changes will keep revealing, each new term finding in the novel something the old terms could not name.

### What continuities run beneath all the changes?

Beneath the changes run continuities: the style-substance problem, the American Dream question, Nick's narration, and the ending have never left the criticism. Every era restated these standing problems in its own vocabulary, which is why the older work remains usable and why the book counts as a classic.

The decade-by-decade view can obscure these continuities if it is read as a mere chronicle of novelty. The style-substance problem the 1925 reviewers posed has never left the criticism; every era has restated it in its own vocabulary, as form and content, as surface and depth, as text and ideology, as aesthetics and politics. The American Dream question Trilling nationalized has never left either; it has been historicized, ironized, demystified, and revived, but no era has found the novel discussable without it. Nick Carraway's narration has been a standing problem since the first readers noticed its peculiarities, and each method has produced its own Nick: the reliable witness, the compromised participant, the desiring subject, the ideological function. The ending has drawn commentary in every era, the famous last pages serving as the passage where each generation tests its method against the novel's most concentrated prose.

The continuities matter because they qualify the article's claim without undermining it. The questions changed, but they changed around a stable core of problems the novel itself poses, and the stability of the core is part of what makes the book a classic. A novel that generated entirely new questions each era, with no carryover, would be a different book to each generation, and the criticism would be a series of accidents. What the record shows instead is a set of durable problems, style and substance, the Dream, the narrator, the ending, re-asked in the vocabulary of each era's methods. The continuity is in the problems. The change is in the vocabularies, the evidence admitted, the readers imagined, the stakes asserted. This is why the older criticism remains usable: a 1950s reading of the ending and a 1990s reading of the ending are answers to the same standing question, given in different critical languages, and the researcher who learns both languages has two answers where the monoglot has one.

The deepest continuity is the novel's own resistance to being settled, the quality that has kept the questions coming. The book is short enough to be mastered and dense enough to resist mastery, certain in its effects and uncertain in its judgments, and that combination generates commentary the way a strong magnet generates filings. Every era has tried to settle it, and every era's settlement has become the next era's problem. The continuity beneath the changes is therefore not a doctrine but a dynamic: the novel proposes, the criticism disposes, and the disposal never quite holds. A history of the criticism that missed this dynamic would mistake the restlessness of the field for mere fashion. The restlessness has a cause, and the cause is in the book.

### Which questions did later eras abandon, and which did they keep?

Later eras abandoned the questions their methods could not ask and kept the ones their methods could rephrase. The 1925 verdict question was abandoned by the 1950s; the Depression's economic question was kept in transformed shape by 1980s Marxist criticism; Trilling's national question was kept by every era and rephrased by each.

The pattern of abandonment and retention is the empirical test of this article's claim. The 1925 question, is it a good book, was abandoned as a critical question by the 1950s, not because it was answered but because the profession stopped asking it; canonicity made the verdict redundant, and the energy moved to how and what it means. The Depression's question, what does the crash prove about the book, was abandoned when the emergency passed, then kept in transformed shape by the Marxist criticism of the 1980s, which asked the same economic questions with theoretical instruments the 1930s lacked. Trilling's question, what does Gatsby stand for as America, was kept by every later era and rephrased by each: as ideology, as racial formation, as gendered fantasy, as textual effect.

The pattern reveals that abandonment is rarely refutation. Eras do not disprove their predecessors' questions; they lose interest in them, because the methods that made the questions urgent have been replaced by methods that make other questions urgent. The New Critics did not refute the reviewers' verdicts; they made verdicts professionally uninteresting. The theorists did not refute the formalists' analyses; they made formalism look like a refusal to ask about power. The identity critics did not refute the theorists' systems; they made the systems look like evasions of the bodies in the text. Each abandonment was also a narrowing, a decision that certain kinds of knowledge did not count, and the honest history keeps the narrowed-out knowledge in view. The reviewers knew something about the experience of reading the novel as news that the theorists, reading it as a system, did not know. The formalists knew something about the sentences that the historicists, reading the sentences as symptoms, did not always know.

What was kept, across all the abandonments, is the more interesting list. The style-substance problem was kept by everyone. The narrator problem was kept by everyone. The Dream was kept by everyone. The ending was kept by everyone. These are the novel's standing questions, the problems its construction poses to any reader with any method, and their persistence is the strongest evidence for the article's central claim in its qualified form: the questions changed, but they changed around a core the novel itself supplied. An era's originality lies in the questions it adds and the vocabularies it brings; its humility should lie in recognizing the questions it inherited. The best criticism of every era has combined the two, the new question asked with full awareness of the old ones, and the history of the field is in large part the record of how often that combination was achieved and how often the new question was mistaken for a replacement rather than an addition.

### How did the novel's politics get reread over time?

The novel's politics got reread in every era because the book shows money, class, race, and gender operating together without announcing a doctrine. Each generation supplied the doctrine the text withholds: the 1920s read manners, the 1930s economics, the 1940s national allegory, the later decades identity and ideology.

The book is political in the way that makes rereading inevitable. The 1920s read the politics as manners, the social comedy of the rich, and judged the characters unworthy rather than the system unjust. The 1930s read the politics as economics, the crash exposing the fraudulence of the prosperity the novel depicted. The 1940s read the politics as national allegory, Gatsby's dream as America's dream, and the reading ennobled the book by enlarging its stakes. The 1950s bracketed the politics to study the form, a bracketing the later criticism would treat as itself a political act. The 1970s restored gender to the political account, the 1980s restored ideology and class, the 1990s restored race and sexuality, and the 2000s through the 2020s have pursued all of these together with the archive's discipline.

The rereadings share a structure worth naming. Each era found in the novel the politics it was prepared to see and accused earlier eras of blindness to the politics they were not prepared to see, and each era was right about both the finding and the accusation. The 1990s were right that the 1950s had bracketed race, and the 1950s would have been right, had they answered, that the 1990s sometimes bracketed form. The honest history does not adjudicate these exchanges as a judge awarding points; it describes them as the mechanism by which the criticism stays alive, each generation's partial vision correcting the last generation's partial vision and introducing a partiality of its own. The novel's politics are inexhaustible not because the book contains every doctrine but because it withholds doctrine while supplying the materials, the money, the bodies, the violence, the dreams, from which doctrines can be built. That is why the political rereading never ends and never repeats: the materials are stable, the builders change.

For the researcher, the practical lesson is to read the political criticism of each era as evidence about both the novel and the era. A 1930s economic reading tells you what the Depression made visible in the book. A 1990s race reading tells you what the culture wars made visible. Neither visibility is an illusion, and neither is the whole book. The scholar who wants the fullest political account reads across the eras, assembling the partial visions into a composite no single era achieved, and the composite is the closest the criticism comes to seeing the novel whole. The series' companion accounts of the political strands give the topical versions of these readings; this article's contribution is the temporal pattern, the demonstration that the politics were always there and always being reread, because the book's way of withholding doctrine is itself the most political thing about it.

### How should a researcher entering the field use this trajectory?

A researcher entering the field should use the trajectory as a map of the questions, not as a verdict on the answers. Learn the standing questions first, then see where a proposed project sits: whether it extends a live strand, revives an abandoned one, or opens a new one.

The distinction determines whether the history is a tool or a trap. The map tells you which questions each era asked, which methods it certified, which evidence it admitted, and which readers it imagined. The trap is treating the latest era's map as the territory, assuming that the questions asked latest are the questions that matter most. They are the questions that matter most to the most recent era, which is a different claim.

The practical use begins with the standing questions. Before adopting any era's vocabulary, the researcher should learn the problems the novel itself poses across all the vocabularies: the style-substance relation, the narrator's reliability and desire, the Dream's content and hollowness, the ending's work. The survey of the scholarship gives the topical arrangement of these problems; this article gives their temporal formation. Together they let a new scholar see where a proposed question sits in the field: whether it extends a live strand, revives an abandoned one, or opens a new one. The second use is methodological self-awareness. Every method in the trajectory made certain evidence visible and certain evidence invisible, and the researcher who knows the pattern can choose a method with open eyes, or combine methods deliberately, rather than inheriting a single era's blindness unknowingly. The third use is the archive. The textual scholarship of the recent decades has made the documentary record accessible, and no new argument about the novel's language, structure, or revision can afford to ignore it; the variorum and the Cambridge materials are the baseline, not the ornament, of current work.

A model plan for a researcher entering the field might run as follows. First, read the novel in the most authoritative text available, with the textual history in view. Second, read one landmark from each era, not the whole corpus but the representative statement: a 1925 review, Trilling's 1945 essay, Bewley's 1954 essay, Fetterley's 1978 chapter, a new historicist study, a 1990s identity-focused study, Churchwell's 2014 book, and a textual study built on the variorum. Third, identify the standing question your project addresses and trace its formulations across those landmarks, noting what each era's vocabulary let it say. Fourth, locate the gap: the question unasked, the evidence unused, the combination untried. The trajectory is most useful not as a story of progress to be continued but as a repertoire of questions to be recombined, and the original contribution often lies in bringing an abandoned question to new evidence, or a new method to a standing question. The field rewards the scholar who knows the history well enough to surprise it.

### What does the whole arc reveal about the novel itself?

The whole arc reveals the novel's capaciousness: its power to sustain incompatible readings without collapsing. A century of criticism has read Gatsby as allegory and pathology, formal triumph and ideological symptom, and the readings have accumulated rather than canceled, which is the evidence of the book's stature.

No single era's criticism could have established this alone. A century of criticism has read Gatsby as national allegory and as private pathology, as formal triumph and as ideological symptom, as feminist exhibit and as masculinist fantasy, as finished design and as unstable text, and the readings have not canceled one another. They have accumulated, each adding a facet the others lacked, and the accumulation is the evidence. A thin book could not have borne this weight. A simple book could not have generated these questions. The trajectory is, among other things, a century-long stress test, and the novel passed.

The arc also reveals the book's peculiar combination of certainty and openness. The novel is certain in its effects: the prose compels, the structure holds, the ending lands, and no era of criticism has disputed these facts. It is open in its judgments: what the effects mean, whom they serve, what they conceal, and these are the questions every era has answered differently. The combination explains the restlessness of the criticism. A book that failed in its effects would have been abandoned. A book that settled its judgments would have been exhausted. Gatsby does neither, and so the conversation continues, each generation finding in the space between the certain effects and the open judgments the room for its own questions. The critics who called the book thin in 1925 were responding, however inadequately, to the openness; the critics who call it inexhaustible are responding to the same quality with better instruments.

The final revelation concerns the relationship between the book and its readers. The trajectory shows that the novel has never been read alone; it has always been read through the concerns of its moment, and the concerns have left their marks on the readings without exhausting the book. This is not a defect of the criticism but its condition, and the history told here suggests that the condition is also the novel's good fortune. A book read only in its own moment dies with the moment. A book reread in every moment lives in all of them, changed by each reading and unchanged beneath the changes, the same sentences answering new questions because they were built, whether Fitzgerald knew it or not, to contain more than any single era could use. The criticism changed its questions. The novel kept answering. That is the whole arc, and it is enough.

## Verdict: The Criticism Changed Its Questions, and the Change Is the Story

The verdict this article defends is the claim stated in its first paragraph, and the evidence laid out era by era supports no softer conclusion. Gatsby criticism moved decade by decade from review to revival to theory to identity and textual study, and the movement is best described not as progress but as a changing of the questions, each era's instruments determining what it could ask and what it had to miss. Each era asked what its methods let it ask, saw what its instruments let it see, and missed what its frame made invisible. The gains were real: the form analyzed, the ideology decoded, the exclusions named, the text documented. The losses were real too: the sentences lost in the system, the world bracketed for the form, the historical distance collapsed in the moral urgency. An honest history records both, and this one has tried to.

The verdict has a corollary for how the field should be used. The older criticism is not obsolete; it is specialized, each era's work remaining the best account of what it set out to explain. The researcher who needs the form reads the formalists. The researcher who needs the ideology reads the theorists. The researcher who needs the exclusions reads the identity critics. The researcher who needs the words reads the textual scholars. The trajectory is a repertoire, not a ladder, and the scholar who treats it as a repertoire has a century of instruments where the scholar who treats it as a ladder has only the top rung. The book sustained all of these readings because it contained the materials for all of them, and that capaciousness, demonstrated across a century of changing questions, is the final evidence for the novel's stature. The criticism did not converge on the book. It circled it, and the circling revealed, from every side, that the book was larger than any single approach.

There is a final implication for the series' research standard, which this article was written to serve. Tracing a criticism trajectory at this depth is the series' research-grade discipline: it refuses the shortcut of the latest consensus and insists on the whole record, gains and losses together. A scholarship that knows its history is harder to fool, less prone to mistaking the current vocabulary for the transparent one, better equipped to recognize when an allegedly new question is an old question in new dress. The verdict is therefore also a method: read the history, use the repertoire, distrust the progress story, and let the novel's demonstrated capaciousness set the standard for what counts as an adequate reading.

A reader who wants the verdict on the reputation in its most compressed form, the overview of how the estimation changed rather than how the questions changed, should consult the [critical-reception overview](/2029/12/17/gatsby-critical-reception/), which gives the long arc of the verdicts this article's decade-by-decade account underwrites. The two articles are built to be read together: the overview tells you the reputation changed, and this article tells you how the questions changed beneath the reputation, era by era, method by method, down to the evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Gatsby criticism change by decade?

Gatsby criticism changed by changing its questions rather than by steadily improving its answers. The 1920s reviews asked whether the novel was good and found it thin. The 1940s revival asked whether Fitzgerald was serious and answered through Trilling's national frame. The 1950s asked how the novel was made and answered with close reading. The 1970s asked whom the narration served and how it worked, bringing feminism and narrative theory. The 1980s asked what ideologies the text encoded, the 1990s asked whom it included and injured, and the recent decades have asked which text we are reading and whose story it is, through textual scholarship and the continuing identity turn. Each era's methods made certain questions askable and others invisible, so the trajectory is a rotation of attention around a stable core of problems the novel itself poses.

### Q: What were the decade-by-decade shifts?

The shifts ran from verdict to reframing to method to suspicion to identity to text. The 1920s delivered mixed review verdicts, praising prose and doubting substance. The 1930s read the novel as a Depression parable of fraudulent prosperity. The 1940s revived it through Wilson's advocacy, Trilling's 1945 reframing essay, and the 155,000-copy Armed Services Edition. The 1950s institutionalized it with Mizener's 1951 biography, Cowley's 1953 edition, and New Critical close reading. The 1960s multiplied the certified readings across the expanding university. The 1970s ruptured the consensus with feminism and structuralism. The 1980s applied high theory. The 1990s centered identity. The 2000s documented the archive, and the 2010s and 2020s have interwoven textual scholarship with the identity turn under the new condition of the 2021 public-domain entry.

### Q: What questions dominated each decade?

Each decade was dominated by the questions its methods could ask. The 1920s asked whether the book was good and important. The 1930s asked what the crash proved about its portrait of wealth. The 1940s asked whether Fitzgerald was a serious artist and whether Gatsby proved it. The 1950s asked how the novel's form carried its meaning. The 1960s asked how the certified readings fit together for the classroom. The 1970s asked whose experience the narration served and how the telling worked. The 1980s asked what ideologies the text encoded and concealed. The 1990s asked whom the novel included, excluded, and injured. The 2000s asked what the archives changed about the reading. The 2010s and 2020s have asked which text we are reading, whose story it is, and how to think about a classic that now belongs to everyone.

### Q: What are the recent critical turns?

The recent turns are the textual and the identity-focused, now interwoven, unfolding under the new condition of the public domain. Textual scholarship, culminating in West's 2019 Cambridge variorum and building on the 2000 Trimalchio edition, has made the novel's compositional history navigable, so arguments about language and structure now reckon with the states of the text. Identity criticism has continued the 1990s project with greater archival depth, pursuing race, gender, class, and sexuality with documentary precision. Digital methods have added computational approaches to diction and pattern. The January 1, 2021 public-domain entry created a genuinely new critical condition: the novel now exists in countless editions and adaptations beyond scholarly supervision, and the criticism asks what authority the scholarly text retains and how proliferation changes the object of study.

### Q: Was the trajectory simple progress?

No, and treating it as simple progress is the principal misconception this article corrects. Each era saw something real and missed something real: the 1920s saw the prose and missed the architecture, the 1950s saw the form and missed the world, the 1980s saw the ideology and missed the sentences, the 1990s saw the exclusions and sometimes missed the historical distance. Later eras did not refute their predecessors so much as lose interest in their questions, as new methods made new questions urgent. The honest account records gains and losses together. What persisted across all the changes was a stable core of problems the novel itself poses, the style-substance relation, the narrator, the Dream, the ending, re-asked in each era's vocabulary. The right verb for the trajectory is changed, not improved.

### Q: How did the novel answer each era's concerns?

The novel answered each era's concerns because it was built to contain more than any single era could use. It gave the 1920s reviewers the prose they praised and the thinness they alleged. It gave the Depression the money and the ashes. It gave Trilling the national figure. It gave the New Critics the controlled form. It gave the feminists the gendered narration, the theorists the ideology, the identity critics the exclusions, the textual scholars the revisable words. The book's combination of certain effects and open judgments meant every method found purchase: the sentences compelled every era, while what the sentences meant stayed open enough for each era's questions. A thin book could not have borne a century of incompatible readings. The accumulation of those readings, none canceling the others, is the evidence of the novel's capaciousness and the reason the conversation continues.

The lens version tells you what happened to the novel's standing: mixed reviews, revival, canonization. ### Q: What sources does a history of the criticism rely on?

A history of the criticism relies on a composite archive, and each era of the trajectory survives in different kinds of documents. The 1920s survive in periodical reviews, named reviewers in named newspapers and magazines. The 1940s survive in revival essays, memorial editing, and publishing records such as the Armed Services Edition. The 1950s survive in biographies, classroom editions, and the journals where close readings appeared. The theory decades survive in academic journals, monographs, dissertations, and edited collections. The recent turns survive in scholarly editions, variorum apparatus, digital archives, and the bibliographies that map the field. The historian of criticism must also use the negative evidence: the thinness of the 1930s record is itself a finding, telling us the novel lived outside the critical conversation in that decade. Attribution discipline governs throughout, naming scholar, work, and year only when verified.

### Q: What role did biography play in the trajectory of the criticism?

Biography played the role of permission at the two moments the criticism most needed it. In the 1940s, Edmund Wilson's memorial work replaced the obituary image of the wasted playboy with the serious artist, removing the obstacle that had made it embarrassing to take the novel seriously. In the 1950s, Arthur Mizener's The Far Side of Paradise, the first full-scale life published in 1951, documented the discipline behind the legend and ended with the famous hedge that the novel could probably be called a classic, the sentence that records the moment the consensus went public. Biography gave teachers a life to attach to the work, and canons run on teachable lives as much as on texts. Later biographical work, including Bruccoli's, continued to supply the documentary foundation the criticism built on. The lesson is that criticism does not canonize novels alone; it canonizes authors, and the author's story is part of the book's critical fortune.

### Q: What was the first feminist reading of the novel?

The landmark first feminist reading was Judith Fetterley's chapter on the novel in The Resisting Reader: A Feminist Approach to American Fiction, published in 1978. Earlier reviewers and critics had discussed Daisy, Jordan, and Myrtle, but always within male-centered frames that treated the women's function in the male drama as the whole question. Fetterley's chapter, titled with the phrase Fitzgerald's droit de seigneur, argued that the novel trains readers to see through male eyes and accept as universal a vision that is partial, and that the criticism celebrating the novel's design was complicit in its gender politics. The chapter changed the question rather than merely adding a perspective: after Fetterley, no serious account could discuss the novel's women without acknowledging the male-centered history of the discussion itself. The Daisy debates on victimhood, agency, and irony that followed all move within the space her chapter opened.

### Q: How has the criticism treated Tom Buchanan over time?

Tom Buchanan has traveled from unexamined villain to structural symptom to racial formation across the trajectory. The 1920s reviewers treated him as one more unworthy character justifying the thinness charge. The mid-century critics read him as the embodiment of corrupt old money, the brute force against which Gatsby's dream defines itself. The 1980s theorists read him as ideology incarnate, the novel's frankest statement of class power. The 1990s transformed the understanding through the race lens: Morrison's 1992 intervention made Tom's white-supremacist doctrines, drawn from real nativist sources like Stoddard's 1920 volume, central rather than incidental, so Tom became the novel's construction of whiteness as a policed identity. Recent criticism has pursued this line with archival precision while debating whether the novel critiques or reproduces Tom's racism. No figure in the book better demonstrates the trajectory's logic: the same character, new questions each era.

### Q: How did non-American critics read the novel?

Non-American criticism read the novel with the distance that made its Americanness strange rather than self-evident, though the trajectory told here is inevitably centered on the American academy where the bulk of the scholarship was produced. British reviewers of the 1920s, like L. P. Hartley in the Saturday Review, judged the book by English standards of the substantial novel and found it absurd, a verdict shaped by a different novel tradition. Later international scholarship approached Gatsby as America's representative fiction, the book that explains the United States to the world, which both flattered and flattened it. Comparative work has set the novel beside other national literatures' treatments of modernity, money, and self-making. The honest history acknowledges its archive's limits: the decade-by-decade account reflects the criticism that shaped the canon, which was overwhelmingly American, and a fuller international history remains work for the scholars of the coming decades.

### Q: Did any single decade get the novel fundamentally wrong?

No single decade got the novel fundamentally wrong, because each decade's reading captured something the book actually contains. The 1920s reviewers were wrong about the thinness but right about the prose. The 1950s formalists were wrong to bracket the world but right about the form. The 1980s theorists were wrong when they lost the sentences but right about the ideology. The 1990s moralists were wrong when they collapsed historical distance but right about the exclusions. Fundamental wrongness would require a reading with nothing to teach, and the trajectory offers no such decade. What the record shows instead is partial rightness: each era's instruments measured the part of the book they could reach, and the parts they could not reach waited for later instruments. The researcher who dismisses any era entirely discards real knowledge. The wiser use is to ask what each era's partial vision saw with precision, and to assemble the partial visions into the composite no single era achieved.

### Q: Which era produced the greatest volume of criticism?

The classroom decades, from the 1960s through the 1980s, produced the greatest volume, though honest counting across a century of journals, books, and dissertations is beyond any single survey. The mechanism was institutional: once the novel became the most taught American book, every term needed fresh readings, every dissertation needed a new angle, every journal needed Gatsby articles, and the profession obliged. The 1960s multiplied the certified readings, the 1970s and 1980s multiplied the methods, and each new method generated its own corpus. The recent decades have produced less sheer volume but greater evidentiary density, as the archive and the variorum raised the cost of entry for serious work. Volume is not the same as importance: a single 1945 essay did more to change the field than hundreds of routine 1960s studies. The historian of criticism counts influence, not pages, and by that measure the thin decades sometimes outweigh the fat ones.

### Q: How should a student use this history in an essay?

A student should use this history to frame a thesis, not to substitute for one. The trajectory shows that every strong reading of the novel answers a question with a history, so an essay that positions its question within that history starts ahead. A practical method: identify the standing question your essay addresses, such as the narrator's reliability or the Dream's hollowness, then show how two eras answered it differently, then offer your own answer as the next move in that sequence. Cite the landmarks by name and year where the record is verified, and attribute generically where it is not. Do not narrate the whole trajectory in an essay about one question; select the eras that bear on your argument. The examiners reward the student who knows that criticism has a history, because that knowledge turns a reading into an argument: not just what the novel means, but why this meaning matters now, against the meanings earlier readers found.

### Q: What is the difference between reception history and criticism history?

Reception history studies how the book was received: sales, reviews, readership, reputation, the verdicts and their fortunes. Criticism history studies how the book was interpreted: the questions scholars asked, the methods they used, the arguments they made, the debates they conducted. The two overlap, because reviews are both reception and the first criticism, and because reputation shapes interpretation. But their archives differ: reception history works with periodicals, sales figures, and reading publics, while criticism history works with essays, monographs, editions, and the profession's internal debates. This article is a criticism history with reception at its edges: it treats the 1925 reviews as the criticism's first data point and the 1945 readership as the revival's demographic engine, but its subject is the changing questions of the scholars, not the changing fortunes of the book. The distinction keeps the inquiry focused on what the field thought rather than what the public bought.

### Q: How does the criticism handle Fitzgerald's intentions?

The criticism has handled Fitzgerald's intentions with increasing discipline and decreasing confidence across the trajectory. The 1925 reviewers invoked intention casually, praising or blaming Fitzgerald for effects they assumed he planned. The New Critics famously bracketed intention as a fallacy, reading the achieved form rather than the author's design. The theory decades treated intention as an ideological effect, what the text lets the author think he meant. Textual scholarship then restored intention in its most documentable form: not what Fitzgerald meant in the abstract, but what he did, revision by revision, draft by draft, as the variorum records. The current position is therefore precise rather than skeptical: scholars claim intentions only where the documents warrant them, and otherwise read the text's effects without attributing them to a plan. The trajectory's lesson is that intention is not one question but several, and the documentary record answers some of them while leaving others permanently open.

### Q: Why does the ending attract so much critical attention?

The ending attracts attention because it concentrates everything the criticism disputes into a few pages: the prose at its most famous, the Dream at its most explicit, Nick's voice at its most lyrical and most suspect, the novel's judgment at its most ambiguous. Every era has tested its method against the last pages, and the tests reveal the methods: the New Critics analyzed the imagery, the theorists decoded the ideology, the identity critics interrogated the universalizing gestures, the textual scholars traced the revisions. The ending is also the passage where the style-substance problem, the standing question of the whole trajectory, appears in its purest form, since no one disputes the beauty and everyone disputes the meaning. A critic who can account for the ending has accounted for the novel, or so each generation has felt. The attention is therefore structural, not fashionable: the ending is where the book's certainties and openings meet, and the criticism will keep returning as long as it has questions.

### Q: What questions has the scholarship not yet answered?

The scholarship has not yet settled whether the novel critiques or reproduces the racism and sexism it depicts, a debate the 1990s opened and every later era has continued without resolution. It has not fully mapped the international reception, the non-American readings that this trajectory can only gesture toward. It has not yet absorbed the full consequences of the public-domain condition, the criticism of a classic that now exists in countless versions. It has not resolved the narrator problem to anyone's lasting satisfaction; Nick remains the standing puzzle. And it has not yet written the history of its own digital transformation, the computational readings still too recent for perspective. These open questions are the field's invitation, not its failure. A century of criticism has answered enormously, and the unanswered remainder is what keeps the conversation alive. The researcher entering the field at this point in the trajectory inherits both the answers and the openings, which is the best possible position.

### Q: How do scholars avoid presentism when writing criticism history?

Scholars avoid presentism by reconstructing each era's questions in that era's terms before judging them in later terms. The discipline requires reading the 1925 reviews inside the frame of the daily notice, the 1950s formalism inside the professionalization of close reading, the 1980s theory inside the academy's methodological revolution, rather than measuring each against the current consensus. The historian asks what a given method made visible and what it made invisible, and treats both the vision and the blindness as historical facts rather than as virtues or vices. Presentism also fails descriptively: an account that treats earlier critics as merely wrong cannot explain why their readings persuaded their contemporaries, and persuasion is part of the evidence. The honest history therefore practices a double vision, understanding each era from the inside while keeping the whole trajectory in view, and it states its own position openly rather than smuggling the present in as the neutral standard.
