One test separates a passing familiarity with American trade law from a working command of the subject. The test asks whether a single statute can be identified that contains almost every instrument of modern American trade policy, from the procedure for negotiating agreements to the remedy for imports that break no rule at all. The Trade Act of 1974 passes that test. Congress passed the measure in December 1974, and President Gerald Ford signed the measure on January 3, 1975, so the year in the name of the statute and the year of signature differ, a distinction that precise citation preserves. The enacted text is Public Law 93-618, printed at 88 Stat. 1978, carried through the 93rd Congress as H.R. 10710.

The statute earns the distinction through combination rather than through any single provision. Title I created fast track, the procedure at sections 151 to 154 under which Congress commits in advance to vote on an implementing bill without amendment and under debate limits, a commitment later renewed under the name examined in [the history of trade promotion authority](/2020/08/15/trade-promotion-authority-explained/). Title III created the unfair practices authority at section 301, directed at foreign acts, policies or practices that are unjustifiable or unreasonable and that burden or restrict United States commerce. Title II carried the safeguard at section 201 for industries seriously injured by fairly traded imports and, in separate chapters, trade adjustment assistance for workers, firms and communities displaced by imports. Title V created the Generalized System of Preferences, extending duty free treatment to eligible articles from designated developing countries. Title IV, at section 402, wrote the Jackson-Vanik emigration condition into trade law, barring nondiscriminatory treatment and federal credits for non-market economy countries that denied freedom of emigration. The namable claim of the statute is "One statute, every tool."

![Trade Act of 1974 and the Instruments of American Trade Policy - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-25.webp)

The occasion for consolidation was a negotiation that the older machinery could not serve. Ministers launched a new round of multilateral negotiations at Tokyo in September 1973, directed at nontariff barriers that a presidential proclamation could not implement, while import competition in steel, textiles, footwear and consumer electronics built congressional support for restriction and organized labor withdrew from the postwar liberalization consensus. The monetary crisis of 1971 and the temporary import surcharge of that year had demonstrated both the leverage of unilateral pressure and the cost of improvisation. A statute written for that moment had to authorize negotiation and discipline pressure at the same time, and had to carry constituencies that could each have blocked a narrower bill. Exporters received the negotiating authority, import sensitive industries received the safeguard, labor received assistance and the unfair practices remedy, and members concerned with Soviet emigration received Title IV.

Combination alone would have produced a miscellany. Calibration produced a system. The safeguard answers injury without wrongdoing, so the statute assigned investigation to the International Trade Commission and the relief decision to the President, with no finding of foreign unfairness at any stage. The unfair practices authority answers wrongdoing, so the statute required a finding about foreign conduct and a burden on commerce before action could follow. Fast track answers a problem of credibility, binding congressional procedure in advance so that a negotiated bargain could not be rewritten line by line after signature. Preferences answer a development claim, adjustment assistance answers a domestic loss, and the emigration condition answers a demand that trade status carry a human price. Lineage discipline completes the design. Section 232, the national security authority, belongs to [the Trade Expansion Act of 1962](/2020/08/01/trade-expansion-act-1962-guide/) and forms no part of the 1974 text, and the safeguard and assistance instruments also descend from the 1962 statute in earlier and narrower forms. The contribution of the 93rd Congress was consolidation under modern standards, procedural invention, and a retaliation authority of general reach, gathered into one enacted text.

Administration of the package divided labor among institutions, and the division explains much of the later record. Presidents negotiated and decided safeguards, the United States Trade Representative investigated unfair practices after the amendments of 1988 transferred that authority from the President, the International Trade Commission investigated injury, and the Department of Labor certified worker groups for assistance. A reader tracing a grievance through [the administration of United States tariff law](/2020/10/01/us-tariff-law-administration/) encounters the same allocation at every stage, because the statute routed each kind of complaint to a designated investigator and a designated decider. Later Congresses amended the package piece by piece in 1979, 1984, 1988, 2002 and 2015, renewed the time limited authorities after lapses, and left the architecture intact. The sections that follow take each instrument in turn, on the terms the statutory text sets, with the record of use stated as dated fact through June 1, 2020.

## Origins, Passage, and the Design of the Package

The Trade Act of 1974 rewards attention to its architecture before attention to any single provision inside it. Most trade statutes of the postwar decades did one thing at a time, granting negotiating authority in one enactment and injury relief in another. The statute enacted as Public Law 93-618, at 88 Stat. 1978, refused that separation. It placed a negotiating procedure, a safeguard remedy, an unfair practices remedy, a regime for trade with non-market economies, a preference program for developing countries, and a domestic assistance program inside one enacted text, and it made each part intelligible through the others. The claim that organizes the statute, and the claim that organizes this guide, can be stated in four words. One statute, every tool. Congress designed a negotiating procedure and a set of unilateral remedies in the same breath, deliberately giving itself both routes, and the deliberateness matters as much as the combination. Neither route was an afterthought attached during floor debate to purchase votes for the other. The text that President Gerald Ford signed on January 3, 1975 presented negotiation and retaliation, liberalization and relief, openness and conditionality, as parts of a single settlement about how the United States would conduct trade policy after the certainties of the early postwar decades had broken down.

That settlement had a long prehistory, and the prehistory explains why a piecemeal statute no longer seemed adequate to the members who wrote it. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements model that had governed American tariff policy since the nineteen thirties rested on a simple delegation. Congress authorized the President to bargain tariff rates down within stated limits, and the resulting agreements took effect without a further vote on every line of the schedule. The model worked tolerably well while the subject of negotiation remained the tariff itself, a border charge that Congress could describe in advance and that the President could adjust by proclamation. The Kennedy Round of negotiations under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, conducted under the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, exposed the limit of that model. The round produced agreements about nontariff barriers, the subsidies, standards, procurement rules, and administrative practices that shaped trade as powerfully as duties did, and those agreements required changes in domestic law that a presidential proclamation could not supply. Congress declined to implement parts of the package that the executive branch had negotiated, and the episode taught both branches a lasting lesson. The Congressional Research Service, in its history of trade promotion authority (Fergusson and Davis, 2019), traced the fast track procedure of 1974 directly to that failure. The tariff authority granted in the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 expired on July 1, 1967, and Congress then left the authority unrenewed for seven years, so the United States possessed no general authority to enter a new round of trade agreements while the agenda of world trade was shifting from tariffs toward the domestic rules behind the border.

The monetary crisis of 1971 converted that institutional embarrassment into an urgent political problem. By the summer of 1971 the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates was collapsing under the weight of dollar outflows, foreign central banks were converting dollars into gold, and the United States recorded trade results that fed a domestic politics of grievance against Japan and the European Community. President Richard Nixon answered on August 15, 1971 with a package that closed the gold window and imposed a temporary surcharge of ten percent on imports. Douglas Irwin, in a 2012 study of the episode for the National Bureau of Economic Research, recorded that the surcharge applied to dutiable imports only, which amounted to roughly half of all imports, and that it remained in effect from August 16 to December 20, 1971. The surcharge was leverage, openly understood as leverage. Treasury Secretary John Connally used the interval to press trading partners toward revaluation of their currencies, and the Smithsonian Agreement of December 18, 1971 delivered a realignment of exchange rates in exchange for removal of the surcharge two days later. Paul Volcker wrote with Toyoo Gyohten in 1992 that the realignment fell well short of what American officials had judged necessary and that removal of the surcharge surrendered the strongest bargaining instrument the United States had held. Members of Congress drew two conclusions that pointed in opposite directions and therefore had to be held together in one statute. Unilateral pressure worked, at least for a season, and deserved a lawful and durable place in the statute book. Unilateral pressure improvised under emergency claims also carried costs, in allied resentment and in domestic uncertainty, and deserved statutory standards, findings, and limits rather than repetition by proclamation.

Protectionist pressure inside Congress supplied the second half of the drafting mood. Import competition in steel, textiles, footwear, and consumer electronics had built a constituency for restriction that the older consensus for liberalization could no longer answer with assurances alone. Organized labor, which had supported the postwar liberalization program through the nineteen sixties, moved toward open skepticism, and proposals for general import quotas commanded serious support on Capitol Hill. The executive branch faced a negotiation it could not credibly enter and a legislature it could not reliably hold back. S. J. Anjaria, writing in the International Monetary Fund journal Finance and Development in 1975, observed that ministers of more than ninety governments had launched a new round of multilateral negotiations at Tokyo in September 1973 and that the substantive phase of those negotiations began only in early 1975, after passage of enabling legislation in the United States. The observation captures the sequence that gave the bill its deadline pressure. The round existed before the authority did. American negotiators sat at a table that foreign governments had already set, armed with an expired statute and a surcharge precedent, and every month of congressional delay weakened the claim that the United States could deliver whatever its negotiators promised.

### Why did Congress place negotiation and retaliation in a single statute?

Congress placed negotiation and retaliation in a single statute because each route strengthened the other. Negotiating authority lacked credibility without remedies for injury and unfair practices, and remedies lacked discipline without consultation, findings, and time limits. The 1974 design therefore joined both routes under one set of congressional controls.

The formal request arrived from the Nixon administration in the spring of 1973. In a message dated April 10, 1973, preserved by the Gerald R. Ford Presidential Library, President Nixon proposed a Trade Reform Act of 1973 and urged Congress to enact it so that trade would move, in the language of the message, away from confrontation and toward negotiation. The request asked for broad authority to bargain over tariffs and nontariff barriers alike, together with authority to respond to payments imbalances and to unfair foreign practices. Congress accepted the destination and rewrote the vehicle. The House Committee on Ways and Means and the Senate Committee on Finance took the executive request apart and rebuilt it around a bargain of their own. The President would receive authority broad enough to negotiate the round that Tokyo was about to launch. Congress would receive, in the same text, a procedure that guaranteed a vote on any implementing bill without amendment and without indefinite delay, consultation rights and negotiating objectives written into law, a strengthened safeguard remedy for injured industries that required no proof of foreign wrongdoing, a retaliation authority aimed at foreign practices that Congress judged unjustifiable or unreasonable, an expanded assistance program for displaced workers, firms, and communities, and a conditional framework for trade with the Soviet Union and other non-market economies that tied market access to freedom of emigration. Each element answered a constituency that could have blocked the whole. Exporters and the administration received the negotiating authority. Import sensitive industries received the safeguard. Labor received the assistance expansion and the unfair practices remedy. Members concerned with Soviet emigration policy received the Jackson-Vanik framework in Title IV. Developing country advocates received the preference program in Title V. A statute that contained only the negotiating authority would have failed for want of the rest, and the rest would have had no occasion for enactment without the negotiating authority to carry the bill.

The Tokyo Round context fixed both the timing and the content of Title I. Ministers meeting at Tokyo adopted the Tokyo Declaration on September 14, 1973, following sessions that began on September 12, 1973, and the declaration committed participating governments to negotiations aimed at the expansion and liberalization of world trade, with attention to nontariff barriers and to the interests of developing countries. The declaration was, in form, a statement of intent among governments. It created no rights under American law and conferred no authority on any American official. It defined the negotiation for which Congress was being asked to legislate, it set a clock that Congress did not control, and it raised the price of failure, because a Congress that refused authority after the declaration would have announced to every other participant that American commitments at the table were provisional in a way that other governments could exploit. At the same time, the very fact that the round addressed nontariff barriers made the old proclamation model unusable and forced the invention that gives the statute its procedural fame. An agreement that changed American statutes could enter into force only through an act of Congress, and an act of Congress under ordinary procedure could be amended line by line and delayed without limit, which no rational trading partner would accept as the basis for concessions. The fast track procedure of sections 151 to 154, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194, answered that dilemma with a precommitment. Congress bound itself in advance to consider an implementing bill under time limits, with limits on debate that excluded the filibuster, and without amendment, provided that the executive observed the consultation requirements and pursued the negotiating objectives that the statute itself prescribed at section 102, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2112, under the authority to enter agreements granted at section 101, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2111. The authority was granted for a defined period, covering agreements entered into during the five years after enactment. Congress lent its lawmaking power under stated conditions and reserved the right to reconsider the loan when the period expired, as later Congresses did in the grants of 1979, 1988, 2002, and 2015, each for a stated period and each separated from the next by a lapse.


The bill that carried this design entered the House as H.R. 10710 in the 93rd Congress. The measure was introduced on October 3, 1973 by Representative Al Ullman of Oregon and was considered by the House Committee on Ways and Means, which reported it as House Report No. 93-571. The House passed an earlier version of the bill on December 11, 1973, by a vote of 272 to 140, a margin that showed a comfortable majority for the package and a substantial minority against it. A bill devoted only to liberalization, in the political climate of late 1973, would have struggled to hold a majority at all, and a bill devoted only to restriction would have faced a presidential veto. The combined package held because each bloc that disliked one title could find another title worth defending, and because the procedural promise of Title I gave members a reason to accept authorities in other titles that Congress itself would supervise. The December 1973 vote was nevertheless only the first chamber acting on a first version. The bill then crossed to the Senate, where the Committee on Finance held it through most of 1974, and the long Senate interval is a substantive part of the passage story rather than a footnote to it. A statute assembled from interlocking bargains invites reopening at every stage, and the Senate stage reopened several of them, above all the terms on which non-market economies could receive nondiscriminatory tariff treatment and American credits.

Senator Russell Long of Louisiana chaired the Senate Committee on Finance throughout the consideration of the bill, and the committee reported the measure as Senate Report No. 93-1298. The Finance Committee under Long functioned as the second drafting center of the statute, testing the package against Senate concerns that included the emigration conditions of Title IV, the administration of adjustment assistance, and the reach of the retaliation authority in Title III. The committee process also carried the bill across a change of presidents. President Nixon, whose administration had requested the legislation in April 1973, resigned on August 9, 1974, and Vice President Gerald Ford succeeded to the presidency on that day, a transition recorded by the Library of Congress in its resource guide to the Ford presidency. The statute did not depend on the political fortunes of the President who had requested it, because Congress had rebuilt the request into a framework that allocated power between the branches on terms that any administration would inherit. The Senate passed the bill on December 13, 1974, by a vote of 77 to 4. The joint conference committee reported its resolution of the differences between the chambers on December 19, 1974, and both chambers agreed to the conference report on December 20, 1974, the House by a vote of 323 to 36 and the Senate by a vote of 72 to 4. The Congressional Research Service, in its compilation of major votes on trade promotion authority drawn from Congress.gov records, preserved that final sequence, and the sequence deserves a plain reading. The conference margins were far wider than the margin on the original House passage thirteen months earlier. A year of Senate revision and conference negotiation had not fractured the coalition behind the package. The year had consolidated the coalition, because the revisions had given skeptical members additional text to defend at home. President Ford signed the bill on January 3, 1975. The statute is the Trade Act of 1974 because Congress passed it in 1974 and because the legislative enterprise belonged to that year, and it became law in 1975 because the signing fell on the third day of the new year. Precision on the point is not pedantry, because the congressional settlement belongs to 1974 and the authorities became available for use only at the signing in 1975.

The passage history clarifies the claim of one statute and every tool, because the tools were calibrated against one another during the fourteen months between introduction and signature. Consider the relationship between the safeguard of Title II and the retaliation authority of Title III. A safeguard under section 201, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2251, answers injury caused by fairly traded imports, and the statute assigned the injury investigation to the International Trade Commission and the relief decision to the President, with no finding of foreign unfairness required at any stage. A proceeding under section 301, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2411, answers foreign conduct, and as enacted in 1974 the provision authorized the President to act against foreign acts, policies, or practices that were unjustifiable or unreasonable and that burdened or restricted American commerce. The two remedies were deliberately asymmetrical. One required injury without wrongdoing and delivered temporary relief measured to the injury. The other required wrongdoing without any necessary showing of domestic injury in the safeguard sense and delivered pressure measured to the practice. A Congress that had enacted only the safeguard would have told injured industries to absorb losses caused by foreign practices that violated commitments. A Congress that had enacted only the retaliation authority would have told industries injured by lawful competition to manufacture an accusation before receiving help. The 93rd Congress refused both distortions and wrote the two standards side by side, and the refusal is the design. The same calibration appears in section 122, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2132, which carried a balance of payments surcharge authority inside Title I. The 1971 surcharge had been improvised under general emergency claims, and the 1974 statute converted the improvisation into a defined authority with statutory conditions, described by Congress in advance rather than asserted by a President in a crisis.

Lineage discipline matters here, because the fame of the 1974 statute tempts attribution of every unilateral authority to its pages. Section 232, the national security authority codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862, belongs to the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and not to the Trade Act of 1974, and no accurate account of the 1974 package may claim it. The safeguard lineage and the adjustment assistance program also begin in the 1962 statute, where eligibility for assistance had been drawn so strictly that few workers qualified, a failure that the 1974 expansion of assistance was written to repair. What the 93rd Congress contributed was therefore not the invention of every instrument from nothing. The contribution was consolidation under modern standards, procedural invention in the fast track, a retaliation authority of general reach in section 301, a preference program of general application in Title V, and an emigration condition in Title IV that no earlier trade statute had carried. Consolidation was the creative act. Instruments that had lived in separate statutes, under separate standards, administered on separate political logics, emerged from the 1974 enactment as one system in which a trading problem could be routed to negotiation, to a safeguard, to a retaliation proceeding, to a preference decision, or to an assistance certification, with the routing itself governed by law.

A roadmap of the six titles states the system in the order in which Congress enacted it. Title I supplies the negotiating authority and the fast track procedure, the authority at sections 101 and 102 for the President to enter trade agreements under stated objectives, the procedural precommitment at sections 151 to 154 for congressional consideration of implementing bills, and the balance of payments surcharge authority at section 122. Title II supplies relief from injury caused by import competition, with the safeguard mechanism of chapter 1 built around section 201 and with trade adjustment assistance in chapters 2 to 4 for workers at sections 221 and following, for firms at section 251 and following, and for communities at section 271 and following. Title III supplies relief from unfair trade practices at sections 301 to 310, the authority through which the United States answers foreign acts, policies, and practices that deny American rights or burden American commerce without justification. Title IV governs trade relations with non-market economy countries at sections 401 to 410, and its centerpiece, section 402, the Jackson-Vanik provision named for Senator Henry Jackson and Representative Charles Vanik and codified at 19 U.S.C. 2432, conditions nondiscriminatory treatment and American credits on freedom of emigration, subject to an annual presidential waiver that Congress may disapprove. Title V supplies preferences at sections 501 to 505, establishing the Generalized System of Preferences under which the President may extend duty free treatment to eligible articles from designated beneficiary developing countries, subject to the eligibility conditions and exclusions of the title. Title VI supplies the general provisions that complete the enactment, the definitions, administrative arrangements, and ancillary rules that allow the five operative titles to function as one statute. Read in that order, the titles move from the conference table to the factory gate to the foreign ministry to the non-market world to the development policy of the United States, and the movement is the argument.

The design also explains the durability that followed enactment. Authorities written as a system could be amended as a system, and later Congresses did exactly that. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 implemented the agreements that the Tokyo Round produced under the 1974 authority, which closed the circle that the Tokyo Declaration had opened in September 1973 and that Anjaria had described in 1975 as dependent on American enabling legislation. The amendments of 1984, enacted as Public Law 98-573, added the term discriminatory to the section 301 standard and extended its coverage, and the amendments of 1988, enacted as Public Law 100-418, transferred the section 301 authority to the United States Trade Representative and added the Super 301 procedure, and each amendment presupposed the 1974 architecture rather than replacing it. The preference program of Title V was renewed repeatedly for stated periods, the waiver cycle of Title IV governed relations with China from the first waiver based treatment on February 1, 1980 through the annual renewals that ended when permanent normal trade relations took effect on January 1, 2002 under Public Law 106-286, and Russia graduated from Jackson-Vanik coverage under Public Law 112-208 in December 2012. The Trade Act of 1974 instead left a standing allocation of roles among Congress, the President, the United States Trade Representative, and the International Trade Commission, together with a standing menu of routes for grievances of different kinds. That allocation, and that menu, are the inheritance that the origins and passage of H.R. 10710 delivered, and the sections that follow take up each instrument in turn on the terms that the 93rd Congress set for it.

## Fast Track and the Negotiating Bargain

Fast track solved a problem of sequence. A trade agreement negotiated abroad had to come home to a legislature with constitutional authority over commerce with foreign nations, and that legislature retained the ordinary power to amend any bill placed before it. A foreign government weighing concessions against an American negotiator therefore faced a second negotiation after the first one closed, conducted by legislators who had not sat at the table and who answered to constituencies exposed to the costs of the bargain in concentrated form. Concessions could be withdrawn in committee, altered on the floor, or delayed until the political moment that had produced them had passed. The Congressional Research Service, in the report on trade promotion authority and the role of Congress prepared by Ian F. Fergusson in 2006 and revised through 2019, described the arrangement as one in which Congress sought to define priorities, to require notification and consultation, to set the terms for entering agreements, and to reaffirm congressional authority by limiting the authority granted. That description captures Title I of the Trade Act of 1974, Public Law 93-618, enacted in December 1974 and signed by President Gerald Ford on January 3, 1975. The statute rearranged the points at which Congress acted, moving influence forward into the negotiation and narrowing final action to approval or rejection of the bargain as presented.

The problem had sharpened because the subject matter of negotiation had changed. Tariff reductions within limits approved in advance could be carried into effect by presidential proclamation under earlier statutes. Agreements reaching nontariff barriers reached further into domestic law. Rules on subsidies, government procurement, product standards, customs valuation, and licensing could not be applied in the United States without changes to statutes that only Congress could enact. The Congressional Research Service account of the background to the 1974 act, prepared for the 107th Congress in 2001, recorded that President Richard Nixon proposed proclamation authority for nontariff agreements under which an agreement would take effect unless Congress legislatively disapproved it, that the House of Representatives passed a version of that proposal, and that the proposal died in the Senate. The arrangement enacted in place of that proposal came from negotiations between Senate members and staff and the administration. Under the compromise, Congress agreed to consider and to vote on implementing legislation without amendment and under limits on debate, provided the President met conditions stated in the act.

Section 101 of the act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2111, supplied the authority to enter agreements. The provision authorized the President, during the period stated in the statute, to enter into trade agreements with foreign countries or instrumentalities of foreign countries whenever the President determined that existing duties or other import restrictions, or other barriers to or distortions of international trade, unduly burdened or restricted the foreign trade of the United States or adversely affected the American economy. Agreements concluded under the tariff authority could be proclaimed within the duty limits the statute set, while agreements on nontariff barriers required submission to Congress together with the draft implementing bill and a statement of administrative action proposed to carry the agreement into effect. The authority ran to the executive as negotiator and proclaimer within stated bounds, and stopped short of power to change domestic law by agreement alone. For nontariff matters, passage of the implementing bill was a required domestic step. The grant was an authority to negotiate and to conclude, conditioned by subject, by purpose, and by time, rather than a transfer of the commerce power itself.

Section 102 of the act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2112, supplied the direction that accompanied the authority. The statute stated an overall negotiating objective of obtaining more open and equitable market access and of harmonizing, reducing, or eliminating barriers to trade, and then stated principal negotiating objectives for particular subjects. The summary of H.R. 10710 published by Congress for the 93rd Congress described principal objectives that included competitive opportunities for exports in manufacturing and agriculture equivalent to the opportunities afforded to similar products in American markets, international safeguard procedures that would permit temporary measures to ease adjustment, and agreements securing fair and equitable access at reasonable prices to supplies of articles important to American economic requirements for which domestic productive capacity was absent or could not easily be developed. Section 102 also addressed particular barriers and distortions and the preservation of American trade remedy law within negotiated outcomes. As instructions, the objectives told negotiators what Congress expected an acceptable agreement to pursue. As conditions, the objectives furnished the standard against which Congress could judge whether the executive had stayed inside the bargain that earned expedited consideration.

### What does the fast track commitment require Congress to surrender and what does it preserve?

The commitment requires Congress to surrender amendment and extended debate on a qualifying implementing bill and to vote on the agreement as negotiated. The commitment preserves congressional direction through objectives stated in advance, notice and consultation during negotiation, committee involvement before submission, and a final vote that can reject the agreement outright.

The surrender was deliberate and limited, and the preservation was the price and the justification for the surrender. I. M. Destler of the Peterson Institute for International Economics, in American Trade Politics, first published in 1986 and issued in a fourth edition in 2005, argued that fast track bridged the division of power between the branches by giving executive negotiators credibility through assurance that Congress would not rework a concluded agreement, while guaranteeing a major congressional role and reducing the vulnerability of members to one sided restrictive pressures. The opposing account treated the identical features as a loss. Senator Robert Byrd of West Virginia, speaking against fast track in the Senate in 2001, warned that Congress must not allow an administration to arrogate to itself the full authority to determine the trade policy of the United States and objected to agreements that would not be subject to the normal rules of debate and amendment. The disagreement concerned whether influence exercised in advance counted as an adequate substitute for power exercised at the end, through amendment.

The procedure itself sits in sections 151 to 154 of the act, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194. Section 151 contains the core expedited procedure for implementing bills for agreements on nontariff barriers, and sections 152 to 154 extend and adapt expedited treatment to specified resolutions and measures connected with the trade title. The Congressional Research Service, in the procedural summary of trade agreement approval prepared by Richard S. Beth, described section 151 as a rulemaking provision for both chambers under which an implementing bill of a defined content qualified for treatment that ordinary bills did not receive. A qualifying bill could contain provisions approving the agreement and the statement of administrative action, together with provisions amending existing law that were necessary or appropriate to implement the agreement. The President submitted the agreement, the draft bill, and the supporting statement, and introduction followed in both chambers.

Committee consideration under section 151 ran on a clock. A committee to which an implementing bill had been referred faced automatic discharge if the committee had not reported the bill within forty five days after introduction, a rule stated for House consideration at section 151(e)(1) and described in the Congressional Research Service procedural summary revised through 2008. Discharge by operation of the statute removed the familiar point of quiet obstruction, the committee decision to hold a measure without action. The House of Representatives was then required to take the vote on final passage on or before the fifteenth day after the bill was reported or after the committee was discharged. Floor procedure in the House limited debate to twenty hours, divided equally between supporters and opponents, made the motion to proceed highly privileged and nondebatable, and barred motions to recommit and motions to reconsider the vote on passage. Appeals from decisions of the chair were decided without debate, as were motions to postpone consideration or to proceed to other business.

Senate procedure under section 151 carried the commitment further, because Senate procedure under ordinary rules permits extended debate that a determined minority can use to prevent a vote. The statute limited debate on an implementing bill in the Senate to twenty hours, applied corresponding limits to debate on motions and appeals, and made the motion to proceed nondebatable. The filibuster was therefore excluded for a qualifying bill not by a change in the majority required for passage, which remained a simple majority, but by removal of the stage at which extended debate could prevent the question from being reached. Senator Byrd, in the 2001 Senate debate, treated that removal as the center of the objection, on the ground that Senate deliberation depended on the possibility of extended debate and amendment. Supporters, among them Senator Charles Grassley of Iowa as chairman of the Finance Committee in the 108th Congress, treated the limit as the necessary counterpart of negotiation, on the ground that a bargain reopened without limit at the domestic stage would have to be renegotiated abroad.

Amendment was barred at the floor stage in both chambers, and the bar on amendment was the element that gave the procedure negotiating value. A foreign counterpart could read the statute and see that the text submitted after negotiation was the text on which Congress would vote, subject to rejection but not to revision line by line. Rejection remained a real possibility, and contested votes on later implementing bills under successor grants demonstrated that the commitment guaranteed consideration rather than approval. Congress promised a prompt decision on the bargain as a whole, inside a stated number of days and hours, and withheld any promise about the outcome. A legislature that can reject the whole agreement retains the final word on whether the agreement becomes domestic law, even though the legislature has renounced the intermediate word of amendment. Whether acceptance or rejection of a complex package, in which concessions in one sector offset concessions in another, amounts to meaningful control was the question on which Destler and Byrd divided.

The commitment was purchased by conditions, and the conditions ran in favor of Congress. Before entering into an agreement under the authority, the President was required to give advance notice to Congress. The Congressional Research Service background report for the 107th Congress, issued in 2001, stated the notice period in the 1974 arrangement as at least ninety days before entering into the agreement and treated notice as one of the conditions on which the assured vote depended. Notice gave the committees charged with trade jurisdiction time to test a nearly concluded bargain against the section 102 objectives, to hear affected interests, and to signal objections while alteration remained possible through the executive rather than through amendment on the floor.

Consultation reached further back than notice of conclusion. The statute required consultation with congressional committees before and during negotiation, and the structure designated the Committee on Ways and Means of the House of Representatives and the Committee on Finance of the Senate as the committees of principal jurisdiction, with consultation extending to other committees with jurisdiction over subjects touched by a negotiation. Members drawn from the revenue committees served as congressional advisers to negotiating delegations under arrangements that the Congressional Research Service described in the 1997 study of fast track implementation by Vladimir Pregelj, with advisers receiving briefings and providing advice on trade policy and implementation. Advisers could press the section 102 objectives while text remained fluid and could carry back to the committees an account of foreign positions that made later judgment more informed. The committees also developed, beginning with the Tokyo Round implementation, an informal practice of considering a draft implementing bill through a process resembling markup before formal submission, a practice the Senate Finance Committee described in the Congressional Record in 2004 as the centerpiece of the fast track process because amendments remained possible at the draft stage even though amendments were barred after formal introduction.

A further consultative layer stood outside Congress. Title I provided for an advisory committee structure through which private sector and other interests could present views on negotiations, including committees for policy, for industry sectors, for agriculture, and for labor, together with intergovernmental consultation with state and local officials. The structure served the executive as a source of technical information and served Congress as an early map of the interests that a concluded agreement would mobilize for or against passage. Advice did not bind negotiators, and the statute treated the committees as channels for views rather than as participants with a vote.

Conformity to the stated objectives formed the third condition, alongside notice and consultation. An agreement that departed from the overall and principal objectives of section 102 weakened the claim of the resulting bill to expedited treatment, because that treatment rested on a representation that negotiation had proceeded inside directions Congress had laid down in advance. The statute created no judicial test for conformity. Enforcement ran through Congress itself, through committee declarations during consultation, through presidential reports on the operation of agreements, and through the power section 151 expressly reserved to each chamber to change the expedited procedures as an exercise of the rulemaking power of that chamber.

Conditionality followed from that structure. Expedited procedure applied to a bill for an agreement entered into under the authority and in compliance with the statutory requirements, and executive failure to meet a stated requirement exposed the bill to treatment as an ordinary bill, with amendment in order, committee delay possible, and Senate debate governed by ordinary rules. Later renewal statutes made the point explicit through procedural disapproval mechanisms under which both chambers could withdraw expedited treatment where consultation had failed, a device stated in the 2015 renewal and described by the Congressional Research Service in the frequently asked questions report by Ian F. Fergusson and Richard S. Beth, version 37, issued in 2019. The 1974 text achieved a comparable result by definition, because a bill outside the statutory description had no claim to the procedure section 151 created. Executive accountability, in the argument advanced by supporters of renewal in 1988, 2002, and 2015, consisted in that exposure, reinforced by the need to return to Congress for any further grant.

The grant itself was made for a period, and the period was short. Section 101 authorized the President to enter into agreements during the five years after the date of enactment, a span that ran from January 3, 1975 and that covered the multilateral negotiation then underway in Geneva under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. The Congressional Research Service background report for the 107th Congress, issued in 2001, stated the grant in the same terms, as authority for agreements reached over the following five years. Expiration was built into the delegation. Congress granted a power that ended on a stated date unless Congress enacted a further grant, and the burden of action at expiration rested on the executive and on supporters of renewal, who had to persuade both chambers to legislate again. A decision to do nothing produced lapse rather than continuation.

The first renewal came in the Trade Agreements Act of 1979, Public Law 96-39, the same statute that implemented the Tokyo Round agreements. The Congressional Research Service report on trade promotion authority frequently asked questions, version 37, issued in 2019, recorded that the authority was renewed four times after the original enactment, in 1979, 1988, 2002, and 2015, and that a short term extension in 1993 accommodated completion of the Uruguay Round negotiations. The 1979 renewal extended the authority for a further eight years, according to the Congressional Research Service background report for the 107th Congress. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, renewed the authority for agreements reached through May 1993, and an amendment enacted as Public Law 103-49 extended the authority for Uruguay Round agreements reached before April 16, 1994. After that date the authority expired, and the Congressional Research Service frequently asked questions report of 2019 recorded lapses from 1994 to 2002 and from 2007 to 2015. The Trade Act of 2002, Public Law 107-210, restored the authority under the name trade promotion authority, and the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, Public Law 114-26, signed on June 29, 2015, restored the authority again. Each renewal arrived attached to a trade statute rather than as an amendment making the 1974 grant permanent.

The pattern of lapse and renewal shows a series of discrete grants rather than a settled transfer of function. During a lapse, the President retained the constitutional power to negotiate, and the free trade agreement with Jordan concluded in 2000 was negotiated and approved through ordinary procedures without fast track, as the Congressional Research Service frequently asked questions report of 2019 recorded. Negotiation and approval without the procedure were possible, but neither carried the advance assurance on timing and amendment that the procedure supplied.

What the procedure carried in the first two decades demonstrated the scale of bargain the mechanism was built to handle. The Tokyo Round of multilateral trade negotiations, conducted from 1973 to 1979 under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, produced agreements on nontariff measures including subsidies and countervailing duties, government procurement, technical barriers to trade, customs valuation, and import licensing, alongside tariff reductions. Implementation required changes to domestic law of exactly the kind that had defeated reliance on proclamation authority, and the Trade Agreements Act of 1979, Public Law 96-39, enacted July 26, 1979, approved and implemented those agreements under the procedure of the 1974 act. The Congressional Research Service table of major votes on trade promotion authority recorded passage in the House of Representatives by 395 to 7 on July 11, 1979 and in the Senate by 90 to 4 on July 23, 1979, margins that reflected a package assembled through the consultative process rather than a bargain contested line by line at the floor stage.

The Uruguay Round supplied the larger test. Launched in 1986 and concluded by the Marrakesh Agreement signed on April 15, 1994, the round produced agreements that established the World Trade Organization and that covered trade in services, intellectual property, agriculture, textiles, and dispute settlement, in addition to goods. The implementing statute, the Uruguay Round Agreements Act, Public Law 103-465, enacted December 8, 1994, traveled under the successor grant in the Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 as extended for that purpose, and the agreements entered into force on January 1, 1995. The Congressional Research Service table of major votes recorded passage in the House of Representatives by 288 to 146 on November 29, 1994 and in the Senate by 76 to 24 on December 1, 1994. The narrower House margin, in contrast to the Tokyo Round vote of 1979, showed the commitment operating under strain. Opponents could not amend the bill and could not extend Senate debate beyond the statutory limit, and opponents retained and exercised the power to vote against the package as a whole. Between the two multilateral implementations, successor grants also carried the North American Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act of 1993, Public Law 103-182.

Confined to what the text permits, Title I permitted the President to negotiate and to enter agreements within stated subjects, for stated purposes, and during a stated period, under objectives Congress enacted and under duties of notice and consultation that Congress imposed, and Title I permitted Congress to bind its own consideration of a resulting bill to a timetable, to a bar on amendment, and to debate limits that removed the filibuster for that class of bill. The statute did not permit the President to change domestic law without an implementing enactment for a nontariff agreement, did not permit expedited treatment for a bill outside the statutory description, and did not permit a grant to survive the period stated in the grant. The facts were a conditional and temporary exchange, in which Congress traded amendment and extended debate at the final stage for objectives, notice, consultation, and a guaranteed vote, and in which the trade expired unless Congress chose to make the trade again.

## The Unfair Practices Authority

Title III of the Trade Act of 1974 carried the heading "Relief from Unfair Trade Practices" and ran from section 301 to section 310, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2411 to 2420, with section 301 itself codified at 19 U.S.C. 2411. The title supplied the enforcement counterpart to the negotiating authority granted elsewhere in the statute. Title I authorized the executive to conclude trade agreements under procedures Congress had fixed in advance, and Title III addressed the opposite contingency, in which a foreign government maintained a practice that no agreement had removed or that an agreement itself forbade. The Congressional Research Service report on the provision, published in 2000, recorded that the title was designed to assure a swift and certain response to foreign import restrictions, export subsidies, price discrimination and other unfair foreign trade practices.

As enacted in 1974, section 301 authorized the President to take action against a foreign act, policy or practice that was unjustifiable or unreasonable and that burdened or restricted United States commerce. Each element of that formulation did distinct work. The subject was an act, policy or practice of a foreign country, a phrase broad enough to cover a statute, an administrative rule, a pattern of enforcement or a settled failure to enforce. The qualifying adjectives were unjustifiable or unreasonable, and the Congressional Research Service summary of the original provision recorded that unjustifiable restrictions were understood as restrictions that violated international law or obligations under previous agreements. The effect element required a burden or restriction on United States commerce, so a practice objectionable in the abstract but without a demonstrated effect on commerce fell outside the grant. The decision maker under the original text was the President, and the authority was a presidential authority, exercised through the trade agencies but vested at the top of the executive branch.

The original standard left considerable room for judgment, and Congress returned to the provision repeatedly to narrow that room, to regularize procedure and to widen the categories of commerce protected. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979, Public Law 96-39, amended section 301 to establish a timetable for investigating and taking action on complaints and to establish procedures and requirements for the Special Trade Representative, the office later known as the United States Trade Representative, to follow during investigations. The Congressional Research Service account of the amendments treated the 1979 act as the procedural turning point in the history of the provision. Before 1979, the movement from complaint to investigation to determination rested largely on executive practice. After 1979, the statute itself supplied the sequence and the deadlines, and a complainant could point to a timetable that the administering office was obliged to meet.

The Trade and Tariff Act of 1984, Public Law 98-573, widened the substantive reach of the provision in three connected ways. First, the 1984 act added the word "discriminatory" to the standard, so that an act, policy or practice that was unjustifiable, unreasonable or discriminatory and that burdened or restricted United States commerce could support action. Second, the 1984 act defined the three operative terms in the statute itself, replacing reliance on general understandings with definitions that later determinations were required to apply. Third, the 1984 act extended coverage expressly to services, investment and intellectual property, fields in which trade barriers took forms other than a tariff or a quota on goods. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the 1984 act expanded the authority to respond to unfair trading practices in services, investment and intellectual property rights, defined the terms unreasonable, unjustifiable and discriminatory, and granted authority to self-initiate investigations.

The definitions added in 1984 and refined thereafter gave each adjective a separate meaning, and the distinctions among them later determined whether action was mandatory or discretionary. A practice was unjustifiable where it violated, was inconsistent with, or otherwise denied the United States the benefits of a trade agreement, or where it violated or denied the international legal rights of the United States. A practice was unreasonable where, without necessarily violating international legal rights, it was otherwise unfair and inequitable. The World Trade Organization panel proceedings of 1999 recorded statutory examples of unreasonable conduct that included denial of opportunities for the establishment of an enterprise, failure to protect intellectual property rights, export targeting, toleration of anticompetitive practices and denial of worker rights. A practice was discriminatory where it denied national treatment or most favored nation treatment to United States goods, services or investment, national treatment meaning treatment no less favorable than that given to domestic products or firms and most favored nation treatment meaning treatment no less favorable than that given to the products or firms of any other country.

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, made the most consequential structural changes since enactment. The 1988 act transferred the authority under section 301 from the President to the United States Trade Representative, who thereafter conducted investigations and made determinations, subject to the specific direction, if any, of the President regarding action. The transfer changed the institutional character of the provision without removing presidential control, because a direction from the President could govern action in a particular case while ordinary administration rested with the Trade Representative. The 1988 act also divided the response into a mandatory track and a discretionary track, a division examined in detail below, and it added the identification procedure known as Super 301, codified at section 310 of the act. The Congressional Research Service report of 2000 recorded that Super 301 required the Trade Representative for 1989 and 1990 to issue a report on trade priorities and to identify priority foreign countries and priority practices that had the greatest effect on restricting United States exports. Identification was not a finding of violation by itself, and identification triggered the investigation that supplied the determination on which action could rest.

### What must be proved before action follows?

Action follows an affirmative determination by the United States Trade Representative that a foreign act, policy or practice denies United States rights under a trade agreement, is unjustifiable, or is unreasonable or discriminatory, and in each covered instance burdens or restricts United States commerce after investigation and consultation procedures have run.

The process began in one of two ways. An interested person could file a petition with the Trade Representative requesting an investigation, and interested persons included firms, workers, trade associations and other private parties representing a significant economic interest affected by the practice. The Trade Representative was required to determine whether to initiate an investigation within 45 days of receipt of a petition, after review of the allegations by the Section 301 Committee. The Congressional Research Service description of the procedure recorded that if the Trade Representative determined not to initiate an investigation, notice of the reasons went to the petitioner and a summary was published in the Federal Register, and that if an investigation was initiated, a summary of the petition was published in the Federal Register with an opportunity for the presentation of views, including a public hearing. Alternatively, the Trade Representative could self-initiate an investigation after consulting with appropriate public and private stakeholders.

Once an investigation began, administration rested with the Section 301 Committee, a subordinate staff level body of the interagency Trade Policy Staff Committee chaired by the Trade Representative. The Congressional Research Service recorded that the Section 301 Committee reviewed petitions, conducted public hearings and made recommendations to the Trade Policy Staff Committee, and that the Trade Representative based decisions on the recommendations provided through that committee structure, an arrangement codified at 15 C.F.R. 2002.3. The Trade Representative was required to request consultations with the foreign government concerned at the initiation of the investigation. If consultations failed to produce a mutually acceptable resolution and the investigation involved a trade agreement, the Trade Representative was required to request proceedings under the formal dispute settlement procedures of that agreement. For investigations that involved no agreement, consultations with the foreign government and consultation with the appropriate domestic trade advisory committees took the place of formal dispute proceedings.

The timetable for determination depended on the character of the case. In an investigation involving a trade agreement, the determination was due on or before the earlier of the date 30 days after the dispute settlement procedure concluded or the date 18 months after the investigation was initiated, a formula recorded in the World Trade Organization panel report of 1999 in its exposition of section 304. In a case involving no trade agreement, the Trade Representative generally made the determination within 12 months after the investigation began, as the Congressional Research Service summarized the rule from the statutory text. Upon an affirmative determination, the Trade Representative determined at the same time what action, if any, the statute required or permitted, and retaliatory action, where imposed, followed within a short statutory interval, described by the Congressional Research Service as implementation within 30 days of the determination.

The mandatory and discretionary tracks divided affirmative determinations according to the gravity of the finding. Action was mandatory where the Trade Representative concluded that the rights of the United States under a trade agreement were being denied, or that a foreign act, policy or practice violated, was inconsistent with, or otherwise denied the United States the benefits of a trade agreement, or that a practice was unjustifiable and burdened or restricted United States commerce. In a mandatory case the Trade Representative was required to take action, subject to limited statutory exceptions, including a determination that the foreign country was taking satisfactory measures to grant the rights of the United States or that action would cause serious harm to the national security of the United States. Action was discretionary where the finding rested on the other two adjectives. Where the Trade Representative determined that a practice was unreasonable or discriminatory and burdened or restricted United States commerce, the Trade Representative could take action if action was determined to be appropriate, and could also determine that no action, or action short of retaliation, best served the statutory purpose of eliminating the practice. Denial of an agreement right and unjustifiable conduct compelled a response, while unreasonable and discriminatory conduct authorized a response whose form and timing remained matters of official judgment.

The range of responses the statute permitted was set out principally in section 301(c). The Trade Representative could suspend, withdraw or prevent the application of benefits of trade agreement concessions to the foreign country concerned. The Trade Representative could impose duties or other import restrictions on the goods of that country, and could impose fees or restrictions on the services of that country, for such time as the Trade Representative determined appropriate. The Trade Representative could also enter into a binding agreement with the foreign government under which that government agreed to eliminate the act, policy or practice, to eliminate the burden or restriction on United States commerce, or to compensate the United States with satisfactory trade concessions. The Congressional Research Service account of retaliation recorded the same three families of response and recorded two further disciplines on their use. Where import restrictions were chosen, the Trade Representative was required to give preference to duties. Where action was mandatory, the action was to affect goods or services of the foreign country in an amount equivalent in value to the burden or restriction imposed on United States commerce.

Action under the title did not end with imposition. Section 306 required the Trade Representative to monitor the implementation of measures taken by a foreign government, and of agreements entered into, to provide a satisfactory resolution of a matter that had been the subject of an investigation. Where monitoring showed that a foreign government had failed to implement an agreement or a satisfactory resolution, that failure was treated under the statute as a denial of United States rights and could support further action. Sections 307 and 309 addressed modification and termination, and the Congressional Research Service summary recorded that the Trade Representative was required to report to Congress the reasons for any modification or termination of action. An action taken under section 301 terminated after four years unless the Trade Representative received a request for continuation and conducted a review of the action and its effects on the United States economy. The four year rule prevented a retaliatory measure from becoming permanent by inertia, while the review procedure allowed continuation where the underlying practice and the burden on commerce persisted.

The use record of the first two decades showed an authority invoked far more often than it was carried through to sanctions. The World Trade Organization panel report of 1999, reviewing the operation of sections 301 to 310, recorded that between 1974 and 1994 the United States initiated nearly 100 investigations under section 301 and that unilateral retaliatory measures were actually imposed in only eight of those cases, although measures were announced in many more. The same record supported the conclusion that in the majority of cases the investigation and the prospect of sanctions produced a bilateral negotiated solution without imposition. That pattern followed from the structure of the statute, because the timetable created a deadline for negotiation, the consultation requirement created a channel for negotiation and the mandatory track created a credible prospect of action after an affirmative finding.

The semiconductor dispute with Japan supplied the most fully documented application of the authority in the 1980s. On June 14, 1985 the Semiconductor Industry Association filed a petition under section 301 alleging that Japan restricted access to the Japanese semiconductor market for United States producers, a filing date recorded in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade panel materials on the dispute.Negotiations produced the Arrangement Concerning Trade in Semiconductor Products, signed on September 2, 1986, under which Japan undertook commitments on market access for foreign based semiconductor producers and on monitoring costs and export prices to prevent sales below cost in the United States and in third markets. Proclamation 5631 of April 17, 1987 then recorded a determination under section 301 that the Government of Japan had not implemented or enforced major provisions of the 1986 arrangement and that the failure was inconsistent with a trade agreement and was unjustifiable and unreasonable and constituted a burden or restriction on United States commerce. The proclamation imposed increased duties on selected imported articles that were products of Japan. A scholarly account of the dispute published by the University of Michigan Press recorded that the sanctions announced on March 27, 1987 took the form of 100 percent retaliatory tariffs on 300 million dollars of Japanese electrical devices, including television sets, laptop computers, disk drive units, stereo equipment and electric motors.

Super 301 added a third posture, in which the initiative came from a statutory identification exercise rather than from a petition or a single dispute. For 1989 and 1990 the Trade Representative was required to report trade priorities, identify priority foreign countries and priority practices and initiate investigations aimed at the elimination of the identified practices. The Congressional Research Service report of 2000 recorded that Japan was the main target of Super 301 during its initial two year operation and that Brazil and India were designated as Super 301 priority countries in 1989, with India designated again in 1990. The practices identified against Japan concerned barriers affecting satellites, supercomputers and wood products among other sectors, and the United States reached separate trade agreements with Japan in 1990 on trade barriers to satellites, supercomputers and wood products, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in 2000. The original Super 301 provision expired after the 1990 cycle. An executive order issued in 1994, Executive Order 12901, reactivated the Super 301 mechanism for 1994 and 1995 with certain changes, as the Congressional Research Service also recorded in 2000.

The establishment of the World Trade Organization on January 1, 1995 changed the setting in which the authority operated. The Uruguay Round agreements, implemented in United States law by the Uruguay Round Agreements Act of 1994, Public Law 103-465, created a dispute settlement system with compulsory jurisdiction over disputes under the covered agreements and with procedures for authorizing suspension of concessions where a member failed to comply with adopted recommendations and rulings. Article 23 of the Dispute Settlement Understanding required members seeking redress of a violation under the covered agreements to have recourse to the dispute settlement procedures and to abide by them, and the Statement of Administrative Action that accompanied the 1994 act recorded the commitment of the United States to base determinations in cases involving a trade agreement on the findings of the dispute settlement system. The Congressional Research Service summary of the later practice stated that after the conclusion of the Uruguay Round in 1995 the United States used section 301 authorities primarily to build cases and pursue dispute settlement at the World Trade Organization. The characteristic outcome of an agreement based case became a panel proceeding and an authorized remedy rather than a unilateral tariff imposed on a domestic determination alone.

The relative disuse of unilateral determinations after 1995 was a change in practice recorded in dated terms by the case count and by the intervals between cases. The Congressional Research Service report on the provision recorded 130 cases under section 301 from enactment in 1974, of which 35 were initiated after the establishment of the World Trade Organization in 1995, figures reflecting initiations through the period covered by that report. The same body of reporting recorded that before 2017 the last section 301 investigation had taken place in 2013 and had involved practices of Ukraine regarding intellectual property rights, and that the investigation ended without retaliation. The statute itself had not been repealed or narrowed during the interval, and the difference between the practice of 1987 and the practice of 2005 lay in the channel through which agreement based disputes were pursued, not in the existence of the authority.

The investigations opened in 2017 and 2018 confirmed that the change after 1995 had been a change in practice rather than in law. In August 2017 the Trade Representative initiated an investigation of acts, policies and practices of China related to technology transfer, intellectual property and innovation, and tariff actions under section 301 followed from 2018, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in its reporting on the investigation in 2020. Those actions rested on findings framed in the statutory terms of unreasonable and discriminatory conduct burdening or restricting United States commerce, the discretionary track created by the amendments of 1984 and 1988, and the responses took the form that the statute placed first in order of preference, namely increased duties on goods of the country concerned. Stated as dated fact, the record through June 1, 2020 showed three distinct phases in the operation of a single continuous authority. From 1975 to 1994 the authority supported frequent investigations and occasional sanctions, including the semiconductor sanctions of 1987 and the Super 301 identifications of 1989. From 1995 to 2016 agreement based disputes were channeled into World Trade Organization settlement and unilateral determinations fell into relative disuse. From 2017 the authority was again used to support unilateral tariff action, on the basis of the same statutory text that had supported the actions of the 1980s.

Title III thus supplied the statutory basis in United States law for unilateral tariff action taken outside any negotiated framework with the country concerned. The basis was statutory rather than inherent, because each element of an action traced to a provision that Congress had enacted or amended. A covered act, policy or practice had to be identified. The practice had to be found unjustifiable, unreasonable or discriminatory under definitions Congress had supplied. A burden or restriction on United States commerce had to be found. An investigation had to be conducted, consultations had to be requested and a determination had to issue within the timetable Congress had set. Action, where mandatory, had to be equivalent in value to the burden found, and action, where discretionary, had to be determined appropriate to obtain elimination of the practice. The responses available were the responses the text permitted, namely suspension of concessions, duties and other import restrictions on goods, fees and restrictions on services, or a binding agreement for elimination of the practice or for compensation. Described by what the text permits, the authority was a conditional and procedural power, broad in the commerce it reached and in the responses it allowed, and bounded by the findings, the deadlines and the equivalence and review requirements written into sections 301 to 310.

## Safeguards and the Serious Injury Test

Title II of the Trade Act of 1974 bore the heading Relief from Injury Caused by Import Competition, and chapter 1 of that title contained the safeguard authority in sections 201 to 216 of the act. Section 201 itself, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2251, set the test that opened the entire proceeding, and the sections that followed governed the investigation, the recommendation, the presidential decision, the duration of relief, and the review of relief already in force. The Congressional Research Service, in a 2018 summary of the authority prepared by Vivian Jones, described section 201 actions as measures designed to provide temporary relief for a domestic industry in order to facilitate positive adjustment to import competition. The statute defined positive adjustment as the ability of an industry to compete successfully with imports after the safeguard ended, or as the orderly transfer of industry resources to other productive pursuits together with the productive transition of dislocated workers. Relief was therefore built around a destination rather than a grievance, and every feature of the design served that framing.

The test itself was stated with precision in the statutory text. Relief became available where an article was being imported into the United States in such increased quantities as to be a substantial cause of serious injury, or the threat of serious injury, to the domestic industry producing a like or directly competitive article. The increase in imports could be absolute or relative to domestic production, as the Congressional Research Service explained in 2018, and the increase had to be linked to the condition of a defined domestic industry rather than to the fortunes of a single firm in isolation. Substantial cause had its own statutory meaning, namely a cause which is important and not less than any other cause, so imports did not need to outweigh every other source of difficulty combined, yet a minor or incidental contribution from imports failed the test. Serious injury likewise had a distinct content, since the Commission weighed the significant idling of productive facilities, the inability of a significant number of firms to operate at a reasonable level of profit, and significant unemployment or underemployment within the industry, together with declines in production, sales, market share, profits, wages, and productivity. Where the claim rested on threat, the Commission examined evidence that serious injury was clearly imminent, including declining sales or market share, rising inventories, and a downward trend in operating indicators.

The feature that separated the safeguard from every other instrument in the statute was the absence of any requirement that a foreign government or a foreign exporter had done anything unfair. A petition under section 201 alleged no dumping, because no comparison between export price and fair value formed any part of the case. A petition alleged no subsidy, because no foreign financial contribution or benefit needed to be identified, measured, or offset. A petition alleged no unjustifiable, unreasonable, or discriminatory foreign practice of the kind that Title III required for action against unfair trade practices. The imports that triggered relief could be priced lawfully, produced efficiently, and sold by exporters in full compliance with their obligations, and the statutory question remained identical, because the question concerned the volume of imports and the condition of the domestic industry rather than the character of the trade. The Congressional Research Service drew the contrast expressly in 2018, noting that section 201 investigations concerned import surges of fairly traded goods, whereas dumping and countervailing duty cases investigated material injury grounded in sales at less than fair value or in subsidization by a foreign government or public entity.

That absence of a wrongdoing requirement shaped the logic of the remedy. Because no foreign party stood accused, relief operated against imports as such and ordinarily applied to imports from all sources rather than to the shipments of identified offenders, subject to statutory exclusions for certain free trade partners and for developing countries beneath defined import shares. Because no foreign party stood accused, the international system attached a compensating discipline, under which affected exporting countries expected an offsetting concession or, after a defined interval, a right to suspend substantially equivalent concessions in return, a structure carried in Article XIX of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of 1947 and elaborated in the Agreement on Safeguards of 1994. Because no foreign party stood accused, the domestic threshold stood higher than in the unfair trade laws, with serious injury rather than material injury and a substantial cause standard rather than a lesser contribution test, so the stringency of the showing substituted for the missing element of fault. Alan Sykes, writing in 1991 on the economic theory of safeguard law, treated that substitution as central to the institution, since a mechanism that taxed lawful commerce demanded stronger proof of harm and firmer temporal limits than a mechanism that answered a legal violation. The safeguard alone, among the instruments of the 1974 act, authorized the government to restrict trade that nobody contended had broken any rule.

### Does a safeguard require proof that foreign trade was unfair?

No. Section 201 relief turns entirely on increased imports, serious injury, and causation. The Commission examines import volumes, idle capacity, profits, and employment, and the President then weighs the national economic interest. Lawful pricing, lawful subsidies abroad as such, and foreign government misconduct play no necessary part in the determination.

The lineage of the instrument ran through the escape clause tradition of American trade agreements law. As early as the 1940s, the United States began including escape clause mechanisms in the trade agreements it concluded, on the reasoning recorded in later accounts of the period that producers injured by the import consequences of negotiated tariff reductions required a defined recourse even though exporters had traded within the agreements themselves. The decision to place an escape clause in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was taken during the drafting meetings of early 1947, according to the negotiating record preserved by the World Trade Organization, and Article XIX of the General Agreement as signed on October 30, 1947, permitted a contracting party to suspend an obligation or withdraw or modify a concession where unforeseen developments and the effect of obligations incurred under the agreement produced imports in such increased quantities as to cause or threaten serious injury to domestic producers of like or directly competitive products.

The 1962 mechanism, however, imposed strict requirements, as the International Trade Commission recorded in its 2009 history of United States trade policy since 1934. Under the 1962 test, a specific tariff concession granted by the United States had to be the major cause of injury, a phrase understood to mean that the concession had caused more harm than all other factors combined, and the injured industry also carried the burden of tying its condition to that identified concession. Few industries could assemble such a showing. The Trade Act of 1974 recast the inherited mechanism around the serious injury standard. The 1974 act removed the burden of linking harm to a specific tariff concession, so injury could follow from import competition generally, and the 1974 act replaced the major cause formulation with the substantial cause formulation, under which increased imports needed to cause injury to a degree not less than any other single cause.

The process began with a petition to the International Trade Commission. A petition could be filed by a trade association, a firm, a union, or a group of workers representing the domestic industry, and investigations could also be set in motion by resolution of the House Committee on Ways and Means or the Senate Committee on Finance, at the request of the President or the United States Trade Representative, or on the initiative of the Commission itself, as the Congressional Research Service set out in 2018. Petitioners carried an obligation that revealed the purpose of the chapter, since a petition had to be accompanied by a plan to facilitate positive adjustment to import competition, or supplemented with such a plan within a defined period after filing, so the industry was required at the outset to state how protection would be used. The Commission then conducted its investigation in two phases, holding hearings, soliciting public comment, and publishing its findings, with a report due to the President within 180 days of the filing of the petition unless an extension applied in a complicated case. In the injury phase the Commission voted on whether the statutory test had been met.

An affirmative determination moved the case into its remedy phase, and the character of the proceeding changed from adjudication toward policy. The Commission considered actions that would address the serious injury and would be most effective in facilitating positive adjustment, and it could recommend an increase in or imposition of a duty, a tariff-rate quota, the modification or imposition of a quantitative restriction, or a combination of those measures. The report then passed to the President, who held the decision and was not bound by the recommendation in either direction, with 60 days after receiving the report to decide which action, if any, to take. The President was directed to take into account the Commission report and recommendation, the efforts of the industry to make a positive adjustment, including the proposals in its adjustment plan, the probable effectiveness of the recommended actions, the extent to which workers and firms already benefited from adjustment assistance and retraining programs, the short term and long term economic and social costs of action weighed against the potential benefits, and the position of the domestic industry in the economy of the United States. Over those factors stood the broader instruction to weigh the national economic interest, including the effect of relief on consumers and on other domestic industries. The menu of presidential actions covered an increase in duties, a tariff-rate quota under which a lower rate applied to a stated quantity and a higher rate applied above that quantity, a quantitative restriction on imports, negotiated agreements with foreign governments limiting exports to the United States, or adjustment measures for domestic firms and workers, together with other steps authorized by law.

Duration limits completed the design, and those limits carried the theory of the instrument. Import relief could run for an initial period of no more than four years. Relief in effect for more than one year had to be phased down at regular intervals during its term, so protection declined on a published trajectory rather than expiring at a single cliff. Extension beyond the initial period required a further proceeding, in which the Commission investigated whether relief remained necessary and whether the industry was making a positive adjustment, and the President could then extend relief subject to an absolute ceiling of eight years in total. Every safeguard therefore carried its own ending inside its grant, and each successive year of relief offered less shelter than the year before.

The early record under the recast standard was active and mixed. The Congressional Research Service recorded in 2018 that the Commission conducted 73 investigations between 1975 and 2001, reaching negative determinations in 32 cases and affirmative determinations in 34 cases, with divided votes in the remainder, and that presidents imposed some form of safeguard measure in 19 cases during that span, in forms that included tariff increases, adjustment assistance, quotas and tariff-rate quotas, and negotiated restraint arrangements. Two grants of the early 1980s illustrated the range of the instrument. The first concerned specialty steel. President Ronald Reagan directed the United States Trade Representative in November 1982 to request a Commission investigation, and the Commission found specialty steel imports to be a substantial cause of serious injury, reporting that imports had depressed domestic prices, captured market share, reduced production and capacity utilization, produced major losses for most producers, and cut employment significantly. On July 5, 1983, President Reagan announced four years of relief combining the two classic forms. Flat rolled products, which accounted for 55 percent of imports in the category, received an additional declining tariff that began at 10 percent for stainless steel sheet and strip and at 8 percent for plate and fell to 4 percent by the fourth year, while imports of stainless steel rod, bar, and alloy tool steel received a global quota that expanded by 3 percent annually.

The second grant of 1983 became the most retold safeguard episode in the statute record. Harley-Davidson, described in the New York Times report of April 1983 as the sole surviving American maker of motorcycles, petitioned for relief as Japanese manufacturers came to dominate every sector of the American motorcycle market. The Commission found the statutory test met for heavyweight motorcycles, and in April 1983 President Reagan ordered an increase in the tariff on imported motorcycles with engines larger than 700 cubic centimeters from the existing rate of 4.4 percent to 49.4 percent in the first year, with the additional duty scheduled to step down to 39.4 percent in the second year, 24.4 percent in the third year, 19.4 percent in the fourth year, and 14.4 percent in the fifth year before returning to the original rate. Harley-Davidson used the interval to reorganize production, improve quality control, and introduce new models after a management buyout completed in 1981, and in 1987 the company asked that the remaining relief end a year ahead of schedule on the ground that protection had ceased to be necessary, as the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel reported in 1987. A White House statement issued under President Bill Clinton in 1999 declared that the Harley-Davidson experience was an example that United States trade policy can work, and defenders of the grant treated the early termination request as evidence that a dated and declining remedy had bought adjustment rather than dependence. Douglas Irwin reached a cooler conclusion in Clashing Over Commerce, the 2017 history of American trade policy, where the recovery was attributed principally to changes the company made in its own products and operations and the tariff was assigned a limited role. A 2009 study by the Research Institute of Economy, Trade and Industry in Japan reached a parallel conclusion, finding that the initiatives of Harley-Davidson itself boosted sales and preserved the company rather than the tariff, primarily because consumers perceived the two kinds of motorcycles as poor substitutes for each other, so higher import prices diverted few buyers.

The largest safeguard action in the record arrived two decades later. At the direction of President George W. Bush, United States Trade Representative Robert Zoellick requested a comprehensive steel investigation in June 2001, covering carbon and alloy flat products, long products, pipe and tube, and stainless and alloy tool steel, in an industry where, as Zoellick stated in 2001, more than 200,000 workers held jobs and many firms stood in or near bankruptcy after a surge of low priced imports that followed the Asian financial crisis of the late 1990s. After a nine month investigation, the Commission found serious injury warranting relief across ten steel product categories. On March 5, 2002, President Bush proclaimed safeguards that took effect on March 20, 2002, consisting principally of additional tariffs ranging from 8 percent to 30 percent across the ten categories on a declining three year schedule. Eight World Trade Organization members challenged the measures in a consolidated dispute. A panel ruled against the United States in 2003, and the Appellate Body, in a report circulated on November 10, 2003, and adopted on December 10, 2003, affirmed the result in considerable part. On December 4, 2003, before the adoption date, President Bush terminated the safeguards by proclamation under section 204 of the act, roughly twenty months into the planned three year term, stating that the measures had achieved their purpose and that changed economic circumstances made the moment right to lift them. Zoellick argued in a statement of December 4, 2003, that the industry had used the breathing space well, pointing to consolidation and restructuring during the relief period. Assessments diverged, since Gary Hufbauer and Jeffrey Schott, in a 2003 analysis for the Peterson Institute for International Economics, estimated that steel prices rose between 1.3 percent and 5.1 percent during 2002 and found little ascertainable change in steel employment, while a 2003 study prepared for the Consuming Industries Trade Action Coalition estimated that higher steel costs had reduced employment in steel consuming sectors by roughly 200,000 jobs during 2002, exceeding total employment in steel production itself, with approximately four billion dollars in lost wages.

A long interval without a completed safeguard grant followed the steel termination, and the next grants arrived in January 2018, stated here as dated fact. A petition touching crystalline silicon photovoltaic cells and modules was filed in May 2017, and a petition touching large residential washers was filed in June 2017, as the Congressional Research Service timeline of 2019 recorded. The Commission made an affirmative injury determination on solar cells and modules on September 22, 2017, and an affirmative determination on large residential washers on October 5, 2017, and transmitted its reports and recommended actions to the President on November 13, 2017, for solar products and on December 4, 2017, for washers. On January 23, 2018, the President proclaimed a four year safeguard measure on solar cells and modules effective February 7, 2018, built as a tariff-rate quota on cells under which an annual quantity of 2.5 gigawatts entered at the ordinary duty free rate and imports above that quantity bore a tariff of 30 percent descending by five percentage points annually, together with a tariff of 30 percent on modules descending by five percentage points annually. On the same day the President proclaimed a three year safeguard measure on large residential washers, built as a tariff-rate quota under which the first 1.2 million finished washers in each year bore a tariff of 20 percent descending to 16 percent across the term and imports above that quantity bore a tariff of 50 percent descending to 40 percent, with a related tariff-rate quota on covered parts.

The time limits and the declining character of relief were not administrative details attached to the instrument but the substance of its theory, and the arguments over that theory divided along lines visible across the whole record. Defenders of the design argued that a lawful and temporary escape made deeper liberalization possible in the first place. John Jackson, in a 1997 discussion of the trading system cited in later Federal Reserve research, presented the safeguard as an escape valve that allowed governments to accept larger and riskier tariff reductions during negotiations because a defined remedy existed if a reduction produced concentrated injury, and Wilfred Ethier developed a formal version of the argument in 2002, showing that governments uncertain of future growth negotiated larger reductions when a temporary right to raise tariffs stood behind the commitment. On that account the occasional safeguard, bought at a bounded cost in a few sectors, purchased permanent liberalization across the whole tariff schedule. The declining schedule and the eight year ceiling served the same account, since a remedy programmed to shrink denied the protected industry any rational expectation of renewal as a substitute for restructuring, and the Harley-Davidson request of 1987 for early termination was offered by defenders, including the Clinton White House in its 1999 statement, as the design working exactly as intended.

Holders of the opposing argument answered that the design discipline weakened in practice at each of its joints. Hufbauer and Schott, in the 2003 Peterson Institute analysis, documented costs that fell immediately on consumers and consuming industries while benefits to the protected industry remained difficult to detect in employment data, and the 2003 Consuming Industries Trade Action Coalition study converted that asymmetry into employment losses in consuming sectors larger than the entire workforce of the protected sector. Irwin, in the 2017 history, generalized the skepticism through the motorcycle case, where the celebrated success dissolved on inspection into a recovery driven by management change and product improvement that the tariff accompanied rather than caused. The statute resolved the argument only in form, since it granted the escape, priced the escape with a demanding injury test, surrounded the escape with findings, plans, and reviews, and then set the escape to expire on a declining schedule whether the industry had adjusted or not. The safeguard bought adjustment time at a stated price and for a stated term, and the statute made no promise beyond the term.

## Preferences and the Emigration Condition

Title V of the Trade Act of 1974, sections 501 to 505, with section 501 codified at 19 U.S.C. 2461, created the American version of an idea circulating among developed countries since the late 1960s. The Generalized System of Preferences departed deliberately from reciprocity. Instead of bargaining for concessions, the United States offered duty-free entry for designated articles from designated developing countries and asked nothing in tariff terms in return. The Congressional Research Service, in its 2019 overview of the program, described the purpose in the terms Congress itself had used in 1974, namely to spur economic development in poor countries through trade and to help those countries diversify economies that depended on a narrow range of primary products.

The machinery of designation had two sides, one for countries and one for articles. The President designated beneficiary developing countries and could withdraw, suspend, or limit a designation, and designated eligible articles after receiving the advice of the International Trade Commission on the likely effect on domestic producers. The Congressional Research Service reported in 2019 that an eligible product had to be imported directly from the beneficiary country and that at least 35 percent of its appraised value had to consist of the growth, product, or manufacture of that country, counting materials produced there and the direct costs of processing performed there. By the final full years before the June 1, 2020 vantage of the Trade Act guide, the program covered more than 3,500 products for all beneficiaries and roughly 1,500 additional products for least developed beneficiaries, figures the Congressional Research Service reported for 2018 and 2019.

Eligibility was never a matter of poverty alone, because the statute wrote a set of mandatory bars into section 502, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2462. Certain countries were excluded by category. The other major developed countries named in the statute could not be beneficiaries, and a communist country could be designated only if its products received nondiscriminatory treatment, if it belonged to the International Monetary Fund and to the World Trade Organization or its predecessor framework, and if it was not dominated or controlled by international communism, conditions the Congressional Research Service summarized in 2019. A country that joined with other countries to withhold supplies from international trade or to raise prices in a manner that caused serious disruption to the world economy was likewise barred, as was a country that granted preferential treatment to the products of another developed country in a manner likely to have a significant adverse effect on American commerce. The remaining bars addressed conduct toward American property and American persons. A country that had nationalized, expropriated, or otherwise seized property owned by an American citizen or by a corporation, partnership, or association at least 50 percent owned by American citizens could not be designated unless the President determined that prompt, adequate, and effective compensation had been or was being paid, that good faith negotiations toward such compensation were in progress, or that the compensation dispute had been submitted to arbitration, and furnished that determination to Congress. A country that failed to act in good faith in recognizing or enforcing arbitral awards in favor of American citizens fell under a parallel bar, as did a country that aided or abetted international terrorism by granting sanctuary from prosecution to an individual or group that had committed an act of international terrorism.

Two further mandatory criteria converted the preference into a lever for labor standards. A beneficiary had to have taken or be taking steps to afford internationally recognized worker rights, a term the statute defined to include freedom of association, the right to organize and bargain collectively, freedom from compulsory labor, a minimum age for the employment of children, and acceptable conditions of work on minimum wages, hours, and occupational safety and health. A beneficiary also had to have implemented its commitments to eliminate the worst forms of child labor. For several criteria, including the expropriation, arbitral award, terrorism, worker rights, and child labor provisions, the statute preserved a presidential escape route, because the bar did not prevent designation if the President determined that designation would be in the national economic interest and reported that determination, with reasons, to Congress. Alongside the mandatory bars stood discretionary factors the President was directed to take into account, among them the expressed desire of the country to be designated, its level of economic development, whether other developed countries extended similar treatment to it, the extent to which it had assured the United States that it would provide equitable and reasonable access to its markets, the extent to which it provided adequate and effective protection of intellectual property rights, and the extent to which it had reduced trade-distorting investment practices. The Congressional Research Service recorded in 2019 that reviews of country practices turned most often on worker rights, intellectual property protection, and market access.

The product side of the title carried the protective half of the bargain. Section 503, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2463, forbade the designation of import-sensitive articles, and the categories named in the statute were the categories in which American import-competing industries had organized most effectively during the postwar decades. The Congressional Research Service, in its 2019 summary of the provision, listed most textile and apparel goods, watches, and some electronic, steel, and glass products among the articles specifically prohibited from preference treatment, and the statutory list also covered footwear, handbags, luggage, flat goods, work gloves, and leather wearing apparel, subject to narrow exceptions that Congress added over time. The exclusions meant that the preference reached furthest where domestic sensitivity was lowest, a pattern that limited the value of the program to beneficiaries whose export strength lay in textiles or footwear. An article also lost eligibility for any period in which it was the subject of an escape clause action or a national security action, so that import relief and preference could not run over the same goods at the same time.

A second product control operated country by country rather than by category. The competitive need limitations in section 504 required the President to terminate duty-free treatment for an article from a particular beneficiary once imports of that article from that beneficiary crossed either of two thresholds in a calendar year, a specified dollar value that rose by five million dollars each year, reaching 175 million dollars in 2016 on the figures the Congressional Research Service reported, or a share equal to 50 percent or more of total American imports of the article. Termination took effect on July 1 of the following calendar year. The logic of the limitation was built into the development rationale of the program, because a supplier able to capture half of the American market for a product had demonstrated a competitiveness that no longer needed a preference margin. The statute softened the rule with waivers. The President could waive a limitation after receiving the advice of the International Trade Commission, upon a determination that the waiver served the national economic interest, and waivers were also available for articles not produced in the United States and, on a de minimis basis, where imports from the beneficiary accounted for a small share of total imports. Least developed beneficiaries stood outside the limitation altogether. Amendments in 2006 directed the President to revoke waivers that had run for five years or more once imports exceeded one and one half times the dollar threshold, a tightening that reflected congressional suspicion that temporary waivers had hardened into permanent entitlements.

Graduation carried the same logic from the product to the country. The President could withdraw or limit preference treatment for a beneficiary whose level of economic development no longer justified it, and the statute required mandatory graduation for a country the President determined to be a high income country as defined by World Bank statistics. The Office of the United States Trade Representative recorded in 2006 that President Reagan had graduated Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan in 1989 and that President Clinton had graduated Malaysia in 1997, in each case on the statutory development criteria. The 1989 graduations removed four of the most successful exporters in the program at the height of congressional argument over trade deficits with East Asia, at the point where the preference system conceded its own success, because the economies removed had reached income levels at which Congress was unwilling to continue a one-way concession.

Nothing in the program was permanent, and the authorization history of Title V became a study in short grants and interrupted operation. Congress first authorized the program for ten years, to 1985, and the Congressional Research Service counted fourteen reauthorizations after the original grant, generally for periods of two to three years, with the extension enacted in the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2018, Public Law 115-141, running to December 31, 2020. The Office of the United States Trade Representative recorded that between 1993 and 2002 the program expired seven times, with lapses that varied between one and fifteen months, and the Congressional Research Service reported that the program lapsed before reauthorization in ten of the fourteen extensions. Congressional practice across those episodes was to renew retroactively to the date of expiration, so that importers could obtain refunds, without interest, of duties paid during a lapse. Retroactivity softened the commercial cost of each lapse without removing the planning cost, since exporters and importers contracted against a preference whose legal life might end before a shipment arrived. The repeated cycle also gave Congress a regular occasion to revisit the eligibility criteria, and several mandatory conditions entered the statute through renewal legislation rather than through the original 1974 text.

Title IV of the act, sections 401 to 410, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2431 and following, addressed a different relationship altogether, trade with non-market economy countries, and it made a single human freedom the price of ordinary commercial standing. Its centerpiece, section 402, titled "Freedom of emigration in East-West trade" and codified at 19 U.S.C. 2432, became known by the names of its two sponsors, Senator Henry M. Jackson of Washington and Representative Charles A. Vanik of Ohio. The immediate provocation was Soviet practice. In August 1972 the Soviet Union began assessing education reimbursement fees, widely called diploma taxes, on citizens seeking to emigrate to nonsocialist countries, fees geared to the level of education received and capable of reaching tens of thousands of dollars, as the Congressional Research Service recounted in its 2000 survey of the amendment. The fees applied in form to all prospective emigrants and fell in practice most heavily on Soviet Jews seeking to emigrate to Israel or the United States, at a moment when emigration applications from Soviet Jews had risen sharply after 1967. Jackson and Vanik proposed in October 1972, as the Council on Foreign Relations recounted in 2009, that the trade benefits Moscow sought under the bilateral trade agreement negotiated in that year, including most-favored-nation treatment and American credits, should be conditioned on an end to the obstruction of emigration, and the Soviet government suspended collection of the fees in late 1972 without conceding the principle behind the proposal.

The architecture that Congress enacted in Title IV translated that proposal into a standing rule. Section 402 provided that products of a non-market economy country were not eligible for nondiscriminatory treatment, and that such a country could not participate in any program of the United States government that extended credits or credit guarantees or investment guarantees, and could not conclude a commercial agreement under the title, if the President determined that the country denied its citizens the right and opportunity to emigrate, imposed more than a nominal tax on emigration or on the visas and documents required for emigration, or imposed a tax, levy, fine, fee, or other charge on a citizen as a consequence of the desire of that citizen to emigrate to the country of choice. A country that satisfied the emigration test could receive nondiscriminatory treatment only through the further steps the title prescribed, including a bilateral commercial agreement containing a reciprocal grant of that treatment. The statute then built two exits from full compliance. The President could report that a country was not in violation of the emigration requirements, a compliance determination that carried reporting duties and annual review. Alternatively, the President could waive the requirements upon determining that the waiver would substantially promote the objective of free emigration, a standard that asked not whether emigration was free but whether the concession of trade benefits would make emigration freer than refusal would. The structure of the title made both routes temporary in the same way: each required regular reporting to Congress, and the waiver route ran through an annual renewal process subject to a congressional joint resolution of disapproval, so that no grant under Title IV was settled for longer than a year at a time unless Congress removed the country from the title by legislation granting permanent status.

The first test of the architecture destroyed the bargain it had been written to govern. The United States and the Soviet Union had signed their trade agreement in October 1972, before the Trade Act passed, and the agreement depended for its American performance on congressional restoration of most-favored-nation treatment and on access to American credits. With Title IV enacted, the Soviet Union faced treatment conditioned on an emigration standard it rejected as interference in internal affairs, and a companion limitation associated with Senator Adlai Stevenson III capped American credits to the Soviet Union at 300 million dollars over four years and barred credits for Soviet oil and gas development, against Soviet hopes for credits on a far larger scale. The Soviet leadership repudiated the trade agreement in January 1975, and the agreement never took effect. The Soviet Union never received nondiscriminatory treatment under Title IV, and emigration from the Soviet Union rose and fell across the 1970s and 1980s under pressures in which the amendment was one factor among several. The episode fixed the reputation of the provision on both sides of the argument that followed it for decades, because one reading held that Congress had defended a principle at the cost of a commercial agreement of modest value, and the opposing reading held that Congress had sacrificed trade and diplomatic flexibility to a condition the target government could not accept.

### Did the emigration condition keep China under annual review for two decades?

Yes. China received waiver-based most-favored-nation treatment beginning on February 1, 1980, and the waiver required renewal each year. Renewals were routine through 1988, contested after the June 1989 crackdown in Beijing, and ended only when permanent normal trade relations took effect on January 1, 2002.

China entered the Title IV system through the normalization of relations between Washington and Beijing and the bilateral trade agreement that accompanied it. The first extension of waiver-based most-favored-nation treatment took effect on February 1, 1980, and renewal through the 1980s proceeded with little congressional resistance, because the strategic case for the relationship and the commercial case for access to the Chinese market pointed in the same direction. The character of the proceeding changed entirely after the suppression of demonstrations in Beijing in June 1989. From 1989 onward, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in its later summaries of the China case, renewal was debated every year, legislation to disapprove the waiver or to attach conditions beyond emigration was introduced in every cycle between 1989 and 1999, and the debate widened from the statutory emigration test to the whole range of Chinese conduct, including human rights, labor practices, weapons proliferation, and the bilateral trade balance. Every such effort failed, and renewal continued each year through 2001, but each cycle required the administration in office to defend the waiver in public.

The contest of 1993 and 1994 tested the leverage theory of the annual cycle in its purest form. President Bill Clinton, who had criticized President George H. W. Bush during the 1992 campaign for an accommodating posture toward Beijing, issued an executive order on May 28, 1993 that conditioned the 1994 renewal on Chinese progress on human rights requirements that went well beyond the emigration standard in section 402. President Bush, in the preceding years, had vetoed congressional legislation that sought to revoke Chinese treatment or to attach conditions to renewal, and the Clinton order moved the conditioning strategy from Congress into the executive. On May 26, 1994, President Clinton announced that the linkage policy had reached the end of its usefulness, renewed treatment for China, and separated, or delinked, future renewals from human rights conditions other than the statutory Jackson-Vanik requirement itself, concluding that retaining treatment, rather than threatening its removal, offered the best opportunity to lay the basis for long-term sustainable progress in human rights and for the advancement of other American interests with China. The sequence supplied each side of the later argument with its strongest exhibit, because the conditioning year had produced no measurable Chinese compliance with the stated requirements, and the delinking decision conceded that the threat of revocation, once made and not executed, lost force with repetition.

The named holders of the two positions repeated their arguments in the renewal debates of the later 1990s and in the permanent status debate of 2000. Representative Nancy Pelosi of California, who had worked on compromise conditioning measures after 1989, announced in June 1996 that Chinese conduct on trade, proliferation, and human rights had become so extreme that she would vote against renewal outright, and Pelosi argued across those debates that unconditional renewal surrendered the strongest American leverage over Beijing and rewarded a government whose barriers kept American exports to a small fraction of the flow in the opposite direction. Senator Max Baucus of Montana took the opposing position in the same period, favoring extension of treatment for China in public debate with Pelosi recorded by the National Press Club, on the ground that commerce and contact served American economic interests and offered a better prospect of change inside China than isolation would. President Clinton carried the engagement position into the permanent status legislation of 2000, arguing that the United States-China trade agreement of 1999 required China to open its markets in unprecedented ways while requiring no reciprocal tariff concessions from the United States. The leverage argument, as stated in Senate hearings on permanent status in 2000, answered that annual review remained the best leverage held by the United States over Chinese behavior on human rights and labor rights precisely because Beijing valued its removal, and that surrendering the review without extracting concessions on those subjects spent the leverage for nothing.

The legal end of the China cycle came through the World Trade Organization rather than through any finding on emigration. An annual, revocable grant of nondiscriminatory treatment sat uneasily with the obligations of WTO members to extend such treatment to one another unconditionally, so Chinese accession required Congress to remove China from the Title IV cycle in advance. Congress did so in the United States-China Relations Act of 2000, Public Law 106-286, which authorized the President to terminate the application of Title IV to China and to grant permanent normal trade relations once China acceded. China acceded to the World Trade Organization on December 11, 2001, and permanent normal trade relations took effect on January 1, 2002, closing a waiver record that had run from February 1, 1980 through the renewal of 2001. The dated record, rather than any rounded span of years, is the measure of the institution Congress built in 1974, because the significance of the China case lay in the regularity of the proceeding, an annual argument over the largest bilateral trade relationship in the American economy, generated by a statute written about emigration from a different country in a different decade.

Russia supplied the closing case on the Soviet side of the title. The Russian Federation received nondiscriminatory treatment from 1992 under a bilateral trade agreement and annual determinations, and Public Law 112-208 recorded in its findings that Russia had been found in full compliance with the freedom of emigration requirements of Title IV since 1994. Russian accession to the World Trade Organization in August 2012 presented the same legal problem that Chinese accession had presented in 2001, because continued subjection to an annual emigration review was incompatible with the treatment owed between WTO members. Congress answered with the Russia and Moldova Jackson-Vanik Repeal and Sergei Magnitsky Rule of Law Accountability Act of 2012, Public Law 112-208, signed on December 14, 2012, which authorized the President to determine that Title IV should no longer apply to Russia and to Moldova and to extend permanent normal trade relations to both. The same statute paired graduation with a new sanctions instrument, named for Sergei Magnitsky, the Russian accountant who had died in a Moscow prison in 2009 after exposing a large tax fraud, imposing visa bans and asset freezes on persons the statute made sanctionable for his death and for other gross violations of human rights. The pairing closed the emigration era in the form in which it had begun, with trade status and human rights legislated in a single measure, although the 2012 instrument operated through targeted sanctions against named individuals rather than a condition on a whole economy. What Title IV demonstrated, across the Soviet repudiation of 1975 and the China renewals of 1980 to 2001, was that a condition written into trade law does not retire when its original occasion passes, because each annual renewal recruits new grievances and new arguments, until a legislature decides that the condition itself has become the obstacle to the policy it prefers.

## Adjustment Assistance and the Older Toolkit

Adjustment assistance runs in the opposite direction from the authorities that surround it in the Trade Act of 1974. Fast track procedure, the unfair practices authority, safeguards, and preferences govern the terms on which goods cross the border, while adjustment assistance governs what the federal government owes to people and places on the domestic side of that border. Title II of the act, titled in 1974 as relief from injury caused by import competition, placed the worker, firm, and community programs in chapters 2 to 4, following the safeguard provisions of chapter 1. Worker assistance begins at section 221 and following, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2271 and following. Assistance to firms begins at section 251 and following. Assistance to communities begins at section 271 and following. Congress in the Trade Act of 1974 paired authority to negotiate lower barriers abroad with a domestic program intended to make the costs of lower barriers bearable at home, and the Congressional Research Service in 2015 described the resulting Trade Adjustment Assistance programs as first created in 1962 and substantially expanded in 1974. A promise to cushion displacement had existed on paper for more than a decade before 1974, drafted so narrowly that it was seldom kept.

The gateway to worker assistance under the 1974 act is certification, and certification is a group determination rather than an individual one. Under section 221, a group of workers, or a representative acting for such a group, petitions the Department of Labor for a determination that the group is eligible to apply for assistance. The Department of Labor investigates the petition. Certification follows where increased imports of articles like or directly competitive with articles produced by their firm contributed importantly to the total or partial separations, or the threat of separations, and to a decline in the sales or production of the firm or subdivision. The phrase contributed importantly carries the whole design. Imports need not be the sole cause of a layoff, and they need not outweigh every other cause combined, but a connection that is merely incidental does not suffice. Individual workers within a certified group then apply for benefits through state agencies cooperating with the Department of Labor. Workers outside a certified group receive nothing under the title, however severe their displacement, and workers displaced for reasons that an investigation attributes to technology or domestic competition rather than to imports fall outside the program altogether. The line the statute draws is a line about the cause of a job loss, not about the severity of its consequences.

### Did Adjustment Assistance Compensate Displaced Workers for Their Losses?

Certification opened access to income support, training, and allowances, but compensation was partial by design and by outcome. Benefits replaced part of lost wages for a limited period, while research on displaced workers recorded earnings losses lasting years after reemployment, often through lower wages in new jobs rather than unemployment alone.

The benefits that certification unlocked were built around the trade readjustment allowance. As enacted in the Trade Act of 1974, section 233 limited payment of trade readjustment allowances to 52 weeks, with up to 26 additional weeks available to a worker completing approved training and up to 26 additional weeks for a worker who had reached the age of 60 on or before the date of separation, subject to a ceiling of 78 weeks in all. The allowance operated in sequence with unemployment compensation rather than in place of it. A displaced worker first drew ordinary unemployment insurance, and the trade readjustment allowance extended income support beyond that point, a structure that the Department of Labor in program materials described as income support paid to eligible workers who had exhausted unemployment insurance. Training stood at the center of the benefit package, and the 1974 act added a training component that the original 1962 program had lacked in developed form, a change the Congressional Research Service in 2015 recorded in tracing the program from its 1962 origins through the 1974 expansion. Alongside training, the act provided a job search allowance reimbursing the costs of seeking work outside the commuting area and a relocation allowance reimbursing the costs of moving to take up work elsewhere. The Department of Labor grouped the benefits in exactly those categories in its description of the program for workers. Income support answered the immediate loss of a paycheck, training answered the loss of firm specific skills, and the search and relocation allowances answered the geographic fact that comparable work might exist only at a distance. Its payments ran for months, while the earnings losses associated with displacement, as later research documented, ran for years.

Assistance to firms under chapter 3 followed the same causal logic through a different administrator. Sections 251 to 264 of the Trade Act of 1974 set out the program for firms, and administration rested with the Economic Development Administration within the Department of Commerce, according to a Government Printing Office summary of the program prepared for Congress in 1998. A firm gained certification where the Secretary of Commerce determined that a significant number or proportion of its workers had been laid off or faced that threat, that its sales or production had decreased absolutely, and that increased imports of like or directly competitive articles contributed importantly to both. A certified firm then had to submit an adjustment proposal, and approval of that proposal unlocked the assistance itself. The benefit, particularly after amendments in 1986 under Public Law 99-272 eliminated the direct loan and loan guarantee benefits, consisted of technical assistance delivered through Trade Adjustment Assistance Centers staffed by specialists in business recovery. The Congressional Research Service, in a 2009 report on the firm program, described those centers as consultants specializing in turnarounds, helping certified firms devise strategies covering marketing, production efficiency, and debt restructuring, with the Economic Development Administration barred from covering more than 75 percent of the cost of an adjustment proposal.

Assistance to communities under chapter 4, beginning at section 271, extended the same reasoning from the worker and the firm to the locality. A community whose employment base rested on a trade injured industry faced losses that neither worker benefits nor firm advice could reach, including a shrinking tax base and the departure of suppliers that had depended on the injured employer. Chapter 4 therefore directed assistance toward communities as communities, supporting the planning and investment through which a locality could develop a broader economic base. In the later form documented by the Congressional Research Service in 2014, assistance to trade impacted communities ran through the Economic Development Administration as grants for strategy development and implementation projects, with implementation grants capped at five million dollars and barred from covering more than 95 percent of a project cost, and with eligible activities that included infrastructure improvement and the construction and equipping of facilities. The Government Accountability Office, reporting in 2012 on one time grants to trade impacted communities, found that some recipient communities might also have qualified under other Economic Development Administration programs, while others would not have met the eligibility or cost sharing terms of those programs.

The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 created adjustment assistance for workers and firms as part of the settlement that authorized the Kennedy Round negotiations, and an executive briefing published by the United States International Trade Commission in 2017 explained the failure of that first version in structural terms. Eligibility criteria were strict, the application process was burdensome, and a determination required a ruling by the United States Tariff Commission tying the injury to the trade concessions themselves. Applicants had to show not only that imports had injured them but that the injury flowed from the tariff reductions the government had granted, a chain of proof that separated the program from the ordinary experience of import competition. The Congressional Research Service in 2015 summarized the outcome plainly, recording that the first Trade Adjustment Assistance programs were enacted in 1962 but little used until the Trade Act of 1974 eased the eligibility requirements. The 1974 act attacked each element of the blockage. Administration of worker petitions moved from the Tariff Commission to the Department of Labor. The requirement of a separate injury finding tied to specific concessions disappeared, replaced by the contributed importantly test. Potential income support rose, and benefits expanded to cover retraining and job search expenses in developed form, changes the United States International Trade Commission briefing of 2017 enumerated in its account of the 1974 revision. The Congressional Research Service in 2015 reported that the number of certified workers rose from about 59,000 in fiscal year 1975 to nearly 600,000 in fiscal year 1980, an increase driven in part by the oil price shocks and recession at the end of that decade, which reduced demand for the large American automobiles around which a substantial share of certifications clustered. The liberalization bargain is visible in those figures. Open markets abroad had been purchased in 1962 with a promise that proved largely illusory in practice, and the 1974 Congress, seeking negotiating authority for the Tokyo Round, paid for the same bargain a second time by making the promise usable.

Later Congresses repeatedly contracted and restored what the 1974 Congress had expanded. The sharpest contraction came in the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981, Public Law 97-35. The Congressional Research Service in 2015 recorded that the 1981 act cut spending by reducing benefits and emphasizing training and other reemployment services, a response to the rapidly increasing program costs of the late 1970s. The summary of the 1981 act published by Congress recorded the mechanism in statutory detail, including a revision of worker eligibility that raised the causal threshold from imports that contributed importantly to imports that amounted to a substantial cause of a firm decline, together with revised requirements and amounts for trade readjustment allowances. Participation through the 1980s ran mostly well below the levels of the late 1970s, according to the Congressional Research Service in 2015. Restoration arrived in stages. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, reauthorized the program through fiscal year 1993, expanded eligibility for trade readjustment allowances, and made training a requirement of the program. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993, Public Law 103-66, reauthorized the program through 1998 with reductions in training funding, and the North American Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act of 1993, Public Law 103-182, added a dedicated component for workers whose job loss was attributable to trade with Canada or Mexico. The Trade Act of 2002, Public Law 107-210, merged that North American component into the general program, created a health coverage tax credit for covered workers, and introduced a wage supplement for reemployed older workers who took new jobs at lower pay, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in 2015. In February 2009, the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, Public Law 111-5, expanded the program again, extending eligibility to service sector and public sector workers, increasing training funding, and increasing the maximum number of weeks of trade readjustment allowances. After a period in which the program reverted toward its earlier limits, the Trade Adjustment Assistance Reauthorization Act of 2015, enacted as title IV of the Trade Preferences Extension Act of 2015, Public Law 114-27, restored the broader eligibility framework in a form modeled on the provisions in effect before January 1, 2014, as the Federal Register notice implementing that act in August 2015 described. The pattern across four decades is one of oscillation around the 1974 settlement rather than departure from it.

The measured record against which those oscillations played out comes from two research traditions that reached compatible conclusions by different routes. The first followed individual displaced workers through administrative earnings records. Louis Jacobson, Robert LaLonde, and Daniel Sullivan, in a study published in the American Economic Review in 1993, used Pennsylvania administrative data combining worker earnings histories with information about their firms and found that high tenure workers separating from distressed firms suffered long term earnings losses averaging 25 percent per year. The same 1993 study found that losses began mounting before separation, that losses were large even for workers who found new jobs in similar firms, and that losses were not confined to a few sectors, findings that undermined any account in which displacement costs were brief. Lori Kletzer, in Job Loss from Imports, published in 2001, turned the lens specifically on import competing displacement, using the Displaced Worker Surveys. Kletzer in 2001 found that two thirds of reemployed import competing workers earned less on the new job than on the old, that one quarter experienced earnings losses exceeding 30 percent, and that the average earnings loss among the reemployed was 13 percent, with older, more tenured, and less formally educated production workers bearing the heaviest losses. The second tradition followed places. David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson, in a study published in the American Economic Review in 2013, compared American commuting zones according to their exposure to rising imports from China between 1990 and 2007 and found that more exposed local labor markets experienced larger reductions in manufacturing employment, increased unemployment, decreased labor force participation, reduced wages, and increased receipt of government transfers. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson in 2013 also found that import exposure predicted increased Trade Adjustment Assistance payments, but that the estimated dollar increase in per capita disability payments in affected regions was more than thirty times as large as the estimated dollar increase in Trade Adjustment Assistance payments, a comparison that locates adjustment assistance as a small instrument pointed at a large loss. Evaluations of the program itself point in two directions, largely through a difference of time horizon. Peter Schochet and colleagues at Mathematica Policy Research, in a Department of Labor sponsored evaluation published in 2012, compared participants under the 2002 amendments with matched unemployment insurance claimants and found that participants earned less than the comparison group over a four year follow up period, a result consistent with workers spending part of that period in training rather than in employment. Benjamin Hyman, in a 2018 study using quasi random assignment of petitions to investigators of differing leniency across some 300,000 displaced workers, found that workers approved for assistance had 50,000 dollars more in cumulative earnings ten years out than comparable workers who were denied, driven by higher incomes and greater labor force participation, while also finding that the annual returns fully depreciated across the same ten year period. Read together, the traditions converge. Displacement from import competing employment imposed earnings losses that were large, concentrated on workers with the most tenure to lose, and measured in years rather than in the weeks that income support covered, and assistance improved the trajectory of workers who received it without restoring the earnings path that displacement had destroyed.

Much of the toolkit that the Trade Act of 1974 assembled was older than the act itself, and one prominent instrument commonly credited to it does not belong to it at all. The escape clause lineage reaches back before the 1974 act by a generation, to trade agreements of the 1940s containing safeguard mechanisms for producers harmed by liberalization even where foreign exporters traded fairly. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade of 1947 carried the principle in article XIX, permitting a party to suspend or modify a concession where increased imports caused or threatened serious injury to domestic producers of like or directly competitive products. The Trade Expansion Act of 1962 carried a safeguard authority of this character in American law, and the Trade Act of 1974 recast that authority as section 201, which the Congressional Research Service in 2018 described as authorizing temporary import restrictions to facilitate positive adjustment by an injured domestic industry. Adjustment assistance itself was a 1962 creation that the 1974 act repaired rather than invented. The 1974 Congress worked with instruments that earlier trade statutes had already built, sharpened some, broadened others, and gathered them into a single act alongside genuinely new devices such as fast track procedure and the generalized system of preferences.

Above all, section 232 belongs in the older column, and the point requires emphasis because the misattribution recurs. Section 232 is the national security authority. It permits the President to adjust imports that the Secretary of Commerce finds are being imported in such quantities or under such circumstances as to threaten to impair the national security. Section 232 is a provision of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862. It is not a provision of the Trade Act of 1974, it does not appear in that act, and no authority exercised under section 232 derives from Public Law 93-618. The Congressional Research Service, in its 2018 overview of section 232 investigations, traced the lineage further back, to the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1954, which first barred duty reductions that might threaten domestic production needed for national defense, through amendments in 1955 and an expansion in the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1958 that set out investigative factors and petition rights, before section 232 of the 1962 act continued the 1958 provisions. The age of section 232, its investigative home in the Commerce Department, and its freedom from any requirement of unfair foreign practice or serious injury all follow from its separate statutory origin, and attributing it to 1974 collapses two different congressional settlements into one.

A belief that unilateral tariff authority in American law is a recent development does not survive contact with the statute book. The authorities date from 1962 and 1974. The national security authority dates from 1962, with antecedents in 1954. The safeguard and adjustment authorities descend from the escape clause practice of the 1940s through the 1962 act. The unfair practices authority of section 301 dates from 1974. Nor were those authorities dormant curiosities in their first decades. The fact record assembled for this article documents section 301 cases through the 1980s, the semiconductor arrangement of 1986 and sanctions against Japan in 1987 that grew out of that practice, and safeguard grants in the 1970s and 1980s including the increased tariffs on heavyweight motorcycles granted in 1983. Section 232 had a comparable early career. The Congressional Research Service, in its 2018 overview summarizing Commerce Department reporting, recorded that from 1962 until the establishment of the World Trade Organization in 1995 the United States initiated 24 section 232 investigations or related actions, found national security threats in eight of those cases, seven involving petroleum and one involving machine tools, and took action against some petroleum imports through embargoes and fees while negotiating voluntary export restraints in the machine tools case. What changed was not the law but the practice under it. After the WTO agreements of 1995 channeled enforcement into multilateral dispute settlement, the older unilateral instruments fell into relative disuse. The Congressional Research Service in 2018 recorded only two section 232 investigations between 1995 and 2017, one concerning petroleum in 1999, in which Commerce found a threat and the President took no action, and one concerning iron ore and semi finished steel in 2001, in which Commerce found no security threat. The revival that followed, visible in the steel and aluminum tariffs applied effective March 23, 2018 at rates of 25 percent and 10 percent respectively under section 232 and in the safeguard tariffs on solar cells and washing machines proclaimed in January 2018 under section 201, was therefore a change in the choices made under standing law rather than a change in the law itself. The statute book did not move. Successive administrations between 1995 and 2016 largely declined to reach for instruments that remained on the shelf, and the administration that took office in 2017 reached for them. Any account that dates the authorities from their revival misstates where responsibility lies, which is with executive choice inside delegations granted a working lifetime earlier.

The act sits in title 19 of the United States Code, where the Trade Act of 1974 occupies chapter 12 and its provisions run from 19 U.S.C. 2101 onward. Its amendment history in outline is a history of the toolkit being renewed piece by piece rather than replaced, in the statutes of 1979, 1984, 1988, 2002, and 2015. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 implemented the Tokyo Round agreements and renewed the negotiating authority first granted in 1974. The Trade and Tariff Act of 1984 revised the unfair practices authority by adding discriminatory practices to its reach, extended coverage toward services, investment, and intellectual property, and reauthorized the generalized system of preferences, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in its 2017 overview of that preference program. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 added the Super 301 procedure, transferred the section 301 authority to the United States Trade Representative, made training a requirement within adjustment assistance, and renewed negotiating authority for the Uruguay Round. The Trade Act of 2002 renewed negotiating authority again, merged the North American adjustment program into the general program, and added the health coverage tax credit and the wage supplement for older workers. The statutes of 2015 completed the outline, with the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, Public Law 114-26, renewing negotiating authority, and the Trade Preferences Extension Act of 2015, Public Law 114-27, reauthorizing adjustment assistance and extending preference programs. Each of those statutes renewed the question that the 1962 Congress first tried and failed to answer, namely what the government owes to the workers, firms, and communities whose losses are the domestic price of gains that the statute book records elsewhere.

## One Statute, Every Tool

A pillar article earns that standing by holding a cluster together, and the Trade Act of 1974 guide performs that function for the trade cluster of the legislation series. Each instrument in the 1974 package generated a later history dense enough to sustain a separate study, and each separate study returns to the 1974 text for the operative standard. The annual waiver cycle for China under Title IV ended through the legislation examined in [permanent normal trade relations for China](/2020/07/15/china-pntr-2000-guide/), yet the meaning of that ending depends on the emigration condition that the 1974 Congress wrote and that Presidents renewed year by year from 1980. The wider shelf of postwar enactments surveyed in [United States trade legislation since 1950](/2020/10/15/us-trade-legislation-since-1950/) places the 1974 statute between the delegation model of the nineteen thirties and the implementation statutes of the nineteen nineties, as the point at which negotiation, retaliation, relief, preference and assistance were first gathered under one set of congressional controls. A reader who commands the 1974 text holds the reference point against which the surrounding studies measure change.

The counter-reading deserves closing form at full strength. Nothing in the prominent unilateral actions of 2017 and 2018 required new statutory authority, because the authorities in question were old. The national security authority dates from the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, with antecedents in the statutes of the nineteen fifties. The safeguard and adjustment instruments descend from the escape clause practice of the nineteen forties through the 1962 act. The unfair practices authority dates from 1974, and the Congresses of 1984 and 1988 widened and regularized that authority rather than creating the category. Nor did the authorities sleep between enactment and revival. Section 301 investigations ran through the nineteen eighties, produced the semiconductor arrangement of 1986 and sanctions against Japan in 1987, and Presidents granted safeguards in the nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, including increased tariffs on heavyweight motorcycles in 1983. The agreements that established the World Trade Organization in 1995 channeled agreement based disputes into multilateral settlement, and the older instruments fell into relative disuse for two decades under administrations of both parties. The revival that followed was therefore a change in practice rather than in law. The authorities are old, and their use is a choice, made by an administration inside delegations that earlier Congresses granted and later Congresses left in place.

Endurance took a different form for each instrument, and the differences confirm the design. Fast track expired and returned in discrete grants, in 1979, 1988, 2002 and 2015, with lapses between grants during which negotiation continued without the advance assurance on amendment and timing. Section 301 moved through three phases on one continuous text, frequent investigation and occasional sanction from 1975 to 1994, multilateral channeling from 1995, and renewed unilateral tariff action from 2017. Safeguards arrived at long intervals, in steel in 2002 and in solar cells and washing machines in 2018, each grant bounded by declining relief and a statutory ceiling. The preference program survived through repeated short authorizations and retroactive renewals, never acquiring the permanence that the development rationale invited. Title IV outlasted the Soviet occasion that produced the title, governed the largest bilateral relationship in the American economy through the China waiver cycles, and closed for Russia only in December 2012, in a statute that paired graduation with targeted sanctions against named individuals. Adjustment assistance oscillated between contraction and restoration, in 1981, 1988, 2002, 2009 and 2015, around the settlement that the 1974 expansion had set.

The research trail left by one statute across five decades rewards an ordered record. A determination under section 301 cites the standard as enacted in 1974 and as amended in 1984 and 1988. A safeguard file joins a Commission injury report to a presidential decision and a declining schedule of relief. A waiver file for China joins an annual presidential determination to a congressional disapproval debate that recurred from 1980 through 2001. Keeping those trails straight across titles, amendments and codifications in title 19 of the United States Code is the practical burden that the statute imposes on every serious reader, and a researcher can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) while tracing each authority from enactment to a dated use. The discipline that the fact record demands is the discipline that the 93rd Congress built into the instruments themselves, namely dated grants, stated findings, named deciders and relief that expires unless a later decision renews the grant.

The statute that resulted from that discipline settled a question that the postwar decades had left open, namely whether a legislature could authorize open trade and lawful pressure in the same enactment without surrendering control of either. The answer given in December 1974 and signed into law on January 3, 1975 was a conditional yes. Negotiation proceeded under objectives and consultation that Congress wrote. Retaliation proceeded under findings and timetables that Congress wrote. Relief proceeded under an injury test that Congress wrote, for a term that Congress limited, toward an adjustment that Congress defined. Preference and emigration policy proceeded under eligibility conditions that Congress wrote and renewed. One statute, every tool, remains the shortest accurate description of Public Law 93-618, because the tools share one text, one allocation of institutional roles, and one theory, that openness and pressure are both legitimate only inside rules stated in advance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Trade Act of 1974 create?

The Trade Act of 1974 created a combined system of trade instruments inside one statute, Public Law 93-618. Title I created fast track, a procedure under which Congress commits in advance to consider an implementing bill without amendment and under time and debate limits. Title II created the safeguard mechanism at section 201 and expanded trade adjustment assistance for workers, firms and communities. Title III created the unfair practices authority at section 301. Title IV created a framework for trade with non-market economy countries, centered on the emigration condition at section 402. Title V created the Generalized System of Preferences for designated developing countries. The creation was a package, designed so that negotiation, relief, retaliation, preference and assistance operated as parts of one settlement.

### Q: Which president signed the Trade Act of 1974?

President Gerald Ford signed the Trade Act of 1974 on January 3, 1975. Congress had passed the measure in December 1974, and the difference between the passage year and the signing year explains the name of the statute, because the legislative enterprise belonged to 1974 while the authorities became available for use only at the signing in 1975. The bill had entered Congress as H.R. 10710 in the 93rd Congress, at the request of the administration of President Richard Nixon in 1973, and the measure crossed a change of presidents during Senate consideration, since President Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974 and President Ford succeeded to the presidency on that day. The enacted text is Public Law 93-618, printed at 88 Stat. 1978.

### Q: What is section 301 in the Trade Act of 1974?

Section 301 is the unfair practices authority of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2411 within sections 301 to 310 of Title III, titled Relief from Unfair Trade Practices. As enacted in 1974, the provision authorized the President to act against a foreign act, policy or practice that was unjustifiable or unreasonable and that burdened or restricted United States commerce. The Trade and Tariff Act of 1984 added the term discriminatory, defined the operative terms, and extended coverage to services, investment and intellectual property. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 transferred the authority to the United States Trade Representative and added the Super 301 identification procedure. Investigation rests with the Trade Representative, and a finding of unfairness is required before action follows.

### Q: What is section 201 in the Trade Act of 1974?

Section 201 is the safeguard authority of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2251 within chapter 1 of Title II, titled Relief from Injury Caused by Import Competition. Relief becomes available where an article is imported in such increased quantities as to be a substantial cause of serious injury, or threat of serious injury, to the domestic industry producing a like or directly competitive article. No finding of unfair foreign practice is required, and that absence is the distinguishing feature of the instrument. The International Trade Commission investigates injury, and the President decides relief, which may take the form of duties, quotas or adjustment assistance for an initial period of up to four years, extendable after further proceedings. Documented grants include heavyweight motorcycles in 1983, steel in 2002 and solar cells and washing machines in 2018.

### Q: What is the Jackson-Vanik amendment in the Trade Act of 1974?

The Jackson-Vanik amendment is section 402 of the Trade Act of 1974, titled Freedom of emigration in East-West trade and codified at 19 U.S.C. 2432, named for Senator Henry Jackson and Representative Charles Vanik. Within Title IV, covering sections 401 to 410, the provision bars nondiscriminatory treatment, credits and credit guarantees for a non-market economy country that denies citizens freedom of emigration. A presidential waiver can suspend the bar, but the waiver requires annual renewal and remains subject to congressional disapproval. The provision was adopted with the Soviet Union in mind, after the Soviet government imposed education fees on citizens seeking to emigrate in 1972. The Soviet Union never received the barred treatment under the title, and the provision later governed waiver based treatment for China from 1980 through 2001 and for Russia until graduation in December 2012.

### Q: Did the Trade Act of 1974 create fast track?

Fast track was created by the Trade Act of 1974, at sections 151 to 154, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194. The procedure is a precommitment by Congress to consider an implementing bill for a trade agreement without amendment, under time limits, and with debate limits that exclude the filibuster. The commitment applies provided the executive meets the consultation requirements and negotiating objectives of the statute, with objectives at section 102 and authority to enter agreements at section 101. The design answered the failure of the proclamation model during the Kennedy Round, when agreements on nontariff barriers required changes in domestic law that Congress declined to enact after negotiation. The authority was granted for defined periods rather than permanently, and later Congresses renewed the grant in 1979, 1988, 2002 and 2015, with lapses between grants.

### Q: What is trade adjustment assistance in the Trade Act of 1974?

Trade adjustment assistance is the domestic assistance program of the Trade Act of 1974, carried in chapters 2 to 4 of Title II. Worker assistance begins at sections 221 and following, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2271 and following, firm assistance at section 251 and following, and community assistance at section 271 and following. The program provides income support, training and related assistance for workers certified as displaced by imports. The 1974 act substantially expanded a program first created by the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, under which eligibility had been drawn so strictly that few workers qualified. The expansion formed the domestic side of the liberalization bargain of 1974, pairing authority to negotiate lower barriers abroad with usable assistance for the workers, firms and communities that bore the concentrated costs of import competition at home.

### Q: What is the public law number of the Trade Act of 1974?

The public law number of the Trade Act of 1974 is Public Law 93-618, denoting the 618th public law enacted by the 93rd Congress. The statute is printed at 88 Stat. 1978, and the bill that carried the measure was H.R. 10710. Congress passed the bill in December 1974 and President Gerald Ford signed the measure on January 3, 1975, so citation by public law number avoids the ambiguity created by the difference between the name year and the signing year. Provisions of the act are codified across title 19 of the United States Code, including fast track at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194, section 301 at 19 U.S.C. 2411, section 201 at 19 U.S.C. 2251, the emigration condition at 19 U.S.C. 2432, preferences at 19 U.S.C. 2461 and adjustment assistance at 19 U.S.C. 2271 and following.

### Q: What is the difference between the safeguard authority and the unfair practices authority?

The difference between the safeguard authority and the unfair practices authority lies in the finding that opens each proceeding and in the institution that investigates. A safeguard case under section 201 requires increased imports, serious injury to a domestic industry, and a causal link stated as substantial cause, and no foreign wrongdoing of any kind is required, because the imports may be fairly traded in every respect. The International Trade Commission investigates and the President decides relief. An unfair practices case under section 301 requires a foreign act, policy or practice that is unjustifiable or unreasonable, with discriminatory added in 1984, and that burdens or restricts United States commerce, and no showing of serious domestic injury in the safeguard sense is required. The United States Trade Representative investigates and determines. One instrument answers injury, and the other answers conduct.

### Q: Does the Trade Act contain the national security import authority?

The Trade Act of 1974 does not contain the national security import authority. That authority is section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, codified at 19 U.S.C. 1862, and no provision of Public Law 93-618 grants the authority or supplies the standard under which the authority operates. Attribution matters because the two statutes embody different congressional settlements, separated by twelve years, different committees of origin in different political conditions, and different tests, since the 1962 authority turns on a finding about national security rather than on injury, unfairness, emigration or eligibility under the 1974 titles. The safeguard lineage and adjustment assistance also begin in the 1962 statute, which the 1974 act recast and expanded, but section 232 received no corresponding treatment in 1974 and remained entirely a creature of the earlier enactment and of amendments to that enactment.

### Q: How long does fast track authority last?

Fast track authority lasts for the defined period stated in the grant that creates the authority, and no grant has been permanent. The original grant in the Trade Act of 1974 covered agreements entered into during the five years after enactment, a span running from the signing on January 3, 1975. Expiration was built into the delegation, so a decision by Congress to take no action produced a lapse rather than a continuation, and the burden of persuasion at expiration rested on supporters of renewal. Later grants followed the same pattern in the renewal statutes of 1979, 1988, 2002 and 2015, each running for a stated period and each separated from the next grant by a lapse. During a lapse, negotiation remained possible under ordinary constitutional authority, but an implementing bill received no advance assurance on timing, amendment or debate limits.

### Q: What does the Generalized System of Preferences do?

The Generalized System of Preferences, created by Title V of the Trade Act of 1974 at sections 501 to 505, with section 501 codified at 19 U.S.C. 2461, authorizes the President to extend duty free treatment to eligible articles from designated beneficiary developing countries. The program departs from reciprocity, because designation asks nothing in tariff concessions in return, and eligibility depends on conditions and exclusions written into the title rather than on a negotiated bargain. Conditions address matters that include worker rights, protection of American property against uncompensated expropriation, and cooperation against terrorism, and exclusions remove import sensitive articles from preference treatment. The program was authorized for a stated period at enactment and was renewed repeatedly by later Congresses, with renewals often enacted after a lapse and applied retroactively, so the preference operated as a standing policy delivered through a sequence of temporary grants.

### Q: How does a safeguard investigation proceed?

A safeguard investigation proceeds in two stages, divided between an injury investigation and a relief decision. The proceeding opens with a petition or a request that brings a defined domestic industry before the International Trade Commission, and the petitioner presents a plan for positive adjustment to import competition. The Commission investigates whether an article is imported in such increased quantities as to be a substantial cause of serious injury, or threat of serious injury, to the domestic industry, weighing import volumes against idled capacity, production, sales, market share, profits and employment. An affirmative determination sends the case to the President with a Commission recommendation on remedy. The President then decides whether relief serves the national economic interest, and relief, where granted, takes forms that include increased duties, quotas or adjustment assistance, for an initial period of up to four years, extendable after a further proceeding on continued necessity and adjustment.

### Q: What happens after an unfair practices investigation finds a violation?

After an investigation under section 301 produces an affirmative determination, the consequence depends on the character of the finding, because the statute divides responses into a mandatory track and a discretionary track. Action is mandatory where the United States Trade Representative determines that rights of the United States under a trade agreement are denied, or that a foreign act, policy or practice is unjustifiable and burdens or restricts United States commerce, subject to limited statutory exceptions. Action is discretionary where the determination rests on a practice found unreasonable or discriminatory, and the Trade Representative decides whether action is appropriate and what form action should take. Determinations follow investigation and consultation with the foreign government concerned, and action, where taken, is directed at elimination of the practice or at compensation through trade concessions, with monitoring of compliance and review of any action taken.

### Q: Why did the Soviet Union never receive trade benefits under the emigration title?

The Soviet Union never received trade benefits under Title IV because the benefits depended on satisfaction of an emigration standard that the Soviet government rejected as interference in internal affairs. Title IV barred nondiscriminatory treatment, credits and credit guarantees for a non-market economy country that denied citizens freedom of emigration, subject to a compliance determination or an annually renewed presidential waiver. The United States and the Soviet Union had signed a trade agreement in October 1972 that depended on congressional restoration of nondiscriminatory treatment and access to credits, and the enactment of Title IV in the Trade Act of 1974 conditioned both benefits on the emigration test. A companion limitation capped American credits at a level far beneath Soviet expectations. The Soviet leadership repudiated the 1972 agreement in January 1975, the agreement never took effect, and no waiver or compliance route delivered the barred treatment to the Soviet Union at any point under the title.

### Q: How do workers qualify for adjustment assistance?

Workers qualify for adjustment assistance through group certification rather than through individual proof of hardship. A group of workers, or a representative acting for a group, petitions the Department of Labor, and certification follows where the investigation establishes that increased imports contributed importantly to separations from employment and to a decline in the sales or production of the firm or subdivision that employed the group. Workers inside a certified group may then apply for benefits through state agencies, while workers outside a certified group receive nothing under the title, and displacement attributed to technology or domestic competition rather than to imports falls outside the program. Benefits for certified workers center on income support through trade readjustment allowances, training, and allowances for job search and relocation. The contributed importantly standard of the 1974 act replaced the stricter showing required under the 1962 program, under which few workers had qualified.

### Q: What negotiating objectives did Congress write into the act?

Congress wrote negotiating objectives into section 102 of the Trade Act of 1974, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2112, alongside the authority to enter agreements granted at section 101, codified at 19 U.S.C. 2111. The objectives function as direction and as condition at the same time. As direction, the objectives state the ends that an acceptable agreement must pursue, across the subjects that a negotiation covers. As condition, the objectives form part of the bargain that earns an implementing bill expedited consideration under sections 151 to 154, because the commitment to vote without amendment and under debate limits rests on negotiation conducted inside directions that Congress laid down in advance. Consultation requirements accompany the objectives, obliging the executive to consult congressional committees before and during negotiation. An agreement reached outside the stated objectives and consultation structure weakens the claim of the resulting bill to fast track treatment and exposes the bill to ordinary legislative procedure.

### Q: How has the act been amended since enactment?

Amendment proceeded piece by piece, with later Congresses revising single instruments inside the 1974 architecture rather than replacing the architecture. The Trade Agreements Act of 1979 implemented the Tokyo Round agreements and renewed negotiating authority. The Trade and Tariff Act of 1984, Public Law 98-573, added discriminatory to the section 301 standard, defined the operative terms, and extended coverage to services, investment and intellectual property. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988, Public Law 100-418, transferred section 301 authority from the President to the United States Trade Representative, divided responses into mandatory and discretionary tracks, and added the Super 301 identification procedure. Negotiating authority returned in the grants of 2002 and 2015. Title IV closed for China through permanent normal trade relations under Public Law 106-286, effective January 1, 2002, and for Russia through Public Law 112-208 in December 2012. The preference program of Title V was renewed repeatedly across the same period.

### Q: Does the Trade Act remain in force?

The record through June 1, 2020 shows the Trade Act of 1974 operating as the governing text for the authorities the act created, in codified and amended form. Provisions of the act stand in title 19 of the United States Code, and the instruments produced dated exercises across the whole period, from safeguard grants in 1983 and section 301 sanctions in 1987 to steel safeguards in 2002, China waiver renewals through 2001, graduation of Russia in December 2012, negotiating grants in 2002 and 2015, and safeguard grants on solar cells and washing machines in January 2018. Amendment altered parts of the original design, including the transfer of section 301 authority to the United States Trade Representative in 1988, and time limited grants inside the act lapsed and returned by later enactment, but no repeal displaced the 1974 statute as the source of fast track, section 301, section 201, Title IV, Title V and adjustment assistance.

### Q: How is the act cited in research?

Research citation begins with the enacted identity of the statute and then descends to the provision under discussion. The full citation is Trade Act of 1974, Public Law 93-618, 88 Stat. 1978, enacted by the 93rd Congress on the bill H.R. 10710, passed in December 1974 and signed on January 3, 1975. Codified citations follow the title 19 arrangement of the United States Code, so fast track is cited at 19 U.S.C. 2191 to 2194, section 301 at 19 U.S.C. 2411, section 201 at 19 U.S.C. 2251, the Jackson-Vanik provision at 19 U.S.C. 2432, the preference authority at 19 U.S.C. 2461 and adjustment assistance at 19 U.S.C. 2271 and following. Amendment citations add the amending statute, as with the changes of 1984 in Public Law 98-573 and of 1988 in Public Law 100-418, and a careful citation distinguishes the text as enacted in 1974 from the text as amended, since standards, deciders and procedures changed between the two states of the text.
