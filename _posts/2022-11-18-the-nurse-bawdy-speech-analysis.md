## Romeo and Juliet in Act 1 Scene 3: A Question About Age That Becomes a Life Story

The Nurse's speech in Act 1 Scene 3 of Romeo and Juliet begins with the plainest question in the scene and refuses, for dozens of lines, to answer it plainly. Lady Capulet has asked how old Juliet is, and the woman who has known the girl since birth responds not with a number but with a memory, then another memory inside the first, then a dead child, an earthquake, a sore breast, a bumped forehead, and a dead husband's dirty joke, told twice. By the time the arithmetic finally arrives, the audience has received something far larger than an age. It has received a whole biography, delivered in prose, by the only person on stage who remembers Juliet before Juliet could remember anything herself. The speech is the first great set piece of the tragedy, and its apparent disorder is the subject of this close reading.

![The Nurse telling her Act 1 Scene 3 reminiscence of weaning, earthquake and Susan - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-60.webp)

The verdict can be stated at the outset. The rambling structure of the reminiscence is not a failure of construction, in the character or in the dramatist. It is the construction. Every digression performs a task that a tidy answer could not perform. The weaning story establishes that this woman's body, and not the mother's, was the child's first home. The earthquake fixes that private history to a public event and gives the speech its strange timekeeping. Susan, named once and buried inside a parenthesis of blessing, explains why another woman's daughter received the whole of a mother's stored affection. The husband's joke plants, inside the play's warmest laughter, the equation between falling and desire that the tragedy will keep solving until the tomb. Apparent disorder is the method. What looks like wandering is a mind moving the way memory moves, by association and return, and Shakespeare trusts the audience to feel the coherence before it can name it.

That trust is the gamble of the scene, and it pays because the speech obeys a hidden discipline. Each loop of the reminiscence comes back to the same few objects: a breast, a child, a fall, a date. The loops widen and tighten. The first circuit is bodily, the taste of wormwood on the dug. The second is chronological, the earthquake eleven years before the scene. The third is mortal, Susan dead and the husband dead, both carried in asides. The fourth is prophetic, the joke about falling backward, which reaches forward across the whole action. A reader who maps those circuits possesses the speech. A reader who treats the passage as comic padding possesses only its reputation. The pages that follow take the circuits one by one, quote the wording from the Arden third series text edited by Rene Weis, and test the speech against the critics who have taken it seriously and the editors who have quarreled over its most famous line.

## Where the Speech Sits in Act 1 Scene 3

The scene is a business scene dressed as an intimate one. Lady Capulet has come to her daughter's chamber with a proposal to deliver, the offer of Count Paris, and she needs the girl's age confirmed before the negotiation opens because the marriage market runs on such figures. The older woman enters with the servant she calls by the single word Nurse, attempts a private conversation, then reverses herself and summons the attendant back into the room, a small choreography of rank that tells the audience the household cannot actually transact its most private business without the woman who raised the child. That dependence is the soil the speech grows in. The whole-character study of this figure, her comedy, her class position, and her later failure, is a separate enquiry; readers who want the full arc can follow [the complete character study of the Nurse as comedian, servant, and betrayer](/2022/11/11/the-nurse-character-analysis/) and return here for the single speech that builds her.

Within the scene, the reminiscence occupies a precise structural slot. It comes before the marriage proposal, and it delays it. Dramatically, the delay does three kinds of work at once. It establishes the emotional facts against which the proposal will be judged, because an audience that has just heard how this girl was weaned, hurt, comforted, and joked over hears the Paris offer with different ears. It establishes the generational facts, because the speech is saturated with a past that Lady Capulet barely shares; the mother must ask the servant how old her own daughter is, and that single need shapes everything the scene later claims about maternal authority. And it establishes the tonal facts, because the tragedy opens its domestic register here, in laughter with grief inside it, long before Mercutio makes wit itself turn mortal in the street.

The little ceremony that precedes the speech deserves its own attention, since it rehearses the scene's whole argument in gesture. Lady Capulet first sends the attendant out, intending a mother's private conference with a daughter, then discovers within a dozen lines that the conference cannot proceed in that form and calls her back, with the revealing explanation that the girl already knows the matter and the attendant has heard its history. Privacy, in this household, is a fiction maintained for exactly as long as no facts are required. The moment a fact is needed, the servant is recalled, and the fact arrives trailing the entire past behind it. The speech is therefore not an interruption of the scene's business but its precondition, summoned by name. Everything the reminiscence contains was, in the strictest sense, asked for, by the person who then spends the speech asking for it to stop. That doubleness, demand and impatience in the same breast, is the household's permanent posture toward the woman it cannot do without, and the play will hold the posture steady until the third act, when the daughter adopts it herself and dismisses the same voice from her counsel with a completeness the mother never attempted. The seed of that dismissal is planted here, in a recall that was always conditional, and the speech's length is partly the sound of the servant taking, while she can, the floor she was given.

The speech also arrives at a hinge in the verse design of the opening act. Around it, the Capulet household speaks the formal blank verse of rank, and Lady Capulet will shortly deliver her extended conceit of Paris as a book, all margin and golden clasp, in polished lines. Between those polished surfaces sits a block of prose, loose, repetitive, full of oaths and endearments and broken chronology. The contrast is the point. Prose in this play is not a lower form of the same language. It is a different instrument, tuned to a different kind of knowledge: the knowledge carried in bodies and households rather than in books and pedigrees. The reminiscence is the prose instrument played at full length for the first time, and its player never again receives so generous a solo.

A word about the text used here is owed before the reading begins. Quotations follow the Arden Shakespeare third series edition prepared by Rene Weis, whose text rests on the second quarto of 1599, the fuller and more authoritative early printing. The first quarto of 1597 carries the speech too, in a compressed and sometimes garbled form consistent with its origin as a reported text, and the differences matter most for editors; the shape of the reminiscence, its sequence of weaning, earthquake, Susan, and joke, survives in both. Line references follow the Arden scene numbering, in which the speech unfolds across the opening movement of 1.3. Where the argument touches a crux, the editorial disagreement is named rather than smoothed away, because one line of this speech has carried an outsized share of the play's dating debate and deserves to have its uncertainty shown.

### Why does the Nurse answer a simple question about age with a story instead of a number?

She answers with a story because a story is the only form in which she owns the fact. The date of Juliet's birth sits in the household's formal memory, but the evidence for it sits in her body and her past: the day she weaned the child, the day the earth shook, the age of her own dead daughter.

Asked for a number, she produces her proofs instead, in the order she lived them. The number emerges at the end of the evidence, earned rather than recited, and the audience believes it precisely because the road to it was so unembarrassingly human.

The direct answer exposes the first layer of the characterization. A servant who answered "fourteen come Lammas" and stopped would be a function. This speaker cannot stop, because for her the fact and the life are the same material. Her opening moves already show the pattern forming. She begins by swearing by her maidenhead at twelve years old, an oath that manages to be pious and bawdy in one breath, then corrects Lady Capulet's guess, then reaches for the wager that she would stake her teeth on the answer, and she has only four teeth left to stake. The joke about the teeth is funny, and it is also information: an aging body, a poor body's losses, offered as collateral for a memory. From the first lines, the speech insists that memory in this household is kept by a body that is itself running out.

The structure that follows is often described as a spiral, and the description holds. The speaker announces the topic, the weaning, touches it, leaves it for the earthquake, returns to the weaning with the wormwood detail, leaves it for Susan, returns through the husband to the fall, retells the fall with additions, and only then, prodded, sums the age. Each return adds one fact and one feeling. The first pass gives an event. The second gives a sensation, the bitterness on the tongue. The third gives a loss. The fourth gives a laugh that turns out to be a prophecy. Nothing in the sequence is decoration. Remove the earthquake and the date loses its anchor. Remove Susan and the devotion loses its explanation. Remove the joke and the play loses one of its deepest rhymes. The spiral is a filing system disguised as its opposite.

Critics have not always granted the passage this design. The older stage tradition treated the reminiscence as a beloved delay, a star turn for a character actress, to be milked and if necessary trimmed, and some editorial commentary followed the stage in apologizing for its length. Barbara Everett's writing on the play pushed against that condescension by treating the speaker as a figure with a genuine inner history, a woman whose past is not background but substance. The position taken here sides with Everett and goes one step further: the speech does not merely reveal a past. It demonstrates a way of knowing, associative, embodied, and exact about everything except sequence, which the rest of the play will repeatedly prefer to the official knowledge of fathers, counts, and friars, and repeatedly suffer for preferring.

## The Weaning: Wormwood on the Dug

The first full memory is a weaning, and its details repay slow attention. The child was taken from the breast on a day the speaker can fix by a public tremor, and to make the separation final she had laid wormwood on her dug, the bitter herb whose taste would teach the small mouth to refuse what it had loved. Sitting in the sun under the dove-house wall, she watched the experiment work. The child tasted, found the bitterness, and fell out with the breast in a small fury the teller remembers with undimmed delight, calling the infant a pretty fool for its indignation. Then the breast shook in the child's hands as the quake arrived, and the teller had to rise and move, and the anecdote folds the domestic and the seismic into a single gesture.

Begin with the word dug. It is the plain, animal word for a breast that suckles, used of cows and of women in the dairy register of speech, and the play will not let a more delicate term near this passage. The choice matters for class and for truth. The aristocratic body in this scene is discussed in metaphors of books and gold. The serving body is discussed in the vocabulary of the farmyard, because that is the vocabulary its owner commands, and the speech never apologizes for the command. When the same word returns in the mouth of the child-refusing breast, the audience hears how completely this girl's earliest sustenance came from the world of work. Juliet was fed, in the most literal sense available, by labor.

Wormwood does quieter work. The herb is bitterness made botanical, proverbial for the taste of a necessary sorrow, and the weaning it enables is the child's first experience of a love deliberately withdrawn for the child's own good. The pattern should be marked, because the play repeats it at scale. Again and again in the tragedy, care expresses itself as deprivation: a father withdrawing consent, a friar withdrawing a bridegroom's hope, a mother withdrawing presence, this same attendant withdrawing counsel at the crisis of Act 3. The first withdrawal is the gentlest and the only wholly successful one. The child survived the wormwood and thrived. The later withdrawals, made by people who fancy themselves wiser, kill. A bitter taste on a breast in a sunny corner of a garden thus becomes the tragedy's template for every harsher separation to come, and the speech plants it in the audience's mind an act and a half before the pattern starts to close.

### What does the wormwood on the dug reveal about how the speech is built?

It reveals that the speech is built from objects handled and tasted rather than from ideas stated. The wormwood is not a symbol the speaker offers; it is a thing she did, laid on her own skin, on a particular day, in a particular patch of sun, under a particular wall.

Her memory stores the world as a sequence of such handlings, and the speech trusts them in order. That is why the passage convinces: its evidence can be touched. The abstraction, a whole philosophy of care through bitterness, is present only because the object is present first.

The dove-house wall completes the little scene's geography of peace. Doves belong to Venus in the iconography the play elsewhere exploits for the lovers, and a dove-house is also a working structure, a source of meat and eggs, tended by servants. The speaker sits beneath it in the sun, at leisure inside her labor, with her mistress and master gone to Mantua. That departure earns a line of its own, and the line is easy to skate past: the great ones were away when the earth moved and the child was weaned. The household's defining transactions, in this telling, keep happening in the parents' absence and the servant's presence. The marriage proposal now pending in the chamber will follow the same distribution of roles, arranged by the present mother, enabled by the absent-minded machinery of servants, and carried, when it turns secret, by the woman doing the remembering here.

There is also the matter of the child's anger, preserved with such relish. The infant who tastes bitterness and falls out with the dug is already, in miniature, the girl who will fall out with every arrangement made for her: with the Paris match, with her father's rage, finally with this very teller. The speaker adores the memory because the temper it records is the temper she loves in the girl, the quickness to refuse what tastes wrong. She cannot know that the same refusal, exercised later on vastly larger matters, will be praised as Juliet's agency by one generation of readers and mourned as the tragedy's engine by another. The speech simply banks the trait, early and fondly, and lets the play draw on the deposit.

The hour of the weaning scene is worth its own sentence of attention, since the teller preserves it as faithfully as the herb and the wall. It is a sunlit hour, out of doors, in the working part of a great house's grounds, with the child old enough to sit, taste, object, and be laughed at, and with the parents absent in another city. Nothing in the little scene is indoors, ceremonious, or supervised by rank. The girl's formation, as the speech remembers it, happened in the open air of the servants' world, in weather and in labor's company, and the contrast with the chamber where the memory is now being retailed, hung with the apparatus of a marriage negotiation, could hardly be plainer. She was raised outside, in every sense the play can load into the word, and she is being disposed of inside. The speech, without announcing any such design, records the whole trajectory in the distance between a dove-house wall and a bridal chamber, and a production that lets the teller glance, even once, toward the room's finery while she describes the sunny wall has understood the geography of the loss.

## Susan: The Child Inside the Parenthesis

At the center of the reminiscence, almost hidden inside its comedy, sits the fact that governs everything the speaker is. She had a daughter of her own. Her name was Susan. Susan and Juliet were of an age, born into the same season of the same household, one to the mistress and one to the servant who would suckle them both in sequence or in overlap. Susan died. The speech gives the death eleven words of theology and no narrative at all. Susan is with God, the teller says. She was too good for her mother. Then, after a blessing called down on all Christian souls, the talk swerves back to Juliet, to the weaning, to the joke, and Susan is not named again in the speech, or anywhere else in the play.

The economy of that treatment is the most eloquent thing in the passage. A grief that can be given a scene gets a scene; this grief gets a parenthesis, because the woman carrying it has never been licensed to make it the subject. Her mourning must travel as an aside inside someone else's story, in the household where she works, about the child who lived. Yet the aside reorganizes the entire speech once it is heard. Before Susan is named, the devotion on display reads as professional warmth, the fondness of a long-serving attendant. After Susan is named, the same devotion reads as transference of the most intelligible kind: the love prepared for a dead child, delivered whole to the surviving one. Every endearment in the speech, and the speech is dense with them, draws on that account.

The parallel the text sets up is exact and pitiless. Two girls, one age, one household nourishment, two fates. The servant's child dies in infancy, unrecorded except in her mother's memory and in this single mention. The mistress's child lives, is cherished, is bargained for by counts, and will die at thirteen with the whole city watching. The speech cannot intend that rhyme, since the teller speaks years before the catastrophe in story time and the dramatist speaks through her with full knowledge. Shakespeare can intend it, and the placement argues that he does. The dead Susan is the first of the play's sacrificed girls, and her unnoticed grave is the ground bass under the famous tomb. When the tragedy ends with golden statues and public mourning for the second girl, the audience that remembers Susan hears the inequality inside the ceremony. One child gets a monument. The other got a parenthesis. The play, which is often accused of sentimentality about its heroine, has quietly lodged that accusation against its own world in its first act.

Barbara Everett's attention to this figure is valuable precisely because it refuses to let Susan evaporate. Everett reads the speaker as a woman whose garrulity is the form her memory takes, and on that reading the Susan aside is not a stray pathos but the speech's center of gravity, the fact around which the loops actually turn. Against that reading stands a more theatrical tradition, running through much performance commentary, in which Susan registers as one more colorful detail in a comic turn, a dab of sentiment to vary the laughter. The disagreement is genuine and worth adjudicating plainly. The theatrical tradition mistakes placement for weight. In a speech where every other element is repeated, elaborated, and returned to, the one element stated once and dropped is marked by its very restraint. Shakespeare repeats what the character enjoys. He states once what she cannot afford to enjoy. Susan is stated once. The verdict here follows Everett: the buried grief is the buried engine, and any performance that lets the aside pass as decoration has misread the map of the speech it is performing.

The theology of the parenthesis repays the same slow reading. She was too good for me, the mother says of the dead child, and the sentence performs a maneuver familiar in the consolation of the poor: the loss is redescribed as a promotion, the child promoted out of a world, and out of a mother's keeping, that were beneath her worth. The maneuver costs the speaker the plain statement of her grievance, since a child too good for her mother cannot also be a child stolen from her mother, and the grievance is duly never stated. What is stated, in the blessing that follows over all Christian souls, is a widening of the grave's company, as if the private loss could be borne by being distributed across the whole communion of the dead. These are the consolations available to a woman with no priest at her elbow and no stone she could commission, and the speech lets them be heard working, efficiently and insufficiently, in real time. Set beside them the consolations the play's official world will offer its own bereaved: statues of gold, a joint monument, a public reconciliation staged over two bodies. Susan's mother had a proverb and a parenthesis. The difference is not in the grief. It is in the budget, and the speech, which keeps showing its audience exactly what everything in this household costs and who pays, shows the price of mourning too.

The name deserves a pause as well. Susan carries, for an audience alert to flower language, the old association of the lily and of Susanna, the chaste heroine of the apocryphal story; the play will later drown its heroine in flower imagery, scattered on a bridal bed turned grave. Nothing so elaborate needs claiming for the naming. The simpler fact suffices. Susan is the only dead child in the tragedy who is named by her own parent on stage, and she is named by the character with the least power to make the naming matter. The speech, which exists to establish how old Juliet is, establishes in passing how easily a child of that house could vanish from every record but one woman's memory. That establishment shadows every later calculation of the heroine's value, including her father's, when he prices her obedience against his honor in Act 3 and finds her suddenly cheap.

## The Listener Inside the Story: Juliet During the Speech

One figure in the chamber says almost nothing while her whole infancy is narrated, and her silence is the speech's most overlooked element. Juliet stands inside her own biography being told by someone else, in front of her mother, with a marriage proposal waiting in the next breath, and the text gives her no interruption, no embarrassment, no plea for the story to stop. Productions must decide what that silence contains. A Juliet who laughs along, easy in the servant's company, establishes the intimacy the later conspiracy will need: this girl trusts the teller with her secrets because the teller has always had them. A Juliet who stands apart, enduring the exposure of her weaning and her toddler's fall while a count's offer hangs in the air, establishes something colder and equally useful: a daughter already being distanced from the household's version of her, listening to her childhood become other people's property in real time.

Either playing is defensible; what is not defensible is treating the silence as emptiness, a young performer waiting for her cue. The speech is addressed, in its asides and endearments, half to the mother who commissioned the facts and half to the girl who is their subject, and its teller keeps glancing, in the text's own choreography of address, toward the person whose life is being spent as anecdote. The endearments land on Juliet. The jest is retold for a room that includes its former toddler, now grown to the very age the jest prophesied, and the doubleness must register somewhere on that listening face: the child who answered Ay is being asked, by the scene's next business, to answer a far larger question in the same monosyllable's spirit. Within days of story time she will be giving assents of her own, in an orchard, in a cell, at an altar, with a comprehension the toddler lacked and a finality the jest never mentioned. The speech makes the audience watch her listen to the first of those assents being laughed over. It is the tragedy's initial lesson in its cruelest technique, the conversion of a person's past into the instrument of her future, performed by the person who loves her best and suspects nothing.

The listener's silence also completes the scene's distribution of knowledge. The mother knows the market. The servant knows the past. The girl, who knows neither fully, is the only one who will have to live in the future both are arranging, and her quiet during the reminiscence is the quiet of someone hearing, perhaps for the first time in this exact form, the materials from which her choices will be built: a body weaned on bitterness survived, a temper that refused what tasted wrong, a household where love and laughter and sudden death have always shared a room. When she does speak in the scene, her lines are models of obedience, a daughter's formal compliance with the proposal's terms. The speech has just shown the audience everything in her that compliance will fail to contain. That is dramatic irony built from a monologue and a silence, and it is among the most economical effects in the play.

## Teeth, Oaths, and Endearments: The Small Change of the Speech

Before the great circuits begin, the speech spends its small change, and the small change is worth counting. The speaker swears by her maidenhead at twelve years old, a vow that compresses her whole sexual biography into a joke at her own expense: the maidenhead was surrendered at roughly the age her charge has now reached, and the teller knows it, and says it in front of the girl's mother, and survives saying it. She offers to wager her teeth on the accuracy of her memory, and the wager is calibrated with a comedian's precision, since she owns only four teeth to stake and would not risk the whole set on a lesser certainty. She calls the child lamb, and pretty fool, and lady-bird, endearments that move between the farmyard, the nursery, and the street. Each of these touches has been catalogued as local color. Together they do structural work that the large circuits depend on.

The oath establishes the speaker's relationship to truth. She swears constantly, by her maidenhead, by her faith, on her life, and the swearing is not carelessness with sacred things. It is the evidentiary system of a person whose word has no institutional backing. A lady's statement is credited by her rank; a servant's statement must be underwritten, oath by oath, by whatever she can pawn: her body, her past, her remaining teeth. The speech's famous reliability, the sense that its facts can be built upon, is manufactured by this machinery of small pledges. When the same woman later gives Juliet counsel that no oath accompanies, in the crisis of Act 3, the absence of her usual underwriting is one of the quiet signals that the counsel is not her true coin. The first act teaches the audience how her honesty sounds, so that the later scene can be heard going false.

The teeth repay the same attention. Four teeth are a census of a life: of diet, of age, of the slow attrition of a body that has worked since childhood and will work until it stops. The speaker offers the census as comedy, because comedy is the currency she is permitted, but the information lodged is not comic. She is old, by the standards of her world and her labor; she has outlived a husband and a child; her instruments of speech are falling out of her head. Against that attrition the memory stands in deliberate contrast, intact, voluble, precise to a day eleven years gone. The speech is a contest between a decaying body and an undecomposed past, and the past wins every round. That victory is the deepest reason the passage moves audiences who cannot say why a garrulous servant has reduced them to something near tears.

The endearments do the third kind of work. Lamb belongs to the vocabulary of sacrifice as much as to that of tenderness, and the play will make its heroine a lamb in earnest before the tomb. Lady-bird carries a street sense alongside its nursery softness in the slang of the period, a doubleness the speaker, who applies it while telling a bawdy story, is unlikely to be innocent of. Pretty fool blesses the child's anger in advance. None of these words is a pet name chosen at random from a stock of servant's chatter. Each one tunes a string the tragedy will later strike: sacrifice, sexuality, and the blessed folly of refusal. The small change of the speech, counted carefully, turns out to be legal tender for the whole play.

## The Earthquake: A Servant's Calendar

Eleven years before the scene, the earth shook. The speaker dates the weaning by it, and the line, rendered in the Arden text as "'Tis since the earthquake now eleven years," has attracted a weight of scholarly attention out of all proportion to its length, because if the quake can be identified with a documented tremor felt in England in 1580, and if the speaker's eleven years are arithmetic rather than atmosphere, the composition of the tragedy can be pulled toward 1591, earlier than the mid-1590s date most scholars favor on other evidence. The line is therefore two things at once: a piece of characterization inside the fiction, and a contested document outside it. Both deserve a hearing; neither should be allowed to swallow the other.

Inside the fiction, the earthquake is a servant's calendar in action. People without letters, ledgers, or legal instruments date their lives by shared public shocks: a quake, a flood, a frost, a king's death, the year the church roof fell. The speaker's chronology is not vague; it is anchored differently, to an event her whole community felt in its body at the same moment. Her memory even preserves the physical comedy of the anchor, the breast shaking, the scramble to stand. When editors and dating scholars seize on the line, they are treating as a document what the fiction presents as a habit of mind, and the habit is the surer evidence of the two. Whatever year Shakespeare wrote, he understood exactly how a woman of this station would count backward, and that understanding is itself a datum about the speech's realism.

Outside the fiction, the debate runs as follows. Rene Weis, in the Arden third series introduction, treats the earthquake reference with the caution of an editor who must print a date range: the 1580 tremor is real, the eleven years are suggestive, but a dramatist may lend a character a remembered quake without keeping a diary, and the stronger stylistic and theatrical evidence clusters the play with the work of the middle 1590s. Other scholars have pressed the line harder, as one strand in a cumulative early-dating case. The adjudication offered here is that the line cannot bear the load alone, and Weis's caution governs: the earthquake is admissible as atmosphere in the dating question and decisive as characterization in the reading question. Scholars who need the play dated have better witnesses. The same household's timekeeping generates the tragedy's other notorious dating tangle when [Capulet rushes the wedding forward, shifting its day from Thursday to Wednesday](/2031/06/06/capulet-wedding-wednesday-or-thursday/) in the later acts; the present article's concern is what the tremor does inside eleven lines of prose, which is plenty.

The deeper work of the earthquake is thematic, and it is easy to miss while the dating argument makes its noise. A quake is the earth's refusal to hold still, arriving without agent or grievance, and the speech makes it the clock of Juliet's childhood. The girl's whole life, from weaning forward, is timed by a convulsion in the ground. The tragedy that follows is built from similar convulsions in its social ground: a street fight that escalates past every intention, a marriage that accelerates past every plan, a father's temper that cracks the household in a single scene. Verona in this play is a city whose foundations move. The woman who weaned its heroine dates her memories by the day the foundations moved in fact, and whether or not Shakespeare intended the emblem, the speech offers it to any production willing to hear the rumble under the comedy.

There is a calendar contrast inside the scene that sharpens the point. Lady Capulet counts her daughter's age forward toward a feast, Lammas-tide, a date in the church and harvest year, because the future is where her interest lies: the marriage, the match, the season of alliance. The servant counts backward from a shock, because the past is where her knowledge lies. Between the two calendars the girl herself has no calendar at all; she is counted, forward and backward, by women who need her age for opposite purposes. The scene will later give Juliet her own fierce temporality, all haste and impatience, in the orchard and the gallery speeches. Here, at the start, she is still a date other people are establishing, and the speech's digressions are the sound of her being established by the person who loves her most and owns her least.

## Lammas, Harvest, and the Forward Calendar

The speech's backward counting eventually hands over to the scene's forward counting, and the handover deserves scrutiny because the two calendars measure the same girl for different markets. Lammas, the feast of the first harvest at the opening of August, is the date toward which the age is summed: come Lammas-eve at night, the girl shall be fourteen. A harvest festival is an apt hinge for a speech that has been steeped in the body's seasons, in feeding and weaning and ripening, since Lammas gathers in what the year has grown and offers it. The household is performing the same operation in its own idiom. What has been grown in the nursery, at cost of a servant's milk and a servant's memories, is about to be offered in the marriage market, and the age must be certified for the offering exactly as a harvest is certified for the fair. The speech's warmth and the scene's business are not two tones accidentally colliding. They are the before and after of a single agricultural fact: things raised are things sold, given, or taken, and the woman who did the raising is the one asked to confirm the crop is ready.

The forward calendar also explains the speech's urgency beneath its ease. Everyone in the chamber can feel the date approaching; the mother has a suitor waiting on it, the father has calculations riding on it, and the servant, who alone remembers when the counting started, performs the sum aloud like a clerk reading a ledger she has kept in her head for fourteen years. Her digressions are sometimes read as resistance to the scene's business, a servant delaying the proposal out of mischief or garrulity. The harvest reading suggests something subtler: she is not delaying the certification, she is dignifying it, insisting that the number be arrived at through the life it counts, rather than read off like a price. The proposal, when it comes, will treat the girl as a volume to be appraised. The speech has already treated her as a history to be honored. The tragedy is, in one of its aspects, the story of which treatment prevails, and the first act has shown the audience both, in the right order, within a single scene.

## The Husband's Joke: Falling Backward

The speech's longest loop, and its only piece of reported dialogue, belongs to a dead man. The speaker's husband, godly and merry in her describing, picked up the toddler Juliet after the child had fallen on her face and cut her brow, and comforted her with a jest whose wording the widow preserves word for word across eleven years. The child had fallen forward; when she had more wit, he promised her, she would fall backward instead, and would she not, Jule? And the child, as if she understood, stopped crying and answered Ay. The teller then delivers the whole anecdote a second time, at her mistress's visible impatience, because the anecdote is the part of the speech she most enjoys, and enjoyment in this speaker is a form of insistence.

The joke needs its meaning stated without coyness, since the speech's design depends on the audience hearing it. Falling backward is the posture of a woman in sexual surrender, and the husband's jest is a prophecy of the child's sexual future, made over a bleeding forehead, in the idiom of a culture that treated such prophecy as fond comedy. The toddler's Ay, her solemn assent to a bargain she cannot parse, completes the jest's mechanism: innocence countersigning desire's contract. The audience laughs at the gap between the words and the understanding. Then the play closes the gap, year by compressed year, until the same girl, grown to the edge of fourteen, falls backward in earnest into the most famous love in the language, and the laughter the speech banked is withdrawn with interest. Few jokes in Shakespeare carry so long a fuse.

What turns the jest from bawdy decoration into structure is its verb. Falling is the tragedy's master motion. Its lovers fall in love at a glance and call the fall a pilgrimage of the eyes. Its Mercutio falls in a street, cursing. Its heroine falls on a dagger; its hero falls on poison beside her; a count falls in a churchyard. Even the household falls, from dignity into public shame, and the city is asked at the end to look at what the falling has cost. The husband's jest, preserved in a servant's memory and delivered before the heroine has spoken a word of love, is the first tuning of that note. The speech therefore performs the tragedy's whole tonal signature in miniature: a fall, treated as comedy, in a household that will learn what falls cost.

### Why does the husband's joke get told twice inside one speech?

Because once is the husband's, and twice is the widow's. The first telling reports the scene: the fall, the jest, the child's answer. The second telling is an act of possession, in which the teller savors every element she owns, the merry man, the clever child, her own place as witness and keeper of both.

Repetition is how oral memory shows its treasures; a written chronicle summarizes, a remembering voice displays. The doubling also serves the dramatist, who needs the jest's wording fixed in the audience's ear, because the whole tragedy is about to become its commentary. What the character does for love, the playwright does for design, and the two motives coincide without friction.

The second telling is also where the speech's social drama sharpens. Lady Capulet interrupts, demands peace, calls for an end to the prating, and the interruption is not rude decoration. It marks the exact boundary of the servant's license: memory is welcome as evidence, unwelcome as entertainment, and forbidden as a claim on the room's time. The teller's response to the check is one of the great small negotiations in the play. She apologizes, promises an end, and finishes the anecdote anyway, at greater length and with fresh detail, before surrendering the floor. Every audience recognizes the maneuver, because every family contains it. The speech's comedy of repetition is thus also a comedy of rank, played for real stakes: whose past gets to occupy the present, and for how long, in a house where the past belongs legally to the family and actually to the servant.

Performance tradition confirms the design by fearing it. The reminiscence is one of the passages most often trimmed in production, on the stated ground of pace, and the trimming habit is a backhanded tribute to the speech's self-sufficiency; it can be cut because it is complete, a whole world in a single speech, detachable in a way that connective dialogue is not. The position argued here is that the cut is always a loss and usually a misunderstanding. Remove the second telling and the jest loses its groove in the ear. Remove Susan and the later betrayal loses its measure, since the audience needs to know the depth of the attachment that the crisis of Act 3 will ask this woman to choose against, and she will choose safety; the full accounting of that choice belongs to [the study of the counsel scene at Act 3 Scene 5 and its aftermath](/2022/11/25/the-nurse-betrayal-of-juliet/) and is only seeded here. Remove the earthquake and the girl's life loses its convulsed clock. The speech is long because the play is loading it with everything the tragedy will spend.

## The Husband: A Voice Kept Alive by Quotation

The husband exists in the play for exactly the length of his joke, and the joke exists because his widow will not let it die. That arrangement deserves a section of its own, because the speech is, among its other offices, an act of preservation performed by the only archivist the man will ever have. He has no name in the text. He has qualities, godly and merry, and he has words, reported with a fidelity that editors reserve for scripture: the question he asked, the promise he made, the pet name he used for the child. Jule, he called her, shortening the heroine's stately name into a household syllable, and the shortening survives in his widow's mouth as the play's only record of the girl being addressed with unceremonious love by a father figure. Capulet, the actual father, will call his daughter many things in the tragedy, including baggage and worse. The dead servant called her Jule, and meant a joke by it, and the joke was kind.

Consider what the preservation costs the preserver. To tell the anecdote the widow must perform her dead husband in front of her employer, lending him her voice, her timing, her breath. Every performance of the speech in the theatre is thus a performance inside a performance: an actress playing a widow playing a merry man teasing a toddler. The layering explains the passage's peculiar emotional weather, laughter with a draught in it. The audience is enjoying a dead man's wit at one remove, kept warm by the person who loved him, and the enjoyment is shadowed by the knowledge that the keeping is mortal too. When this teller dies, the husband's joke dies with her, and Susan's name, and the date of the weaning, and the whole archive of Juliet's first years. The speech is a library announcing, in its liveliest pages, the date of its own burning.

The husband's theology is worth a sentence as well, because the speech distributes its religion carefully. Susan is with God. The husband was godly. The teller swears by her maidenhead and her faith in the same paragraphs. The household's official religion, by contrast, will be administered by a friar with a pharmacopoeia and a talent for stratagem, whose consolations arrive as plans and whose plans arrive too late. Against that machinery the speech sets a folk faith of blessings and survivals, untutored, bawdy, and entirely sincere, in which the dead are with God and the living are to be fed, comforted, and laughed with. The tragedy never asks its audience to choose between these religions in so many words. It simply arranges for one of them to be present at every crisis and the other to be missed at all of them, and the speech is where the arrangement is first displayed.

## Prose, Rhythm, and the Knowledge of the Body

The reminiscence is written in prose, and the prose is engineered as carefully as any verse in the act. Its rhythms are the rhythms of a voice thinking aloud: long accumulating sentences stitched with "and" and "by and by," sudden short verdicts, oaths used as punctuation, endearments dropped like stitches into the fabric. The speaker calls the child lamb and lady-bird, calls her late husband by his qualities, calls her own younger self into the witness box. Syntax keeps being interrupted by the pressure of the next association, and the interruptions are placed, not accidental; they cluster at the speech's emotional joints, around Susan, around the jest's punchline, around the moments when the mistress's patience is being managed. A critic of Shakespearean prose reads such patterning as the signature of a dramatist who thought in prose as a distinct psychology, a way for a mind without formal rhetoric to display an order that rhetoric would only flatten.

### How does the speech's prose rhythm differ from the verse spoken around it?

The verse around the speech moves in measured lines, one thought to a breath, shaped by rank and occasion. The prose moves in runs and stops, thought piling on thought with "and" and "by and by," broken by oaths, endearments, and sudden short verdicts. Verse declares; this prose remembers, and the remembering is the meaning.

Set the speech beside the verse that follows it in the same scene and the design declares itself. Lady Capulet's praise of Paris proceeds by a single sustained metaphor, the book and its golden clasp, marched through line after line with a courtier's patience. It is beautiful and it is dead on the page of the scene, because its occasion is a transaction and its language is the language of appraisal. The servant's prose, disorderly, repetitive, and alive, carries everything the scene knows about love that has actually been practiced: feeding, comforting, joking, burying. The tragedy's sympathies are assigned in that contrast, early and permanently. When the play later needs a language for love as it is lived rather than appraised, it will give that language to the young in verse of a newplainness. The prose reminiscence is where the audience first learns to trust the lived register over the appraised one.

Class is the medium of that lesson, and the speech is exact about class in a way the play's grander statements are not. This is a woman whose wealth is a dead husband's wit, a dead daughter, four remaining teeth, and an unrivaled knowledge of one living girl. Her speech spends that wealth freely, since spending is the only use of it. The wider company of Verona's women, the mother who must ask her servant for her daughter's age and the daughter who will shortly outgrow every woman in the house, forms the frame for this voice; a full comparison of the three belongs to [the survey of Juliet, her mother, and her attendant as the women of Verona](/2023/05/05/women-of-verona-romeo-and-juliet/), while the present reading stays with the solo. Even inside the solo, the social fact hums: the most complete human being in the scene, by the evidence of memory, feeling, and speech, is the one the household can dismiss with a phrase about holding her peace.

Teachers who present the speech's architecture visually, laying its loops out so that students can see a digression return to its object, often find that the map does the persuading that paraphrase cannot; a classroom deck of the map survives well when it is prepared as a viewable slide file through a tool such as the [ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), which lets a seminar move between the speech's circuits and the text without breaking the thread of discussion. The point stands outside any single classroom: this passage repays diagramming because its order is real, and a structure that can be drawn can be defended. The table later in this article draws it.

## The Critical Conversation: Everett, Weis, and the Prose Question

Three lines of commentary converge on the speech, and their friction is instructive. Barbara Everett's criticism restores the speaker's interiority, reading the garrulity as memory's form and Susan as its buried center. Rene Weis's editorial work, in the Arden third series, restores the speech's documentary surface: its text, its cruxes, its dating line, each annotated with a scholar's reluctance to overclaim. A third line, the study of Shakespearean prose associated with editors and critics who track the play's alternation of verse and prose as a meaning-bearing system, restores the speech's form: the prose is a chosen instrument, and its apparent looseness is scored. Each line corrects a laziness in the others. Interiority without the text drifts into sentiment. The text without the form drifts into footnotes. The form without the grief drifts into technique.

The sharpest disagreement among them concerns the earthquake, and it has already been adjudicated here in Weis's favor on the dating question, with the literary readers keeping the characterization question. A second disagreement, quieter, concerns repetition: the theatrical tradition that treats the doubled jest as a performer's indulgence versus the formal reading that treats the doubling as scored design. On that question the adjudication runs the other way, against the theatrical tradition and for the formalists, for the reason given in the speech-map below: the second telling adds information, the fresh detail of the child's brow and the exact wording of the assent, and information-adding repetition is composition, not indulgence. A speech can be enjoyed by its speaker and engineered by its author at the same time; indeed the engineering here consists largely of making the enjoyment plausible.

Susan Snyder's structural account of the tragedy casts a further, clarifying light across the speech, and it belongs in this conversation even though her subject is the whole play rather than the passage. Snyder reads the work as a comedy that turns tragic, its first movement built from the materials of festive comedy, blocking parents, young lovers, witty servants, a marriage plot, until the deaths of the third act break the comic frame beyond repair. The reminiscence sits squarely inside that first movement and shows the frame already carrying its fracture: a marriage plot being launched in a chamber where a dead child has just been named, a festive servant whose festivity is a form of mourning, a bawdy jest whose punchline is a prophecy of the heroine's ruin. On Snyder's account the turn, when it comes, is devastating because the comedy was genuine. The speech supports her with unusual completeness, since its comedy is at once genuine and grave-laden, and it refines her by showing that the tragic material is not waiting outside the comic frame for its cue in Act 3. It is inside the frame from the first act, in a parenthesis, being blessed.

The complication the brief for this article demands, whether the repetition is mere comedy or characterization, thus resolves into the both-at-once verdict, and the resolution can be given its sharpest form. The comedy is the characterization's delivery system. Strip the laughter and the facts remain, a weaning, a death, a jest, but the woman vanishes, because the woman is precisely the person who delivers those facts in that order, at that length, with those asides, against that impatience. Characterization in drama is never a list of traits; it is a way of talking sustained under pressure. Under the mild pressure of a mistress's interruption and the deep pressure of two graves, this speaker talks in loops, and the loops are the woman. The speech is a masterpiece of the method because nowhere else in the play is so much person built from so little event: a question about a birthday, answered.

## The Speech in the Early Texts and in the Editors' Hands

A close reading owes its readers an account of the ground it stands on, and the ground under this speech is unusually instructive. The reminiscence survives in both early printings of the tragedy, the first quarto of 1597 and the second quarto of 1599, and the pattern of its survival teaches something about the passage itself. The 1597 text, generally understood as a version reported from performance and printed in a compressed state, keeps the speech's itinerary entire: the question of age, the weaning, the earthquake, Susan, the fall, the jest. Whole stretches of the play were squeezed in that printing, arguments thinned, transitions lost, and yet the servant's ramble crosses the bottleneck with its loops intact. A passage that actors remembered well enough to reproduce in sequence, digressions and all, was a passage that performance had already marked as load-bearing. The reported text is flawed evidence for wording and strong evidence for structure, and for this speech structure is the argument.

The 1599 text, printed from a manuscript of far higher authority, gives the wording its edge: the oaths placed, the blessing over Christian souls set in its parenthesis, the husband's question and the child's monosyllable preserved with a fidelity that suggests the copy itself prized them. Modern editions built on that quarto, the Arden third series under Rene Weis and the Oxford edition under Jill L. Levenson alike, annotate the speech with a distinctive mixture of confidence and humility: confidence about what the words are, humility about what two of them can be made to prove. The earthquake line attracts the long notes, as it has since editors first tried to spend it as a date. The Susan lines attract short ones, since the text there is stable and the meaning, once stated, seems to annotate itself. The disparity in note length is a small parable of scholarship: the line that might date the play gets the apparatus, while the line that explains its emotional economy gets a sentence, and a close reading exists to redress exactly that distribution of attention.

One further textual fact belongs to the reading rather than the footnotes. The speech's prose is prose in both quartos, unlineated, in a play where the choice between verse and prose is elsewhere so expressive. No compositor turned it into broken verse, no editor since has proposed to. The tradition of the text thus ratifies the formal reading offered earlier in this article: the ramble's unmeasured periods are its author's measure, the sound of a mind and a station that the pentameter would falsify. When students meet the passage in a modern edition, its very appearance on the page, a grey block of prose walled in by verse, is the first interpretation they receive, and it is the right one: something different is happening here, in a different medium, and the difference is the subject.

## The Speech in the Classroom and the Anthology

The passage has a second life as a teaching text, and that life has shaped what generations of readers take the character to be. Anthologies of great speeches reach for the reminiscence when they need the play's comedy represented, and excerpting, however generous, performs an interpretation before any teacher speaks. Print the jest without the weaning and the figure becomes a bawdy turn. Print the weaning without Susan and she becomes a sentimental one. Print Susan, as a few brave selections do, and the whole tonal register of the excerpt changes in a student's hands: the laughter gets a floor of grief under it, and the class discussion that follows is about characterization rather than about color. The speech is unusually vulnerable to this treatment because its meaning lives in its sequence, and sequence is the first thing an excerpt sacrifices. A close reading such as this one exists partly as a protest against that sacrifice, mounted on the evidence of the passage itself: remove any circuit and the map shows a gap with a function written in it.

For students meeting the tragedy whole, the speech offers a rarer gift, a way into the play's difficulty that does not begin with the lovers' verse. The language of the reminiscence is concrete where the sonnet language is figurative, its sentences broken where the lovers' are balanced, its knowledge bodily where theirs is aspirational. A reader who can follow how the prose builds a person, loop by loop, has learned the central skill the rest of the work demands, which is attention to how speech characterizes under pressure. That is why the passage so often becomes the moment a class begins to trust the play. It is also why its Susan sentence so often becomes the moment a class begins to argue about it, since a single parenthesis that half the room missed and half the room cannot forget is a perfect engine for teaching rereading. The speech, in the classroom as in the theatre, rewards exactly the virtues it embodies: patience with digression, memory for returns, and respect for what is said only once.

## Why the Speech Is Misread, and What It Costs

The standard misreading treats the reminiscence as a beloved obstacle: the funny bit before the scene starts, the turn audiences applaud and students skip. Three habits feed it. Anthologies excerpt the jest without Susan, teaching the speech as bawdy comedy with the grief amputated. Productions trim the second telling, teaching it as length rather than design. And the character's later failure in Act 3 teaches readers to demote everything she says early, as if the counsel scene's cowardice retroactively cheapened the first act's love. Each habit is understandable and each is wrong in the same way: all three mistake the speech's surface temperature for its depth. The passage is warm. It is also the tragedy's foundation trench, dug in prose, in which the audience is shown the depth of the attachment that the catastrophe will measure itself against.

There is a further cost, paid by readers rather than audiences, when the speech is filed as relief. The filing teaches a habit of attention that the rest of the play then punishes: the habit of treating servants' talk as weather rather than information. This tragedy hides its most consequential facts in exactly such talk, in porters' errands, servants' gossip, an apothecary's poverty, a watchman's report, and a reader trained by the first act to skim the prose will miss the machinery of the catastrophe assembling itself in the same register. The reminiscence is the training ground where that habit is either formed or broken, and Shakespeare stakes sixty lines on breaking it early. A student who has learned, from this speech, that a parenthesis can carry a tragedy's engine reads the whole work differently thereafter, slower at the servants' doors, quicker at the graves.

Restore the speech and several later effects recover their voltage. The mother's formality in the marriage interview reads colder, because the audience has a standard of warmth to measure it by. The girl's trust in her attendant as go-between reads as the most natural thing in the world, because the audience has watched that trust being manufactured across eleven years of feeding and joking. And the eventual counsel to marry Paris lands with its full obscenity, because the woman giving it is the woman of the wormwood and the parenthesis, and the fall she once joked about has become the fall she is now recommending as policy. The speech is the tragedy's memory of what love looked like when it was safe. The play keeps returning to unsafe love thereafter, and the contrast never stops working.

## The Speech on Stage: Kept, Cut, and Played

The stage history of the reminiscence is a history of respect struggling with appetite. The role of the Nurse became, across the centuries of the play's performance life, one of the great character parts, a prize for performers whose art was transformation rather than glamour, and the first act speech is the part's foundation stone, the moment where the performer's whole conception must declare itself. A Nurse who plays the speech as pure clowning has decided the evening's terms: the tragedy will have a comic servant in it, warmly regarded and finally peripheral. A Nurse who lets Susan land, who slows almost imperceptibly at the blessing and lets the room feel the temperature change, has decided different terms: the tragedy will have a second mourner in it from the first act onward, and the final procession of the bereaved will include a woman the text does not even place in the tomb scene's speeches. The speech is long enough, and early enough, to function as that fork in an interpretation's road, and the choice made inside it propagates through every scene the character later plays.

The cutting tradition tells the same story from the other side. Promptbooks and acting editions across the performance centuries trimmed the reminiscence as a matter of routine, usually by sacrificing the second telling of the jest and thinning the weaning's detail, on the reasoning that an audience's patience is a budget and the scene's business is waiting. The reasoning is coherent and the loss is measurable, in the terms this article has used throughout: cut the second telling and the jest leaves the ear before it has set; thin the weaning and the wormwood template vanishes, and with it the tragedy's earliest statement of care through bitterness; cut Susan, as some brisk productions have come close to doing, and the character's later collapse loses the very scale against which its smallness is judged. A passage can survive such treatment because its reputation protects its outline, and still be diminished in every particular that makes it more than an outline.

Film translated the problem rather than solving it. The camera can hold on a face through the Susan parenthesis in a way the stage cannot enforce, and screen versions that trust the speech have discovered its cinematic property: it is a monologue that contains its own flashbacks, needing no cutaway, since the teller's face supplies the weaning, the tremor, and the jest in succession. The celebrated screen performances of the role lean on exactly that trust. Where a film instead hurries the passage, the loss shows at once in the thinness of the later scenes, whose emotional claims were underwritten in this one. The speech, in short, behaves in every medium as it behaves in the reading offered here: as capital, deposited early, on which the whole enterprise draws.

There is a lesson in the speech's stage fortunes for the play's larger reputation. The tragedy is habitually described as a young people's play, all first love and early death, and its older woman is filed as relief from that intensity. The performance record corrects the filing every time the speech is played whole: audiences settle, in the first act, into the keeping of a voice in its sixties of fictional years, tooth-poor and grief-rich, and discover that the evening's deepest continuities run through her. The young lovers burn; the old servant remembers; and a tragedy, which is a machine for making memory hurt, needs its rememberer established early and at length. Shakespeare, who built the machine, gave the establishment sixty lines of prose and trusted them. The stage, at its best, has trusted them too.

## What the Speech Teaches the Play to Do

Step back from the passage and its influence on the surrounding work comes into view, because the tragedy keeps reusing the speech's methods at larger scales. Its great set pieces are all, in their several ways, reminiscences under pressure: a friar reconstructing a plan, a prince demanding an account, survivors at a tomb competing to narrate how the catastrophe assembled itself. Again and again the play stops its action so that someone can tell, in sequence and with digressions, how the present disaster was manufactured out of small remembered moments, a message undelivered, a quarrel misheard, a vial misunderstood. The first and fullest of those tellings is this one, and it is the only one delivered in happiness, before the events it assembles have acquired their fatal charge. The audience learns the method here, on safe material, a weaning and a jest, so that it can follow the method later, on material that is anything but safe, and recognize the same loops, the same returns to a handled object, the same buried fact surfacing in a parenthesis.

The speech also teaches the play its scale of value. After it, no audience can accept the household's official accounting of what matters, the counts and contracts and golden clasps, without hearing the counter-account that prose has already filed: milk, bitterness, a bump on a brow, a name spoken once. The tragedy will end with the official accounting restored, speeches by princes, promises of statues, and the counter-account will be present there too, in the bodies on the floor and in the knowledge, carried by everyone who heard the first act whole, of whose labor and whose losses actually composed the story. That is a large consequence for sixty lines of a servant's prose to carry, and the passage carries it without strain, because its method was never decoration. It was the play, learning in public how it intended to remember everything.

## Closing: Disorder as a Form of Knowledge

The reminiscence ends, as it must, in submission: the teller grants her mistress the age, praises the girl with a final burst of appetite and affection, wishing her a good husband and adding, with the husband's jest still warm in the room, the hope that she lives to see Juliet married once. Then the scene moves to its business, and the speech is over, and nothing in it has been wasted. A question about a number produced a weaning, a tremor, a grave, a jest, and a calendar. The number is the least of it. What the speech finally demonstrates is that knowledge in this play lives where the official world least expects it: in a servant's looping prose, in a body that fed the heroine, in a memory that cannot file its grief anywhere but inside someone else's story. Shakespeare characterizes through apparent disorder because, for this woman, disorder is the true shape of a life accurately remembered. The verdict of this close reading is that the shape is deliberate, the speech is a masterpiece of construction, and its longest joke is also its saddest sentence, still waiting, eleven years on, for the child who answered Ay to grow into the meaning of the question. Read whole, with its grief left in and its repetitions left standing, the reminiscence does the largest work any single speech in the first act is asked to do: it gives the tragedy a memory deep enough to wound, a servant intelligence enough to trust, and a theory of knowledge, that what is loved is what is remembered in detail, against which every later failure of attention in the play can be measured and found wanting. The friar will fail to attend to a letter. A father will fail to attend to a daughter. A mother will fail to attend to a room. The woman of this speech attends to everything, down to the taste on a child's tongue eleven years gone, and the play opens by proving it, at length, in prose, before asking its audience to watch a world perish of exactly the inattention she never practices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What exactly does the Nurse say about Susan in Act 1 Scene 3?

In the Arden third series text, the whole of it occupies a breath: Susan and Juliet, she says, were of an age; well, Susan is with God; she was too good for her mother. A blessing on all Christian souls follows, and the talk returns to Juliet. No cause of death, no age in months, no scene of loss is given. The restraint is the meaning. A servant's dead child receives a parenthesis inside the living child's story, and the speech's whole economy of love is explained by that parenthesis: the affection prepared for Susan was transferred, entire, to the girl who survived to be weaned, joked over, and cherished in her place.

### Q: Why does it matter that Susan and Juliet were of an age?

The shared age turns a household arrangement into a substitution. A wet nurse commonly suckled her employer's child alongside or after her own, and when the attendant's own infant died, the employer's infant inherited the whole store of maternal attention the bereaved woman carried. Because Susan and Juliet matched in age, the replacement was seamless: the breast, the songs, the watching, all continued, redirected to the surviving girl. The speech's extraordinary devotion, its eleven years of retained detail, its endearments, become legible as transferred motherhood rather than professional fondness. The tragedy later depends on that legibility, since the bond being betrayed in Act 3 must first be understood at full strength.

### Q: Why does the Nurse date the weaning by an earthquake rather than by a calendar year?

Because a public shock is how a person without ledgers, letters, or legal papers fixes a private date. The earthquake was felt by the whole community in its body at the same moment, which makes it a shared clock more reliable, for her, than any named year. The speech even preserves the tremor's physical comedy, the breast shaking as she sat under the dove-house wall, which welds the date to a sensation she cannot misremember. Editors have tried to convert her habit of mind into a document for dating the play itself, but inside the fiction its function is characterization: her chronology is anchored to events, not abstractions.

### Q: What is the dug that the Nurse keeps mentioning in the weaning passage?

The dug is the breast that suckles, named in the plain animal vocabulary of the dairy and the farmyard, the word used for the teats of cattle and, without delicacy, for a nursing woman. Its bluntness is deliberate. The aristocratic body elsewhere in the scene is handled in metaphors of books, gold, and margins, while the serving body is named in the language of work, because that is the language its owner commands and the speech never apologizes for it. The word also fixes the scene's truth claim: this girl's first food came from labor, from a working body, and the tragedy keeps returning to that body whenever it needs love in its least rhetorical form.

### Q: What does the wormwood episode show about the Nurse's memory for physical detail?

It shows a memory that stores the world as things handled, tasted, and sat beneath, in sequence. She recalls the herb she laid on her own skin, the sun, the dove-house wall, the child's expression of disgust, the very word for the small anger that followed. Nothing is summarized and nothing is vague; eleven years have not blurred a single surface. That sensory precision is the speech's warrant. A teller who remembers the bitterness on a breast can be trusted with a birth date, and the audience extends to her large claims the credit her small accuracies have earned. Shakespeare builds the authority of the whole reminiscence out of such deposits.

### Q: What does falling backward mean in the husband's joke?

It names, in the idiom of a fond and bawdy household, the posture of a woman yielding sexually, and the joke is a prophecy disguised as comfort. The toddler has fallen on her face and cut her brow; the husband promises her that when she has more wit she will fall the other way, and asks the child to agree. The comedy runs on the gap between the words and the understanding of the child who solemnly assents. The play then closes that gap over five days of story time, as the same girl falls backward in earnest into love, marriage, and death, and the preserved joke turns into the tragedy's longest-fused piece of dramatic irony.

### Q: Why does the baby Juliet answer Ay to the husband's question?

The Ay is the punchline the widow has preserved for eleven years, and its preservation is the point. A child too young to understand the jest answers it with a grave affirmative, as if countersigning a contract whose terms she cannot read, and the household laughs because innocence has endorsed desire without knowing its name. For the audience, the moment cuts deeper. Juliet's whole story can be read as a series of assents given before their consequences are visible, to a dance, to a marriage, to a potion, to a dagger. The speech opens that pattern in a nursery, in laughter, with the child's own voice supplying the yes that the tragedy will keep collecting.

### Q: Why does Lady Capulet keep trying to cut the speech short?

Rank is asserting its timetable. The reminiscence is welcome to the mistress as evidence for the age she needs, and unwelcome as entertainment, memory, and a claim upon the room's attention, because a servant's license to speak is measured in usefulness. The interruptions, the calls for peace and an end to prating, mark that boundary precisely. What makes the exchange comic rather than cruel is the negotiation that follows: the attendant apologizes, promises to finish, and finishes at greater length, with fresh detail, before yielding. The scene thus stages, inside one speech, the household's true constitution, in which the past belongs legally to the family and actually to the servant.

### Q: How much of Act 1 Scene 3 does the reminiscence actually occupy?

It dominates the scene's opening movement, running from the first question about Juliet's age until the marriage proposal can finally be put, and in performance it commonly takes several unbroken minutes. The proportion is the argument. Shakespeare gives a servant's memory more continuous stage time than any statement of the scene's official business, the Paris match, receives in the same scene, and he places the memory first so that the proposal is heard against it. An audience that has lived inside the weaning, the earthquake, and the jest measures the offer of a count differently from an audience that has merely been told the girl is nearly fourteen. Sequence is persuasion here.

### Q: What does the weaning passage reveal about the work of a wet nurse in the Capulet household?

It reveals a total, bodily, years-long commission performed in the parents' absence. The great ones were at Mantua when the child was weaned; the servant sat in the sun with the infant, managed the bitterness that ended the nursing, absorbed the child's anger, and kept the date. Feeding, weaning, comforting, remembering: the household's reproductive labor appears in the speech as one woman's continuous service, lightly regarded and completely indispensable. The passage also shows why such a figure could become a second mother in fact. She holds the child's earliest history in her own flesh, a credential no parent in the scene can match and none can take from her.

### Q: Does the speech ever give the Nurse a name or a family of her own beyond Susan?

No name of her own is ever spoken, in this speech or anywhere in the play; she exists by function, as Nurse, and by relationships, as wife to a dead merry man and mother to a dead daughter. The speech is therefore her only genealogy, assembled from a husband recalled by his jest and a child recalled by her age-mate's survival. That anonymity cuts two ways. It marks her station, since the play names even its minor gentlemen. It also concentrates her identity into voice: deprived of a name, she becomes the most recognizable speaker in the tragedy, identified instantly by rhythm, oath, and endearment whenever she opens her mouth.

### Q: How does the speech handle the eleven years between the weaning and the scene's present?

It treats them as a single held breath. The weaning, the earthquake, Susan's death, and the husband's jest are narrated as if they belonged to one continuous yesterday, while the intervening decade of the girl's growing, the very years that produced the young woman standing in the chamber, passes without a single anecdote. The compression is revealing. The teller's Juliet is still essentially the child of the speech, weaned, toddling, assenting, and that frozen image explains much of the attendant's later conduct: she serves a memory of a little girl while the actual girl accelerates into decisions the memory cannot govern. The speech's time scheme is its quiet tragedy in advance.

### Q: Why is the joke's repetition funny on stage rather than merely long?

Because the audience watches two performances at once: the anecdote, and the teller's enjoyment of the anecdote, which is funnier than the jest itself. The second telling adds fresh detail, the size of the bump on the child's brow, the exact wording of the question and the assent, so the ear is rewarded rather than taxed. Skilled delivery lets the house laugh at recognition, the universal spectacle of a beloved story being told by the person who owns it, against visible impatience, until the impatience itself joins the comedy. Shakespeare scores the repetition as escalation, not recurrence, and productions that trim it routinely discover that the laugh they were protecting was inside the part they cut.

### Q: How do actors keep the speech's digressions clear for an audience?

The reliable method is to play the returns, not the departures. Each loop of the reminiscence comes back to a physical object or a name, the dug, the wall, Susan, the husband, the child, and a performer who lands those returns with a small change of focus gives the audience a thread through the maze. The interruptions from Lady Capulet help rather than hinder, since each check lets the teller restart with visible relish. What defeats the speech is a single blurred warmth; what releases it is articulation, the sense that every association was chosen by a mind that knows exactly where it has been, however roundabout the road looks on a map.

### Q: What does Barbara Everett's reading of the Nurse change about this speech?

It changes its center of gravity. Where an older theatrical tradition treated the reminiscence as a comic turn with a dab of sentiment, Everett's criticism treats the speaker as a woman with a genuine inner history, whose garrulity is the shape her memory takes and whose dead daughter is the buried fact the whole performance of fondness is built around. On that reading the Susan aside stops being decoration and becomes the engine: the devotion to Juliet is transferred motherhood, and the loops of talk are how a grief with no licensed outlet keeps circulating. Productions and readings that follow Everett play the speech slower at its center and find the tragedy already present in the comedy.

### Q: Why do editors disagree about how much weight the earthquake line can bear as dating evidence?

The disagreement is about what kind of statement the line is. Read as a document, it points to the documented English tremor of 1580 and, eleven years on, to a composition date near 1591, earlier than most other evidence suggests. Read as characterization, it is a servant's way of counting, entirely capable of being atmospheric rather than arithmetical, and no dramatist's diary. Rene Weis, editing the Arden third series, gives the line its due while declining to let it outvote the stylistic and theatrical evidence that clusters the play in the middle 1590s. The caution is methodological: one vivid line, spoken in character, is a frail foundation for a chronology.

### Q: Which early text of the speech should a reader trust, the 1597 or the 1599 quarto?

The 1599 second quarto, the copy text of the Arden and Oxford editions, carries the fuller and more coherent version, with the reminiscence's sequence intact and its wording sharp. The 1597 first quarto preserves the speech's shape but in the compressed, sometimes garbled form associated with a text reported from performance, valuable as evidence of how the passage played and dangerous as a sole authority for what it says. For the Susan lines and the wording of the husband's jest, readers should follow a modern edition based on the second quarto and consult its notes for the variants. The speech's design, notably, survives in both texts, which itself suggests how firmly the sequence was fixed in performance.

### Q: How does the speech plant the ending of the play inside a comic memory?

Through the fall. The husband's jest makes falling backward the child's destined posture, and the tragedy proceeds to literalize the figure at every level: lovers falling at a glance, a wit falling in the street, a count falling in a churchyard, a girl falling on a dagger in a tomb. The nursery scene even supplies the sound track, a household laughing at a fall that drew blood from a forehead, reassured by a merry man's voice. When the final falls come, the audience has been tuned since the first act to hear them in the jest's key. Comedy in this play is rarely an escape from the catastrophe; here it is the catastrophe, remembered in advance and told with love.

### Q: Why does the Nurse remember the day under the dove-house wall in such sensory detail?

Because that day is the hinge of her own life as well as the child's. On it she ceased, in the most physical sense, to be the infant's food, and began the long second career of attendant, witness, and keeper of the record that the speech itself performs. Memory keeps such thresholds in high resolution: the sun, the wall, the doves overhead, the masters away in Mantua, the bitterness, the tremor moving through her own body as she held the child. The detail also certifies her. A witness who can reproduce the weather of a day eleven years gone is a witness whose central claim, the girl's age, a household can safely build a marriage negotiation upon.
