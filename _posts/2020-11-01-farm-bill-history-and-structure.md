On December 20, 2018, the President signed the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, and a legislative process that had consumed the 115th Congress reached a formal end in a single signature. The act had arrived in Congress as H.R. 2. The Senate had approved the conference report by 87 to 13 on December 11, 2018, and the House had approved the conference report by 369 to 47 on December 12, 2018. The signature gave the act a coverage period running from fiscal 2019 through 2023, with authorizations running through September 30, 2023, and closed a cycle that had survived a failed House passage vote on May 18, 2018, and passed the House on June 21, 2018, by a margin of two votes, 213 to 211. A bill that had fallen short in one chamber during the spring ended the year with overwhelming majorities in both chambers, and that contrast framed the central puzzle of the vehicle that the signature brought into law.

The act that emerged from that process carried a farm name and a far wider contents list. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 carried twelve titles, from Commodities and Conservation through Nutrition, Credit, Rural Development, Research, Forestry, Energy, Horticulture, and Crop Insurance to Miscellaneous. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment placed the cost of the act at 428 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2023, the five year life of the act, with 76 percent of that amount, 326 billion dollars, in the nutrition title and the remaining 24 percent, 102 billion dollars, mostly in crop insurance, commodity programs, and conservation. A reader who encountered the act through its short title alone would have expected a statute about farming. A reader who followed the titles and the projected dollars encountered a statute about food assistance first and about farming second, administered as a single package because Congress had chosen, across successive reauthorizations, to legislate for production and consumption inside one vehicle.

That vehicle had a precise legal identity. The farm bill is a recurring omnibus reauthorization, a single enactment that amends and extends many underlying laws at once rather than a single self contained statute with one subject and one place in the United States Code. Each enactment suspends or amends permanent law for a defined coverage period, replaces program designs that an earlier enactment created, and extends authorizations that would otherwise expire. The Congressional Research Service, in In Focus IF11126, counted eighteen farm bills enacted from 1933 through 2018, a sequence that included the enactments of 1933, 1938, 1948, 1949, 1954, 1956, 1965, 1970, 1973, 1977, 1981, 1985, 1990, 1996, 2002, 2008, 2014, and 2018. The intervals between enactments varied across the early decades of the sequence, and the rhythm of the later decades settled at roughly five years, with each bill typically covering five crop or fiscal years.

The money explained why the vehicle held together, and the coalition explained how. By the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018, prepared as an extension of the 2014 bill, projected outlays totaled 867 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2028, and the nutrition title accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of the total. Crop insurance accounted for about 9 percent, commodity programs for about 7 percent, and conservation for about 7 percent under the same baseline and period, while the other eight titles together accounted for about 1 percent. Those shares described a snapshot rather than a fixed property, because nutrition outlays were countercyclical and participation driven, rising during recessions, while commodity outlays were price driven, rising when market prices fell. The political exchange that paired the largest title with the farm titles had a dated origin. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, made food stamps a permanent program administered by the Department of Agriculture, with legislative jurisdiction in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, became the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program, after the House and Senate Agriculture Committees agreed to combine the programs for the 1973 reauthorization. From that combination a logroll emerged in which urban members supplied votes for commodity support and rural members supplied votes for nutrition assistance, with neither side commanding a floor majority alone.

Beneath the coalition and the budget stood two deeper foundations, one constitutional and one statutory. The constitutional foundation was laid during the 1930s and the early 1940s, when United States v. Butler in 1936 struck down the processing tax of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 while adopting a broad reading of the spending power, and Wickard v. Filburn in 1942 sustained a penalty on wheat consumed on the farm where the wheat was grown, on the ground that activity trivial in itself was reachable when, taken together with that of others similarly situated, the activity exerted a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce. The statutory foundation was permanent law, principally the non expiring commodity support provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949, Public Law 81-439, enacted on October 31, 1949. Modern farm bills suspended or amended permanent law for a coverage period rather than repealing permanent law, so expiration threatened reversion to parity based support, benchmarked to the 1910 to 1914 base period. The Commodity Credit Corporation, created in 1933 and given a permanent federal charter by the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948, supplied the funding vehicle through which mandatory commodity payments and many other farm bill payments flowed through Treasury borrowing authority.

The thesis of the account that follows can be stated in one sentence. The farm bill is the recurring omnibus where food assistance and farm support are bound in a single vehicle held together by coalition and forced by permanent law. The omnibus form explains why twelve titles with different administrators, different delivery systems, and different constituencies moved through one committee process and one conference. The coalition explains why a nutrition title accounting for roughly three quarters of projected outlays traveled with commodity titles accounting for a far smaller share, and why the House experiment of 2013, in which a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill passed as separate measures and neither half became law on its own, ended in recombination and enactment as the Agricultural Act of 2014. Permanent law explains why the cycle repeated on schedule even when floor majorities failed, because expiration was never an absence of law but a return to an older and higher support structure that no majority in the episodes examined chose to accept. The long arc runs from the adjustment statutes of the 1930s, through the postwar consolidations of 1948 and 1949, through the combination of 1973 and the conservation and market turns of 1985 and 1996, to the act signed on December 20, 2018. Read along that arc, the farm bill appears as a durable legislative institution whose form recorded, in titles and deadlines, the bargains that American food and farm policy required for passage.

## A Nutrition Bill With a Farm Name

The test for understanding the farm bill is a simple one, and the test has nothing to do with acreage. A reader who follows the money, title by title and dollar by dollar, reaches a different description of the vehicle than a reader who follows the name alone. By the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018, prepared as an extension of the 2014 bill, projected outlays under the farm bill totaled $867 billion over fiscal 2019 to 2028, and the nutrition title accounted for $663.8 billion of that total, about 77 percent. Crop insurance accounted for $78.0 billion, about 9 percent, under the same baseline and period. Commodity programs accounted for $61.1 billion, about 7 percent, and conservation accounted for $59.7 billion, about 7 percent, under the same baseline and period. The other eight titles together accounted for about 1 percent. A vehicle in which more than three quarters of projected spending feeds people through a nutrition title, and in which the programs most closely associated with planting, harvesting, and commodity prices together claim a far smaller share, earns a plainer description than the one on the cover. A nutrition bill with a farm name. The phrase names the arithmetic that any serious account of the vehicle has to confront first, before committees, coalitions, and commodity programs enter the explanation.

That arithmetic also explains why the vehicle took the form it took. Food assistance and farm support were not joined by accident, by administrative convenience, or by a drafting error that later Congresses failed to correct. The combination was deliberate. The Congressional Research Service account of the 1973 reauthorization describes the House and Senate Agriculture Committees agreeing to combine the programs for that reauthorization after separate treatment through 1973, at a moment when both authorizations were scheduled to expire together in the early 1970s and appropriations fights had targeted both. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, became the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program. The Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, Public Law 95-113, then carried the Food Stamp Act of 1977 inside the farm bill. From that architecture a durable political exchange emerged, described in the legislative record as a logroll in which urban members supplied votes for commodity support and rural members supplied votes for nutrition assistance, with neither side commanding a floor majority alone. Defenders of the arrangement, including members of the agriculture committees who negotiated successive bills, described mutual dependence and broad geographic representation as strengths of the combined vehicle. Fiscal critics on both flanks, including members who sought separate consideration of the nutrition title in 2013, described mutual insulation from scrutiny as the cost of the same arrangement.

### Is the farm bill a single law?

The farm bill is not a single law in the sense of one self contained statute. Each enactment is an omnibus act that amends and extends many underlying laws at once, and the 2018 act carried twelve titles covering programs that reach far beyond farming itself in daily operation.

The distinction matters because a single statute ordinarily has a single subject, a single set of operative provisions, and a single place in the United States Code where a reader can follow the law from beginning to end. The farm bill has none of those properties. An enactment that carries the short title of a farm bill is a container. Inside the container sit amendments to commodity law, amendments to conservation law, amendments to trade authorities, a reauthorization of nutrition assistance, amendments to agricultural credit law, provisions for rural development, provisions for research and extension, provisions for forestry, provisions for energy programs, provisions for horticulture, amendments to crop insurance law, and a miscellaneous title that gathers provisions fitting none of the other categories cleanly. Each part amends a different body of underlying law, each part is administered under different program rules, and each part reaches a different population through a different delivery system. The unity of the farm bill is legislative and political rather than codified and textual. The unity lies in the act of passage, in a single vote in the House and a single vote in the Senate on a single conference report, and in a single signature by the President.

An omnibus reauthorization, understood mechanically, performs several operations at once, and each operation has legal consequences that a simple extension would not have. An omnibus act amends existing law. A provision in a farm bill may strike language in an underlying statute, insert new language, substitute a new formula for an old formula, change an eligibility rule, alter a payment rate, or revise a definition that controls how an entire program operates. An omnibus act also extends authority that would otherwise expire. Programs authorized for a fixed number of years receive new authorization periods, funding authorities receive new end dates, and pilot programs either receive further life or reach termination. An omnibus act also replaces provisions outright. A program created in one farm bill may be repealed and succeeded by a differently designed program in the next farm bill, with transition rules governing producers or participants who entered under the earlier design. The Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, signed February 7, 2014, provided an example in the commodity title, where the 2014 act ended direct payments and created the Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage programs in place of the earlier structure. A single enactment may perform all of those operations across different titles at the same time, with each operation taking effect under the effective date and applicability rules stated in the act.

The phrase omnibus carries a precise implication in that mechanical account. The word describes a vehicle that carries many unrelated or loosely related measures in one legislative package, measures that could have moved as separate bills and that, in other contexts, often did move as separate bills. The farm bill before 1973 treated food assistance separately. The omnibus form did not create those programs. The omnibus form gathered the reauthorization of those programs into one negotiation, one committee process centered in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees, one floor debate in each chamber, one conference committee when the chambers disagreed, and one final vote on a conference report that members accepted or rejected as a package. The package quality is central to the politics of the vehicle and central to the law of the vehicle. A member who opposed a single title faced a vote on the entire act. A provision that might have failed as a freestanding bill could survive as one element of a package supported for other reasons. A provision that commanded broad support could carry weight for provisions with narrower constituencies.

A reauthorization also carries a coverage period, and the coverage period governs the temporal life of the act. A farm bill does not ordinarily rewrite the underlying law for all time. A farm bill authorizes and amends programs for a defined span, typically five crop or fiscal years, and the provisions operate for that span under the terms the act sets. When the coverage period ends without a successor enactment or an extension, the amendments that a farm bill made to permanent law cease to control in the manner the act provided, and permanent law revives. Permanent law, in the farm bill context, refers principally to the non expiring commodity support provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949, Public Law 81-439, enacted October 31, 1949. Modern farm bills suspend or amend permanent law for the coverage period rather than repealing permanent law, so expiration carries consequences far beyond a lapse in authorization. The mechanism of permanent law, the parity formulas embedded in the older statutes, and the dairy provisions that revert first under that mechanism belong to a separate part of the account, and the point required here is narrower. The farm bill is temporary by design against a permanent backdrop, and that design creates the deadline pressure that drives each successive negotiation.

Counting the series clarifies the rhythm that the coverage period produces. The Congressional Research Service, in In Focus IF11126, counts eighteen farm bills enacted from 1933 through 2018. The years in that count are 1933, 1938, 1948, 1949, 1954, 1956, 1965, 1970, 1973, 1977, 1981, 1985, 1990, 1996, 2002, 2008, 2014, and 2018. The intervals vary. War, depression, party control of Congress, commodity price conditions, and floor failures all disturbed the calendar at different points. The underlying rhythm, across the later decades of the series, settled at roughly five years, with each bill typically covering five crop or fiscal years. Five years is long enough for a commodity program design to operate through several planting and marketing cycles, for a conservation program to enroll land under contracts that extend beyond a single season, for nutrition rules to govern participation through changing economic conditions, and for the Congressional Budget Office to project costs over a budget window that reveals the fiscal scale of the package. The roughly five year cycle is therefore both a legal feature and a political feature. Legally, expiration forces action. Politically, the expectation of a next bill disciplines bargaining in the bill under consideration, because a faction that loses a provision in one cycle can organize for the next cycle.

The most recent enactment in the Congressional Research Service count gives the abstract definition a concrete identity. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, was enacted by the 115th Congress as H.R. 2, and the act was signed December 20, 2018. The 2018 act covered fiscal 2019 through 2023, through September 30, 2023. The 2018 act carried twelve titles. In order, the titles were Title I, Commodities, Title II, Conservation, Title III, Trade, Title IV, Nutrition, Title V, Credit, Title VI, Rural Development, Title VII, Research, Extension, and Related Matters, Title VIII, Forestry, Title IX, Energy, Title X, Horticulture, Title XI, Crop Insurance, and Title XII, Miscellaneous. Title counts vary across the series, and the twelve title structure of the 2018 act is a property of that act rather than a fixed property of the vehicle. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008, by comparison, carried fifteen titles. Congress organizes each bill according to the programs and negotiations of that cycle.

The passage path of the 2018 act illustrates the breadth of the vehicle and the fragility of the coalition that carries the vehicle through Congress. In the House, the first passage vote failed 198 to 213 on May 18, 2018. A bill that combined farm support and food assistance, negotiated through the House Agriculture Committee, fell short on the House floor despite the committee process that produced the bill. The House then passed the bill 213 to 211 on June 21, 2018, a margin of two votes that left no room for further defections and that demonstrated how narrow the combined coalition could become even in a successful vote. Conference negotiations reconciled the chamber bills during the autumn of 2018. The Senate approved the conference report 87 to 13 on December 11, 2018. The House approved the conference report 369 to 47 on December 12, 2018. The contrast between the spring and early summer House margins and the December conference margins carries information that a single final vote total would hide. A farm bill in draft, with contested provisions open for amendment, can command only a bare majority or fall below a majority altogether. A farm bill as a finished conference package, with the titles balanced against one another and with the alternative framed as expiration and reversion to permanent law, can command an overwhelming bipartisan majority in both chambers. Breadth exposed the bill to objections from every direction during drafting, and breadth assembled the constituency that carried the conference report through both chambers by margins that no single title could have produced alone.

The money in the 2018 act itself reinforces the opening claim while also requiring a qualification that any honest treatment has to carry. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment placed the cost of the 2018 act at $428 billion over the five year life of the act, fiscal 2019 to 2023, with 76 percent, or $326 billion, in the nutrition title and the remaining 24 percent, or $102 billion, mostly in crop insurance, commodity programs, and conservation. The Congressional Budget Office also estimated the act as budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 in comparison with extension of prior law. The nutrition programs inside Title IV explain the scale. The largest program in the title is the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, the program renamed from food stamps by the 2008 act effective October 1, 2008, and authorized as open ended mandatory spending funded through appropriations, with benefits driven by eligibility and participation rather than by a fixed annual grant amount set in the farm bill itself. Title IV also carries the Emergency Food Assistance Program, the Commodity Supplemental Food Program, the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, the Senior Farmers Market Nutrition Program, and nutrition incentive programs. The combined cost of the title reflects the combined scale of household food need as measured through program participation during the projection period.

The qualification concerns how shares behave over time. A percentage drawn from a baseline is a snapshot, not a fixed property of the vehicle, and the Congressional Budget Office projections that produce a nutrition share of about 77 percent in one presentation and 76 percent in another presentation reflect different periods and different measures rather than a disagreement about the identity of the largest title. Nutrition outlays are countercyclical and participation driven, rising during recessions as household income falls and eligibility expands, and falling during expansions as participation contracts. Commodity outlays are price driven, rising when market prices fall below the levels that trigger support under the formulas in the commodity title, and falling when market prices rise above those levels. The claim that the farm bill is a nutrition bill with a farm name therefore rests on a structural observation that survives the qualification. Across the baseline presentations prepared by the Congressional Budget Office for the 2018 cycle, across the ten year window and across the five year window, the nutrition title accounted for roughly three quarters of projected outlays, a share so large that no plausible movement in commodity prices or nutrition participation within the projection assumptions displaced nutrition from first place by a wide margin. Dollar share is not a measure of regulatory importance or of acres affected. Dollar share is a measure of where the federal money goes, and by that measure the vehicle named for farming spends most of the money on food.

A hub treatment matters for reasons that go beyond arithmetic. No single subject account can explain a vehicle that touches commodity support, conservation, trade, nutrition, credit, rural development, research, forestry, energy, horticulture, and crop insurance at once, because the interactions among the titles are part of how the law operates and part of how the law passes. Commodity support affects planting decisions, planting decisions affect land use, land use affects conservation enrollment, and conservation conditions attach to program eligibility in ways that link Title I and Title II in the daily administration of a farm operation. Trade provisions in Title III govern the promotion and food aid dimensions of American agricultural production in foreign markets, connecting domestic support decisions to export outcomes that feed back into the prices that determine commodity payments. Credit provisions in Title V govern access to operating and ownership loans for producers who cannot obtain commercial credit on comparable terms, and the viability of those producers affects the constituency for every other farm title. Rural development provisions in Title VI address water systems, broadband, business programs, and community facilities in the same counties where commodity and conservation programs operate, so that a rural county experiences the farm bill as a combined federal presence rather than as a set of separate statutes. Research and extension provisions in Title VII fund the land grant university system, forestry provisions in Title VIII govern national forest management, and energy provisions in Title IX support bioenergy and rural energy programs. Horticulture provisions in Title X govern specialty crops, organic production, and, under the 2018 act, hemp, which the 2018 act removed from the Controlled Substances Act framework and made an agricultural commodity through Title X and conforming provisions. Crop insurance provisions in Title XI govern the federal crop insurance program, delivered through private insurers under a federal framework, which by the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018 accounted for a larger share of projected outlays over fiscal 2019 to 2028 than the commodity title itself.

Committee jurisdiction reinforces the need for a hub treatment. The House Agriculture Committee and the Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry hold jurisdiction over the combined vehicle, including, through the placement established by the Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, jurisdiction over food assistance administered by the Department of Agriculture. No single committee outside the agriculture committees explains the vehicle, because no single committee outside the agriculture committees assembles the package. Other committees hold jurisdiction over appropriations that fund discretionary portions of the act, over tax provisions that intersect with agricultural policy, and over trade agreements that shape the markets into which American producers sell. None replaces the central negotiation in which the agriculture committees balance commodity support against conservation conditions, nutrition benefit rules against eligibility provisions, crop insurance delivery against fiscal cost, and research funding against rural development funding, all inside one bill that has to attract a House majority, a Senate majority capable of ending debate, and a presidential signature within a coverage deadline set by the expiration of the prior act. No single constituency explains the vehicle either, since commodity producers, nutrition program participants, conservation organizations, rural lenders, crop insurers, specialty crop growers, forest owners, researchers, and anti hunger organizations each defend a portion of the package and depend, in the final vote, on the portions defended by the others. The 2013 attempt to separate the vehicle into a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill, examined later in the full account, tested that dependence under floor conditions and produced the clearest modern evidence of how completely the combined form had become the operating form of the legislation.

The questions that follow from the identity of the vehicle organize the remainder of the account, and each question arises directly from a feature established in the definition. The constitutional question arises from the age of the series and from the New Deal origins of the first enactments in 1933 and 1938, when federal power to regulate agricultural production and to spend for farm support faced sustained challenge in the Supreme Court and emerged through decisions that defined the reach of the commerce power and the spending power for the rest of the century. The money question arises from the division of projected outlays among the twelve titles of the 2018 act, from the difference between mandatory and discretionary funding inside the vehicle, and from the role of the Commodity Credit Corporation, created in 1933 and given a permanent federal charter by the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948, as the funding vehicle through which mandatory commodity and many other farm bill payments flow through Treasury borrowing authority. The coalition question arises from the deliberate combination of nutrition and farm titles in 1973 and from the logroll that combination created between urban and rural members across successive reauthorizations. The separation question arises from the 2013 cycle, when the House passed a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill as separate measures, neither House half became law separately, and the Agricultural Act of 2014 recombined the titles in a conference agreement. The permanent law question arises from the suspension rather than repeal of the 1938 and 1949 statutes, from parity prices benchmarked to the 1910 to 1914 base period and computed and published by the Department of Agriculture under the 1938 act framework, and from the deadline pressure that reversion creates for every Congress that allows a coverage period to approach expiration without a successor act.

## The Constitutional Foundations

The farm bill rests on a constitutional settlement reached in two Supreme Court decisions separated by six years, one that destroyed the first federal farm statute and one that sustained its successor. Between those decisions, Congress rebuilt the structure of federal farm support twice, first by changing the stated purpose of federal payments and then by changing the constitutional power on which the regulatory scheme depended. The resulting lineage explains the architecture of every later farm bill. Federal farm legislation after 1942 combined spending programs grounded in a broad reading of the taxing and spending power with regulatory programs grounded in a broad reading of the commerce power, and the combination was not accidental. Each element of the later vehicle answered a specific constitutional objection that the litigation of the 1930s had identified. The spending element answered the question of how far Congress could go in raising and distributing money for purposes not enumerated elsewhere in Article I. The regulatory element answered the question of how far Congress could go in controlling the quantities of farm commodities that reached the market. The first question was answered in a way that favored Congress even in a case Congress lost. The second question was answered in a way that removed the principal barrier the first decision had erected.

The constitutional problem arose from the place of agriculture in the federal system as courts had described that system before the 1930s. The Constitution granted Congress power to tax and spend for the general welfare, power to regulate commerce among the states, and power to make laws necessary and proper for carrying those grants into execution. The Tenth Amendment reserved to the states, or to the people, the powers not delegated to the federal government. Agricultural production fell, in the conventional reading of the period, on the reserved side of that line. Planting, cultivating, and harvesting took place within a single state, on land governed by state law, and the product entered interstate commerce, if at all, only after production had ended. The distinction between production and commerce thus carried decisive legal weight. Congress could regulate the shipment and sale of commodities across state lines, but a statute that dictated how much a farmer could grow reached conduct that preceded commerce and belonged, under that distinction, to state authority. Federal power over farm prices presented a related difficulty, because prices for commodities sold in national markets were formed by the aggregate of countless local decisions about planting and marketing. The designers of the first farm statute understood that difficulty and attempted to route around it. The attempt failed, but the manner of its failure shaped everything that followed.

The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933, signed on May 12, 1933, attacked the collapse of farm prices through production control financed by a dedicated tax. The mechanism joined three elements into a single plan. The first element was a tax on the first processing of farm commodities, imposed on the processors who converted raw commodities into marketable goods. The second element was a fund created by the proceeds of that tax. The third element was a system of payments to farmers who agreed to reduce production, by taking acreage out of cultivation or by curtailing output in other ways specified by the Secretary of Agriculture. The economic logic was direct. Restricting supply was expected to raise the prices farmers received toward a level the statute treated as fair, and the processing tax supplied the money to compensate farmers for the production they surrendered. The legal logic was more circuitous, and deliberately so. Congress did not order any farmer to plant less. Participation rested on agreement, and the inducement was a payment rather than a penalty. The revenue measure, standing alone, resembled an ordinary excise tax, a category Congress had long been permitted to levy. The constitutional bet underneath the statute was that a tax to fund a regulatory scheme could accomplish, through purchase, a result that a direct command could not accomplish through prohibition. If the tax was valid as a tax and the payments were valid as spending, the argument ran, the combination was valid as well, even though its purpose and effect were to control the volume of agricultural production across the country.

### Why did the Supreme Court strike down the first farm statute?

The Court held that Congress had used a tax on commodity processing to purchase compliance with production controls that the Constitution left to the states. Because the tax and the payments formed a single regulatory plan aimed at agricultural production, the tax fell as a means to an unconstitutional end.

United States v. Butler, 297 U.S. 1 (1936), decided on January 6, 1936, by a vote of six to three, with Justice Roberts writing for the Court, dismantled the 1933 statute by refusing to evaluate its parts in isolation. The processors who paid the tax challenged it, and the Court treated the tax, the appropriation of its proceeds, and the payments to farmers as components of one plan whose object was the regulation of agricultural production. That characterization decided the case. Regulation of production, the Court held, lay within the powers reserved to the states, and Congress could not achieve indirectly, through the leverage of a tax and a payment, a regulatory result that the Constitution withheld from direct federal legislation. The processing tax was, in the language of the opinion, "but a means to an unconstitutional end." The sentence mattered beyond the fate of the tax itself because it stated a method of review. A court examining a federal farm program would look past the formal category of the instrument Congress had chosen and ask what the program as a whole was designed to do. A tax that functioned as the financing arm of a forbidden regulation shared the fate of the regulation. The voluntariness of the farmer agreements did not save the plan, because the Court treated the payments as the instrument by which compliance was secured and the tax as the instrument by which the payments were funded. Severing the tax from the regulatory object was precisely the move the opinion rejected.

The second holding of Butler pointed in the opposite direction and proved more durable than the judgment itself. The Court confronted a disagreement as old as the early Republic over the scope of the power to tax and spend for the general welfare. One reading, associated with James Madison, confined federal spending to purposes connected with the other enumerated powers, so that the general welfare clause added no independent spending authority. The competing reading, associated with Alexander Hamilton, treated the taxing and spending power as a separate substantive grant, limited by the requirement that its exercise serve the general welfare but not confined to the subjects enumerated elsewhere in Article I. The Butler Court adopted the Hamiltonian reading. Congress could therefore raise revenue and spend it for national purposes that no other clause specifically assigned to the federal government. The holding removed a ceiling that the narrower reading would have placed over the entire structure of federal spending programs, including farm programs. Yet the holding did not save the Agricultural Adjustment Act, because the Court distinguished between spending for the general welfare and using money as the lever of a regulatory scheme that invaded powers reserved to the states. The distinction was narrow and, to the dissenting justices, unstable, but its practical consequence was clear. Future farm legislation could draw on a spending power of generous scope, provided the program could be defended as spending rather than as regulation of production wearing the dress of spending. Drafters of later farm statutes worked inside that distinction for years, and the design of the conservation programs that followed the decision showed the distinction at work in statutory form.

The bridge between the fallen statute and its durable successor was the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936. Congress enacted the measure in the same year as the Butler decision and re-aimed federal payments at soil conservation. The change was substantive as well as rhetorical. Under the 1933 act, payments compensated farmers for reducing production, and the opinion in Butler had identified that object as the constitutional defect. Under the 1936 act, payments compensated farmers for adopting soil conserving practices and for shifting acreage away from soil depleting crops. The federal interest asserted by the statute was the protection of the land itself, a resource whose erosion carried consequences beyond the boundaries of any single farm or state. The payments continued to support farm income and continued to influence what farmers planted, since diverting acreage out of depleting crops affected the supply of those crops. The legal theory, however, had changed. The program presented itself as an exercise of the spending power for a purpose the Butler opinion had left open, the expenditure of federal funds on a problem of national concern, rather than as a scheme to fix the quantity of production. Soil conservation also supplied a rationale that connected naturally to the physical condition of American farmland during the drought years of the 1930s, when wind erosion had made the cost of depleting practices visible on a continental scale. The 1936 act therefore performed two functions at once. It kept a flow of federal payments moving to farmers after the 1933 mechanism had been invalidated, and it tested whether the spending route, detached from an avowed production control objective, could sustain a federal farm program. The longer lasting answer came from a different power altogether.

The Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, signed on February 16, 1938, rebuilt the farm program on the commerce power. The design choice at the center of the statute was to regulate commerce in commodities rather than production itself. Marketing quotas addressed the quantity of a commodity that could be sold or moved into the channels of commerce, rather than the quantity a farmer could grow in the field. Acreage allotments apportioned the national acreage for a commodity among producing regions and individual farms, supplying the administrative framework within which quotas operated. Parity supplied the price objective, expressed through price relationships benchmarked to the period from 1910 to 1914, a base period the farm legislation of the era treated as representing a fair exchange relationship between the prices farmers received and the prices farmers paid. Nonrecourse loans, extended through the Commodity Credit Corporation, allowed a farmer to place a commodity under loan at a support rate and to forfeit the commodity to the corporation rather than repay the loan if the market price fell below that rate, a mechanism that supported prices by withdrawing supplies from the market into federal hands. Each device connected to commerce at a different point. Quotas governed marketing. Loans governed the storage and release of commodities that would otherwise press on market prices. Allotments governed planting only as the predicate for the marketing controls the statute imposed. Much of the 1938 act entered permanent law, a status that gave its framework a persistence later farm bills had to reckon with, since later legislation characteristically suspended or amended the permanent provisions for a coverage period rather than repealing them outright. The constitutional exposure of the 1938 structure remained untested at its outer edge until a single Ohio wheat farmer refused to pay a penalty assessed under it.

Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), presented the outer edge in modest figures. Roscoe Filburn, an Ohio farmer, received an allotment of 11.1 acres for wheat under the regulatory program. He sowed 23 acres. The excess production drew a penalty of 49 cents per bushel on 239 excess bushels, a sum of about 117 dollars. The wheat at issue had not been sold and had not crossed a state line. Filburn consumed the wheat on his own farm, in three forms specified in the record. Part fed his livestock. Part was ground into flour for household use. Part was held as seed for the next planting. His argument followed the logic that had prevailed in Butler and in the older production and commerce distinction. Wheat consumed where it was grown, by the household and animals of the grower, never entered commerce at all, and a penalty imposed on its production therefore regulated a purely local act beyond the reach of Congress. The amount involved reinforced the argument from triviality. One farmer excess of 239 bushels could not, by any measurable standard, affect a national wheat market. A unanimous Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Jackson, rejected both branches of the argument and sustained the penalty, and the reasoning of the opinion redefined the boundary that Butler had assumed.

The factual premise of the decision was scale. Home consumed wheat was not a marginal category in the national market. Wheat consumed on the farm where it was grown amounted to above twenty percent of national production, a share large enough that its treatment under the quota system determined whether the system could work at all. A farmer who supplied household flour, livestock feed, and seed from an unregulated home harvest withdrew demand from the commercial market in three directions at once. If the exemption Filburn claimed had been granted to every farmer in his position, the aggregate withdrawal of demand would have undercut the price support objective of the statute on a national scale. The opinion therefore treated the relevant economic actor as the class rather than the individual. The holding, stated in the form later doctrine repeated, was that activity trivial in itself is reachable by Congress when, taken together with that of others similarly situated, the activity exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce. The aggregation principle converted the smallness of the individual case from a defense into an irrelevance. Courts applying the principle asked whether the class of activity, in the aggregate, substantially affected interstate commerce, and the answer for wheat production was supplied by the structure of a commodity market in which fungible supplies from every source competed with one another and set a single national price.

The decision is generally considered the most expansive reading of the commerce power in the Court's case law, and the qualification in that description reflects the breadth of what the opinion sustained. Earlier doctrine had asked whether the regulated activity was itself commerce, or whether it directly affected commerce, and had treated production as a category apart. Wickard asked instead whether the regulated activity, viewed in the aggregate, affected a market that was plainly interstate in character. Under that test, the line between production and commerce lost its categorical force for economic activity. Growing wheat for home consumption was production in the older vocabulary, local in location and noncommercial in disposition, and the Court sustained federal control over it because of its economic relationship to a national market. The opinion also resolved the tension that Butler had left between the importance of the federal objective and the local character of the regulated conduct. Importance alone had never been the test. After Wickard, the test for economic regulation was the aggregate effect of the regulated class on interstate commerce, and a statute addressed to a national commodity market satisfied that test even as applied to a participant whose individual contribution to the market was too small to measure. The marketing quota scheme of the 1938 act, as amended and applied to Filburn, survived constitutional challenge on that basis, and the regulatory foundation of the modern farm program acquired a judicial endorsement that no later farm bill drafter had reason to abandon. Congress legislated on farm commodities after 1942 against the background of a commerce power that reached production for home use when production in the aggregate shaped national supply and demand.

Read together, Butler and Wickard marked out a constitutional space with generous dimensions on both of its sides. On the spending side, Butler held that the power to tax and spend for the general welfare was not confined to the enumerated powers, even as the same opinion destroyed the particular spending scheme before the Court. Farm programs built as expenditures, including payments for conservation practices and the price support operations conducted through federal lending, could rest on that broad spending foundation. On the regulatory side, Wickard held that Congress could reach local production through the commerce power when production in the aggregate substantially affected an interstate market. Farm programs built as regulation, including marketing quotas and acreage allotments tied to those quotas, could rest on that broad commerce foundation. The two holdings also carried a shared lesson about statutory design. The 1933 act fell because its tax, its fund, and its payments formed a single plan aimed at an object the Court placed outside federal power, and no drafting device that merely relabeled the object could have survived the method of review Butler announced. The 1938 act survived because its successor scheme was aimed at commerce in commodities and because the Court, by 1942, evaluated that aim through the aggregate effects of the regulated activity rather than through the older categories. Design was therefore destiny in a specific sense. The same policy of stabilizing farm income could be unconstitutional in one statutory form and constitutional in another, depending on the power invoked, the object stated, and the connection drawn between the regulated conduct and the national market.

Every later farm bill operated inside the space the two decisions defined. The omnibus structure that developed over the following decades combined commodity support, conservation, credit, and eventually nutrition and other programs in a single recurring reauthorization, and each component drew on one or both of the foundations the litigation had settled. Spending programs distributed payments, funded conservation practices on private land, and financed the operations of the Commodity Credit Corporation, which received a permanent federal charter under the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948 and served as the funding vehicle through which mandatory commodity payments and many other farm bill payments flowed, using its authority to borrow from the Treasury. Regulatory programs governed the marketing of commodities whose production, after Wickard, Congress could reach through its aggregate effect on interstate commerce. The permanent law left standing by the 1938 act, together with the Agricultural Act of 1949, preserved the older support framework beneath the recurring bills, so that the constitutional settlement of 1936 to 1942 remained embedded in the statutory basement of the modern vehicle rather than receding into a closed historical episode. Parity prices continued to be computed and published by the Department of Agriculture under the framework of the 1938 act, and the threat of reversion to permanent law after an expiration gave the settlement a continuing procedural force in every later reauthorization debate. The lineage thus explains more than the survival of particular programs. It explains why the farm bill took the form of a spending and regulatory omnibus rather than a code of direct federal commands to farmers, why its commodity titles run through a federally chartered corporation rather than through annual appropriations alone, and why its drafters framed controls in the language of marketing and commerce. The form of the vehicle is the fossil record of its constitutional litigation, legible in every title that later Congresses added to the structure Butler narrowed and Wickard sustained.

## Twelve Titles and One Budget

The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, signed on December 20, 2018, organized the programs of the Department of Agriculture into twelve titles that ran, in the main, through September 30, 2023. A title in a farm bill is a grouping device rather than a subject boundary drawn by logic alone. Related programs were placed together because a single committee process, a single funding stream, or a single administering agency held them together, and other programs landed in a title because the omnibus vehicle offered room for a provision that had no natural home elsewhere. Reading the act title by title reveals two structures at once, an administrative map of which part of the Department runs which program, and a fiscal map of where the projected money concentrates, and the two maps look very different. Four titles accounted for nearly all projected mandatory spending, while eight titles carried much of the act's policy range, its grant programs, its research base, and its rural infrastructure authority on a small share of the projected total.

The number twelve describes one act rather than a permanent architecture. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 carried fifteen titles, and earlier farm bills carried other counts, because Congress added, merged, and separated titles as programs grew or as jurisdiction and negotiation required. A title count is therefore a record of how a particular Congress arranged a particular bill. The arrangement in the 2018 act placed commodity support first and nutrition fourth, a sequence inherited from earlier bills rather than a ranking by size.

### Which farm bill title receives the most money?

The nutrition title, Title IV, receives the most money by a wide margin. In the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028, nutrition accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of 867 billion dollars projected under an extension of the prior act.

Title I, Commodities, authorizes the income support and price support core around which farm bills were first built. The title covers the major program crops, including corn, soybeans, wheat, rice, cotton, and peanuts, together with sugar and dairy, and it operates through elections and payments administered by the Farm Service Agency. Producers with base acres chose between Price Loss Coverage, which paid when the effective price for a covered commodity fell below a statutory reference price, and Agriculture Risk Coverage, which paid when revenue for a crop, measured at the county level or for an individual farm, fell below a benchmark built from earlier prices and yields. Marketing assistance loans gave producers financing against a harvested crop at statutory loan rates, with loan deficiency payments available when market prices fell below the loan rate, and the sugar program combined loans to processors with supply management features. Dairy producers received a rewritten margin program, Dairy Margin Coverage, which paid when the margin between the all milk price and average feed costs fell below a coverage level selected by the producer. The title also carried standing disaster assistance for livestock, honeybees, and farm raised fish, tree assistance for orchardists and nursery growers, and the Noninsured Crop Disaster Assistance Program for crops outside the crop insurance system. Payments under the title flowed through the Commodity Credit Corporation, the government corporation chartered in 1948 whose Treasury borrowing authority finances mandatory farm program spending.

Title II, Conservation, pays producers to change how land is used, retired, or managed, and it divides between land retirement, working lands assistance, easements, and partnerships. The Conservation Reserve Program, administered by the Farm Service Agency, took environmentally sensitive cropland out of production under contracts of ten to fifteen years in return for rental payments and cost sharing, and the 2018 act raised the enrollment cap in steps from 24 million acres in fiscal 2019 to 27 million acres by fiscal 2023 while reducing rental rates relative to earlier law. The Natural Resources Conservation Service administered the working lands programs. The Environmental Quality Incentives Program shared the cost of conservation practices on land in production, from irrigation improvements to nutrient management, while the Conservation Stewardship Program paid producers who maintained an existing conservation base and adopted additional practices across an operation. The Agricultural Conservation Easement Program funded easements that kept farmland in agricultural use and restored wetlands, and the Regional Conservation Partnership Program was recast as a stand alone program with separate funding, through which partner organizations matched federal money for projects in defined areas. Conservation compliance provisions tied eligibility for other farm benefits to protection of wetlands and highly erodible land, so the title reached beyond its own contracts.

Title III, Trade, joins two functions that share a dependence on foreign markets, export promotion and international food assistance, and administration divides along the same line. The Foreign Agricultural Service administered the export and development side of the title. The 2018 act consolidated several export promotion authorities into an Agricultural Trade Promotion and Facilitation Program with permanent mandatory funding, inside which the Market Access Program shared the cost of overseas promotion with producer and trade groups, the Foreign Market Development Program supported longer term market building through cooperators, and smaller authorities served emerging markets and specialty crop exporters facing technical barriers. Food assistance ran through three main channels. Food for Peace, the largest, provided emergency and nonemergency assistance and was administered by the United States Agency for International Development, while the Foreign Agricultural Service administered Food for Progress, which monetized donated commodities to fund agricultural development, and the McGovern-Dole International Food for Education and Child Nutrition Program, which supplied commodities for school feeding and maternal and child nutrition projects. The 2018 act also removed the requirement that a fixed share of Food for Peace commodities be monetized and raised the minimum allocation for nonemergency assistance.

Title IV, Nutrition, authorizes domestic food assistance for low income households, administered by the Food and Nutrition Service, and one program dominates the title. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program provided benefits on electronic cards that households redeemed for food at authorized retailers, with eligibility and benefit levels set by household income, deductions, and a benefit formula tied to the cost of a defined food plan. The title also carried commodity distribution programs with narrower constituencies. The Emergency Food Assistance Program supplied commodities to food banks and emergency feeding organizations, the Commodity Supplemental Food Program supplied food packages to low income older adults, the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations supplied commodities in place of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program for participating households, and the Senior Farmers Market Nutrition Program supplied coupons for produce at farmers markets. Nutrition incentive authorities supported projects that increased fruit and vegetable purchases by benefit recipients. The 2018 act reauthorized the title's programs with program integrity and employment and training provisions, while leaving the benefit structure of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program largely in the form set by earlier law. The fiscal weight of the title came from entitlement design rather than from the number of its programs, a point developed in [the commodity and nutrition titles compared](https://insightcrunch.com/2021/02/15/farm-bill-commodity-vs-nutrition/), because benefits went to every household that met the statutory criteria, and outlays therefore moved with participation and benefit levels rather than with a fixed annual allotment.

Title V, Credit, governs federal farm lending, a field in which the Farm Service Agency acted as a lender of last resort beside a separate cooperative lending system. The title amended the authorities under which the agency made direct farm ownership loans for the purchase and improvement of farmland and direct operating loans for equipment, livestock, and annual production costs, each restricted to family sized farms unable to obtain adequate credit elsewhere on reasonable terms, and under which the agency guaranteed loans made by commercial and Farm Credit System lenders to borrowers who met program conditions. The 2018 act raised the statutory limits on direct and guaranteed ownership and operating loans, adjusted eligibility and graduation provisions that pushed successful borrowers toward commercial credit, and addressed loan servicing for distressed borrowers. The Farm Credit System, a network of cooperatively owned, federally chartered institutions regulated by the Farm Credit Administration, operated under its own statutory authority that farm bills periodically amend. The credit title illustrates why projected outlays misstate a title's reach, because loans and guarantees create federal exposure measured in credit subsidy cost rather than in face value, and repayments and fees can make the scored cost of a large loan portfolio small or negative.


Title VI, Rural Development, directs assistance to rural places rather than to farm production, and the Rural Development mission area of the Department of Agriculture administered the title through three services. The Rural Housing Service ran single family and multifamily housing loans and guarantees, rental assistance, and community facilities loans and grants for buildings such as hospitals, fire stations, and libraries. The Rural Utilities Service ran loans and grants for water and waste disposal systems, electric distribution and generation through rural electric cooperatives, and telecommunications and broadband service. The Rural Business-Cooperative Service ran business and industry loan guarantees, cooperative development assistance, and value added producer grants that helped producers capture processing and marketing margins. The 2018 act reauthorized the title's programs, raised the population ceiling for eligibility under several programs to 50,000 residents, expanded the broadband loan program to permit grants, and prioritized projects addressing substance use disorder through community facilities and distance learning and telemedicine authorities.

Title VII, Research, Extension, and Related Matters, funds the knowledge system behind the rest of the act, and four agencies divided the work. The National Institute of Food and Agriculture administered extramural research, education, and extension, including competitive grants through the Agriculture and Food Research Initiative, capacity funds distributed to land grant universities under the Hatch Act framework, and extension funding under the Smith-Lever Act framework that supported the Cooperative Extension System in the states. The Agricultural Research Service conducted intramural research in federal laboratories on crop and animal production, food safety, and natural resources. The Economic Research Service produced economic analysis of farm, food, and rural conditions, and the National Agricultural Statistics Service produced the official statistics on acreage, production, and prices on which farm program administration and market reporting depended. The 2018 act reauthorized research authorities through fiscal 2023, gave mandatory funding to a limited set of programs, and left the bulk of the research budget to annual appropriations, so the title's projected mandatory outlays understated the scale of the federal research establishment by a wide margin.

Title VIII, Forestry, is the province of the Forest Service, and the title addresses both the National Forest System and assistance to state and private forestry. The 2018 act expanded Good Neighbor Authority, under which states, counties, and tribes performed restoration work, including timber sale and fuels reduction work, on national forest land under agreements with the agency, and it extended authorities for addressing insect infestations and disease outbreaks and for landscape scale restoration across ownership boundaries. The title carried authorities on stewardship contracting, wildfire management, and markets for wood products. Forestry spending under the farm bill was small because the Forest Service drew its principal operating funds from annual appropriations handled outside the farm bill's mandatory accounts, so the title functioned mainly as a source of management authority rather than as a source of program money.

Title IX, Energy, promotes renewable energy and energy efficiency in farm and rural settings through a cluster of programs divided between Rural Development agencies and the Farm Service Agency. The Rural Energy for America Program, administered within Rural Development, offered grants and loan guarantees for renewable energy systems and efficiency improvements for agricultural producers and rural small businesses. Related authorities supported biorefinery construction and conversion through loan guarantees, payments to producers of advanced biofuels, assistance for the production and marketing of biobased products, and the Biomass Crop Assistance Program, administered by the Farm Service Agency, which supported the establishment and delivery of biomass feedstocks. The 2018 act extended the title's programs through fiscal 2023, reduced mandatory funding relative to earlier farm bills, repealed two smaller authorities, and added a grant program for carbon utilization and biogas education. The energy title also showed the budget mechanics of small titles, because a program with mandatory funding below the level needed to sustain a baseline could lose that baseline between bills, after which reauthorization itself scored as new spending.

Title X, Horticulture, serves the specialty crop sector, defined in statute around fruits, vegetables, tree nuts, dried fruits, and nursery and floriculture crops, together with organic agriculture and local food systems, sectors that sat largely outside the Title I payment structure. The Agricultural Marketing Service administered the title's central grant authority, the Specialty Crop Block Grant Program, which distributed funds to state departments of agriculture for projects that enhanced the competitiveness of specialty crops, and the same agency ran the National Organic Program, which set and enforced the standards behind the federal organic label, along with certification cost sharing for organic producers and handlers. Plant pest and disease management authorities, administered with the Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service, funded surveillance and response work against threats to specialty crop production, and farmers market and local food promotion authorities were combined in a Local Agriculture Market Program. The title also carried the act's hemp provisions. The 2018 act removed hemp from the framework of the Controlled Substances Act and treated hemp as an agricultural commodity, with production regulated under plans approved by the Department of Agriculture or administered by the Department directly.

Title XI, Crop Insurance, amends the federal crop insurance program, a permanently authorized program delivered as a public and private partnership under the Federal Crop Insurance Act. The Risk Management Agency administered the program and the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation carried its financial obligations. Private insurance companies, operating as approved insurance providers under standard reinsurance agreements with the Corporation, sold and serviced policies, adjusted losses, and shared underwriting risk with the federal government, while the government subsidized a statutory share of producer premiums, reimbursed part of the companies' administrative and operating expenses, and absorbed catastrophic risk through reinsurance. Producers bought yield or revenue coverage for individual crops, whole farm revenue products, and specialized products for livestock, forage, and specialty crops, and the 2018 act adjusted coverage for several product types and extended the reach of the program while leaving its permanent structure intact. Because the program held permanent authority, expiration of the farm bill would not have ended it, and the title existed to set policy terms and subsidy structures. Premium subsidies were mandatory spending without a fixed annual cap in the authorizing statute, so outlays rose and fell with commodity prices, participation, and the value of insured production.

Title XII, Miscellaneous, gathers provisions that crossed title boundaries or served constituencies the other titles did not organize. Livestock and poultry provisions addressed animal health through authorities administered by the Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service, including a national animal disease preparedness and response program and a national animal vaccine and countermeasures bank funded by the 2018 act. Outreach and training authorities combined assistance for beginning farmers and ranchers with assistance for socially disadvantaged and veteran producers in a Farming Opportunities Training and Outreach program, and the title carried provisions on topics as varied as citrus disease research funding, cotton and wool trust funds, and protections related to livestock marketing and to agricultural labor and food supply chains. A miscellaneous title is sometimes described as a remainder, but Title XII in the 2018 act included programs with dedicated mandatory funding and durable constituencies.

The money followed a distribution far more concentrated than the policy. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline, which projected spending under an extension of the 2014 act, put total mandatory outlays at 867 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2028. Within that baseline, the Congressional Budget Office projected the nutrition title at 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent of the total, crop insurance at 78.0 billion dollars, about 9 percent, commodities at 61.1 billion dollars, about 7 percent, and conservation at 59.7 billion dollars, about 7 percent. The remaining eight titles together, trade, credit, rural development, research, forestry, energy, horticulture, and miscellaneous, accounted for about 1 percent of projected mandatory outlays. The Congressional Budget Office projection at enactment of the 2018 act itself was smaller because the period was shorter. The Office projected 428 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2023, with 76 percent of that amount in the nutrition title, and scored the act as budget neutral across the ten year window from fiscal 2019 to 2028, meaning that projected outlays under the act matched projected outlays under an extension of the prior act over that period. The two figures describe different things. The ten year baseline describes the inherited structure of spending that Congress faced when the bill was written, while the five year projection describes the expected cost of the act during the years the act itself governed.

The mandatory and discretionary division explains why the spending map and the authority map diverge. Mandatory spending is provided in the authorizing law itself, and for most farm program accounts the money flows through the Commodity Credit Corporation, which finances payments and then receives appropriations to restore its net realized losses, a mechanism that lets Title I commodity payments, Title II conservation contracts, and Title XI premium support operate under funding formulas and amounts written into the farm bill rather than under annual decisions by appropriations committees. The nutrition title occupies a hybrid position specified on its own terms. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program is open ended mandatory spending, in that benefits are owed to households meeting statutory criteria, and the program is funded through appropriations, so Congress finances the entitlement through the annual process without converting benefits into a discretionary grant subject to a fixed allotment. Discretionary programs, by contrast, receive only an authorization of appropriations in the farm bill. An authorization sets a ceiling and a legal basis for spending, and no money moves until an appropriations act provides it, at a level that may match the authorization, fall below it, or in some cases exceed the pattern the authorization implied. Research, most rural development programs, forestry operations, international food assistance, farm loan program levels, and much of the horticulture and energy portfolio sat on the discretionary side of the line. The distinction controls what an authorization guarantees. A mandatory title guarantees a funding rule, subject to later amendment by Congress, while a discretionary authorization guarantees an opportunity to seek funding each year, and the practical security of a rural water program or a research initiative depended on appropriations outcomes that the farm bill text did not determine.

The nutrition share itself requires a counter reading, stated at full strength, because the 77 percent figure from the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline is a snapshot of one projection drawn at one point in an economic cycle. Nutrition outlays are countercyclical and participation driven. When employment and wages fall, more households meet the income criteria for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, enrollment rises, and outlays rise without any change in the statute. Commodity outlays run on the opposite driver. Title I payments are price driven, rising when market prices fall below reference prices or revenue benchmarks and shrinking when prices are high, so a period of depressed prices expands the commodity share mechanically. Crop insurance outlays also move with prices, because higher prices raise the insured value of production and therefore premium subsidies and potential indemnities. A baseline drawn beside a recession would show a larger nutrition share, a baseline drawn beside a commodity price collapse would show larger commodity and crop insurance shares, and a baseline drawn in a period of high prices and low unemployment would compress the farm side of the distribution relative to nutrition. Cross year comparisons that ignore the driver behind each title mislead, because a change in share may record a change in economic conditions rather than a decision by Congress, and the shares in the April 2018 baseline were produced by the conditions the Congressional Budget Office projected for fiscal 2019 to 2028, not by any allocation rule written into the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018.

The twelve titles thus describe a statute with two centers of gravity. Fiscally, the act rested on nutrition, crop insurance, commodities, and conservation, the four titles that carried about 99 percent of projected mandatory spending in the Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline for fiscal 2019 to 2028. Institutionally, the act rested on a wider base, because credit supplied a lending channel for producers whom private credit did not reach, research supplied productivity gains on which commodity economics depended, and the rural development, forestry, energy, horticulture, trade, and miscellaneous titles supplied authorities that rural communities, specialty sectors, and foreign assistance programs used each season. A reader who followed only the money would miss most of what the act authorized, and a reader who followed only the program descriptions would miss how concentrated the money was.

## The Coalition Built Into the Vehicle

The farm bill survived as a recurring legislative vehicle because its contents reached far beyond the farms named in its short titles. By the time of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, the measure carried twelve titles that covered commodities, conservation, trade, nutrition, credit, rural development, research, forestry, energy, horticulture, crop insurance, and miscellaneous provisions. That breadth was not an accident of drafting or a habit of congressional housekeeping. Breadth was the political foundation of the vehicle. A bill concerned only with price support for a limited set of crops spoke directly to a limited set of districts and states. A bill that paired commodity programs with food assistance, conservation payments, rural credit, and research spoke to districts with very different economies, from a packing plant to a grocery store checkout. The coalition was therefore built into the structure of the legislation before any individual vote was cast, and each reauthorization asked members to weigh a package whose largest parts depended on one another for passage.

The dependence between commodity support and nutrition assistance became the central example of that design. The two program families served different populations, operated through different delivery systems, and answered to different measures of success. Commodity programs responded principally to prices, production, and farm income. Nutrition assistance responded principally to household income, employment, and food need. Yet from 1973 onward the two families traveled through Congress in the same vehicle, under the care of the same committees, and under the discipline of the same expiration dates. The arrangement lasted because the institutional facts came before the political bargain and because the bargain, once made, gave both sides a reason to defend the vehicle that carried the other side as well as their own.

### Why are food stamps in an agriculture bill?

Food stamps sit in an agriculture bill because the Food Stamp Act of 1964 placed the program in the Department of Agriculture under the Agriculture Committees, and the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973 first combined its reauthorization with commodity programs in a single farm bill.

The sequence matters because a common misunderstanding reverses the order of events. Food assistance did not enter an agriculture bill as a late addition pasted onto unrelated legislation at the moment of enactment. The jurisdictional decision came first, in 1964, and the combined bill followed nine years later, in 1973. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, made food stamps a permanent program. The statute placed administration in the Department of Agriculture, and legislative jurisdiction rested with the Agriculture Committees of the House and the Senate. Departmental administration and committee jurisdiction created a shared institutional home for programs that reached both ends of the food system, from production and marketing to household consumption. The legislative path described in [the Food Stamp Act of 1964 and the history of SNAP](https://insightcrunch.com/2019/09/01/food-stamp-act-1964-snap-history/) began with that jurisdictional placement, and every later decision to combine or separate the programs had to reckon with it.

A shared committee home did not by itself require a shared bill. Committees with jurisdiction over two programs may report two measures, move them on separate calendars, and defend them in separate conferences. The Agriculture Committees held that option during the first years of the permanent food stamp program, and Congress used it. Through the early 1970s the commodity programs and the food stamp program moved on separate tracks even though the same department administered them and the same committees supervised them. Separate movement preserved a formal distinction between farm income policy and household food assistance, but it also exposed each program to a separate political fate. Each measure needed its own majority, its own place on a crowded floor schedule, and its own defense against amendments and appropriations pressure. The question before the committees in the approach to the 1973 reauthorization was narrower and more practical: whether two programs with a shared home, overlapping expiration pressure, and a shared history of fiscal attack gained or lost strength by traveling together.

The road to combination ran through expiration dates and appropriations fights. Both program families were scheduled to expire together in the early 1970s, and appropriations fights had targeted both. A program that depends on periodic reauthorization faces a recurring moment of vulnerability when its authority approaches an end date, and a program that depends on annual funding faces a second arena in which opponents may restrict, condition, or reduce operations without repealing the authorizing statute outright. During the separate track period, commodity programs and food assistance each carried those vulnerabilities in separate proceedings. Combination changed the arithmetic of attack. An effort to isolate food assistance had to contend with members assembled to renew commodity programs, and an effort to isolate commodity support had to contend with members assembled to renew food assistance. The House and Senate Agriculture Committees agreed to combine the programs for the 1973 reauthorization, and the agreement converted an administrative and jurisdictional kinship into a legislative coalition.

The combination point has a precise statutory marker. The Congressional Research Service states that the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, was the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program. That sentence fixes 1973 as the year in which the modern combined vehicle took form. Earlier farm legislation had addressed commodities, conservation, credit, and related subjects. Earlier food assistance legislation had proceeded separately after the permanent program was established in 1964. The 1973 act joined the two lines of legislation in one enactment and then repeated the pattern in later cycles until combination became the expected architecture of a farm bill. Expectation itself acquired force. Staff work, committee planning, budget estimates, stakeholder testimony, and conference preparation proceeded on the assumption that a farm bill contained a nutrition title, and departures from that assumption required an affirmative decision to break the vehicle apart rather than a decision to assemble it.

The contents of the 1973 act show the bargain working in both directions inside the same enactment. For commodity producers, the act introduced target prices and deficiency payments, a support architecture that tied payments to the gap between a statutory target and market conditions rather than relying only on earlier mechanisms. In the same act, Congress cut the payment limitation to 20,000 dollars. The pairing deserves close attention because it defeats any simple account in which one side of the coalition merely extracted benefits and paid nothing. Producers received a redesigned system of support. That system arrived with a tighter ceiling on payments to a single recipient, a concession to members and organizations concerned with the concentration of federal farm payments. Food assistance, for its part, received the protection of the combined vehicle and a reauthorization carried by a majority assembled for more than nutrition alone. In exchange, food assistance entered a legislative process shaped by farm bill bargaining, in which nutrition provisions had to survive committee markups and conference negotiations dominated by the full range of titles in the bill. The bargain was not a gift in either direction. The bargain was an exchange of votes, protection, and concessions, recorded in statutory text.

The Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, Public Law 95-113, deepened the combination and gave it a second statutory foundation. The 1977 farm bill carried the Food Stamp Act of 1977 inside the larger measure, so the nutrition half of the vehicle received a full reauthorization statute of its own within the omnibus enactment. The nesting of one act inside another captured the dual character of the arrangement. Food stamps retained a distinct statutory identity, a distinct authorizing act, distinct eligibility rules, and a distinct administrative apparatus within the Department of Agriculture. At the same time, the fate of that act depended on the fate of the farm bill that carried it. Separation in text coexisted with combination in procedure, and the procedure governed passage. A vote for the farm bill was a vote for the Food Stamp Act of 1977, and a vote against the farm bill placed the food stamp reauthorization at risk along with the commodity, conservation, and credit titles.

The 1977 act also changed the terms on which households received assistance by ending the purchase requirement. Under the purchase requirement, a participating household had to devote part of its own cash to obtain its coupon allotment, a design that tied the benefit to a household transaction at the point of entry into the program. Elimination of the requirement removed that cash outlay as a condition of participation and converted the benefit into a direct supplement to household food purchasing power. The change belonged to nutrition policy in substance, and its placement inside the 1977 farm bill demonstrated the procedural point with unusual clarity. A major liberalization of food assistance moved through Congress not as a freestanding welfare measure but as part of an agriculture act whose other titles addressed farm prices, conservation, and rural matters. Members whose districts contained few food stamp recipients voted on the purchase requirement as part of a bill that carried provisions of direct concern to their farms and rural communities. Members whose districts contained few farms voted on commodity provisions as part of a bill that carried the end of the purchase requirement. Each side supplied votes that the other side needed.

Later cycles preserved the architecture while the names and the budget shares changed. The act of 2008 renamed the food stamp program the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, effective October 1, 2008, and the renamed program remained inside the same vehicle that had carried its predecessor since 1973. The Congressional Budget Office figures for the following decade illustrate why the nutrition title loomed so large in farm bill politics by the time of the 2018 act. In the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018 for extension of the 2014 bill, projected spending totaled 867 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2028, and the nutrition title accounted for 663.8 billion dollars, about 77 percent. Commodities accounted for 61.1 billion dollars, about 7 percent, in the same baseline. Those figures require the qualification that the baseline was a snapshot rather than a fixed property of the vehicle, because nutrition outlays were countercyclical and participation driven, rising in recessions, while commodity outlays were price driven, rising when prices fell. Even with that qualification, the baseline explained a central fact of the coalition. The smaller half of the bill in budget terms supplied much of the bill in constituency terms for farm districts, and the larger half in budget terms supplied much of the bill in constituency terms for districts where agriculture played a minor role.

The mechanics of the logroll explain how such an exchange renewed itself across five decades. A logroll in legislative practice is an agreement among members to support one another's measures in combination when each measure lacks a sufficient majority on its own. The farm bill logroll rested on a durable geographic division. Commodity support drew its strongest backing from members representing districts and states where farming, ranching, and related processing supplied a large share of employment and income. Nutrition assistance drew backing from members across a wider map, with particular strength in urban districts and in rural districts where household incomes were low. Neither bloc commanded a floor majority alone. The farm bloc faced a structural limit rooted in representation itself, because seats in the House of Representatives follow population and farm dependent districts held a minority of seats in the chamber. The nutrition bloc faced a different limit, because food assistance standing alone attracted opposition from members who treated the program as welfare spending subject to reduction, work conditions, and eligibility restriction. Combined, the two blocs assembled a majority with a geographic reach that neither possessed separately, and the Senate added a further layer to the exchange because statewide constituencies in farm states contained both producer communities and low income households within the same electorate.

Committee jurisdiction converted the logroll from a floor tactic into an institution. Because the Agriculture Committees held jurisdiction over both program families, a single committee in each chamber drafted the vehicle, heard the witnesses, conducted the markup, and reported the bill. Jurisdiction determined which members developed expertise in both halves of the bargain and which members had a professional stake in the survival of the combined bill. Committee leaders who delivered a farm bill delivered nutrition assistance as part of the achievement, and committee leaders who defended nutrition provisions did so from within the institution that also wrote commodity policy. A proposal to strip one title threatened the committee product as a whole and placed the burden of disruption on the member proposing separation. Jurisdiction also shaped the alternatives available to opponents. A member who wished to cut nutrition spending inside a farm bill had to move an amendment against the reported bill or support a separate measure through a committee process designed around combination. The default setting favored the package, and in legislative practice the default setting often decides outcomes before a final vote is taken.

Conference practice reinforced the default. Farm bills regularly passed the House and the Senate in different forms, with different commodity provisions, different nutrition rules, and different budget consequences. A conference committee then reconciled the two versions into a single report that each chamber considered as a package. Conferees drawn heavily from the Agriculture Committees negotiated across titles, trading concessions in one title for gains in another, and produced a report that members accepted or rejected as a whole rather than title by title. A member dissatisfied with the nutrition title in a conference report faced a choice between accepting the full settlement and voting to reject the commodity provisions that the same report contained, and a member dissatisfied with the commodity title faced the mirror image of that choice. Package voting at the conference stage raised the cost of defection for both blocs and bound winners and losers in each title to the fate of the whole.

The single bill structure supplied the final mechanical element. Expiration dates forced action, because the authorizations carried in a farm bill ran for a defined period and the approach of an end date required Congress to legislate again. Renewal under deadline pressure favored the existing architecture, since drafting a combined bill from a combined predecessor was a known exercise with known committee procedures, known budget baselines, and known stakeholder coalitions. Separation required affirmative reconstruction of two legislative tracks, two committee products, two floor strategies, and two conferences or their equivalents, all within the same deadline. Under those conditions, inertia favored combination, and the repeated use of the combined form entrenched the coalition that the form expressed. The experience of 2013 and 2014 showed the strength of the entrenchment under stress. The House passed a farm only measure and a nutrition only measure as separate bills in 2013 after a combined bill failed, and neither half became law on its own. The two halves were joined again for conference, and the enacted Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, recombined commodities and nutrition in a single statute. Separation proved possible as a parliamentary maneuver and unworkable as a governing outcome in that cycle.

Defenders of the combined vehicle, including members and committees that defended the structure across successive reauthorizations, stated the case for the logroll in terms of representation and mutual dependence. In that account, the combination produced a broader geographic majority for both halves of the bill than either half could assemble alone, and a broader majority produced legislation that spoke for more of the country. Farm programs gained the votes of urban and suburban members whose constituents bought food and had little direct stake in commodity prices. Nutrition assistance gained the votes of rural members whose districts also contained households that received benefits, grocers that redeemed them, and local economies in which benefit spending circulated. Defenders described the connection as substantive as well as tactical, because a national food system links production and consumption in a single chain, and a single committee and a single bill allowed Congress to legislate for the chain as a whole. Mutual dependence, in the defense account, also imposed reciprocal restraint, since excess on one side endangered the votes that the other side supplied. The result, defenders maintained, was durable legislation renewed on a regular cycle for programs that served populations with little political power to protect themselves in separate proceedings.

Budget watchdog organizations and separation advocates stated the opposing case at equal strength. In that account, the combination insulated each half of the bill from the scrutiny that the half would face standing alone. A freestanding nutrition bill would require its defenders to justify eligibility rules, benefit levels, and total cost in a debate devoted to those questions, with a vote that recorded each member's position on food assistance and nothing else. A freestanding commodity bill would require its defenders to justify payment levels, eligibility for support, and the distribution of payments among producers in a debate devoted to farm policy alone. The combined bill, in the critical account, blurred both debates. Members voted for nutrition provisions that they had publicly criticized in order to secure commodity provisions for their districts, and members voted for commodity provisions that they had publicly criticized in order to secure nutrition provisions for theirs. Watchdog organizations described that exchange as a mechanism that raised total spending on both sides, because each bloc purchased the votes of the other with appropriations rather than with persuasion, and because no bloc had an incentive to demand savings in the title that supplied its partners' votes. Separation advocates drew the conclusion that the two program families belonged in separate bills precisely because the families served different purposes, answered to different measures of need, and deserved separate judgments from the members who voted on them.

Each account identified a real feature of the structure. The combination did broaden the majority available to both halves, as defenders maintained, and a broader majority is a genuine legislative achievement in a chamber organized by geography. The combination also did soften the accountability of a separate vote on each half, as watchdog organizations and separation advocates maintained, and softened accountability is a genuine cost in a legislature that records positions through votes. The farm bill carried both consequences in the same text because the consequences arose from the same mechanism. A package vote assembled members who would not have voted for the parts in isolation, and assembly and insulation were two descriptions of that single fact from two evaluative positions. Congress revisited the choice at every reauthorization from 1973 through the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 and resolved the choice in favor of combination in every enacted farm bill of that period. Jurisdiction had come first, in 1964. Combination followed, in 1973. Deepening followed, in 1977, and renaming followed, in 2008. The coalition held because the vehicle was built to hold it, and the vehicle was rebuilt, cycle after cycle, by members who depended on the coalition for the provisions that their own constituents required.

## The Bill That Split in Two

The farm bill coalition rested on a legislative arrangement rather than on agreement about a single program. Commodity support drew its strongest support from districts and states where farm production formed a large share of employment and income. Nutrition assistance drew its strongest support from districts and states where population was concentrated in cities and suburbs and where farm employment formed a small share of the local economy. Neither constituency, counted alone in the House of Representatives, supplied a reliable floor majority for the other constituency's priorities. The combined bill solved that arithmetic problem by placing both sets of provisions in one vehicle, under the jurisdiction of the House and Senate Agriculture Committees, so that a member voting for the vehicle voted for the whole bargain at once. The events of 2012, 2013, and 2014 subjected that arrangement to a direct procedural test. A combined bill failed in the House. A farm bill stripped of its nutrition title then passed the House. A separate nutrition bill then passed the House by a similarly narrow margin. Neither half completed the legislative process on its own. Enactment arrived only after the two halves were recombined and sent to conference against a combined Senate bill, and the resulting conference report passed both chambers and was signed into law as the Agricultural Act of 2014. The sequence, as recorded in the chronology maintained by the Congressional Research Service, supplied the clearest modern demonstration that the combination was load bearing.

Understanding the test requires attention to what expiration meant for the underlying statutes. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 supplied the authorizations and program terms that governed commodity support, conservation, crop insurance, and nutrition assistance for its coverage period. That act expired on September 30, 2012. Expiration did not erase the older permanent law that modern farm bills suspended or amended for their coverage periods, principally provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949. Expiration instead raised the prospect of reversion to that permanent law if no successor or extension was enacted. For dairy, the reversion point arrived first, because dairy support under permanent law operated on a calendar year basis beginning January 1 after expiration. The Agricultural Act of 1949 mandated dairy support at 75 to 90 percent of parity, carried out through government purchases of manufactured dairy products. The Congressional Research Service, in its account of the 2013 to 2014 endgame, cited a milk support price near 40 dollars per hundredweight under reversion against an average all milk price near 20 dollars per hundredweight during 2013. That gap explained why the calendar, and the dairy calendar in particular, pressed on negotiators even when other titles generated the sharpest floor disagreement.

### Did the split farm bills become law?

No. Neither half became law on its own. The House passed a farm only bill and a separate nutrition bill in 2013, but the two measures were recombined by House Resolution 361 before conference, and enactment came only through the recombined Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, signed February 7, 2014.

The runway to the split began in the final year of the 2008 act. During 2012 the Senate acted on a combined successor and the House did not. The Senate passed S. 3240, a combined farm bill, on June 21, 2012, by a vote of 64 to 35. The House passed no farm bill during 2012. The absence of a House passed bill meant that no conference committee could be formed, because conference requires comparable measures passed by both chambers. The disagreement was therefore not between two texts but between action in one chamber and inaction in the other, a posture that left extension as the only near term legislative option once the September 30, 2012 expiration date had passed.

Extension arrived as part of a larger fiscal measure rather than as a farm bill in its own right. The American Taxpayer Relief Act, Public Law 112-240, was signed on January 2, 2013, and Title VII of that act extended the expired 2008 bill for one year. The extension ran through September 30, 2013 for the covered programs generally, with dairy provisions running to December 31, 2013. The differing dates reflected the differing clocks in the underlying law. The general extension tracked the federal fiscal year, which ended on September 30. The dairy date tracked the calendar year turn at which permanent law dairy support would have taken effect. The extension bought time without settling any of the contested questions. Program terms largely carried forward, the combined structure carried forward with them, and the same floor arithmetic that had blocked a House bill during 2012 awaited the committees when work resumed in the new Congress.

The new Congress first attempted the conventional route, and for a time the conventional route appeared viable in the Senate. The Senate passed a combined bill, S. 954, on June 10, 2013, by a vote of 66 to 27. The margin was two votes wider than the Senate margin of the prior year, and the bill again joined the farm titles and the nutrition title in a single measure. Senate passage established one half of the predicate for conference. If the House passed a combined bill of its own, even a combined bill with substantially different spending levels and program terms, the two chambers could resolve the differences in conference and present a single report to both floors. That path depended entirely on the House producing a combined vehicle that could command 218 votes, or a majority of members voting when a quorum was present, on the House floor.

The House combined bill failed that test ten days later. H.R. 1947, the House combined farm bill, failed on June 20, 2013, by a vote of 195 to 234. The defeat carried information that a committee approval or a leadership scheduling decision could not supply on its own. A combined bill that joined farm programs and nutrition assistance, at the spending levels and under the terms the House Agriculture Committee had reported, could not assemble a majority in the full House. Opposition came from more than one direction, and the structure of the vehicle meant that a member opposed to the nutrition terms and a member opposed to the commodity terms both recorded votes against the same bill for opposite reasons. The combined form, which in earlier cycles had pooled support, in that instance also pooled opposition. The question that followed the defeat was diagnostic as much as tactical. Leadership and committee members had to determine whether the combined bill had failed because the bargain itself lacked a majority or because particular terms within the bargain had driven away members who might support the components under a different structure. The split strategy of July and September 2013 was the method chosen to answer that question on the record.

The first half of the split reached the floor in July. The House stripped the nutrition title from the vehicle and passed a farm only bill, H.R. 2642, on July 11, 2013, by a vote of 216 to 208. The Congressional Research Service chronology recorded the composition of that vote with unusual precision because the composition was the point. No Democrat voted in favor of the bill. Twelve Republicans voted against the bill. The majority therefore consisted entirely of members of one party, assembled without a single vote from the other party, and it prevailed by eight votes. A margin of that size left no room for defections beyond those already recorded and no evidence of the broad geographic coalition that combined bills had traditionally claimed as their justification. The farm only bill demonstrated that commodity, conservation, credit, rural development, research, forestry, energy, horticulture, and crop insurance provisions, separated from nutrition assistance, could pass the House on a party line basis. The same vote demonstrated the limits of that achievement. A bill passed by one chamber on a party line vote of 216 to 208 faced a Senate that had passed a combined bill by 66 to 27 and a conference process in which the nutrition title could not simply be treated as absent, because the Senate text contained the title and the Senate conferees represented a chamber that had voted for the combined structure twice in two years.

The historical weight of the July vote lay in its departure from four decades of practice. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, was the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program, according to the Congressional Research Service. From that act forward, through the Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, Public Law 95-113, which carried the Food Stamp Act of 1977 inside the farm bill, and through each successive reauthorization, the farm titles and the nutrition title had moved in the same vehicle. The July 11, 2013 passage of H.R. 2642 was the first time since the combination of the early 1970s that a chamber had passed a farm bill without the nutrition provisions. The break severed, for purposes of one House passed bill, the jurisdictional and electoral linkage that had defined the farm bill as an institution since 1973.

The second half of the split followed after the August recess, on a separate track and under separate terms. The House passed a nutrition only bill, H.R. 3102, on September 19, 2013, by a vote of 217 to 210. The margin was one vote narrower than the margin for the farm only bill, and the total in favor was one vote higher, a pairing that underscored how closely divided the House was on each component when the components stood alone. The nutrition bill proposed reductions of roughly 40 billion dollars over ten years in nutrition assistance, as scored at the time, a figure that placed the House position far from the Senate position in the combined S. 954. Standing alone, the nutrition bill made that distance explicit and attributable. Members recorded a vote on nutrition assistance as a discrete question, separated from any judgment about commodity programs or crop insurance, and the resulting 217 to 210 tally showed that a bare majority existed for the House terms and that nearly half the chamber opposed those terms even without the added weight of a farm title attached. Taken together, the July and September votes answered the diagnostic question posed by the June defeat with considerable precision. Each half of the former combined bill could command a bare House majority on its own terms. Neither half commanded anything resembling the broader majorities that the Senate had recorded for the combined bill, and neither half, as a freestanding vehicle, supplied a plausible route to enactment through a Senate committed to the combined form.

Procedure then intervened, and procedure determined what happened next. Neither half became law. A bill passed by the House alone has no legal effect. Enactment requires passage of the same text by both chambers and presentation to the President, or passage over a presidential veto by two thirds of both chambers. H.R. 2642 and H.R. 3102 were different bills with different titles, different program terms, and different bill numbers. The Senate had passed neither of them. The Senate had instead passed S. 954, a combined bill that corresponded in scope to the union of the two House measures rather than to either one individually. For the House and Senate to enter conference, the two chambers needed positions on the same legislative vehicle that a conference committee could reconcile. The separate House bills, in their separate forms, did not supply that predicate in a usable way. The split had clarified House preferences title by title while simultaneously complicating the bicameral mechanics required to turn any preference into a statute.

The recombination step resolved the mechanics by undoing the split for conference purposes. On September 28, 2013, the House adopted House Resolution 361, which combined the two House passed bills into a single text under H.R. 2642 so that the House could go to conference with the Senate on a combined measure. The resolution was a procedural instrument rather than a new substantive bill, and no separate floor majority for a combined farm bill was recorded at that stage in the form of a passage vote on a combined text. The importance of the step lay in what it conceded. To use the conference process at all, the House position had to be presented as one combined text set against the Senate combined text of S. 954. The stage at which the two chambers negotiated their differences was also the stage described in the series account of [how conference committees reconcile House and Senate bills](https://insightcrunch.com/2022/03/15/conference-committees-explained/), where conferees appointed by each chamber work from the two passed texts and produce a single report that neither chamber may amend. Recombination was therefore not a cosmetic packaging choice layered on top of a successful split strategy. Recombination was the admission, in procedural form, that the split strategy had reached the limit of what a single chamber could accomplish and that further progress required restoring the structure the split had set aside.

Conference occupied the final months of 2013 and the opening weeks of 2014, against the dairy deadline that the American Taxpayer Relief Act had set for December 31, 2013 and against the general extension deadline of September 30, 2013, which had already passed by the time the conference report was completed. The pressure was concrete and dated. Permanent law dairy support at 75 to 90 percent of parity, implemented through government purchases of manufactured dairy products, stood behind the negotiation as the default that would govern if conferees failed. Conferees therefore negotiated not against an absence of law but against an old law that neither chamber's majority regarded as a workable governing framework for dairy markets in 2014. That default strengthened the incentive to finish a combined report even among members who had voted for a split vehicle or for deeper nutrition reductions than the conference report ultimately contained.

The conference report was filed on January 27, 2014. The House approved the report on January 29, 2014, by a vote of 251 to 166. The Senate approved the report on February 4, 2014, by a vote of 68 to 32. Each margin told a distinct part of the story. The House margin of 251 to 166 exceeded the totals recorded for either half of the split, exceeding the 216 votes for the farm only bill by 35 votes and the 217 votes for the nutrition only bill by 34 votes, and the report passed the House with a cushion of 85 votes rather than a cushion of eight or seven. The Senate margin of 68 to 32 exceeded the 66 to 27 margin for S. 954 and again crossed the sixty vote threshold. Because a conference report is not amendable on either floor, each chamber voted on the identical recombined text, with the commodity titles and the nutrition title bound together in a single up or down decision. The totals showed that the recombined bargain, at the terms conferees had negotiated, assembled larger majorities in both chambers than either separated component had assembled in the House. The Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, was signed on February 7, 2014, and the combined structure became law again in full.

The substantive terms of the 2014 act marked the scale of the settlement that recombination made possible. The act recombined the commodity titles and the nutrition title in one statute. The act ended direct payments, and the act created two new commodity programs in their place, Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage, through which producers made program elections tied to different forms of revenue and price protection. Changes of that breadth required agreement across titles, because the savings, the coverage choices, and the distribution of support among commodities, conservation, and nutrition assistance were negotiated as parts of one budgetary and political whole. The conference settlement joined the two decisions, as every enacted farm bill since 1973 had joined them, and the joining was the condition on which the individual decisions became law.

The lesson of the episode, stated as analysis of the recorded votes and the recorded procedural steps, concerns the difference between passage and enactment. Passage by one chamber is a necessary stage in the legislative process and a meaningful measure of preference within that chamber. Enactment is a separate achievement that requires concurrent majorities in two chambers for the same text and, in the ordinary course, a presidential signature. The split of 2013 isolated the first achievement from the second with unusual clarity. The farm only bill passed. The nutrition only bill passed. Neither enactment followed. The House then recombined the two passed bills by resolution, entered conference on the recombined text, and obtained enactment for the recombined bill by wider margins than either half had obtained. Defenders of the combined structure, including members and committee leaders who had maintained the combined vehicle across earlier cycles, described the outcome as confirmation that mutual dependence between rural and urban constituencies reflected the underlying distribution of votes in Congress rather than a courtesy between committees. Fiscal critics of the combined structure, including organizations and members who had argued that joining the titles insulated both commodity programs and nutrition assistance from separate scrutiny, could point to the June 2013 defeat of H.R. 1947 and to the narrow split votes as evidence that separate consideration produced closer votes and more explicit recorded positions on each component. Both readings drew support from the same chronology, and neither reading altered the central procedural fact. When a statute was finally enacted, the statute was combined.

That central fact is the strongest modern evidence that the coalition behind the farm bill was structural rather than decorative. A decorative coalition would have been expected to dissolve once a chamber demonstrated that each component could pass alone. The House demonstrated exactly that during the summer and fall of 2013, by margins of 216 to 208 and 217 to 210. Dissolution did not follow. The Senate position remained combined, the conference mechanism required a combined House text, the dairy reversion default pressed for a comprehensive settlement, and the final majorities formed around a combined report. The coalition therefore operated at the level of bicameral process and not merely at the level of floor rhetoric or committee custom. The 2013 split remained, within the record available at the November 1, 2020 vantage of the series, the one sustained attempt by a chamber to legislate the two halves apart, and its failure to produce a law in split form defined the boundary of what the farm bill coalition could be asked to survive.

## Permanent Law and the Five-Year Clock

Every modern farm bill operates against a background that most statutes do not possess. Behind the temporary programs that dominate debate in a reauthorization year stands an older body of commodity law that never expired. That body of law is called permanent law, and its persistence explains both the rhythm of the farm bill cycle and the urgency that gathers as an expiration date approaches. A new farm bill does not ordinarily repeal the older statutes and replace them with a fresh code. A new farm bill instead suspends or amends the older provisions for a defined coverage period, layering modern program designs over foundations laid in the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and in [the Agricultural Act of 1949 and the permanent law backstop](https://insightcrunch.com/2020/12/01/agricultural-act-1949-permanent-law/). The Agricultural Act of 1949, Public Law 81-439, enacted on October 31, 1949, supplied the second of those foundations, and the two statutes together form the reversion point to which commodity policy returns when a temporary bill lapses without a successor or an extension.

The technique of suspension rather than repeal carries consequences that shape legislative behavior. Because the permanent provisions remain on the books in suspended form, expiration does not create a vacuum in the commodities covered by those provisions. Expiration instead reactivates a program design that Congress built for an earlier agricultural economy, with price support levels and administrative methods drawn from the middle of the twentieth century. Legislative leaders, committee members, and Department of Agriculture officials therefore treat expiration not as a neutral pause but as an affirmative change in policy, one that no participant in the reauthorization process designed for later conditions. The Congressional Research Service has described the arrangement in terms of a forcing device, and the description captures the effect. The prospect of reversion forces a decision. Congress may enact a new bill, extend the existing bill for a limited period, or allow older law to govern until a later enactment displaces it again. Inaction is itself an outcome, and the structure of permanent law makes that outcome conspicuous, dated, and uneven in its effects across commodities.

### What happens if Congress lets the farm bill lapse?

If Congress lets a farm bill lapse without an extension or a successor, the temporary suspensions expire and permanent law from 1938 and 1949 revives for supported commodities, dairy first, while commodities without permanent support lose authority and nutrition assistance continues through appropriations rather than ending automatically.

The unevenness in that answer is the central fact about a lapse. A farm bill contains many titles, and those titles rest on different legal foundations. The commodity title rests most directly on the permanent law framework because the support programs for the major field crops and for dairy trace their statutory ancestry to the acts of 1938 and 1949. Other titles have distinct authorization histories, and the nutrition title rests on separate legislation administered through the farm bill vehicle since the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973 first incorporated food stamp reauthorization into the omnibus measure. A lapse therefore does not produce a single uniform shutdown. A lapse produces a differentiated result in which some programs revert to older and higher support formulas, some programs lose the authority under which payments proceeded, and some programs continue because separate appropriations or separate permanent authority sustain them.

The mechanics begin with the drafting method of modern farm bills. A typical provision in a modern commodity title does not state a permanent rule for all future years. A typical provision instead amends or suspends specified sections of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 or the Agricultural Act of 1949 for the crop years or fiscal years covered by the new act. Price Loss Coverage, Agriculture Risk Coverage, marketing assistance loans at modern loan rates, and the dairy margin programs of later decades all function as temporary overlays. Each overlay carries an expiration tied to the coverage period of its parent act, and the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, followed that pattern with coverage through September 30, 2023. When the overlay expires and Congress has enacted neither a successor nor an extension, the suspended text governs again by its own terms. The older program returns because Congress never repealed it.

The parity concept gives reversion its economic force. Parity, as developed in the legislation of the 1930s and carried into the acts of 1938 and 1949, expresses a relationship between prices farmers receive and prices farmers pay, benchmarked to the base period from 1910 to 1914. That base period came to represent, in the statutory scheme, a balance between agricultural and nonagricultural prices that policymakers of the era treated as equitable. Parity prices are computed and published by the Department of Agriculture under the framework of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, and the computation translates the base period relationship into a price level for a given commodity in a later year. A support level expressed as a percentage of parity therefore moves with the general structure of prices rather than remaining fixed at a nominal dollar figure set in a committee report. Support at a high percentage of parity in a period when farm prices have fallen relative to the parity benchmark can stand far above market prices, and government intervention at that level requires purchases, loans, or payments on a scale that transforms federal involvement in the affected market.

Dairy illustrates the scale of that transformation with unusual clarity, and the dairy provisions of the Agricultural Act of 1949 account for the phrase that dominated coverage of the 2013 to 2014 endgame. The act of 1949 mandates support for milk at 75 to 90 percent of parity, and the Department of Agriculture carries out that mandate through government purchases of manufactured dairy products, including butter, cheese, and nonfat dry milk, at prices calculated to allow handlers to pay producers the support level for milk. The 1949 mechanism obliges the government to enter the market as a buyer of storable products in whatever quantities are offered at the support price, accumulating stocks whose storage and disposition become federal responsibilities. During the endgame that produced the Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, the Congressional Research Service cited prices under which reversion implied a milk support price near 40 dollars per hundredweight against an average all milk price near 20 dollars per hundredweight in 2013. A support price at roughly double the market average would have redirected milk into manufactured products purchased by the government, raised consumer prices for dairy products as market prices moved toward the support level, and placed the Department of Agriculture in the position of holding inventories that commercial channels had not absorbed. That arithmetic, rather than any rhetorical invention, is why reversion concentrated minds.

Timing compounds the dairy problem and explains why dairy is described as the first commodity to reach the cliff. Dairy support under permanent law operates on a calendar year or crop year beginning January 1 after expiration, a schedule the Congressional Research Service has identified as the reason dairy reverts before commodities whose crop years begin later in the calendar. A farm bill expiring on September 30 therefore leaves a three month interval before dairy reversion takes hold, and that interval concentrates negotiation. The endgame of 2013 demonstrated the pattern. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 expired on September 30, 2012, and the American Taxpayer Relief Act, Public Law 112-240, signed on January 2, 2013, extended the 2008 bill through September 30, 2013, with dairy programs extended through December 31, 2013. When the Agricultural Act of 2014 was signed on February 7, 2014, the dairy cliff had provided the deadline pressure under which the final agreement closed.

The partial character of the permanent law backstop deserves equal attention because public discussion of expiration often treats reversion as comprehensive. Permanent law would support a defined set of commodities, principally dairy, wheat, rice, cotton, corn, and other feed grains. For those commodities, a lapse substitutes the older support architecture, including nonrecourse loan concepts from the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and parity based support from the act of 1949, for the modern programs of price loss and revenue coverage. The substitution is disruptive precisely because the machinery functions. Commodity Credit Corporation purchases, loan activity, and support operations would proceed under formulas that assume a midcentury market structure, with acreage and production relationships unlike those of the period in which the lapse occurred. Processors, cooperatives, and producers in the covered commodities would face a program that pays, lends, and buys at levels detached from prevailing market signals, and the Department of Agriculture would administer that program under statutory instructions that leave limited discretion to moderate the result.

A second group of commodities stands in the opposite position because no permanent law supports them. Several commodities that receive support under modern farm bills, among them soybeans, peanuts, sugar, and dry peas and lentils, hold no permanent law support and would lose their programs if a farm bill lapsed without an extension. Soybeans occupy an instructive place in that group because of the scale of soybean production and the dependence of soybean growers on the price loss and revenue programs, marketing loans, and related structures authorized through modern acts. For those commodities, expiration produces absence rather than reversion. No older formula revives, no government purchase program activates, and no parity calculation supplies a floor. The contrast between the two groups explains why expiration politics never align commodity organizations in a single bloc. Organizations representing covered commodities fear an unworkable program, organizations representing uncovered commodities fear no program at all, and both fears press Congress toward action through different channels and with different preferred remedies.

Nutrition assistance occupies a third position that separates it from the permanent law question altogether. Nutrition programs, principally the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, do not depend on the Agricultural Act of 1949, and a farm bill lapse does not by itself end food assistance. The nutrition title is reauthorized through the farm bill vehicle because of the coalition history that joined food assistance to the agriculture committees in 1973 and carried the Food Stamp Act of 1977 inside the Food and Agriculture Act of 1977, Public Law 95-113, but the program operates as authorized mandatory spending funded through appropriations. Appropriations therefore determine whether benefits continue during a gap in authorization, and appropriations practice has sustained nutrition assistance while reauthorization remained pending. A lapse strains the legislative coalition and may place nutrition reauthorization at the center of political dispute, as the separate House passages of a farm only bill and a nutrition only bill in 2013 demonstrated. A lapse does not, by operation of permanent commodity law, terminate benefits or suspend the statutory eligibility structure of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.

The Commodity Credit Corporation supplies the financial plumbing that connects permanent law to modern practice in quiet years as well as in a crisis. The corporation was created in 1933 and received a permanent federal charter under the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948. Through that charter, the corporation operates as a government owned corporation within the Department of Agriculture, and its borrowing authority with the Treasury provides the funding channel through which mandatory farm bill payments flow. Commodity loans, support purchases, and price loss and revenue payments in modern acts move through the corporation rather than through a separate appropriation for each payment at the moment of disbursement. The arrangement gives commodity programs a standing financial capacity that matches the timing of farm markets, in which obligations arise when prices fall or harvests conclude. The corporation that would execute parity support after a lapse is the same corporation that executes modern support in ordinary years, and its charter and borrowing authority form part of the permanent structure that temporary bills use without recreating.

Extensions occupy the middle ground between enactment and reversion. An extension carries existing programs forward for a defined period, preserving modern program parameters while Congress completes work on a successor, as the one year extension in the American Taxpayer Relief Act at the opening of 2013 did after the 2008 act expired. An extension requires affirmative legislation and much of the same coalition assembly as a new bill, though the policy content is agreement to continue rather than agreement to redesign.

The five year rhythm of the modern cycle grew out of the postwar consolidations that organized the permanent law structure. The acts of 1948 and 1949 consolidated the measures of the preceding fifteen years into standing frameworks, with the act of 1949 establishing the enduring support schedules that later bills would suspend. The Congressional Research Service counts 18 farm bills from 1933 through 2018 in the full sequence. A roughly five year interval became customary because commodity programs were authorized for a set of crop years and because the agriculture committees developed an expectation that each generation of programs would be revisited before price relationships and production patterns moved too far from the assumptions on which the programs rested. Each act writes its own expiration into law, converting that expectation into a dated obligation to legislate again.

The period after the postwar consolidations tested the forcing device early. In 1956, President Dwight D. Eisenhower vetoed H.R. 12, the first farm bill veto in the sequence recorded by the Congressional Research Service, and the veto stood. Congress then passed a revised bill built around the Soil Bank, an acreage retirement and conservation program enacted as Public Law 84-540, and the President signed the revised measure. The episode established that presidential resistance could reshape a farm bill within a single cycle without breaking the cycle itself, and that a veto operated against the same permanent law background as an expiration. Inaction would have revived older support levels at high percentages of parity, a prospect that administrations seeking lower and more flexible support had strong reasons to avoid.

Subsequent decades layered new purposes onto the commodity foundation without displacing the reversion structure. The Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, marked a turning point in the composition of the vehicle by joining food stamp reauthorization to the commodity titles and by introducing target prices and deficiency payments, developments treated in full in the discussion of the farm bill coalition elsewhere in this article and noted in this section only for periodization. The conservation turn arrived with the Food Security Act of 1985, which created the Conservation Reserve Program and authorized long term retirement of environmentally sensitive cropland under contract. Conservation had appeared in farm legislation since the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936 bridged the interval after United States v. Butler, but the act of 1985 gave conservation a programmatic scale and a lasting place in the title structure of later bills. The Conservation Reserve Program enrolled land for contract periods, paid annual rental payments to participants, and established conservation as a constituency with its own stake in timely reauthorization, since contracts, rental rates, and enrollment authority all depended on the statutory cycle.

The market turn of the 1990s tested whether the cycle could move policy away from support rather than merely adjust its levels. The Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996, Public Law 104-127, styled Freedom to Farm in political debate, decoupled fixed payments from production decisions for a seven year period, replacing deficiency payments tied to market prices with production flexibility contract payments set on a declining schedule. The design rested on the premise that producers would respond to market signals rather than to program incentives and that fixed payments would ease a transition toward reduced federal direction of planting decisions. Falling prices in the late 1990s interrupted that trajectory. Congress responded with emergency market loss payments that supplemented the fixed payments on several occasions during those years, and the Farm Security and Rural Investment Act of 2002, Public Law 107-171, restored counter cyclical support as a standing feature of the commodity title, pairing fixed direct payments with payments triggered when prices fell below statutory reference levels. The sequence from 1996 to 2002 demonstrated the limits of statutory redesign against market conditions. A bill could decouple payments and announce a transition, and the same Congress or a successor Congress could reattach support to prices when market returns collapsed, all within the framework of temporary amendments resting on the permanent law base.

The act of 2008 occupied a distinct procedural place in the sequence as the only farm bill enacted over presidential vetoes, a distinction recorded by the Congressional Research Service. The full account of that enactment belongs to the treatment of the 2008 act in this article and is not repeated in this compressed periodization.

The Agricultural Act of 2014 rebuilt commodity support around a choice between revenue and price protection after a reauthorization process that had consumed the better part of two years. The act ended direct payments, the fixed payments whose origins lay in the 1996 decoupling, and created Agriculture Risk Coverage, which protected against revenue losses, and Price Loss Coverage, which paid when market prices fell below reference prices. Dairy policy moved toward margin protection based on the difference between milk prices and feed costs, a redesign undertaken with the 1949 purchase program standing as the reversion alternative throughout the negotiation. The act of 2014 also recombined the commodity and nutrition titles after the House had passed them separately in 2013, restoring the omnibus form that has carried the coalition since 1973. In structural terms, the 2014 act confirmed that reform within the cycle proceeds by replacing one temporary overlay with another. Direct payments, a fixture for nearly two decades, ended through the same statutory method that had created them, while permanent law beneath the overlay remained untouched.

The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 continued the architecture of the 2014 act while making changes of its own. Producers retained the election between Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage, with adjustments to reference price mechanics and yield provisions, and the conservation, credit, trade, research, forestry, energy, horticulture, crop insurance, and miscellaneous titles carried forward the twelve title structure of that act. Among the changes of 2018, the treatment of hemp drew particular notice. The act of 2018 removed hemp from the Controlled Substances Act framework and treated hemp as an agricultural commodity. The hemp provisions illustrated the breadth the omnibus vehicle had acquired. A bill whose forcing device rested on dairy purchases and parity formulas from 1949 also served as the instrument for reclassifying a plant under federal law.

Periodization of the full cycle therefore reveals a pattern that a list of enactment dates conceals. The foundational period from 1933 to 1949 built the machinery of production control, parity, nonrecourse loans, and the Commodity Credit Corporation, and consolidated that machinery into permanent law. The postwar decades adjusted support levels and added acreage retirement, food assistance, and conservation while leaving the foundation in place. The period from 1973 to 1985 joined nutrition to the vehicle and gave conservation a major program, and the period from 1996 to 2002 attempted a market oriented transition before restoring counter cyclical support. Through every phase, the temporary character of each enactment preserved the forcing device, and every phase ended with a date on which the foundation would again govern if legislators failed to act.

That persistence raises an analytical question about why Congress retains a reversion point that nearly every participant describes as unworkable. Part of the answer lies in the difficulty of repeal. Repealing permanent law without enacting a replacement would remove support authority for the covered commodities entirely, creating the vacuum that the suspension technique was designed to avoid, and would require agreement on an end state for federal commodity policy that no coalition has assembled. Part of the answer lies in the utility of the device itself. Committees charged with an omnibus bill spanning twelve titles in the act of 2018 face a recurring coordination problem, and a deadline with real consequences solves part of that problem by giving leadership a reason to schedule floor time and reluctant members a reason to accept a conference agreement. The distribution of costs under reversion, heavy for dairy, severe in a different way for uncovered commodities, and indirect for nutrition, shapes the bargaining that a deadline sets in motion.

The five year clock, viewed across the whole sequence from 1933 through 2018, has produced a distinctive form of legislative durability. Programs change substantially between bills, constituencies enter and leave the coalition, and budget shares shift between nutrition, crop insurance, commodities, and conservation as economic conditions change, yet the vehicle returns on schedule because the statutes demand a return. The farm bill endures as an institution not because any single enactment settled agricultural policy but because no enactment was permitted to settle it permanently. Each Congress that writes a farm bill writes an expiration date into its own work and thereby binds a future Congress to revisit questions of price support, risk management, land retirement, food assistance, credit, and rural development as a connected set. Permanent law stands behind that discipline as both a historical artifact and an active threat, a body of 1938 and 1949 provisions whose parity formulas and purchase mandates remain capable, on any January 1 following an expiration, of demonstrating that the cost of failing to legislate is not stasis. The cost is a program from another era, operating at full statutory force, in markets that have moved beyond the assumptions on which that program rested.

## The Vehicle That Holds

The six sections of this account describe one institution from six directions, and the descriptions converge on a single thesis. The farm bill is the recurring omnibus where food assistance and farm support are bound in a single vehicle held together by coalition and forced by permanent law. The arithmetic of the first section established the identity of the vehicle, because a bill in which the nutrition title accounted for about 77 percent of projected outlays in the Congressional Budget Office baseline of April 2018 for fiscal 2019 to 2028, at 663.8 billion dollars of an 867 billion dollar total, could not be understood through the farm name on the cover. The constitutional account established the foundations, because the spending power reading adopted in United States v. Butler and the commerce power holding in Wickard v. Filburn defined the space inside which every later title operated. The tour of the twelve titles of the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 established the range, from commodity elections and conservation contracts to trade promotion, rural water systems, and crop insurance. The coalition account established the politics, because the combination settled in 1973 joined constituencies that commanded no floor majority alone. The split of 2013 established the limit of separation, and the permanent law account established the clock, because reversion to the acts of 1938 and 1949 waited behind every expiration date.

Gathered under the thesis, the evidence also corrects [the myths that gather around the farm bill](https://insightcrunch.com/2021/03/15/farm-bill-myths/), among them the belief that most farm bill money reached commodity producers, the belief that food assistance entered the vehicle as a late and accidental addition, and the belief that the twelve title structure of the act of 2018 described a permanent architecture rather than the arrangement of one Congress. The record points the other way on each count. Nutrition held the largest share across both the ten year baseline presentation and the five year projection at enactment of the act of 2018. Jurisdiction came first, in the Food Stamp Act of 1964, and combination followed by deliberate committee agreement in 1973. Title counts varied across the series, with fifteen titles in the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008 and twelve in the act of 2018. A reader tracing eighteen enactments across eight decades, from the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933 to Public Law 115-334, has practical reason to [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html), because the meaning of the vehicle lies in comparisons across cycles that no single enactment supplies alone.

The strongest objection to the thesis deserves statement at full strength. A coalition reading can understate how often the vehicle nearly failed. The Senate passed a combined bill in June 2012 by 64 to 35, and the House passed no farm bill during 2012 at all. The House combined bill failed on June 20, 2013, by 195 to 234, and enactment in that cycle arrived only in February 2014, well past the expiration of September 30, 2012. In the following cycle, the first House passage vote on the act of 2018 failed on May 18, 2018, by 198 to 213, and the bill passed the House a month later by two votes. A vehicle that repeatedly required extensions, recombination by resolution, and second floor attempts looks less like a stable institution and more like a recurring accident, and a coalition account has to explain the failures rather than explain the failures away.

The record absorbs the objection rather than defeating it, because the near failures display the same mechanisms that the successes display, operating under strain. The split bills of 2013 passed the House by 216 to 208 and 217 to 210 and became law in neither case, while the recombined conference report passed the House by 251 to 166 and the Senate by 68 to 32. The failed House vote of May 2018 preceded a conference report that the House approved by 369 to 47 and the Senate approved by 87 to 13. Permanent law supplied the pressure that converted deadlock into settlement in each episode, because the alternative to a conference agreement was reversion to parity based dairy support at 75 to 90 percent of parity under the Agricultural Act of 1949, a default that the Congressional Research Service priced during the 2013 to 2014 endgame at a milk support price near 40 dollars per hundredweight against an average all milk price near 20 dollars per hundredweight in 2013. The coalition did not prevent failure at intermediate stages. The coalition determined the shape of the recovery from failure, and permanent law determined that recovery arrived before reversion governed.

Placed on the long congressional arc, the farm bill belongs to a small class of enactments that Congress rebuilds on a schedule written into law. The foundational period from 1933 to 1949 built production control, parity, nonrecourse loans, and the Commodity Credit Corporation, and consolidated that machinery into permanent law. The combination of 1973 joined nutrition to the vehicle, the Food Security Act of 1985 created the Conservation Reserve Program, and the Federal Agriculture Improvement and Reform Act of 1996 attempted a decoupled transition that the Farm Security and Rural Investment Act of 2002 partly reversed through restored counter cyclical support. The act of 2014 ended direct payments and created Agriculture Risk Coverage and Price Loss Coverage, and the act of 2018 carried the twelve title structure forward through fiscal 2019 to 2023. Across that arc, programs rose and fell, majorities narrowed and widened, and one chamber tested separation and returned to combination. The vehicle held because coalition supplied the votes, permanent law supplied the deadline, and the omnibus form supplied the package inside which both forces could act at once.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is actually in a farm bill?

A farm bill is an omnibus act whose contents reach far beyond commodity support. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 carried twelve titles covering commodities, conservation, trade, nutrition, credit, rural development, research and extension, forestry, energy, horticulture, crop insurance, and miscellaneous provisions. Inside those titles sat income and price support for covered commodities, conservation contracts and easements, export promotion and international food assistance, domestic food assistance through the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and smaller commodity distribution programs, farm ownership and operating loans, rural housing, water, broadband, and business programs, agricultural research and extension through land grant universities, national forest management authorities, rural renewable energy programs, specialty crop and organic programs, the federal crop insurance system, and provisions on animal health, beginning farmers, and other subjects that crossed title boundaries.

### Q: How often does a farm bill get renewed?

A farm bill is renewed on a cycle of roughly five years. Each enactment typically covers five crop or fiscal years, and the authorization periods written into the act set the date by which Congress faces the choice of enacting a successor, passing an extension, or allowing expiration to revive permanent law. The Congressional Research Service counted eighteen farm bills enacted from 1933 through 2018, and the intervals in that sequence varied, because war, depression, party control of Congress, commodity price conditions, and floor failures all disturbed the calendar at different points. The later decades of the sequence settled into the roughly five year rhythm, with enactments in 2002, 2008, 2014, and 2018, although the cycle that produced the Agricultural Act of 2014 consumed the better part of two years after the act of 2008 expired on September 30, 2012.

### Q: How many titles does a farm bill have?

The number of titles varies from one farm bill to the next, because Congress adds, merges, and separates titles as programs grow and as jurisdiction and negotiation require. The Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 carried twelve titles, running in order from Commodities through Conservation, Trade, Nutrition, Credit, Rural Development, Research, Extension, and Related Matters, Forestry, Energy, Horticulture, and Crop Insurance to Miscellaneous. The Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008, by comparison, carried fifteen titles. A title count is therefore a record of how a particular Congress arranged a particular bill rather than a fixed property of the vehicle, and no statute fixes the number at twelve or at any other figure. The arrangement also does not rank the titles by size, since the 2018 act placed commodity support first while the nutrition title, placed fourth, accounted for the largest share of projected outlays.

### Q: Why did food assistance end up in the farm bill?

Food assistance entered the farm bill through a jurisdictional decision followed by a deliberate legislative combination. The Food Stamp Act of 1964, Public Law 88-525, signed in August 1964, made food stamps a permanent program administered by the Department of Agriculture, with legislative jurisdiction in the House and Senate Agriculture Committees. Through the early 1970s the commodity programs and the food stamp program moved on separate tracks, although both were scheduled to expire together and appropriations fights had targeted both. The House and Senate Agriculture Committees agreed to combine the programs for the 1973 reauthorization, and the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973, Public Law 93-86, became the first farm bill to incorporate reauthorization of the food stamp program. The combination created a durable exchange in which urban members supplied votes for commodity support and rural members supplied votes for nutrition assistance.

### Q: What happened when the House split the farm bill in 2013?

The House split followed the failure of a combined bill. H.R. 1947, the House combined farm bill, failed on June 20, 2013, by 195 to 234. The House then passed a farm only bill, H.R. 2642, with the nutrition title stripped, on July 11, 2013, by 216 to 208, with twelve Republicans voting no and no Democrat voting yes. A separate nutrition only bill, H.R. 3102, passed on September 19, 2013, by 217 to 210. Neither half became law on its own. On September 28, 2013, House Resolution 361 combined the two House passed bills into H.R. 2642 so the House could go to conference with the Senate, which had passed a combined bill. The conference report passed both chambers, and the Agricultural Act of 2014, Public Law 113-79, signed February 7, 2014, recombined commodities and nutrition in one statute.

### Q: Was a farm bill ever passed over a veto twice?

Yes, and the episode belongs to the Food, Conservation, and Energy Act of 2008. President George W. Bush vetoed H.R. 2419 on May 21, 2008, and Congress overrode the veto on May 22, 2008, by 316 to 108 in the House and 82 to 13 in the Senate, enacting Public Law 110-234 without Title III, the trade title, which a clerical error of 34 pages had omitted from the enrolled bill. Congress re-passed the complete bill as H.R. 6124, by 306 to 110 in the House and 77 to 15 in the Senate. The President vetoed that bill on June 18, 2008, and Congress overrode the veto on the same day, by 317 to 109 in the House and 80 to 14 in the Senate, enacting Public Law 110-246, which repealed Public Law 110-234. Per the Congressional Research Service, the 2008 act is the only farm bill enacted over a veto, and the override happened twice in one season.

### Q: Which committees write the farm bill?

The House Committee on Agriculture and the Senate Committee on Agriculture, Nutrition, and Forestry write the farm bill. The two agriculture committees hold jurisdiction over the combined vehicle, including nutrition assistance, because the Food Stamp Act of 1964 placed the food stamp program in the Department of Agriculture under the agriculture committees, and the combination of 1973 carried that jurisdiction into the omnibus reauthorization. Each committee drafts, hears witnesses, and marks up a bill for its own chamber. The appropriations committees play a separate role, because discretionary programs authorized in the farm bill receive money only through appropriations acts, and the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program is funded through appropriations as open ended mandatory spending. When the House and Senate pass different versions, a conference committee drawn heavily from the agriculture committees merges the two chambers bills into a single conference report that each chamber accepts or rejects as a package.

### Q: What did Wickard v. Filburn have to do with the farm bill?

Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942), sustained the regulatory foundation on which the farm bill commodity programs rest. Roscoe Filburn, an Ohio farmer, received an allotment of 11.1 acres of wheat under the program built by the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, sowed 23 acres, and was penalized 49 cents per bushel on 239 excess bushels, about 117 dollars, for wheat consumed on his own farm as livestock feed, household flour, and seed. A unanimous Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice Jackson, sustained the penalty. Home consumed wheat amounted to above twenty percent of national production, and the Court held that activity trivial in itself is reachable by Congress when, taken together with that of others similarly situated, the activity exerts a substantial economic effect on interstate commerce. The decision is generally considered the most expansive reading of the commerce power in the Court case law.

### Q: What is the difference between the farm bill and the agriculture appropriations bill?

The farm bill and the agriculture appropriations bill perform different operations. The farm bill is an authorization act. A farm bill creates programs, sets eligibility rules and payment formulas, amends underlying law, and, for mandatory programs, provides funding in the authorizing law itself, with most farm program payments flowing through the Commodity Credit Corporation. The appropriations bill is an annual act that provides budget authority for a single fiscal year. Discretionary programs authorized in the farm bill, including much of research, rural development, forestry operations, and international food assistance, receive no money until an appropriations act provides funding, at a level that may match the authorization or fall below the authorization. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program occupies a hybrid position, because benefits are owed as open ended mandatory spending to households meeting statutory criteria and the program is funded through the annual appropriations process.

### Q: What happens when a farm bill expires?

Expiration does not create a single uniform shutdown, because the titles of a farm bill rest on different legal foundations. For commodities covered by permanent law, principally dairy, wheat, rice, cotton, corn, and other feed grains, the temporary suspensions in the expired act lapse and the support provisions of the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 and the Agricultural Act of 1949 revive. Dairy reverts first, because dairy support under permanent law operates on a calendar year beginning January 1 after expiration, at 75 to 90 percent of parity through government purchases of manufactured dairy products. Several commodities in modern bills, among them soybeans, peanuts, and sugar, hold no permanent law support and would simply lose authority. Nutrition assistance does not end by operation of permanent law, because the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program operates as authorized mandatory spending funded through appropriations. Congress may also pass an extension, as the American Taxpayer Relief Act did in January 2013.

### Q: How many farm bills have been enacted?

The Congressional Research Service, in In Focus IF11126, counted eighteen farm bills enacted from 1933 through 2018. The years in that count are 1933, 1938, 1948, 1949, 1954, 1956, 1965, 1970, 1973, 1977, 1981, 1985, 1990, 1996, 2002, 2008, 2014, and 2018. The count treats each omnibus reauthorization in the sequence as one farm bill, including the two postwar consolidations of 1948 and 1949 that organized the permanent law structure and the Agriculture and Consumer Protection Act of 1973 that first incorporated food stamp reauthorization into the vehicle. The final enactment in the count, as the sequence stood on November 1, 2020, was the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334. The sequence contains no enactment between 1956 and 1965 and none between 2014 and 2018, which records how the roughly five year rhythm bent under floor failures, veto disputes, and conference delays without breaking the recurring form.

### Q: When was the first farm bill passed?

The first farm bill in the Congressional Research Service count was the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1933, signed on May 12, 1933. The act attacked the collapse of farm prices through production control financed by a tax on the first processing of farm commodities, with the proceeds paid to farmers who agreed to reduce acreage. The Supreme Court struck the scheme down in United States v. Butler, 297 U.S. 1 (1936), holding that the processing tax was part of a plan to regulate agricultural production, a matter reserved to the states, and that the tax was but a means to an unconstitutional end. The same opinion adopted the broad reading that the spending power is not limited to the enumerated powers. Congress answered with the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act of 1936, which re-aimed payments at soil conservation, and then with the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938, which rebuilt the program on the commerce power and entered permanent law.

### Q: What is the most recent farm bill?

Anchored to the date of this account, November 1, 2020, the most recent farm bill was the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018, Public Law 115-334, enacted by the 115th Congress as H.R. 2 and signed on December 20, 2018. The act covered fiscal 2019 through 2023, through September 30, 2023, and carried twelve titles. Passage required two House attempts, because the first passage vote failed by 198 to 213 on May 18, 2018, before the House passed the bill by 213 to 211 on June 21, 2018, and the Senate passed a Senate bill by 86 to 11 on June 28, 2018. The conference report was filed on December 10, 2018, the Senate approved the report by 87 to 13 on December 11, 2018, and the House approved the report by 369 to 47 on December 12, 2018. The Congressional Budget Office projected the act at 428 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2023 and estimated the act as budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 against extension of prior law.

### Q: Who administers farm bill programs?

The Department of Agriculture administers most farm bill programs, through agencies organized by function. The Farm Service Agency administers commodity programs, farm loans, and the Conservation Reserve Program. The Natural Resources Conservation Service administers working lands conservation programs and easements. The Food and Nutrition Service administers the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program and the other nutrition title programs. The Foreign Agricultural Service administers export promotion and part of international food assistance, while the United States Agency for International Development administers Food for Peace. The Rural Development mission area administers housing, utilities, and business programs through three services. The National Institute of Food and Agriculture, the Agricultural Research Service, the Economic Research Service, and the National Agricultural Statistics Service divide research, education, extension, and statistics. The Forest Service administers forestry programs, the Risk Management Agency and the Federal Crop Insurance Corporation administer crop insurance delivered through private companies, and the Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service and the Agricultural Marketing Service administer animal health, plant pest, marketing, and organic programs.

### Q: Why does the farm bill include forestry and energy titles?

Forestry and energy sit inside the farm bill because the omnibus vehicle gathers the programs of the Department of Agriculture into one reauthorization, and both fields run through department agencies and rural constituencies. The Forest Service administers the National Forest System and assistance to state and private forestry, and farm bills supply management authorities, including Good Neighbor Authority, stewardship contracting, and authorities for insect infestations, disease outbreaks, and landscape scale restoration, while the principal operating funds for the Forest Service arrive through annual appropriations outside the farm bill mandatory accounts. The energy title promotes renewable energy and energy efficiency in farm and rural settings, through the Rural Energy for America Program, biorefinery loan guarantees, payments to producers of advanced biofuels, biobased product support, and the Biomass Crop Assistance Program. Both titles also widen the coalition that carries the vehicle, because forest landowners, rural electric and bioenergy producers, and rural communities hold a stake in timely reauthorization alongside commodity producers and nutrition constituencies.

### Q: What is the difference between mandatory and discretionary spending in the farm bill?

Mandatory spending is provided in the authorizing law itself, while discretionary spending requires a later appropriations act. For most farm program accounts, mandatory money flows through the Commodity Credit Corporation, which finances payments under formulas and amounts written into the farm bill and then receives appropriations to restore net realized losses, so commodity payments, conservation contracts, and crop insurance premium support operate without annual funding decisions by appropriations committees. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program is mandatory in character, with benefits owed to households meeting statutory criteria, and is funded through appropriations. Discretionary programs receive only an authorization of appropriations in the farm bill, which sets a ceiling and a legal basis for spending. No money moves until an appropriations act provides funding, at a level that may match the authorization or fall below the authorization. Research, most rural development programs, forestry operations, international food assistance, and farm loan program levels sit on the discretionary side of the line.

### Q: What role does the Commodity Credit Corporation play in the farm bill?

The Commodity Credit Corporation is the funding vehicle through which mandatory commodity payments and many other farm bill payments flow. The corporation was created in 1933 and received a permanent federal charter under the Commodity Credit Corporation Charter Act of 1948. Operating as a government owned corporation within the Department of Agriculture, the corporation uses borrowing authority with the Treasury to finance payments at the moment obligations arise, rather than waiting for a separate appropriation for each disbursement. Nonrecourse loans under the Agricultural Adjustment Act of 1938 ran through the corporation, allowing a producer to place a commodity under loan at a support rate and forfeit the commodity rather than repay when the market price fell below the loan rate. In modern acts, price loss and revenue payments, marketing assistance loans, support purchases, and many conservation and other mandatory payments move through the corporation, and appropriations later restore the corporation for net realized losses. The corporation that executes modern support in ordinary years is the same corporation that would execute parity support after a lapse into permanent law.

### Q: Why do farm bills so often pass after their deadline?

Farm bills pass after deadlines because the negotiation spans twelve or more titles, two chambers, and a conference, while expiration carries consequences that fall unevenly and therefore permit delay that a harder deadline would forbid. The cycle that produced the Agricultural Act of 2014 shows the pattern in full. The act of 2008 expired on September 30, 2012, the Senate had passed a combined bill in June 2012, and the House passed no bill during 2012. An extension inside the American Taxpayer Relief Act, signed January 2, 2013, carried programs through September 30, 2013, with dairy through December 31, 2013. The House combined bill then failed in June 2013, the split bills passed in July and September 2013, recombination followed by resolution, and the conference report was filed only on January 27, 2014. Extensions, appropriations for nutrition assistance, and the January 1 dairy reversion date each softened the deadline at a different point, and conferees used the interval to assemble majorities that the earlier texts had failed to assemble.

### Q: How does the Congressional Budget Office baseline shape a farm bill?

The baseline supplies the measuring rod against which a farm bill is written and scored. The Congressional Budget Office April 2018 baseline projected spending under an extension of the 2014 bill at 867 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2028, with the nutrition title at 663.8 billion dollars, crop insurance at 78.0 billion dollars, commodities at 61.1 billion dollars, and conservation at 59.7 billion dollars. Drafters work against those projections, because a provision that raises projected outlays against the baseline requires offsets elsewhere in the bill or a recorded increase in spending. The Congressional Budget Office projected the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018 at 428 billion dollars over fiscal 2019 to 2023 and estimated the act as budget neutral over fiscal 2019 to 2028 in comparison with extension of prior law. The baseline also frames public argument about the bill, because share figures drawn from the baseline, such as the nutrition share of about 77 percent, describe projected conditions for a defined period rather than an allocation rule written into the act.

### Q: Does the farm bill cover food safety inspection?

No. Food safety inspection of meat, poultry, and processed egg products runs under separate permanent statutes administered by the Food Safety and Inspection Service of the Department of Agriculture, outside the farm bill reauthorization cycle. The Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011 is likewise a separate law, administered principally through the Food and Drug Administration, and no farm bill title gathers the food safety statute book into the omnibus vehicle.
