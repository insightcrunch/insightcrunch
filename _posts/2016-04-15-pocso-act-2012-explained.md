The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, known everywhere by its acronym as the POCSO Act, is the statute India built when it accepted that the general criminal law had failed children. Its long title states the design in one line: an Act to protect children from offences of sexual assault, sexual harassment and pornography, and to provide for the establishment of Special Courts for the trial of such offences. Yet the long title understates what Parliament actually did in 2012. The Act did not merely add new offences to the statute book. It imposed a duty to report on every person in the country, it created a class of courts whose procedure is organised around a single witness, and it reversed the ordinary burden of proof for its principal offences. A reader who understands only the offence definitions understands the least distinctive part of the statute.

![The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012 and its child-centred offences, reporting duty and Special Court procedure - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The tension inside the Act is visible from its first operative choices. It defines a child as any person below the age of eighteen years, and it then makes consent legally irrelevant across that entire span, so that the same provision that protects a six year old from a predator also governs two seventeen year olds in a relationship neither family approves of. It reverses the burden of proof in the name of convictions that the ordinary law had failed to deliver, and in doing so it departs from the presumption of innocence that the rest of the criminal law treats as foundational. It demands that doctors, teachers and counsellors report what they learn, and in doing so it places the professional duty of confidence in direct conflict with the statutory duty to inform the police. Every one of these choices was deliberate, and each one has generated the argument that has followed the Act since its commencement. This article states the Act as enacted in 2012: the offences and their original punishments, the reporting duty, the child-centred procedure, the presumptions, and the two judgments that settled the meaning of its most disputed phrases. The substantial amendment of 2019, which raised punishments and restructured the offences involving material depicting children, is flagged where it changed the original position and is analysed in its own article.

## The gap the POCSO Act filled: sexual offences against children before 2012

Before November 2012, a sexual offence against a child in India was prosecuted, if it was prosecuted at all, under provisions of the Indian Penal Code that had been drafted for a different subject. The rape provision protected only female victims and defined the offence around a single act, so that a large part of the abuse that children in fact suffer, being non-penetrative acts, acts against boys, and acts committed through objects or through forcing a child to act on another person, fell outside it entirely. Conduct that fell short of rape was charged as outraging the modesty of a woman, a provision framed around adult female modesty rather than around harm to a child, and carrying a maximum sentence measured in a small number of years. Abuse of boys was prosecuted, where it was prosecuted, under the provision on unnatural offences, a section written to criminalise a category of sexual act rather than to protect a class of victim, and one under which the child could be treated in principle as a participant rather than as the person harmed. None of these provisions contained a single rule about how a child was to be questioned, where a child's statement was to be recorded, whether a child could be kept away from the accused in court, or how quickly a trial was to finish.

The failure was structural rather than incidental, and it had been documented for years before Parliament acted. The Ministry of Women and Child Development's Study on Child Abuse: India 2007, the largest official survey of its kind, reported that 53.22 per cent of the children surveyed had faced one or more forms of sexual abuse, and that a large share of that abuse was committed by persons known to the child and in positions of trust. The constitutional text offered hooks for a special law: Article 15(3) permits the State to make special provision for children, and Article 39 directs policy towards securing that children are not abused and that childhood is protected against exploitation. India had also acceded to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992, and Article 34 of that Convention obliges states to protect children from all forms of sexual exploitation and abuse. What was missing was a statute that treated the sexual abuse of a child as a distinct legal event with its own definitions, its own procedure and its own forum.

The courts had marked the gap without being able to fill it. In Sakshi v. Union of India in 2004, the Supreme Court declined an invitation to expand the definition of rape in the Penal Code by interpretation, holding in substance that a definition written by the legislature could not be rewritten by a court, and that the remedy for an inadequate offence definition lay with Parliament. The one jurisdiction that had legislated on its own was Goa, whose Children's Act of 2003 dealt with offences against children at state level, and its existence demonstrated both that a dedicated statute was possible and that a patchwork of one state law was no answer for the rest of the country. The lesson of the pre-2012 position is the one this series returns to across its statute pillars: where the offence definition itself is the defect, no amount of prosecutorial energy repairs it, because cases are lost at the point of classification before they are ever argued on evidence.

### Why did India need a separate statute for sexual offences against children?

India needed a separate statute because the Penal Code named no offence that matched the conduct. Its rape provision covered one act and one sex of victim, its modesty provision was written for adult women, and its unnatural offences provision treated the act rather than the child as the wrong.

No provision anywhere supplied a child-appropriate procedure for reporting, examination or trial either, so the cases that were registered were processed through machinery designed for adult complainants, and convictions failed at the classification stage before the evidence was ever weighed. The Bill of 2011 was not the first attempt to repair this. The Department of Women and Child Development had prepared a draft offences against children bill in the middle of the previous decade, and the Ministry of Women and Child Development circulated a revised draft Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Bill in 2010, so the enacted text was the product of several years of drafting in which the central choices, the age of eighteen, the inclusion of boys, the reporting duty and the presumptions for the principal offences, were settled before the Bill reached Parliament. The Standing Committee stage then tested those choices against evidence from the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights, child rights organisations, state governments and legal experts, and the Act that emerged for passage retained each of them. The passage history matters for interpretation: when a court is later asked to read down the age line, or to soften a presumption, it is being asked to undo a choice that was not inadvertent but was examined in committee and carried through both Houses, and the age question itself was pressed in debate by members who wanted a lower threshold or an exception for consensual relations between adolescents, and was answered by the government with the line of eighteen held firm.

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 23 March 2011 by Krishna Tirath, the Minister of State for Women and Child Development, and was referred to the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, which reported in December 2011 after taking evidence on the draft. The Rajya Sabha passed the Bill on 10 May 2012 and the Lok Sabha on 22 May 2012. The Act received the assent of President Pratibha Patil on 19 June 2012, was published in the Gazette the following day, and was brought into force on 14 November 2012, the date observed as Children's Day, together with the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Rules, 2012 made under it. It is Act 32 of 2012. The five months between assent and commencement were used to frame the Rules and to begin the designation of Special Courts, and the gap matters for one legal reason: the Act operates prospectively, so conduct before 14 November 2012 remained governed by the Penal Code provisions it displaced, however inadequate those provisions were.

## The architecture of the Act: definitions that fix the field

The Act runs to forty six sections in nine chapters, and its architecture is best understood as three machines bolted together. The first machine, in Chapters II and III, defines offences and punishments, and adds abetment and attempt. The second machine, in Chapters V and VI, governs what happens when an offence is suspected: who must report it, to whom, how the report is recorded, how the child's statement is taken and how the child is medically examined. The third machine, in Chapters VII and VIII, governs the trial: the Special Court, its powers and procedure, the presumptions it must apply, and the miscellaneous provisions on compensation monitoring and rule making that close the Act. Chapter IV, a single chapter on abetment and attempt sitting between the offences and the reporting provisions, belongs functionally to the first machine. The definitional clause, Section 2, does the quiet work on which everything else depends.

Section 2(d) defines a child as any person below the age of eighteen years. The definition is one line long, contains no exception, no gradation by age band, and no reference to the child's understanding, maturity or marital status, and it is the single most consequential sentence in the Act. Every offence in the statute is defined by reference to it, which means the prosecution in any POCSO case must prove age as a threshold fact, and it means that the protective and the contested consequences of the Act flow from the same source. The definition of the offences is tied back to the operative sections: aggravated penetrative sexual assault bears the meaning Section 5 gives it, sexual assault the meaning in Section 7, and so on through the definitions clause, which also defines domestic relationship, shared household, the Special Court constituted under Section 28 and the Special Public Prosecutor appointed under Section 32. The Act is gender neutral in both directions. The child protected may be a boy or a girl, a point the Penal Code's scheme could not accommodate, and the offender is described throughout as whoever, with no assumption about the sex of the accused, a drafting choice that matters in cases involving women accused of offences against children and in cases involving abuse within institutions staffed by either sex.

The Act sits inside a wider body of child law that this statute does not replace: the juvenile justice legislation governing children in conflict with the law and children in need of care and protection, the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act under which the national and state commissions that monitor this Act are constituted, and the general law on marriage, labour and trafficking that fixes its own age lines for its own purposes. The definitional frame across that whole field, and the way the statutes divide the work between them, is set out in [the guide to child protection laws in India](/2016/04/01/child-protection-laws-india-guide/). Within its own field, however, POCSO is complete: it defines the offences, prescribes the punishments, creates the forum, writes the procedure and supplies the evidentiary rules, which is why a prosecution under it can be conducted almost entirely from within the four corners of the Act.

The miscellaneous chapter contains one provision that practitioners in hospitals need and rarely know. Section 41 provides that the provisions of Sections 3 to 13 shall not apply in the case of a medical examination or medical intervention or treatment of a child where that examination, intervention or treatment is undertaken with the consent of the child's parent or guardian. The provision is the Act's own answer to the fear that ordinary clinical contact, a gynaecological examination, an internal examination of an injured child, the treatment of an adolescent in a maternity ward, might be characterised as an offence by a complainant or a hostile relative: bona fide treatment with parental or guardian consent is placed outside the offence sections altogether. It is a narrow shield, confined to medical acts and to consent given by a parent or guardian, and it does not touch the reporting duty, which continues to apply to what the treatment discloses; but within its field it is the clause that allows the Act's severity and the practice of medicine to coexist.

## The offences: escalating pairs of definition and punishment

The offence structure is the most conventional part of the Act, and its logic is a ladder. Six offences are defined, and five of them are arranged as pairs in which a definition section is followed immediately by a punishment section, with an aggravated form of the two assault offences carrying a heavier scale. The definitions are drafted by describing conduct rather than by naming a moral category, which is the sharpest break with the Penal Code tradition the Act replaced: there is no offence of outraging modesty here, and no element that requires the prosecution to characterise the child's dignity rather than prove the accused's act and intent.

Penetrative sexual assault is defined by Section 3. A person commits it by penetrating, to any extent, the child's body with the penis, or by inserting to any extent an object or a part of the body other than the penis into the vagina, urethra or anus of the child or making the child do so with such person or any other person, or by manipulating any part of the child's body so as to cause penetration, or by applying the mouth to the sexual organs of the child or making the child do so. Three features of the definition carry the weight. The phrase to any extent removes the argument, familiar from the older law, about how much penetration a conviction requires. The inclusion of objects and of parts of the body other than the penis captures forms of abuse the rape definition could not reach. And the repeated formula of making the child act on the accused or on a third person captures abuse in which the child's own body is used as the instrument, a pattern the older law had no vocabulary for at all. The punishment under Section 4, as enacted in 2012, was imprisonment of either description for a term not less than seven years but which could extend to imprisonment for life, together with liability to fine.

Two consequences of the definition deserve emphasis because they distinguish prosecutions under this Act from prosecutions under the law it displaced. The first is that consent is not an ingredient. The section defines the offence by the act and the age of the person acted upon; it contains no consent element for the prosecution to negative and no consent defence for the accused to raise, so that the trial of a penetrative assault under this Act is a trial about what was done and how old the child was, and not about the child's conduct, clothing, history or apparent willingness, matters that had consumed trials under the old law and had made the complainant the effective accused. The second is that the definition reaches conduct in which nothing the older law recognised as intercourse took place at all, including penetration by objects and by fingers and oral acts, each of which is a common form of the abuse of younger children and each of which previously had to be charged, if it was charged at all, as an unnatural offence or as an outrage to modesty carrying a fraction of the sentence. The definitional choice was therefore also a sentencing choice: classifying the act correctly at the charge stage carried the case onto the correct punishment scale without further argument, which is why the framing of the charge sheet, the statement of the child's age, and the selection between the plain and aggravated forms are the points at which POCSO cases are most often won or lost before the trial begins.

Sexual assault, defined by Section 7, is the offence that governs touching. A person commits it who, with sexual intent, touches the vagina, penis, anus or breast of the child, or makes the child touch the corresponding parts of that person or of any other person, or does any other act with sexual intent which involves physical contact without penetration. The definition therefore has a specified limb, naming the parts of the body, and a general limb, covering any other act done with sexual intent involving physical contact short of penetration. The hinge of the whole section is the phrase sexual intent, which is what separates an offence from the ordinary physical contact of care, medicine, sport and family life, and which later produced the Act's best known litigation, examined below. The punishment under Section 8 as enacted was imprisonment of either description for a term not less than three years and up to five years, with liability to fine.

Sexual harassment is defined by Section 11 and punished by Section 12 with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years, together with fine. The definition is a catalogue of conduct that involves no necessary physical contact at all: uttering any word or making any sound or gesture, or exhibiting any object or part of the body, with the intention that the child shall see or hear it; making a child exhibit the child's body or any part of it so that it is seen by the accused or another person; showing any object to a child in any form or media for pornographic purposes; repeatedly or constantly following or watching or contacting a child, directly or through electronic, digital or any other means; threatening to use, in any form of media, a real or fabricated depiction through electronic, film or digital or any other mode of any part of the child's body or of the child's involvement in a sexual act; and enticing a child for pornographic purposes or giving gratification for that purpose. The catalogue matters because it brings the early stages of grooming, the showing of material to a child, persistent electronic contact and threats built on images, inside the criminal law as completed offences rather than as preparatory conduct the law could only watch.

The offences concerning pornographic material occupy Chapter III. Section 13 defines the use of a child for pornographic purposes: using a child in any form of media, including programmes and advertisements transmitted by television channels or the internet or any other electronic form or printed form, whether or not intended for personal use or for distribution, for the purposes of sexual gratification. The section specifies that this includes representing the sexual organs of a child, using a child engaged in real or simulated sexual acts with or without penetration, and the indecent or obscene representation of a child. Section 14(1) as enacted punished the use of a child for pornographic purposes with imprisonment of either description for a term which could extend to five years and fine on a first conviction, and up to seven years and fine on a subsequent conviction. Where the person using the child also directly participated in the acts depicted, the 2012 text graded the punishment by the offence committed: participation amounting to penetrative sexual assault carried a term of not less than ten years extending to imprisonment for life, participation amounting to its aggravated form carried rigorous imprisonment for life, and participation amounting to sexual assault or its aggravated form carried terms of six to eight years and eight to ten years respectively. Section 15 as enacted was a far narrower provision than the one that later replaced it: it punished a person who stored pornographic material in any form involving a child for commercial purposes, with imprisonment of either description which could extend to three years, or with fine, or with both. Storage for any other purpose, and viewing as such, stood outside Section 15 in the Act as passed in 2012, which is the position the Supreme Court was later asked to revisit after the 2019 amendment had rebuilt the section, as the discussion of that judgment below explains.

Section 13's breadth should not pass without remark. The use offence extends to any form of media, expressly including programmes and advertisements, and it is complete whether or not the material was intended for personal use or for distribution, so that the producer of a sexualised depiction of a child for an advertisement is inside the section although no abuse in the contact sense has occurred and nothing has been sold. The three specified forms, representing the sexual organs of a child, using a child engaged in real or simulated sexual acts, and the indecent or obscene representation of a child, move outward from the record of an offence to the manufacture of a depiction, which is the point at which this Act's scheme for material offences departs from merely punishing the circulation of evidence of abuse and instead punishes the creation of the depiction itself. The indecent or obscene representation limb is the widest and the least litigated, and its presence in the section is a reminder that the legislature of 2012 was legislating for the advertising and entertainment industries as well as for the individual offender, a breadth the later arguments about possession have tended to obscure.

Abetment and attempt close the offence scheme. Section 16 defines abetment in the three familiar forms of instigation, conspiracy and intentional aiding, and provides expressly that a person who, by wilful misrepresentation or by wilful concealment of a material fact he is bound to disclose, voluntarily causes or procures or attempts to cause or procure a thing to be done, is said to instigate the doing of that thing. Section 17 punishes abetment, where the offence abetted is committed in consequence, with the punishment provided for the offence itself, so that the adult who procures a child for another's abuse, or who arranges and facilitates it, stands in the same sentencing position as the principal. The abetment provisions do quiet work in the institutional cases the aggravated sections anticipate, because the person who runs the institution in which abuse occurs, and who knowingly supplies the access, the privacy or the silence that makes it possible, is reachable as an abettor even where no act of his own against the child can be proved, and the wilful concealment limb of Section 16 is drafted for exactly the official or manager who learns of abuse and buries it. Section 18 punishes an attempt to commit any offence under the Act, and an attempt to abet one, with imprisonment of any description provided for the offence for a term which may extend to one half of the imprisonment for life or, as the case may be, one half of the longest term of imprisonment provided for the offence, or with fine, or with both. The inclusion of a general attempt provision matters in this field more than in most, because a large share of abuse is interrupted, resisted or disclosed at a stage where the completed offence cannot be proved, and a statute without an attempt clause would have left that conduct to the uncertain reach of the Penal Code's general attempt provision.

The boundary between Section 7 and Section 11 is the boundary between contact and conduct, and it allocates a large volume of real cases. Conduct that involves touching the child, or causing the child to touch, with sexual intent is sexual assault; conduct that sexualises the child's environment without contact, showing material, exhibiting the body, following, messaging, threatening with images, is sexual harassment. The two offences are charged together frequently, because a course of grooming that begins with messages and material often ends in contact, and the charges then narrate the sequence in the order the statute lays down. The drafting also leaves a deliberate overlap: some acts, such as making a child exhibit the child's body so that it is seen by the accused, appear in the harassment catalogue although they are steps towards contact offences, which shows that the catalogue was written to capture the offender's purpose rather than the mechanics of the act. At the foot of the ladder, one further mechanical point governs practice. For the offences punishable with up to three years, being sexual harassment under Section 12 and storage under the original Section 15, the limitation provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure apply in the ordinary way, and the delay that is endemic in the disclosure of abuse has repeatedly had to be excused under the Code's power to extend limitation in the interest of justice, a friction the Act does nothing to remove and which prosecutors meet by pleading the circumstances of disclosure from the outset.

## The aggravated forms: when the offender, the child or the manner raises the offence

The aggravated offences are where the Act states its theory of harm. Section 5 lists the circumstances in which penetrative sexual assault becomes aggravated penetrative sexual assault, and Section 9 applies the corresponding list to sexual assault, and the lists repay attention because they are organised around three ideas rather than one. The first idea is the abuse of a position. The offence is aggravated where the offender is a police officer and commits the act within the limits of the police station to which he is appointed, in the premises of a police station, in the course of his duties, or in circumstances where he is known or identified as a police officer; where the offender is a member of the armed forces or security forces deployed in an area; where the offender is a public servant; where the offender is on the management or staff of a jail, remand home, protection home, observation home or other place of custody or care and protection established by law; where the offender is on the management or staff of a hospital, government or private, in that hospital; and where the offender is on the management or staff of an educational institution or religious institution, in that institution. The list extends beyond officials to any person in a position of trust or authority towards the child, and to the owner, manager or staff of an institution providing services to the child, and it reaches the child's own household by covering a relative of the child through blood or adoption or marriage or guardianship or foster care, a person in a domestic relationship with a parent of the child, and a person living in the same or shared household with the child.

The household clauses put to work the two definitions in Section 2 that matter most outside the offence sections. A domestic relationship means a relationship between two persons who live or have at any point lived together in a shared household, where they are related by consanguinity, marriage or a relationship in the nature of marriage, adoption, or as family members living together as a joint family; and a shared household means a household where the person charged lives or has lived at any time in a domestic relationship with the child. The vocabulary is borrowed from the domestic violence legislation, and it serves a specific prosecutorial purpose. In the large share of cases where the accused is a relative, a parent's partner, a tenant, a domestic worker or a person taken into the home, the aggravated charge does not depend on proving a formal relationship of authority over the child, because co-residence in a domestic relationship with the child is itself the aggravating fact, and the prosecution's task is the factual one of proving the household rather than the legal one of proving a trust relationship.

The second idea is the vulnerability of the particular child. The offence is aggravated where the child is below twelve years of age, a far younger line than the Act's general threshold of eighteen and the one place where the 2012 text gradates by age; where the offender takes advantage of the child's mental or physical disability; and, for penetrative assault, where the offender knows the woman to be pregnant, a clause addressed to the child who is herself pregnant. The third idea is the manner and the consequences: committing the assault by a group of persons acting in furtherance of a common intention, which the Act names gang penetrative sexual assault; using deadly weapons, fire, heated substances or corrosive substances; causing grievous hurt or bodily harm or injury to the sexual organs of the child; committing the assault repeatedly on the same child; physically incapacitating the child or causing mental illness or an impairment of any kind, temporary or permanent, that renders the child unable to perform regular tasks; and, in the course of the assault, committing murder. For sexual assault, Section 9 adds a circumstance with no counterpart in Section 5: committing the act in the course of communal or sectarian violence, a clause that records the legislative awareness that children are targeted in collective violence and that the ordinary assault definition did not express that aggravation.

The punishments state the consequence. Section 6 as enacted punished aggravated penetrative sexual assault with rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than ten years but which could extend to imprisonment for life, and liability to fine. Section 10 as enacted punished aggravated sexual assault with imprisonment of either description for a term not less than five years and up to seven years, and liability to fine. The architecture is deliberate: the aggravated form is not a sentencing consideration left to judicial discretion within a single wide range, but a distinct offence with its own definition section and its own floor, so that a charge must plead and prove the aggravating circumstance, the circumstance becomes an issue at trial rather than an argument at sentencing, and the accused knows from the charge sheet which scale of punishment he faces. That design choice explains why so much POCSO litigation concerns classification, whether the offender stood in the listed relationship to the child, whether the child was below twelve, whether the act was repeated, because classification at the charge stage fixes the sentencing floor before a word of evidence about the act itself has been tested.

## The reporting duty: every adult in India is a potential informant

Chapter V contains the provision most likely to touch a reader of this article who has nothing to do with the criminal process. Section 19(1) provides that notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, any person, including the child, who has an apprehension that an offence under the Act is likely to be committed, or who has knowledge that such an offence has been committed, shall provide that information to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or to the local police. Two features of the sentence deserve to be read slowly. The duty is triggered not only by knowledge but by apprehension that an offence is likely to be committed, which makes the duty prospective and preventive rather than merely evidentiary: a teacher who learns facts suggesting that abuse is about to occur is under the same statutory obligation as a witness to a completed act. And the duty falls on any person, without qualification by relationship, profession or proximity. There is no exception for the parent who would rather settle the matter within the family, no exception for the doctor bound by confidence, no exception for the employer conducting an internal inquiry, and no exception for the relative who fears the consequences for the accused. The Act knows about those pressures and answers them by making the duty universal and by penalising its breach.

The mechanics of the report are prescribed because the legislature understood that a report badly taken is a case badly begun. Every report given under Section 19 is to be recorded in writing, assigned an entry number, read over to the informant, and entered in a book maintained by the police unit for that purpose. Where the report is made by a child, it is to be recorded in simple language and, where the child does not understand the language of the statement, with the assistance of a translator or interpreter. The police who receive the report carry their own obligations beyond recording: where the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police is satisfied that a child against whom an offence has been committed is in need of care and protection, it must record its reasons in writing and make immediate arrangement for the child's care and protection, including admitting the child into a shelter home or to the nearest hospital, within twenty four hours of the report. Within the same twenty four hours, and without any loss of time, the police must report the matter to the Child Welfare Committee and to the Special Court or, where no Special Court has been designated, to the Court of Session, including the child's need for care and protection and the steps taken. Section 19(7) protects the informant: no person incurs any liability, civil or criminal, for giving information in good faith under the section, a shield that matters most for exactly the professionals, neighbours and junior staff most likely to fear a defamation claim or a disciplinary consequence for speaking.

The threshold of the duty deserves precision, because it is widely misunderstood in both directions. The duty is to provide information, not to establish a case. An apprehension that an offence is likely to be committed does not require the informant to have verified anything, to have confronted anyone, or to be satisfied that an offence will in fact occur; the statute assigns verification to the police and adjudication to the Special Court, and it assigns to the citizen only the act of transmission. That allocation is what makes the duty workable for a teacher acting on a child's fragmentary disclosure or a doctor acting on an injury pattern, and it is also what makes the duty demanding: the informant is not entitled to wait for certainty, to conduct a private investigation first, or to substitute a judgment about the family's circumstances for the report. The corollary, in Section 22, is that the informant who transmits in good faith is protected even if the information proves wrong, and only the informant who transmits in order to humiliate, extort, threaten or defame is exposed. Read together, the two sections draw the line the whole reporting scheme depends on: transmission is compulsory and protected, evaluation is institutional, and malice is the only punishable motive.

Section 20 adds a specialised duty for those who handle material. Any personnel of the media, or of a hotel, lodge, hospital, club, studio or photographic facility, by whatever name called, irrespective of the number of persons employed, who comes across any material or object which is sexually exploitative of a child, including pornographic or sexually related material or an obscene representation of a child, through the use of any medium, must provide that information to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police. The section is aimed at the points of discovery built into ordinary commerce: the photo laboratory that receives film, the studio that is handed a device, the hotel staff who find material in a room, the hospital that encounters images in the course of treatment. It converts those encounters from occasions for private disposal into triggers for a report, and it does so without regard to the size of the establishment.

The duty is enforced by Section 21, which punishes failure to report or failure to record. Any person who fails to report the commission of an offence under Section 19 or Section 20, or who fails to record such an offence as Section 19 requires, is punishable with imprisonment of either description which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both. The second limb is directed at hierarchy: any person in charge of a company or an institution, by whatever name called, who fails to report the commission of an offence under Section 19 in respect of a subordinate under his control, is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to one year and with fine, the fine being mandatory rather than alternative in that limb. In practice a large share of reports reach the police through an institutional relay the Act does not name but its duty makes possible: the national Childline service on 1098, the child helpline through which children and adults report abuse and neglect, receives the disclosure, involves the police and the Child Welfare Committee, and thereby performs the Section 19 act for an informant who would not have walked into a police station. The Act's duty is deliberately indifferent to the channel: knowledge held by any person must reach the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police, and the helpline, the school, the hospital and the neighbour are all conduits to the same two destinations. The section then states its one exemption, and the exemption is instructive about the Act's whole approach: the failure to report provision does not apply to a child. A child who knows of abuse, whether of himself or of another child, and says nothing, commits no offence, because the legislature placed the burden of disclosure on the adult world and refused to criminalise the silence of the class the Act exists to protect.

Section 22 guards the other side of the reporting system. A person who makes a false complaint or provides false information against any person in respect of an offence under Sections 3, 5, 7 or 9, solely with the intention to humiliate, extort, threaten or defame that person, is punishable with imprisonment which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both. The requirement that the falsity be coupled with one of those four purposes means that a mistaken report, an exaggerated report, and a report that cannot be proved are none of them offences under the section; only a report made to injure is. The statute then protects children twice over in the same section: where a false complaint has been made or false information provided by a child, no punishment shall be imposed on that child, so that the child who exaggerates, confuses or invents cannot be prosecuted for it; and a person who is not a child who makes a false complaint or provides false information against a child, knowing or having reason to believe it false and thereby victimising the child, is punishable with imprisonment which may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both. Section 23 completes the chapter by regulating the media: no report in any media shall disclose the identity of a child, including name, address, photograph, family details, school, neighbourhood or any other particulars that may lead to disclosure of identity, unless the Special Court permits disclosure for reasons recorded and in the interest of the child; the publisher or owner of the media is jointly and severally liable for the acts and omissions of an employee; and contravention is punishable with imprisonment for a term that shall not be less than six months and may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both.

The duty leaves two questions that practitioners ask and the Act answers only in part. The first is what the informant's position is after the report is made. The report sets the criminal process in motion, but the informant is not the prosecutor: the case becomes the State's case, the informant may be examined as a witness, and a teacher or doctor who reported will ordinarily be required to speak to the fact and circumstances of the disclosure rather than to the truth of what was disclosed. The good faith shield in Section 19(7) protects the act of reporting from civil and criminal liability; it does not exempt the informant from the witness box, and it does not protect a report made in bad faith, which is the space Section 22 occupies. The second question is professional confidence. The Act contains no exception for information received in a professional relationship, so a disclosure made to a doctor in a consultation, to a counsellor in a session, or to a priest or teacher in confidence is as reportable as a disclosure made to a neighbour, and the statutory duty overrides the professional obligation of confidence because a professional rule cannot displace an Act of Parliament. The objection pressed by medical and counselling bodies, that compulsion will drive the problem underground by deterring disclosure to the very professionals best placed to help, is a real cost of the design, and it is examined in the age of consent discussion below; the legislature's answer, embodied in the text, is that concealment by offenders, families and institutions was the greater evil, and that a discretionary duty is the arrangement that had already failed.

The provision that has changed institutional behaviour most is the one aimed at the person in charge. Section 21(2) makes the head of an institution personally liable where an offence by a subordinate under his control goes unreported, and the failure it punishes is the failure to transmit what Section 19 obliged him to transmit once the matter reached him. The practical consequence, visible in the compliance practice of schools, hospitals, residential homes and sports bodies, is the growth of written internal protocols that do two things: they require any member of staff who receives a disclosure or forms an apprehension to escalate it immediately to a designated officer, and they require the report to the police to be made and documented the same day, precisely because delay is the point at which institutional self protection usually intervenes. A school that investigates first and reports later has inverted the statute: the Act assigns investigation to the police and adjudication to the Special Court, and assigns to the institution only the duties of reporting, of preserving the child's first account without repeated questioning, and of cooperating with the care decisions that follow.

### What happens after a POCSO report reaches the police?

A report sets two clocks running and two institutions in motion. Within twenty four hours the police must arrange the child's care and protection and report the matter to the Child Welfare Committee and the Special Court. The child's evidence must be recorded within thirty days of cognizance, and the trial completed within a year, as far as possible.

Between the report and the trial, the statement, the medical examination and the care decisions follow the sequence the table below sets out.

## The child as witness: statement, medical examination and the protections of Chapter VI

If the reporting chapter is the Act's demand on adults, Chapter VI is its promise to the child, and it is drafted as a set of instructions about place, person and manner rather than as general exhortations to sensitivity. Section 24 governs the recording of the child's statement by the police. The statement shall be recorded at the residence of the child or at a place where the child usually resides or at the place of the child's choice, and as far as practicable by a woman police officer not below the rank of sub-inspector. The police officer recording the statement shall not be in uniform. The officer making the investigation shall, while examining the child, ensure that at no point of time the child comes in contact in any way with the accused. No child shall be detained in the police station in the night for any reason. And the police officer shall ensure that the identity of the child is protected from the public media, unless otherwise directed by the Special Court in the interest of the child. Each clause removes a specific and familiar injury of the ordinary process: the child taken to a police station, the child questioned by a uniformed officer of rank and authority, the child kept waiting where the accused is also waiting, the child held overnight, the child's name in the press before any trial has occurred. Courts have repeatedly observed, when these provisions are breached, that they are made for the benefit of the child and not of the accused, so that a breach is a ground for censure of the investigation rather than an automatic ground for acquittal, but the deeper point is that the quality of the first statement decides much of what follows, and a first statement extracted in a frightening setting is unreliable evidence and a second injury at once.

Section 25 takes the statement out of police hands for the record that matters most. Where the statement of the child is recorded by a Magistrate under the provision of the Code that governs the recording of statements and confessions, the Magistrate shall record the statement as spoken by the child, and the statement is recorded in the presence of the parents of the child or of a representative of the child's choice. Section 26 adds the assistance provisions that run across both the police and the Magistrate routes: the statement is recorded as spoken by the child; where the child is temporarily or permanently mentally or physically disabled, the Magistrate or the police officer shall seek the assistance of a translator or an interpreter or a special educator, or of any person familiar with the manner of communication of the child, and the statement may be recorded by audio video electronic means wherever possible. The significance of the special educator clause is easy to miss and hard to overstate. For a child with an intellectual disability, or a deaf child, the ordinary process produced silence that was recorded as inconsistency, and inconsistency was then used to destroy the witness; Section 26 makes the communication support a statutory entitlement rather than a favour dependent on the initiative of a particular officer.

Behind these provisions stands an older evidentiary question the Act assumes rather than answers: whether a young child is a competent witness at all. The general law, in Section 118 of the Indian Evidence Act, treats every person as competent to testify unless the court considers that the person is prevented by tender years, among other causes, from understanding the questions put or from giving rational answers to them, and the test is applied by the judge in a preliminary examination of the child before the evidence begins. The POCSO scheme layers its protections onto that foundation. The child who passes the competency inquiry gives evidence under the shelter of Sections 33 to 38, with questions filtered through the judge and the accused screened from view; the child who cannot yet understand questions may still be the source of the disclosure that begins the case, recorded under Sections 24 and 25 and spoken to by the adult who received it. The distinction matters in the youngest cases, where the prosecution frequently stands on the disclosure, the medical evidence and the circumstances rather than on testimony from the child, and where the defence attack is correspondingly directed at the reliability of the chain between the child's first words and the witness box.

Section 27 governs the medical examination, and it is written to end the practice under which medical evidence was gathered late, insensitively, or not at all. The examination of the child is to be conducted in accordance with Section 164A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the provision that governs the medical examination of a victim of rape, in a government hospital or a hospital recognised by the government, by a registered medical practitioner. Where the child is a girl, the examination shall be conducted by a woman doctor. The examination shall be conducted in the presence of the parent of the child or any other person in whom the child reposes trust or confidence, and where neither such person is available, in the presence of a woman nominated by the head of the medical institution. The provision carries a consequence for the interface between medicine and prosecution that returns in the age of consent discussion below: because a medical encounter with an adolescent patient can disclose a sexual history, and because Section 19 makes the resulting report compulsory, the hospital is not a confidential space for a person below eighteen in the way it is for an adult, and practitioners are obliged to understand that the consultation itself may trigger a criminal case.

The examination also carries a prohibition the courts have had to state because practice lagged behind principle. The so-called two-finger test, a per vaginum examination once used to comment on whether a victim was habituated to sexual intercourse, was held by the Supreme Court in Lillu v. State of Haryana in 2013 to have no scientific basis for the conclusions drawn from it and to violate the victim's privacy and dignity, and the Court directed that it not be conducted; the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare's guidelines for medico-legal care of victims of sexual violence, issued in 2014, carry the same position into hospital protocol, and the reasoning applies with still greater force to a child examined under this Act. The point belongs in an account of Section 27 because the section's protections, a woman doctor for a girl child, a trusted person present, a recognised facility, are directed at the same end as the prohibition: the medical encounter is part of the child's evidence and part of the child's experience of the system, and the Act treats the second of those as a subject for legislation rather than as a matter of bedside manner.

## Special Courts and the conduct of the trial

Section 28 requires every State Government, in consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court, to designate for each district a Court of Session to be a Special Court to try offences under the Act, its stated object being to ensure a speedy trial. Where a Court of Session had already been notified as a children's court under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005, or as a Special Court under any other law in force, that court is deemed to be a Special Court under this section, a deeming provision that explains why the POCSO forum in many districts is a designated sessions court carrying the work alongside its ordinary docket rather than a new court created for the purpose. The Special Court is also the forum for an offence under Section 67B of the Information Technology Act, 2000 in so far as it relates to the publication or transmission of material depicting children in a sexual act or conduct, so that the online and the contact offences arising from one course of conduct are tried together rather than split between two courts. By Section 31, the provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure, including its provisions on bail and bonds, apply to proceedings before a Special Court, and the Special Court is deemed to be a Court of Session for that purpose. By Section 32, no person is appointed a Special Public Prosecutor unless he has been in practice as an advocate for not less than seven years, a threshold that converts the prosecution of these cases from a general assignment into a specialisation the statute itself requires.

### How are POCSO Special Courts different from ordinary criminal courts?

A Special Court is a designated Court of Session with a different procedural code for the witness at its centre. It takes cognizance without the case being committed to it, conducts the trial in camera, keeps the child from seeing the accused while testifying, routes the questions of both sides through the judge, and bars aggressive questioning.

Section 33 collects the powers and duties that make that difference operational. The Special Court may take cognizance of an offence without the accused being committed to it for trial, which removes one whole stage of delay from the ordinary sessions route. Where the prosecutor or the defence counsel intends to put any question to the child during the trial, those questions shall be communicated to the Special Court, which shall in turn put them to the child, so that cross examination is filtered through the judge rather than delivered face to face. The court shall create a child-friendly atmosphere by allowing a family member, a guardian or a friend or a relative in whom the child has trust to be present in the court. It shall ensure that the child is not called repeatedly to testify in the court, and it may grant frequent breaks to the child during the trial. It shall not permit aggressive questions or character assassination of the child and shall ensure that the dignity of the child is maintained at all times during the trial, and it shall ensure that the identity of the child is not disclosed at any time during the course of investigation or trial. Section 36 adds the separation rule for the act of testifying itself: the Special Court shall ensure that the child is not exposed in any way to the accused at the time of recording the evidence, while at the same time ensuring that the accused is in a position to hear the statement of the child and to communicate with his advocate, and for that purpose the court may record the statement through video conferencing or by making use of single visibility mirrors or curtains or any other device. Section 37 requires trials to be conducted in camera, in the presence of the parents or any other person in whom the child has trust or confidence. Section 38 provides for the assistance of a translator, interpreter or, where the child has a mental or physical disability, a special educator or a person familiar with the manner of communication of the child, or an expert, during the recording of evidence. Section 40 secures the child's own representation: the child is entitled to the services of a legal counsel of the child's choice, and where the child's family is unable to afford one, the Legal Services Authority shall provide free legal aid, a right that matters because the child's interests and the prosecution's interests are not always identical, most sharply where the family itself is divided about the prosecution.

The identity protection does not rest on Section 23 alone. In Nipun Saxena v. Union of India, decided in December 2018, the Supreme Court issued directions for victims of sexual offences generally, holding that the identity of such a victim is not to be disclosed in any media, that first information reports and other identity bearing documents in such cases are not to be placed in the public domain, that records disclosing identity are to be kept in sealed cover, and that the bar extends even to a victim who has died, unless the next of kin authorise disclosure. In the practice of the Special Courts the protection appears as the anonymised cause title, the child referred to by initials or simply as the victim, and the exclusion of identifying particulars from judgments that will be published and reported, a discipline that trial and appellate courts have had to enforce against the habits of their own record keeping as much as against the press.

Section 35 sets the clocks the table above records: the evidence of the child shall be recorded within a period of thirty days of the Special Court taking cognizance of the offence, with the reasons for any delay recorded by the court, and the Special Court shall complete the trial, as far as possible, within a period of one year from the date of taking cognizance. The phrase as far as possible makes the one year period a duty of effort rather than a limitation period, and the thirty day period is directed at the single most valuable piece of evidence in the case, because a child's account recorded promptly is fuller, less rehearsed and less open to the suggestion of tutoring than the same account recorded after a year of family pressure. Section 34 settles two border questions. Where the offence is committed by a child, that child is dealt with under the juvenile justice legislation rather than under this Act's punishments, the Act protecting children as victims without making child offenders into adult criminals. And where a question arises before the Special Court about whether a person is or is not a child, the court must satisfy itself on the point and record its reasons, because age is the jurisdictional fact on which the whole proceeding depends and a wrong finding on age dissolves the court's authority over the case.

Because age is the jurisdictional fact, its proof is a distinct discipline within the trial, and the Act's silence on method has been filled by the rules and the case law of the adjoining juvenile justice system. The hierarchy the courts apply gives primacy to documents: the date of birth certificate from the school first attended and matriculation records, the certificate of birth issued by a municipal authority or a panchayat, and, only in the absence of such documentary proof, a medical opinion from a duly constituted medical board. Radiological estimation of age by ossification is treated as the last resort rather than the first, for a reason the Supreme Court stated in Jaya Mala v. Home Secretary in 1982 and has repeated since: the margin of error in such estimation can run to about two years on either side, so that a medical estimate placing a person close to the line cannot by itself carry the prosecution across it. Where the margin matters, courts have taken on the threshold question the view more favourable to the accused, which produces the practical consequence that prosecutions founded on a child said to be just below eighteen stand or fall on school and birth records rather than on medical evidence, and that the preservation of those records by families and schools is part of the unglamorous infrastructure on which the Act depends.

### How does POCSO treat a case where the accused is also a child?

The Act protects children as victims and declines to punish children as offenders under its own scales. Section 34 directs that a child who commits an offence under it is dealt with under the juvenile justice legislation, and requires the Special Court to satisfy itself, with reasons recorded, whether a person before it is a child at all.

The direction reflects a judgment about the design of the whole scheme: the same Parliament that made the Act's punishments severe for adults refused to route a child offender into those scales, leaving the disposition of a child accused to the reformative machinery of the juvenile justice system, under which a child found to have committed an offence is dealt with by the Juvenile Justice Board or the Children's Court and not by sentence of imprisonment. The age question Section 34 reserves to the Special Court is correspondingly practical, because it decides forum as well as liability: a finding that the accused is a child moves the case out of the Special Court's punishment jurisdiction altogether, and a finding that the victim is not a child dissolves the Special Court's authority over a case that belongs to the ordinary courts under the general law.

## The presumptions: Sections 29 and 30

The evidentiary provisions are the point at which the Act departs most visibly from the ordinary criminal law, and they should be stated exactly. Section 29 provides that where a person is prosecuted for committing or abetting or attempting to commit any offence under Sections 3, 5, 7 and 9, the Special Court shall presume that such person has committed or abetted or attempted to commit the offence, as the case may be, unless the contrary is proved. Section 30 provides that in any prosecution for an offence under the Act which requires a culpable mental state on the part of the accused, the Special Court shall presume the existence of that mental state, but it shall be a defence for the accused to prove that he had no such mental state with respect to the act charged as the offence in that prosecution. Section 30(2) then fixes the standard the accused must meet in a way that softens the apparent severity of the scheme: for the purposes of the section, a fact is said to be proved only when the Special Court believes it to exist beyond reasonable doubt, and not merely when its existence is established by a preponderance of probability. In practice the two presumptions operate in sequence rather than as a single reversal. The prosecution must still prove the foundational facts of the case, that the person before the court is a child, that the accused is the person charged, and the act alleged, and appellate courts have consistently read the presumptions as arising only on that foundation rather than as substitutes for it; but once the foundation is laid, the accused who wishes to say that he did not do the act, or that he acted without sexual intent, must prove the contrary, and must prove it to the criminal standard. The justification offered for the design is evidentiary rather than punitive. Sexual offences against children are committed in private, ordinarily without witnesses, frequently within households, and are disclosed late; a rule that placed the entire persuasive burden on a child witness in those conditions is a rule whose predictable output is acquittal, and the pre-2012 record was the demonstration. The objection is equally structural and is stated at full strength in the assessment below: a presumption of the offence itself, rather than of a subsidiary fact, reverses the premise on which the rest of the criminal law rests, and its combination with mandatory minimum sentences means that an error at trial is an error with a long sentence attached to it.

The presumptions also shape the two stages at which most cases are actually influenced, bail and appeal. At bail, a court dealing with an offence under Sections 3, 5, 7 or 9 confronts a scheme in which the trial court is directed to presume the offence once the prosecution's foundational facts are established, and while the presumption is a rule for the trial and not a statutory bar to bail, its presence has stiffened the approach of courts asked to release an accused on the footing that no case is disclosed at all. On appeal, the discipline runs the other way: appellate courts have set aside convictions where the prosecution's foundational proof failed, most often on the age of the child or on the identification of the act alleged, treating the presumptions as machinery that begins to operate only after the prosecution has discharged its initial burden on evidence, and not as a substitute for that evidence. Read together, the two lines of practice produce the working rule that practitioners recognise: the presumptions decide cases in which the foundational facts are established and the accused offers no account capable of displacing them, and they decide nothing in a case where the prosecution's own proof of age or act is infirm. That is a narrower instrument than the text read alone suggests, and a broader one than the ordinary law allows, and both halves of that description belong in any honest account of the settlement the courts have reached with these sections. The objection deserves its strongest form alongside the justification: the presumption of innocence is not a technicality but the organising principle that allocates the risk of error in a criminal trial to the State, and a legislature that reverses it for the offence itself, in a field where a large share of prosecutions arise inside households and turn on one person's word against another's, accepts a higher risk of wrongful conviction as the price of a higher rate of conviction overall. Parliament made that trade in 2012 with the pre-2012 acquittal record in front of it; the courts, by insisting on the foundational facts, have moderated its operation without disowning it.

## Compensation, support and monitoring

Compensation under the Act as enacted was delivered through the Rules rather than through a charging section in the Act itself, and the arrangement is worth stating precisely because it is so often misdescribed. Rule 7 of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Rules, 2012 empowers the Special Court, in appropriate cases, on its own motion or on an application filed by or on behalf of the child, to pass an order for interim compensation to meet the immediate needs of the child for relief or rehabilitation at any stage after the registration of the first information report, with the interim amount adjusted against the final compensation. The Special Court may likewise recommend the award of compensation where the accused is convicted, and equally where the case ends in acquittal or discharge, or where the accused is not traced or identified, if in the opinion of the court the child has suffered loss or injury as a result of the offence. The award is made through the victim compensation framework under Section 357A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the factors the Rules direct attention to include the type of abuse and the gravity of the offence and the severity of the mental or physical harm suffered; the expenditure incurred or likely to be incurred on medical treatment for physical and mental health; the loss of educational opportunity caused by absence from school through trauma, injury, treatment, investigation and trial; the loss of employment where the child was working; the relationship of the child to the offender; whether the abuse was a single incident or continued over a period; and whether the child became pregnant as a result of the offence. Two features of the scheme distinguish it from the ordinary fine. Compensation does not depend on a conviction, so that the child whose evidence cannot carry a prosecution is not thereby denied relief; and interim compensation is available from the stage of the first information report, because the needs the scheme addresses, treatment, relocation, schooling interrupted, do not wait for a verdict. The 2019 amendment later wrote a compensation power into the Act itself at Section 33(8), under which the Special Court may, in addition to the punishment, direct payment of such compensation as may be prescribed to the child for any physical or mental trauma caused or for immediate rehabilitation, which converted the rule based scheme into a statutory direction without changing its logic.

In operation the money reaches the child through the state and district legal services machinery that administers the victim compensation schemes, which is why the speed of compensation in practice tracks the administrative capacity of that machinery rather than the terms of any individual order, and why interim compensation, designed for the weeks after the report when the costs of treatment, travel and relocation fall due, is the limb of the scheme whose failure is felt most sharply. The fine imposed on a convicted accused was, under the Act as enacted, a separate matter and should not be confused with it: a fine was a punishment payable to the State, it was not earmarked for the child, and a sentencing court that intended the child to benefit had to use the compensation route rather than assume that a heavy fine reached the victim. The 2019 amendment later altered that position for the principal offences, as the amendment outline below records.

The remaining provisions build the supporting cast. Section 39 requires State Governments to prepare guidelines for the use of non-governmental organisations, professionals and experts, and persons having knowledge of psychology, social work, physical health, mental health and child development, to be associated with the pre-trial and trial stages to assist the child, which is the statutory root of the support person who accompanies a child through the process in districts where the system works as designed. Section 43 requires the Central Government and every State Government to take all measures to give wide publicity to the Act through the media, including television, radio and print, at regular intervals, and to train police officers and other officers concerned in its implementation periodically, an acknowledgement by the legislature itself that a reporting duty nobody knows about is a duty that will not be performed. Section 44 entrusts the monitoring of implementation to the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions, in the manner prescribed, including inquiry into specified cases and the inclusion of the monitoring report in the commissions' annual reports. Section 45 confers the rule making power under which the Rules of 2012, and their successors, are made. The Rules of 2012 are not a formality appended to the Act; they carry part of its operative scheme, including the procedures for the medical examination and for the care of the child, the manner in which the Child Welfare Committee is to be informed and is to act, the provision for persons to support the child through the process, and the compensation code in Rule 7 that the previous paragraph describes. A reader who stops at the fortieth section of the Act therefore misses the layer at which several of its entitlements are actually specified, and the layering has a practical consequence: amendments to the Rules can change the working of the compensation and support schemes without a parliamentary amendment, which is one reason the replacement of the Rules of 2012 by a successor set in 2020 altered the administration of the Act while leaving its text untouched.

The monitoring duty in Section 44 also deserves its weight. The National Commission and the State Commissions are not advisory ornaments in this scheme: they are charged with monitoring the implementation of the Act's provisions in the manner prescribed, which has led them to inquire into the designation of Special Courts, the appointment of Special Public Prosecutors and the constitution of the support machinery in particular states, and to report the position in their annual reports. Their findings, state by state, are the most consistent official source for the implementation gaps discussed below, and the pattern those findings repeat, districts in which the designated court carries a full ordinary docket, prosecutors holding the charge in addition to other work, support persons untrained or unappointed, is a pattern of form satisfied and substance deferred, which is the characteristic failure mode of a statute that creates institutions by designation rather than by establishment. The general position described in this article is legal information about a statute of general application; it is not advice on any individual case, and anyone facing a specific report, investigation or proceeding should take advice on its facts, because limitation, forum and the facts of age proof are unforgiving in this field.

## The relationship with the general criminal law

POCSO did not repeal the Penal Code provisions on sexual offences, and an act against a child will very often be an offence under both codes at once. Section 42 resolves the overlap by a greater punishment rule: where an act or omission constitutes an offence punishable under this Act and also under specified provisions of the Indian Penal Code or under any other law for the time being in force, the offender found guilty of the offence shall be liable to punishment under this Act or under the other law, whichever provides for punishment which is greater in degree. The rule prevents the double punishment that a literal reading of two codes might produce while ensuring that the existence of the older, milder provision cannot be used to undercut the newer one. The comparison between the two codes as systems, including what the eventual replacement of the Penal Code by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita did to the offences that run parallel to this Act, is drawn in [the comparison of the Penal Code and its successor](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/); for this Act the operative point is narrower, that POCSO is the code of first resort for a victim below eighteen and the Penal Code operates beside it subject to the greater punishment rule. One alignment followed quickly. When POCSO was passed, the age below which sexual intercourse was rape under the Penal Code still stood at sixteen, so that for a year the two statutes fixed different ages for substantially the same protection; the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 raised the Penal Code threshold to eighteen and brought the general law into line with the line POCSO had drawn, which is a useful reminder that in 2012 it was the special statute, not the general code, that set the direction of travel.

## The judicial anchors: what touching means, and what possession means

Two Supreme Court decisions define the working meaning of the Act's two most misreported phrases, and both were decided against High Court rulings that had narrowed the statute. In January 2021 the Nagpur Bench of the Bombay High Court, in judgments delivered on 15 and 19 January of that year, held that pressing the breast of a child over her clothes, without skin to skin contact, did not amount to sexual assault under Section 7, and convicted the accused instead under the milder Penal Code provision on outraging modesty, with a second judgment in the same set applying the same logic to a case under the aggravated provisions. The reasoning treated the word touch as requiring direct contact between skin and skin. The decisions produced an immediate public reaction and a stay of the acquittal, and the appeals reached the Supreme Court at the instance of the Attorney General for India, the State of Maharashtra and the National Commission for Women among others. In Attorney General for India v. Satish, decided on 18 November 2021 by a bench of Uday Umesh Lalit, S. Ravindra Bhat and Bela M. Trivedi, JJ., the Supreme Court set aside the High Court judgments and restored the convictions recorded by the Special Courts. The Court held that the most important ingredient for constituting the offence of sexual assault under Section 7 is sexual intent and not skin to skin contact with the child; that restricting touch or physical contact to skin to skin contact would be a narrow and pedantic interpretation of the provision and would lead to an absurd result, since touching a child with sexual intent through gloves, clothing or a sheet would on that reading fall outside the offence altogether; and that the length of the minimum punishment prescribed for the offence, or the age of the victim, could not be a ground for reading the definition down so as to let the conduct escape the section. The decision is the clearest modern statement in this field of a principle the article has already used: in a protective statute, the definitional question is what the accused did and with what intent, and not what physical accident of clothing or covering attended the act.

The second decision concerns material rather than contact, and it cannot be understood without the amendment that preceded it. As enacted, Section 15 reached only storage for commercial purposes. The 2019 amendment substituted a new Section 15 with three distinct limbs, considered in the amendment article: a person who stores or possesses material involving a child but fails to delete or destroy it or report it to the designated authority, with an intention to share or transmit it, is liable to a fine of not less than five thousand rupees, rising on a second or subsequent offence to not less than ten thousand rupees; a person who stores or possesses such material for transmitting, propagating, displaying or distributing it, except for the purpose of reporting or for use as evidence in court, is punishable with imprisonment up to three years, or fine, or both; and a person who stores or possesses such material for a commercial purpose faces three to five years and fine on a first conviction and five to seven years with fine on a subsequent conviction. In January 2024 the Madras High Court, quashing a prosecution founded on two video files found on the accused's telephone, held that mere possession or storage or viewing of such material, without an intention to transmit, was not an offence under Section 15 or under Section 67B of the Information Technology Act. In Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish, decided on 23 September 2024 by a bench of D. Y. Chandrachud, C.J., and J. B. Pardiwala, J., the Supreme Court set aside the quashing and restored the prosecution. The Court held that Section 15 creates distinct offences and that possession in the section includes constructive possession, meaning control over material with knowledge and the power to determine its fate, so that a person who views material and neither deletes it nor reports it, in circumstances attracting the first limb of the section, commits the offence the limb describes; that possession of this character is an inchoate offence, complete in itself though it anticipates the graver offences of transmission and distribution; and that the presumption of culpable mental state under Section 30 is capable of applying even at the stage of a petition to quash proceedings, since the question at that stage is whether the foundational facts alleged, taken as they stand, attract the provision. The Court also addressed the language of the field directly. It directed that courts should not use the expression child pornography in orders and judgments, and recommended that Parliament consider substituting the expression child sexual exploitative and abuse material, on the ground that the older term borrows the vocabulary of lawful adult entertainment for conduct that is a record of abuse. The practical consequence for any summary of the Act is that the once common statement that mere possession is not an offence in India is wrong twice over: wrong about the amended section, and wrong about the construction the Supreme Court has placed on it.

The judgment also reached the intermediaries through whom such material moves. The Court treated the obligations of an online intermediary as engaged by the presence of such material on its service: an intermediary that wishes to keep the safe harbour the Information Technology Act affords it must act against material of this character by removing it and reporting it, and passive hosting is not a neutral position once the material is shown to be within its systems. The holding connects the two statutes the prosecution in that case had joined, Section 15 of this Act and Section 67B of the Information Technology Act, and it extends the reporting philosophy of this Act, under which discovery creates a duty, to the online offences as well, so that the platform stands in a position analogous to the studio and the photographic facility that Section 20 has obliged since 2012.

The two decisions also illustrate the asymmetry in how this Act develops. Its contact offences were settled by interpretation of words Parliament wrote in 2012, intent and touch, and the settlement required nothing from the legislature. Its material offences could not be settled that way, because the original Section 15 had been drafted for a world of physical storage for commercial purposes and did not contain the words from which a viewing offence could be built; the legislature had to return in 2019 and rebuild the section before the Court could construe it in 2024. A statute pillar's judicial history is therefore two histories: the history of the courts working out what the enacted text means, and the history of the text being replaced where its first drafting had run out, and the POCSO Act supplies a clean example of each.

## A case through the Act: from disclosure to judgment

The statute is best tested against the sequence it prescribes, and a single worked case shows how the machines described above engage one another. Take the case the aggravated sections were written for. A girl of thirteen, resident in a school hostel, tells the hostel warden that a member of the institution's staff has been calling her to his room and touching her, and on two occasions has committed acts that answer to the definition in Section 3. The warden's position is fixed by Section 19 before she has decided anything: she has knowledge, the duty to report to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police has attached, and the option of handling the matter by transferring the staff member or warning him is not an option the Act gives her. If she suppresses the report she commits an offence under Section 21(1); if the principal, told of the matter, buries it, the principal commits the heavier offence under Section 21(2) as the person in charge of the institution in respect of a subordinate under his control. The report is recorded in writing, read over and entered in the book. Because the child is in residential care and the alleged offender is inside the institution, the police record their reasons and make immediate arrangements for her care and protection, and within twenty four hours the matter is before the Child Welfare Committee, which must decide where she is to live while the case proceeds, and before the Special Court.

Classification happens next, and it is the decisive legal act of the case. The acts alleged are penetrative, so the charge is framed under Section 3; the accused is on the staff of an educational institution and committed the acts in that institution, and the acts are said to have been repeated, so two of the circumstances in Section 5 are engaged and the charge is of aggravated penetrative sexual assault, punishable under Section 6 with rigorous imprisonment beginning at ten years rather than at the seven years of Section 4. The child is below eighteen, which the prosecution will prove from the school admission register and her birth certificate, the first entries in the documentary hierarchy, because the whole jurisdiction depends on that proof and the defence is entitled to test it, including by medical estimation if the documents are absent or impeached, with the margin of error in such estimation resolved on the threshold question in the accused's favour. The medical examination is conducted under Section 27 by a woman doctor, in the presence of a person the child trusts, and its findings will be evidence about injury and timing, not about consent, which the Act has removed from the case. Her statement is recorded at a place of her choice by a woman officer of the required rank who is not in uniform, and then recorded again by a Magistrate under Section 25, as spoken by her, in the presence of a representative of her choice; the two statements, taken early and in the prescribed manner, are the spine of the prosecution, and departures from the prescribed manner are the first thing the defence will look for.

At the trial the machinery of Chapter VIII governs the encounter. The Special Court takes cognizance without committal. Her evidence is to be recorded within thirty days of cognizance; she testifies without seeing the accused, who hears her and instructs his advocate from behind the arrangement the court has made; the defence questions reach her through the judge, who excludes the aggressive and the character directed; she is not recalled repeatedly; a support person is present; and the proceedings are in camera, with her identity withheld from the record that will be published. The prosecution having proved the foundational facts, her age, the identity of the accused, and the acts, Section 29 requires the court to presume the offence unless the contrary is proved, and Section 30 requires it to presume the culpable mental state, which in a touching and penetration case is the intent with which the acts were done; the accused's answer, that the child was elsewhere, or that the acts did not occur, or that the complaint was fabricated at the instance of a colleague with a grievance against him, must be proved by him to the standard Section 30(2) prescribes if it is to displace the presumption, though the prosecution's foundation, if it cracks on age or on the child's own consistency, fails before the presumptions are reached. Interim compensation has been available since the first information report, on the Rule 7 factors, and does not depend on the outcome. If the court convicts, the sentence begins at the Section 6 floor and the fine is a punishment payable to the State, with compensation to the child addressed separately; if it acquits, the compensation recommendation may still be made, the Committee's care orders continue on their own track, and the institution's exposure under Section 21, if a report was suppressed anywhere along the chain, is unaffected by the acquittal of the principal accused. Every stage of that sequence is a stage the Act wrote. Under the law that preceded it, the same facts would have produced a modesty charge or an unnatural offences charge, a statement taken at a police station, a trial in open court facing the accused, no presumption, no timeline, no compensation, and a substantial probability of collapse at the classification stage before any of that was reached.

## The age of consent: the design problem at the centre of the Act

The Act's single age line at eighteen produces its hardest cases, and the brief for this article requires them to be stated without advocacy. The mechanism is simple. Because a child is a person below eighteen and because the offences are defined without any consent element, a sexual act involving a person of sixteen or seventeen is an offence under the Act however willing the younger party was, and the stated consent of that party is legally irrelevant to liability, relevant only, if at all, at sentencing. Where the younger party is below sixteen, the 2019 amendment's raised minimum for penetrative assault makes the consequence severe even on a single act. The cases that reach the courts in this category have a recurring shape. An adolescent couple elope or are discovered; the girl's parents, objecting to the relationship on grounds of caste, religion, family honour or the boy's circumstances, report the matter; the reporting duty then makes the criminal process compulsory rather than discretionary, because the police who receive the report cannot lawfully decline to record it and the doctor who examines the girl cannot lawfully stay silent; the girl frequently resiles from the prosecution account, testifies that the relationship was voluntary, or turns hostile; and the case ends either in an acquittal bought at the cost of a full trial for both families, or in a conviction of a young man for an act the complainant herself describes as consensual. The same age line runs across the adjoining statutes, and the adjacent question of marriage below the statutory age, which engages its own Act and its own line at eighteen for women, is treated in [the article on the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act](/2016/06/15/prohibition-child-marriage-act-2006/).

The sharpest collision the line produced was inside marriage. The Penal Code's rape provision carried an exception under which sexual intercourse by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under fifteen years of age, was not rape, so that between 2012 and 2017 the statute book simultaneously treated a girl of sixteen as a child incapable of consent for the purposes of this Act and as a wife capable in law of being subjected to intercourse by her husband without the protection of the rape provision. In Independent Thought v. Union of India, decided on 11 October 2017, the Supreme Court resolved the conflict in favour of the protective statute, reading down the exception so that the age in it is read as eighteen years, and holding that sexual intercourse with a wife below eighteen is rape. The judgment is an anchor for this article because of its reasoning as much as its result: the Court treated the POCSO definition of a child as the legislature's settled statement of the age of protection, and harmonised the older code to it, rather than treating the marital exception as a considered carve out from the new Act. The episode shows the direction of influence running from the special statute to the general law, and it forecloses the argument, still occasionally made, that marriage is an answer to a charge under this Act: a marriage to a child may be voidable or void under the marriage statutes, but it is not a defence to a POCSO charge, and after Independent Thought it is not a defence to a rape charge either.

The evidence about the scale of the category is substantial enough to be stated with attribution. A study by the Enfold Proactive Health Trust together with UNICEF India, authored by Swagata Raha and Shruti Ramakrishnan and published in December 2022, examined all 7,064 judgments delivered under the Act between 2016 and 2020 in Assam, Maharashtra and West Bengal, and found that 1,715 of them, which is 24.3 per cent, were what the researchers classified as romantic cases, in which the court record showed a consensual relationship between the child and the accused. In 46.6 per cent of those romantic cases the girl was between sixteen and eighteen years of age. Studies of individual states by the Centre for Child and the Law at the National Law School of India University found romantic cases forming a smaller but still material share of the cases analysed, ranging from about five per cent in Karnataka to about a fifth in Delhi, Assam, Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh. High Courts in several states, among them Karnataka and Madhya Pradesh, formally drew the problem to the attention of the Law Commission, and the Chief Justice of India publicly urged lawmakers in December 2022 to address the growing concern about the criminalisation of consensual adolescent relationships under the Act.

The protective case for the bright line, stated at full strength, is that the line is the protection. If consent were an element to be proved or disproved, every prosecution for the abuse of a fifteen, sixteen or seventeen year old would become an inquiry into the child's willingness, conducted in cross examination, in a courtroom, against a child who may be dependent on the accused or on the family that supports him. The grooming process works precisely by manufacturing apparent willingness, and a consent inquiry rewards it. A bright line removes the inquiry altogether: the prosecution proves age and act, and the child's state of mind is not an exhibit. The line also holds the position against child marriage and trafficking, where the claim that a girl of sixteen consented is the standard defence of the adults who arranged the transaction, and where any lowering of the age of consent would be used at once as a shield in those cases. Supporters of retention add that the alternative lines are not stable either: a line at sixteen recreates every one of these problems for children of fourteen and fifteen, and close in age exemptions create new litigation about the accused's age and the gap, in place of litigation about willingness.

The countervailing case, also at full strength, is that a law which cannot distinguish exploitation from adolescence will produce injustice at a predictable rate and will damage its own protective purpose. The Enfold figures show that roughly a quarter of the decided cases in three major states were not the cases the Act was imagined for. Those cases consume the Special Courts' time to the prejudice of the abuse cases waiting behind them; they expose the girl to a medical examination, repeated statements and cross examination she did not seek; they frequently result in the incarceration of a young man with no allegation of force; and they teach families and adolescents that the criminal law is an instrument of parental discipline rather than of protection, which deters reporting of genuine abuse in communities that have watched the statute used in this way. A further strand of the objection is medical. Because Section 19 obliges a medical practitioner to report, an adolescent who needs contraception, treatment for a sexually transmitted infection, or a termination of pregnancy faces the certainty that attending a hospital will generate a police report and a case against her partner; the interaction between the reporting duty and adolescent access to care, including the care governed by [the article on the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act](/2015/08/01/mtp-act-abortion-law-india/), is documented by practitioners as a deterrent to seeking treatment at all, which converts a child protection duty into a barrier between adolescents and the health system.

The Law Commission of India examined the question on references from the High Courts and reported in its 283rd Report, titled Age of Consent under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, submitted to the Ministry of Law and Justice on 27 September 2023, under the chairmanship of Justice Ritu Raj Awasthi. The Commission's conclusion must be stated exactly, because it is regularly misreported as a recommendation to lower the age. It concluded that it is not advisable to tinker with the existing age of consent, holding that reducing the age would have a direct and negative bearing on the fight against child marriage and child trafficking, and that carving out a blanket exception for consensual acts above sixteen would have unintended consequences of a severe nature. What it recommended instead was the introduction of guided judicial discretion in sentencing: amendments to Sections 4 and 8 that would allow a Special Court, in a case involving the tacit approval in fact, though not consent in law, of a child between sixteen and eighteen, to impose a sentence below the statutory minimum, guided by stated considerations, with corresponding changes recommended in the juvenile justice legislation and the Penal Code. The recommendation is a recommendation; it has not been enacted, and the Act continues to operate with the bright line and without the discretion. The design problem the Commission was circling is the honest place to leave the question. A single bright line cannot distinguish exploitation from adolescence, because the distinction is one of fact and relationship rather than of age. Every proposed repair relocates the difficulty rather than removing it: lowering the line moves it to a younger child; a consent inquiry reintroduces the cross examination the Act was written to prevent; a sentencing discretion leaves the arrest, the trial and the stigma intact and relieves only the sentence; and prosecutorial restraint, the option several High Courts have exercised case by case, depends on the disposition of individual judges in a system that was given a mandatory statute precisely because discretion had failed.

That restraint has in fact been exercised, and unevenly. Individual High Courts, confronted with couples who had married, or with a complainant who testified that the relationship was voluntary and asked for the proceedings to end, have in some cases quashed prosecutions or moderated sentences to the period already undergone, reasoning that a conviction would serve neither the child nor the purposes of the Act; other benches, on materially similar facts, have declined to intervene, reasoning that a court cannot write into the statute an exception Parliament withheld. The result is a map on which the outcome of a romantic case depends appreciably on the forum in which it is tried, which is itself among the strongest arguments made by those who want the question settled by legislation rather than accumulated from discretionary decisions taken case by case.

The Commission's survey of other jurisdictions, recorded in the report, found that the comparator systems it examined, among them the United States, Canada, Japan, Australia and South Africa, generally fix a lower age of consent and protect adolescents through close in age exemptions for consensual activity between peers, an architecture the Commission considered and declined to recommend for India. The comparative point is worth holding in both directions: India's line at eighteen without a close in age exemption is at the strict end of the range the Commission surveyed, and the systems at the other end purchase their flexibility by accepting the consent inquiries and the evidential contests that the Indian legislature, and the Commission after it, were unwilling to reopen.

### Why is the age of eighteen in POCSO so contested?

Because a single line does two jobs at once, in opposite directions. It is the rule that keeps a child's apparent willingness out of every abuse trial, which is the protection the Act was written to give, and the rule that makes every sexual act involving a person of sixteen or seventeen an offence regardless of consent.

## The record in practice: a procedural statute judged by its procedure

The series thesis for this article is that a statute whose innovation is procedural must be assessed by whether the procedure is followed, and the implementation record is therefore the real verdict on the Act. The record is mixed in a patterned way. The elements that depend on the text alone have worked: offences are charged under the Act rather than improvised from the Penal Code, boys are complainants in a way the old law did not permit, and the offence definitions have survived judicial scrutiny with their meaning clarified rather than narrowed. The elements that depend on building institutions have lagged in the manner the series has recorded for other statutes. In many districts the Special Court is a designated Court of Session carrying its ordinary docket as well, because Section 28 permits designation and Section 28(2) deems an existing children's court to be a Special Court, so the statutory form is satisfied without a dedicated court, dedicated staff or a dedicated cause list existing. The Union Government's answer to the designation problem was a funding scheme rather than an amendment: in 2019 the Centre approved a scheme of fast track special courts, funded from the Nirbhaya Fund, under which 1,023 such courts were to be set up across the country, 389 of them exclusively for cases under this Act, with the cost shared between the Centre and the states. The scheme addressed the right defect, since an exclusive court with a dedicated prosecutor and staff is the institutional form Section 28 imagines; but a scheme court is a funded post rather than a permanent institution, and its continuation depends on extensions of the scheme, so that the map of genuinely exclusive POCSO courts remains the map of a programme rather than of a settled court structure. The Special Public Prosecutor threshold of seven years of practice is met on paper in most districts, but the appointment is frequently an additional charge rather than a specialisation, and the support persons contemplated by Section 39 depend on state guidelines and on non-governmental organisations whose presence is uneven across districts. The timelines of Section 35 are the clearest measure. An independent analysis of disposed cases, reported in the press on the study's release, found an average disposal time of about 510 days, against the one year period the section prescribes as far as possible, with only a small number of states disposing of convictions within the statutory period. Figures of this kind move with the year and the data set and should be read for their direction rather than as a standing measurement: across every available count, disposal runs well beyond the statutory year, and the thirty day period for the child's evidence is met in a minority of cases. Pendency has compounded, and the same study reporting found three acquittals for every conviction across the cases it analysed, a ratio driven in large part by witnesses, including the child, turning hostile in the long interval between the report and the trial, which is precisely the interval Section 35 was written to compress. The reasons witnesses turn hostile in this field are structural and should be named: the accused is frequently a relative, a neighbour, a teacher or the family's earner, so the household that reported under compulsion of the duty spends the years of the trial living with the consequences of its own report; the child grows older and is pressed to settle; and the support structures that might hold the child's first account steady, the support person, the counsellor, the compensation that arrives in time, are the very elements whose provision is most uneven. An acquittal recorded as founded on a hostile witness is therefore often the end of a process in which the offence chapters of the Act performed exactly as drafted and the procedure chapters were never delivered, which is the recurring finding this article has recorded from the commissions' monitoring and from the appellate courts' complaints about investigation.

The reporting duty shows the same pattern from the other side. Prosecutions under Section 21 for failure to report are rare relative to the ubiquity of the duty, and the duty's real effect has been institutional rather than prosecutorial: schools, hospitals and residential institutions have adopted internal protocols, designated officers and documentation practices because the personal liability of the person in charge of the institution under Section 21(2) concentrates attention in a way that a duty without a penalty would not have done. The Act's publicity and training obligations in Section 43 are the least performed of its provisions, and the recurring judicial complaint in appeals under the Act is not that the law was misunderstood but that its procedural clauses were ignored by investigators who had never been trained in them, statements taken at the police station, children produced before the accused, medical examinations delayed. That complaint, repeated across High Courts, is the implementation verdict in one sentence: the statute's distinctive contribution is a procedure, and the procedure is the part of the statute most often not delivered.

## The 2019 amendment in outline

Because the article states the Act as enacted, the amendment of 2019 is recorded here at headline level and no further. The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (Amendment) Act, 2019, which is Act 25 of 2019 and which came into force on 16 August 2019, raised the punishment scales across the offence chapter, most sharply by introducing the death penalty as a possible punishment for aggravated penetrative sexual assault and by raising the minimum term for penetrative sexual assault, with a still higher minimum where the child is below sixteen. It inserted a definition of child pornography into Section 2, enhanced the punishments in Section 14, substituted the three limb Section 15 set out in the discussion of the Harish judgment above, wrote the compensation direction into Section 33(8), and added Section 42A, which declares that the Act operates in addition to and not in derogation of any other law, and that in the event of inconsistency the Act's provisions have effect over the other law. None of that changed the Act's architecture: the definitions of the offences, the reporting duty, the procedure chapters and the presumptions stand where the Act of 2012 put them, and the argument the amendment conducted was an argument about sentencing severity. The full treatment of what the amendment changed, clause by clause, and of the capital punishment debate it opened, belongs to [the article on the POCSO Amendment Act of 2019](/2016/05/01/pocso-amendment-act-2019-explained/), and is not rehearsed here. Two of its smaller changes deserve record because they altered the working of the original scheme rather than its scales. The amendment directed that the fine imposed under the principal punishment sections be just and reasonable and be paid to the child for medical expenses and rehabilitation, which for the first time connected the punitive fine to the victim rather than leaving compensation entirely to the Rule 7 route; and by inserting a statutory definition of child pornography into Section 2 it gave the material offences a defined subject matter they had previously drawn from the general language of Section 13. Neither change disturbed the reporting duty, the procedure chapters or the presumptions, which is the clearest evidence that by 2019 the argument about this Act had narrowed to the single question of how severely its offences should be punished.

## Assessment and verdict

The Act's achievement is best stated in its own terms. It took a field in which the offence definitions did not fit the conduct, the victims of one sex were legally invisible, and no procedural protection existed anywhere, and it replaced that field with a complete code: definitions matched to the conduct, an aggravated scale that names the abuse of trust as the central aggravation, a reporting duty that binds every adult, a procedure organised around the witness, a forum built for the case, presumptions that answer the evidentiary conditions of private crime, and compensation that does not wait for a conviction. Judged as drafting, it is the most complete special criminal statute of its period in India, and its central insight, that in offences against children the process is a second site of harm and must be legislated for as carefully as the offence, has been absorbed into the general law's later treatment of vulnerable witnesses, most visibly when the Supreme Court in Mahender Chawla v. Union of India in December 2018 approved a Witness Protection Scheme for witnesses generally, extending to all trials a logic this Act had applied to one class of witness six years earlier.

The claim this article has carried can be stated plainly at the end. This is a statute written around the witness. Count its distinctive provisions and almost none of them define wrongdoing: the place of the statement, the rank and dress of the officer, the night that may not be spent in a police station, the screen between the child and the accused, the questions routed through the judge, the bar on repeated testimony, the thirty day clock, the identity that may not be printed, the compensation that does not await a verdict. The offence ladder could have been enacted as an amendment to the Penal Code in a dozen sections. What could not have been enacted that way is a procedural code for a single class of witness, and it is that code, rather than the ladder, that later law has borrowed.

Its exposed points are equally identifiable, and they are the mirror image of its strengths. The bright line at eighteen is both the protection and the source of the romantic cases that occupy a quarter of the decided docket in the states studied. The presumptions are both the answer to private crime and the sharpest departure from the ordinary law's premises, and they sit beside mandatory minima that leave a sentencing judge little room when a case at the margins goes wrong. The reporting duty is both the engine of institutional compliance and a barrier between adolescents and medical care. And the procedure, which is the Act's real innovation, is the part most dependent on institutions that were designated rather than built, so that the distance between the Act on paper and the Act in a district court remains the operative criticism, ahead of any criticism of its text. A reader keeping structured notes on this statute, its section map and the two judgments that fixed its meaning will find it useful to hold them in one private workspace: [the ReportMedic document viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) serves that purpose for the files, drafts and tables a reader assembles around an article of this length. The verdict the record supports is that the Act of 2012 got its statute right and its institutions half built, and that the live question about it is not what it says but whether the thirty days, the woman officer out of uniform, the screen between the child and the accused, and the support person beside the child are present in the district where the next report is made.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What offences does the POCSO Act create?

The Act creates a graded set of offences in its offence chapters. Section 3 defines penetrative sexual assault and Section 5 its aggravated form; Section 7 defines sexual assault, meaning touching with sexual intent, and Section 9 its aggravated form; Section 11 defines sexual harassment, covering words, gestures, exhibition, showing material to a child, persistent following or electronic contact and threats using images; Section 13 covers the use of a child for pornographic purposes; and Section 15 covers the storage of pornographic material involving a child. Abetment is punishable under Section 17 with the punishment for the offence itself, and an attempt is punishable under Section 18 with up to one half of the term provided for the completed offence.

### Q: Is the POCSO Act gender neutral?

Yes, in both directions. Section 2(d) defines a child as any person below the age of eighteen years, so the protection covers boys and girls equally, which the Penal Code scheme it displaced did not do, since the rape provision protected only female victims and abuse of boys had to be prosecuted under unrelated provisions. The offences are also drafted against whoever commits them, without any assumption about the sex of the accused, so a woman can be charged under the Act on the same terms as a man. Gender neutrality was one of the central reasons for a separate statute: the abuse of boys was a large and largely invisible part of the problem the general law could not name.

### Q: What makes a sexual offence against a child aggravated?

An offence becomes aggravated when one of the circumstances listed in Section 5, or in Section 9 for sexual assault, is present. The lists turn on three things. The first is who the offender is: a police officer, a member of the armed forces or security forces, a public servant, the management or staff of a jail, remand home, hospital, educational or religious institution, a person in a position of trust or authority, or a relative or a person sharing the child's household. The second is the child: a child below twelve years, or a child whose mental or physical disability is taken advantage of. The third is the manner: use of weapons or corrosive substances, grievous hurt, repetition, gang commission, or causing mental illness or lasting impairment.

### Q: Who must report an offence under POCSO?

Everyone. Section 19(1) obliges any person, including the child, who has knowledge that an offence under the Act has been committed, or an apprehension that one is likely to be committed, to provide that information to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or to the local police. There is no exception for parents, relatives, doctors, teachers, counsellors or employers, and no exception based on confidentiality. Section 20 adds a specific duty for personnel of the media and of hotels, lodges, hospitals, clubs, studios and photographic facilities who come across material that is sexually exploitative of a child. A person who reports in good faith is protected by Section 19(7) from any civil or criminal liability for giving the information.

### Q: What special procedure protects a child during investigation?

Chapter VI rewrites the investigative encounter. Under Section 24 the child's statement is recorded at the child's residence, the place the child usually resides or a place of the child's choice, as far as practicable by a woman police officer not below the rank of sub-inspector, who is not in uniform; the child must not be brought into contact with the accused at any point; and no child may be detained in a police station in the night for any reason. Under Section 25 a Magistrate records the child's statement as spoken, in the presence of the parents or a representative of the child's choice, and Section 26 provides interpreters, translators and special educators where the child needs them. Section 27 requires a girl child's medical examination to be conducted by a woman doctor, in the presence of a parent or a person the child trusts.

### Q: Does POCSO presume the accused is guilty?

For the principal offences, the Act reverses the burden in a defined way. Section 29 provides that where a person is prosecuted for committing, abetting or attempting an offence under Sections 3, 5, 7 or 9, the Special Court shall presume that the person committed the offence unless the contrary is proved. Section 30 separately requires the court to presume the existence of the culpable mental state the offence requires, while allowing the accused to prove that he had no such mental state. Section 30(2) sets the accused's standard high: a fact counts as proved only when the court believes it beyond reasonable doubt, not on a preponderance of probability. The prosecution must still prove the foundational facts, including the child's age and the act alleged, before the presumptions do their work.

### Q: Is viewing child sexual abuse material an offence?

Under the Act as amended in 2019 and construed by the Supreme Court, viewing can be. Section 15 as substituted contains three distinct offences, including the case of a person who stores or possesses material involving a child and fails to delete or destroy it or report it to the designated authority with an intention to share or transmit it, which carries a fine of not less than five thousand rupees. In Just Rights for Children Alliance v. S. Harish, decided on 23 September 2024, the Supreme Court set aside a Madras High Court decision that mere possession and viewing were no offence, holding that possession includes constructive possession through control over the material, and restored the prosecution. The Court also directed that the material be described as child sexual exploitative and abuse material rather than by the older expression.

### Q: What compensation can a child victim receive?

Compensation under the original Act was provided through Rule 7 of the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Rules, 2012, working with the victim compensation framework in Section 357A of the Code of Criminal Procedure. The Special Court may order interim compensation at any stage after the registration of the first information report, to meet the child's immediate needs for relief or rehabilitation, and that amount is adjusted against the final award. It may also recommend compensation where the accused is convicted, and equally where the case ends in acquittal or discharge or the accused is not traced or identified, provided the child has suffered loss or injury. The assessment weighs the gravity of the offence, the harm suffered, medical expenditure, and the loss of education or employment caused by the offence.

### Q: What is the punishment for penetrative sexual assault under the POCSO Act?

As enacted in 2012, Section 4 punished penetrative sexual assault with imprisonment of either description for a term not less than seven years, which could extend to imprisonment for life, together with liability to fine. The aggravated form, where Section 5 applies, was punished under Section 6 with rigorous imprisonment for a term not less than ten years, extending to imprisonment for life, and fine. Those were the original scales, and they are the ones stated in this article's account of the Act as passed. The Amendment Act of 2019 raised them substantially, increasing the minimum terms, adding a higher minimum where the child is below sixteen, and introducing the death penalty as a possible punishment for the aggravated offence.

### Q: What is the difference between sexual assault and sexual harassment under POCSO?

The dividing line is physical contact. Sexual assault under Section 7 requires an act done with sexual intent that involves physical contact without penetration: touching the child's sexual organs, making the child touch the accused or another person, or any other act with sexual intent involving such contact. It carries three to five years under Section 8, or five to seven years in the aggravated form. Sexual harassment under Section 11 requires no contact at all. It covers uttering words or making gestures or exhibiting objects intending the child to see or hear them, making a child exhibit the child's body, showing a child material for pornographic purposes, repeatedly following, watching or contacting a child including by electronic means, threatening to use a depiction of the child, and enticing a child for pornographic purposes. Section 12 punishes it with up to three years and fine.

### Q: What timelines does the POCSO Act set for recording evidence and completing the trial?

Section 35 sets two clocks. The evidence of the child must be recorded within thirty days of the Special Court taking cognizance of the offence, and the Special Court must record its reasons for any delay beyond that period. The trial itself is to be completed within a period of one year from the date of taking cognizance, as far as possible. The qualifying phrase matters: the one year period is a duty of effort rather than a limitation period whose expiry ends the prosecution, and a trial that runs longer is not thereby invalid. The purpose of the shorter clock for the child's evidence is evidentiary as much as humane, since a child's account recorded promptly is less exposed to pressure, rehearsal and the fading of memory than one recorded after years of waiting.

### Q: What is the role of the Child Welfare Committee in a POCSO case?

The Child Welfare Committee is the authority for the child's care, protection and rehabilitation, running parallel to the criminal case rather than inside it. Section 19 requires the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police to report the matter to the Committee within twenty four hours of receiving a report, together with the child's need for care and protection and the steps taken, and the police must make immediate arrangements for care, including admission to a shelter home or the nearest hospital, where that need appears. The Committee then decides the questions the criminal court does not: where the child is to live, whether the child can safely remain in the home, what institutional or foster arrangement is required, and what restoration and rehabilitation the child needs while the prosecution proceeds on its own track.

### Q: Can the media publish the name, photograph or school of a child in a POCSO case?

No. Section 23 forbids any report in any media from disclosing the identity of a child, and the prohibition is drawn widely: it covers the child's name, address, photograph, family details, school, neighbourhood and any other particulars that may lead to the disclosure of the child's identity. Disclosure is permitted only where the Special Court allows it, for reasons recorded in writing, and considers it to be in the interest of the child. The publisher or owner of the media is jointly and severally liable for the acts and omissions of an employee, so the duty cannot be delegated away inside a newsroom. Contravention is punishable with imprisonment for a term of not less than six months and up to one year, or with fine, or with both. Section 24(5) places a matching duty on the police to protect the child's identity from the public media.

### Q: What is the penalty for failing to report child sexual abuse under POCSO?

Section 21 punishes two failures. A person who fails to report an offence as Section 19 or Section 20 requires, or an officer who fails to record a report as Section 19 requires, is punishable with imprisonment of either description which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both. The penalty is heavier for hierarchy: a person in charge of a company or an institution who fails to report an offence committed by a subordinate under his control is punishable with imprisonment which may extend to one year and with fine, the fine being compulsory in that limb. Section 21(3) exempts one class entirely: the failure to report provision does not apply to a child, so a child who stays silent about abuse, whether of himself or of another child, commits no offence under the section.

### Q: Can a child be punished for making a false complaint under POCSO?

No. Section 22 punishes a false complaint or false information only where it is made against a person in respect of an offence under Sections 3, 5, 7 or 9, solely with the intention to humiliate, extort, threaten or defame that person, and the punishment is imprisonment which may extend to six months, or fine, or both. Section 22(2) then provides expressly that where a false complaint has been made or false information provided by a child, no punishment shall be imposed on that child, whether the account was mistaken, exaggerated or invented. The protection is deliberate: the legislature refused to let fear of a false complaint prosecution deter children from reporting. The section does punish an adult who makes a false complaint against a child, knowing or having reason to believe it false, with up to one year.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide about skin to skin contact under POCSO?

In Attorney General for India v. Satish, decided on 18 November 2021 by a bench of Uday Umesh Lalit, S. Ravindra Bhat and Bela M. Trivedi, JJ., the Supreme Court set aside judgments of the Nagpur Bench of the Bombay High Court which had held that pressing a child's breast over her clothes, without skin to skin contact, was not sexual assault under Section 7. The Court held that the most important ingredient of the offence is sexual intent and not skin to skin contact; that confining touch or physical contact to direct contact between skin and skin would be a narrow and pedantic construction leading to an absurd result, since touching through clothing, gloves or a sheet would then escape the section; and that the severity of the minimum punishment is no reason to read the definition down. The convictions recorded by the Special Courts were restored.

### Q: What did the Law Commission recommend about the age of consent under POCSO?

The Law Commission's 283rd Report, titled Age of Consent under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 and submitted to the Ministry of Law and Justice on 27 September 2023, recommended against lowering the age. It held that reducing the age of consent from eighteen would have a direct and negative bearing on the fight against child marriage and child trafficking, and that a blanket exception for consensual acts above sixteen would carry severe unintended consequences. Its positive recommendation was narrower and is often misstated: guided judicial discretion in sentencing, through amendments to Sections 4 and 8, so that a Special Court could impose less than the statutory minimum in cases involving the tacit approval in fact, though not consent in law, of a child aged between sixteen and eighteen. The recommendation remains a recommendation and has not been enacted.

### Q: What happens when the same act is an offence under both POCSO and the Penal Code?

Section 42 applies a greater punishment rule. Where an act or omission constitutes an offence punishable under the POCSO Act and also under specified provisions of the Indian Penal Code or under any other law in force, the offender is liable to punishment under this Act or under the other law, whichever provides for the punishment that is greater in degree. The offender is therefore tried and sentenced once, on the heavier of the two available scales, rather than punished twice for the same act or allowed to claim the benefit of the older and milder provision. In practice a charge sheet in a case involving a child will ordinarily invoke the POCSO offences as the primary charges, with the Penal Code provisions pleaded in the alternative where the conduct fits both codes.

### Q: What must a school do when a child discloses sexual abuse?

The school's duty is statutory and personal, and it is not discharged by an internal inquiry. On receiving a disclosure, the staff member who learns of it, and the institution, must report the information to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or the local police under Section 19(1), because the duty attaches to knowledge and to apprehension alike and admits no exception for handling the matter within the institution. The person in charge of the school carries the heavier exposure: under Section 21(2), a person in charge of an institution who fails to report an offence committed by a subordinate under his control faces imprisonment of up to one year and a fine. The school should also preserve the child's account without repeated questioning, since the child's formal statements are recorded by the police under Section 24 and by a Magistrate under Section 25, and should cooperate with the care and protection steps the police and the Child Welfare Committee initiate.

### Q: Can a child have a lawyer and a support person during a POCSO case?

Yes to both, on different foundations. Section 40 entitles the child to the services of a legal counsel of the child's own choice, and where the child's family is unable to afford one, to free legal services provided through the Legal Services Authority. The right matters because the child's interests do not always coincide with the prosecution's: a child may wish to resist a prosecution the family has set in motion, or to press for compensation the prosecutor has not pursued. The support person rests on Section 39, under which State Governments must prepare guidelines for the involvement of non-governmental organisations, professionals and experts in psychology, social work, physical and mental health and child development, to be associated with the child at the pre-trial and trial stages and to assist the child through the process.
