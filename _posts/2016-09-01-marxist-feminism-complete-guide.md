Marxist feminism is a distinct tradition within feminism, with origins in the nineteenth century rather than the twentieth, and a central claim that sets it apart from every neighbouring school. The claim is not that class matters more than sex. It is that women's oppression is bound up with the way a society organises production and the renewal of its people together, and in particular with the unpaid labour, performed overwhelmingly by women in households, through which the workforce itself is fed, rested, raised, and renewed. A feminism built on that claim asks different questions from a feminism built on legal exclusion, and different questions again from one built on an autonomous system of male domination. It also has a history that standard accounts get wrong in a consequential way: an organised socialist women's movement, with its own theorists, newspapers, conferences, and legislative record, existed for more than half a century before the feminism of the nineteen seventies took up the argument in the universities. This guide treats the tradition whole: the pre-history, the founding text, the three theoretical positions into which the tradition divides, the strongest objection raised against its most ambitious version, and the record of the states that claimed to act on its programme.

One warning belongs at the threshold, because nearly every error in the secondary coverage grows from ignoring it. Marxist feminism is not one position. It contains three theoretical positions that contradict one another on the basic question of what women's oppression is, and the writers who hold them have spent decades saying so in print. A guide that irons the three into a single doctrine called Marxist feminism, class with a feminist supplement, misdescribes the tradition at exactly the point where the tradition is most interesting. The three positions share ancestors, texts, and an enemy in the purely formal equality of liberal feminism, but they give different accounts of the mechanism that subordinates women, and the difference in mechanism produces different politics. Keeping them separate is the first discipline this article observes, and the three positions grid below exists to make the separation findable at a glance.

A second warning concerns the cast. The names that dominate accessible coverage of this tradition are the academic names of the nineteen seventies: the essayists of the domestic labour debate, the theorists of dual systems, the campaigners for wages for housework. Those writers matter, and each receives a full hearing in what follows. But behind them stands an earlier movement generation that the academic phase largely forgot it had inherited: August Bebel, whose book on women and socialism outsold every other theoretical text the socialist movement produced; Clara Zetkin, who edited a women's newspaper with a mass circulation and carried the proposal for an international women's day into a conference of socialist women; Alexandra Kollontai, who wrote the most searching early work on the family and sexual relations and then administered welfare in a revolutionary government; and Eleanor Marx, who carried the argument into the English movement in her father's lifetime. Recovering that generation is not antiquarianism. It changes the assessment of what the tradition is, because a tradition with institutions and a legislative record is a different object from a tradition consisting only of arguments.

The third preliminary point concerns evidence. The tradition makes claims about states as well as about theory, since governments claiming Marxist authority held power across much of the twentieth century and legislated extensively on marriage, divorce, abortion, childcare, and women's employment. That record is genuinely double-edged, and this guide reports it double-edged: the early Soviet legal transformation was the most far-reaching emancipation enacted anywhere in its decade, and the reversal that followed from the middle nineteen thirties was equally real; high female participation in employment across the socialist states coexisted for decades with a double burden of waged work and unpaid housework and with the near-absence of women from political leadership. Neither the achievements nor the reversals are trimmed here to fit a verdict, because the tradition's own theorists treated the record as the test of their theory, and a guide owes its readers the test in full.

## What Marxist feminism claims

The canonical definition can be stated in one sentence, and the rest of this section unpacks it. Marxist feminism analyses women's oppression as bound up with the capitalist arrangement of production and reproduction, and particularly with the unpaid labour through which labour power is renewed, rather than as a separate system of male domination or as a problem of legal exclusion. Each clause of that sentence marks a boundary. Against liberal feminism, it denies that women's subordination consists fundamentally in exclusion from rights, professions, and offices that men monopolise, since formal equality has repeatedly coexisted with substantive subordination. Against the radical feminist account of patriarchy as a self-standing system, it denies that male domination floats free of the way a society organises its material life. And against a crude Marxism that treats the woman question as a distraction from class, it insists that the reproduction of the workforce is not a private sideshow to production but one of production's own conditions, performed daily in kitchens and bedrooms rather than factories.

### How does Marxist feminism differ from other feminisms?

It locates women's oppression in the capitalist organisation of production and reproduction, particularly the unpaid labour that renews labour power daily and generationally, rather than in legal exclusion or in an autonomous system of male domination. Its remedy therefore requires changing that organisation.

The term labour power does heavy work in the definition and deserves a plain statement. Labour power is the capacity to work, the one commodity the worker owns and sells; its value, in Marx's analysis, is set by the cost of the goods and services that keep the worker alive, fit, and replaced by a next generation. But those goods do not prepare themselves. Purchased food must be cooked, purchased clothing washed and mended, a purchased dwelling cleaned, children borne and raised, the sick and the exhausted tended back to capacity. The labour that performs these transformations is mostly unpaid, mostly female, and mostly invisible to an economics that counts only waged hours. Marxist feminism begins where that invisibility is treated as a fact to be explained rather than a natural background. The household, on this account, is not outside the economy. It is the economy's unacknowledged department, and the woman question is the question of who staffs it, on what terms, and under whose control.

### What does the tradition mean by the reproduction of labour power?

It means the work, daily and generational, that returns workers to the labour market able to work: feeding, clothing, rest, care in sickness, and the bearing and raising of the next generation of workers. The tradition's point is that this work is socially necessary, mostly unpaid, and assigned to women as a sex.

From the definition, a characteristic method follows. Where liberal analysis asks whether a law or a hiring practice treats women and men alike, Marxist feminist analysis asks who performs the reproductive labour a given arrangement presupposes, and what happens to that labour when the arrangement changes. A policy that draws women into full-time employment while leaving the household untouched does not abolish reproductive labour; it compresses it into evenings and weekends, producing the double burden that socialist states later reproduced at scale. A wage system that pays a man a family wage and a woman a supplementary wage is not merely prejudiced; it encodes an assumption about which sex cooks. The method is materialist in the strict sense: follow the work, including the work nobody is paid for, and the pattern of subordination becomes legible as an organisation rather than a mystery of attitudes. Attitudes matter, and the tradition has things to say about ideology, but its founding gesture is to locate the pattern in the division of labour first and to treat the attitudes as the division's rationalisation.

The definition also fixes the tradition's account of what emancipation would require, and here the three positions begin to diverge in ways the later sections keep separate. If women's oppression is rooted in the organisation of reproduction, then emancipation requires transforming that organisation: socialising parts of housework and childcare, reorganising the remainder, and ending the assignment of the residue to women as a sex. Bebel's nineteenth-century version of the remedy joined two demands, women's entry into social production on equal terms and the conversion of private housekeeping into social industry, communal kitchens and laundries replacing the isolated household's duplicated toil. Later positions radicalised or revised the remedy: the autonomist wing demanded a wage for housework as a lever against the household itself, while the unitary theorists of social reproduction treated the wage demand as a category error and looked to collective provision and the reorganisation of working time. What no version accepts is that emancipation is complete when a woman may sign a contract, enter a profession, or sit in a legislature while the second shift waits at home unchanged.

A final clarification separates this tradition from two of its labels. Socialist feminism, as the term settled in the nineteen seventies, usually names the dual systems position examined below, the view that capitalism and patriarchy are two systems requiring a politics that fights both; the identification of the two labels in popular coverage is one of the errors this guide corrects in its own section. Materialist feminism, associated above all with French writers such as Christine Delphy, shares the method of following material relations rather than ideas, but locates women's exploitation in a distinct patriarchal mode of production centred on marriage, which makes it a cousin rather than a member of the Marxist feminist family. The guide keeps the family boundary where the brief for the tradition itself keeps it: Marxist feminism names the positions that explain women's oppression through the way capitalism organises making goods and making life together, whether they conclude that the system is one or that two systems interlock.

## The pre-history correction: a movement before the academy

The standard story of feminism's development, repeated in textbooks and reference pages, runs in waves: a first wave of suffrage campaigning, a trough, then a second wave from the late nineteen sixties in which theory was made. Marxist feminism enters that story in the second wave, as one of the schools the nineteen seventies produced, distinguished by its insistence on class. The story is not false about the suffrage campaigns or about the academic flowering, but about this tradition it is wrong by more than half a century, and the error is the one this series names the pre-history correction. An organised socialist women's movement, with its own theory, its own institutions, and a legislative record in government, existed from the eighteen eighties onward. What the nineteen seventies did was not to invent Marxist feminism but to rediscover it, under changed conditions, after a long interruption; and the rediscovery was partial, because the academic second phase never recovered the mass readership the movement phase had commanded.

### Why does the story usually begin in the wrong decade?

Because the tradition's academic phase, from the late nineteen sixties, wrote the histories, and it wrote them from the university outward. The movement phase had published in party newspapers, conference reports, and agitational pamphlets that libraries kept badly, so the story as usually told starts where the footnotes start, in the journals, two generations late.

Consider the scale the correction restores. The German Social Democratic Party, the largest socialist party in Europe before the First World War, maintained a women's movement inside itself with its own newspaper, its own conferences, and its own theoretical literature, at a time when German law until 1908 forbade women to belong to political associations at all, a prohibition the movement circumvented through educational associations and the newspaper's subscription network. The newspaper, Die Gleichheit, edited by Clara Zetkin from the early eighteen nineties, reached a circulation in six figures before the war, a readership no feminist journal of the academic phase has approached. The movement's theoretical text, Bebel's book of 1879, ran through dozens of editions in the author's lifetime and was translated across Europe and beyond; historians of the socialist movement describe it as the movement's best-selling theoretical work on any subject, read in workers' households that owned few books. Whatever one thinks of its arguments, a literature with a six-figure newspaper and a bestseller is not a footnote to a later debate. It is a public.

The correction also restores a sequence that the wave story scrambles. The socialist women's movement did not trail behind the bourgeois suffrage movement as its left wing; on the central strategic question it took a different line, refusing alliances that subordinated the demand for universal suffrage to campaigns for a limited, property-qualified women's vote, and insisting that working women needed the full democratic franchise and economic transformation together. Zetkin's position, carried through the socialist women's conferences, held that the emancipation of women would be won by working women in their own movement or not at all, a formulation that annoyed both the bourgeois feminist organisations and the male leadership of her own party, which preferred the woman question deferred until after the revolution. That internal argument, between a movement that had built women's institutions and a party leadership that tolerated them conditionally, runs through the whole pre-history and explains much of its later fragility. The movement was real, and it was never secure inside its own house.

Why did the interruption happen, so completely that the second phase could mistake itself for a beginning? The causes are historical rather than mysterious. The First World War split the international socialist movement whose women's conferences had coordinated the work; the Russian Revolution drew the most energetic organisers into state-building, where the women's department was dissolved within little more than a decade and its agenda subordinated; fascism physically destroyed the German movement, the tradition's centre of gravity, murdering or exiling its cadres and burning its literature; and the Cold War made the surviving texts suspect in the West while the states claiming Marxist authority in the East codified an official line on the woman question that froze inquiry rather than continuing it. A tradition can survive defeat; what it cannot easily survive is the loss of its institutions of transmission, its newspapers, schools, and conferences. The academic phase rebuilt transmission inside universities, which preserved the arguments and lost the public. Both halves of that sentence belong in any honest account of the tradition's shape now.

The pre-history correction matters beyond chronology because it changes what the theory can be assessed against. A purely academic tradition stands or falls by its arguments. A tradition that once ran a mass movement, held governmental power in one state, and legislated on the family across several more can also be assessed against its record, and its own best theorists insisted on exactly that assessment. Kollontai judged the revolution by what it did for the women queuing at the communal kitchens she organised. The dual systems objection examined later in this guide draws much of its force from the same record: if male domination survived the abolition of capitalist property in the states that attempted it, the objection runs, then male domination was never a function of capitalist property in the first place. The pre-history is therefore not a decorative prologue. It is the evidence base on which the tradition's internal argument has always been conducted, and recovering it is what allows the argument to be stated at the strength its participants gave it.

## The bestseller, the editor, and the commissar

Three figures carry the pre-history between them, and each represents a different kind of contribution the academic phase undervalues: the populariser who gave the movement its shared text, the organiser who gave it institutions, and the theorist-administrator who tested its programme in government. August Bebel was a co-founder and leader of German social democracy, a turner by trade, and the author in 1879 of the book published in English as Woman and Socialism. The book's argument joined historical sweep to a practical programme. Women, Bebel argued, had been the first slaves, subordinated with the rise of private property and the monogamous family; their emancipation therefore required the same transformation that would emancipate labour, together with specific measures: entry into social production, the vote, equal education, and the socialisation of domestic labour, the private household's cooking, cleaning, and childcare converted into collective services conducted as social industry. The book was banned at times, prosecuted, and refuted from pulpits, and it sold without pause, edition after edition, in the dozens across the author's lifetime, reaching readers, very many of them women, who encountered in it the first account most had seen of their own condition as a historical arrangement rather than a fate.

### What did Bebel's book argue that made it a bestseller?

It argued that women's subordination began with private property rather than with nature, that it would end only when women entered social production as equals and private housekeeping became collective provision, and that socialism therefore owed women a programme, not a promise. Workers' households bought it because it treated their kitchens as politics.

Clara Zetkin's contribution was organisational before it was textual, though her texts were formidable: a pamphlet of 1889 on the women workers' question, a celebrated address to the Gotha congress of 1896 insisting that women workers be organised as workers, and three decades editing Die Gleichheit, which she turned from a small paper into the movement's women's organ with a mass readership. Zetkin's strategic line, that proletarian women must fight alongside the men of their class against capital while fighting inside their own movement for the woman question to be taken seriously, defined the socialist women's movement's posture toward both bourgeois feminism and its own party. She opposed collaboration with campaigns for a restricted women's suffrage that would enfranchise propertied women on the same unequal terms as propertied men, arguing that such a reform would strengthen the possessing classes and divide working women from the demand for universal suffrage. The line cost the socialist women allies and respectability; it also gave them a politics that was theirs, argued in their own conference and their own paper, rather than borrowed from a wealthier movement whose programme ended at the polling station.

Alexandra Kollontai brought the argument into government and into the territory where it remains most distinctive: the family, sexuality, and the emotional organisation of everyday life. A Menshevik turned Bolshevik, an agitator among women workers before the revolution, she became People's Commissar for Social Welfare in the first Soviet government, among the first women anywhere to hold cabinet office, and from that post and through the women's department she helped to build, she drove the legal and institutional programme examined in the record section below. Her theoretical work, beginning with The Social Basis of the Woman Question in 1909 and running through essays of the early nineteen twenties on the family and on what she called winged Eros, insisted that the transformation of production would not by itself transform intimate life: new forms of comradely relations between the sexes had to be constructed, against both bourgeois possessiveness and the puritanism she found creeping into her own party. That insistence, together with her part in the Workers' Opposition, made her inconvenient; the party posted her abroad, to diplomatic missions in Norway, Mexico, and Sweden, where she remained for most of the rest of her life, a theorist of women's emancipation representing a state that had shelved the question. Her biography is the record section in miniature: achievement, reversal, and a theorist who lived long enough to watch both.

Around these three stood a wider cast the movement phase produced and the academic phase rarely names together. Eleanor Marx, the founder's youngest daughter, co-authored with Edward Aveling an early pamphlet on the woman question in 1886, organised women workers in the gasworkers' and other unions, and treated the subordination of women and the exploitation of labour as connected products of the property system in lectures across Britain and America. Inessa Armand, a Bolshevik organiser who had run a feminist journal in pre-war France before concluding that only revolution would serve women, headed the Soviet women's department in its first years and organised the great congress of working and peasant women that launched its work. Rosa Luxemburg, whose main labours lay elsewhere, wrote on women's suffrage and class struggle in 1912 with characteristic edge, arguing that the proletarian woman's enfranchisement was a weapon the whole movement needed. None of these women waited for permission from the nineteen seventies to theorise their condition. The academic phase's ignorance of them was a fact about archives and transmission, not about the tradition's actual contents.

## Institutions: conferences, a newspaper, and a women's department

Movements are their institutions, and the socialist women's movement built more of them than the standard story records. From 1907, the movement coordinated internationally through conferences of socialist women attached to the Second International, the first at Stuttgart, the second at Copenhagen in 1910. The Stuttgart conference established an international women's secretariat, with Zetkin at its head, and recognised Die Gleichheit as the movement's international organ, binding the national women's movements into something with a centre, a press, and agreed lines. The form matters as much as the fact: this was a women's institution inside a mixed movement, autonomous enough to set its own agenda, which is precisely the organisational form the dual systems theorists of a later generation would argue, in theoretical terms, that feminism required. The movement phase practised in organisation what the academic phase would later debate in print.

### Where did the proposal for an international women's day come from?

From the Copenhagen conference of socialist women in 1910, where Clara Zetkin proposed that women in every country hold an annual day of demonstration for their demands, above all the vote. The proposal carried without a fixed date; the first observances followed in 1911 across Austria, Germany, Denmark, and Switzerland, drawing very large crowds.

The women's department of the Russian party, the Zhenotdel, founded in 1919 and led first by Armand and then by Kollontai, was the pre-history's most ambitious institution, because it worked inside a state. Its methods were those of the movement phase translated into administration: delegate meetings of working and peasant women that elected representatives and raised grievances upward; literacy campaigns; travelling exhibitions and legal advice points; the organisation of communal kitchens, laundries, and nurseries that were simultaneously services and propaganda for the socialisation of housework; and in Central Asia, eventually, campaigns against the veil and against the legal subordination of women in customary law, campaigns that met fierce resistance and cost women activists their lives in some districts. The department's premise was Kollontai's theoretical point in institutional form: legislation could change women's formal position quickly, but the material organisation of their days, and the attitudes of the men around them, would change only through sustained organised work among women themselves. Its abolition in 1930, on the stated ground that the woman question had been resolved, is one of the central exhibits in the record section, because the resolution was declared at almost exactly the moment the reversal of the legal gains began.

### What institutions did the socialist women's movement build?

An international conference of its own from 1907 with a permanent secretariat, a newspaper of mass circulation as its organ, women's sections and conferences inside the socialist parties of Europe, and, after 1917, a women's department of the ruling party in Russia that ran campaigns, services, and delegate meetings across a continent.

The third institutional form, the women's sections and conferences inside national parties, is the least visible and was the most widespread. German, Austrian, Russian, and other movements maintained organised women's work between the wars of position inside their own ranks: recruiting women members in workplaces and tenements, training women agitators, and forcing the woman question onto congress agendas against leaderships that found it divisive or secondary. The record of those internal struggles is instructive for the theory sections of this guide, because the complaint that Marxist movements subordinate women's demands to an allegedly general interest, deferring them until after the revolution, was not invented by the feminists of the nineteen seventies. It was the standing grievance of the movement's own women organisers from the eighteen nineties, documented in their conference reports. When Heidi Hartmann published the dual systems objection in 1979, she was giving theoretical form to an experience the tradition's women had been reporting for eighty years. The objection's longevity inside the tradition is part of its strength, and the guide returns to it after the theoretical positions are on the table.

## The founding text and its foundations

Every tradition in this series has a founding text, and Marxist feminism's is Engels's The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, published in 1884 and built from Marx's notebooks on the American anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan, whose Ancient Society had arranged human history in stages of savagery, barbarism, and civilisation. The full treatment of the book, its argument chapter by chapter and its long afterlife, belongs to the series guide to [engels origin of the family guide](/2016/11/01/engels-origin-of-the-family-guide/); the pillar's task here is narrower, to state what the text contributed to the tradition and on what foundations, since the three positions all descend from it and the strongest objections to the tradition frequently begin by attacking its anthropology. Engels argued that the earliest human societies held property in common and traced descent through the mother; that the domestication of animals and the growth of herds created a surplus controlled by men; and that men, wanting to pass that surplus to their own children, overthrew mother right and imposed the monogamous family, an event he called the world-historical defeat of the female sex. On this account the subordination of women is not natural or eternal. It arrived at a datable point in prehistory, together with private property and the state, and the first class opposition in history, Engels wrote, coincides with the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage.

### What did Engels call the overthrow of mother right?

He called it the world-historical defeat of the female sex: the moment when growing wealth in men's hands made descent through the father a material interest, mother right was abolished, and the monogamous family was imposed to guarantee heirs. The phrase matters because it dates women's subordination, making it historical and therefore changeable.

Two features of the argument shaped everything the tradition later did. The first is its materialism about the household. The monogamous family, for Engels, is an economic unit before it is a sentimental one: within it, the husband stands as the possessing class and the wife as the propertyless labourer, her domestic service the first form of unpaid labour exacted inside a property relation. Later theorists who had never reckoned with Morgan still worked with that move, the refusal to treat the family as a refuge from economic analysis. The second feature is the remedy's shape. If women's subordination entered history with private property and the family's economic function, it can leave history when women re-enter social production on a mass scale and domestic labour is converted from private service into public industry. Bebel's programme and the early Soviet experiments with communal dining and laundries are that sentence of Engels translated into policy, which is why the record of those experiments matters to the theory and is not a separate topic.

The foundations, however, have not held in the form Engels left them, and intellectual honesty requires the pillar to say so plainly. Morgan's unilinear stages, through which every people was supposed to pass in the same order, have been abandoned by anthropology; descent through the mother, where it existed, did not amount to rule by women, and matriliny is not matriarchy; and the picture of an original communism overturned at a single stroke by herding wealth reads, against the ethnographic record assembled since, as a nineteenth-century construction from the materials available in Marx's study. Feminist anthropologists of the nineteen seventies who went back to the text with sympathy, among them Eleanor Leacock, who edited Morgan and defended a revised version of Engels's egalitarian prehistory, and Karen Sacks, who reworked the argument around women's relation to production, treated the book as a research programme to be corrected rather than a finding to be repeated. That is the settled position this guide adopts: the founding text contributed the questions, the method of asking after the material organisation of kinship and housework, and a fallible first answer whose anthropology later scholarship has superseded. A tradition whose founding text requires correction is in the normal condition of traditions, and Marxist feminism's later positions are best read as successive corrections conducted from inside.

### What happened to the anthropology beneath the founding text?

It was superseded. Fieldwork replaced Morgan's stage scheme with a record of kinship systems too various for any single ladder, and descent through women turned out not to mean government by women. The text survives in the tradition as a source of questions and method, not as a reliable prehistory.

One further point about the founding text matters for the fairness of what follows. Engels wrote the book as an executor, fulfilling, as he put it, a bequest: Marx had filled notebooks with excerpts from Morgan intending a treatment he did not live to write. The tradition's founding text is thus a posthumous construction, assembled by the surviving partner from the dead partner's reading, and it carries the marks of that assembly, confident where its sources were thin. The academic phase sometimes treated the book's weaknesses as a scandal to be confessed or concealed. The movement phase had treated it more sensibly, as a weapon and a starting point; Bebel's bestseller, which predates it by five years, had already made the essential argument, that women's subordination is historical and bound to property, without depending on Morgan at all. The tradition never stood on one text alone, and assessing it by the fate of Morgan's stages alone repeats, in reverse, the error of assessing it by its best arguments alone.

## The three positions

The tradition's internal division is the fact about it that accessible coverage most reliably flattens, so this section proceeds slowly. Three positions, each with canonical texts, named theorists, and a politics, divide Marxist feminism. They agree on the enemy: a feminism content with formal equality, and a Marxism content to postpone the woman question. They agree on the site: the household and the labour performed in it. They disagree about the system. The unitary position holds that one system, capitalism, organises both production and reproduction, and that women's oppression is explained by the place of reproductive labour within that single organisation. The dual systems position holds that two systems, capitalism and patriarchy, each with its own logic and history, interlock in women's lives, and that neither is reducible to the other. The autonomist position holds that reproductive labour is not merely necessary to capital but directly productive of value for it, and builds on that claim the demand for wages for housework. The grid below fixes the differences in a findable form; the prose that follows gives each position in its own terms, as the run rules of this series require, before any of them is criticised.

The three positions grid:

The unitary position received its classic statement in Lise Vogel's Marxism and the Oppression of Women, published in 1983, and its developed form is social reproduction theory, whose full treatment belongs to the article on [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/). Vogel's construction begins from a refusal: she declines both the dual systems multiplication of systems and the older Marxist habit of treating women's oppression as a feudal leftover destined to wither as capitalism matures. Her question is narrower and harder. What, in the organisation of a capitalist society, continually reproduces women's subordination, generation after generation, even as its legal forms change? Her answer locates the mechanism in the reproduction of labour power. Capital requires workers renewed daily and replaced generationally; that renewal happens largely outside the wage relation, in households, performed mostly by women; and the assignment of the work to women, together with the dependence it creates, is the material root from which women's oppression in capitalist society continually regrows. Male domination, on this account, is older than capitalism, a point Vogel concedes at the outset and the objection section below presses hard; what capitalism did was to reorganise an inherited subordination around the separation of production from reproduction, the workplace from the home, and to give it a function inside its own reproduction.

### What does the unitary position claim reproductive labour produces?

Labour power itself. On Vogel's account the cooking, cleaning, care, and childbearing performed in households produce and renew the commodity the worker sells, which is why capital has a standing interest in how that work is organised and why its assignment to women is a feature of the system, not an accident of custom.

The dual systems position was the dominant form of Marxist and socialist feminism in the late nineteen seventies and nineteen eighties, and its classic statements are Heidi Hartmann's essays of 1976 and 1979 and the collection Capitalist Patriarchy and the Case for Socialist Feminism, edited by Zillah Eisenstein in 1979, from which the compound term capitalist patriarchy entered the language. Hartmann's argument, stated here in brief because the objection section gives it at full strength, is that Marxist analysis is sex-blind: its categories, capital, labour, reserve army, describe a world of ungendered agents, and explain why capital benefits from women's cheap labour without explaining why it is women who are subordinated to men, in the household and outside it, before capitalism and after its abolition where that has been attempted. Her remedy is a second system. Patriarchy names a set of relations among men, materially based in men's control over women's labour, which predates capitalism, accommodates itself to it, and would survive its abolition unless fought on its own terrain by an autonomous feminist movement. Socialist feminism, in the vocabulary that settled from this work, is the politics of the hyphen: two systems, two movements, one alliance.

### What does the dual systems position claim that the unitary position denies?

That patriarchy is a system in its own right, with its own material base in men's collective control of women's labour, irreducible to the needs of capital. The unitary position denies the second system: for Vogel, one system, differently organised across history, is enough, and multiplying systems explains the interaction of nothing.

The autonomist position came out of Italy and out of operaismo, the workerist current that read Marx from the standpoint of the working class's refusals, and its founding text is Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James's The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community, published in 1972. Its move was to refuse the boundary the other positions patrol. Where the unitary account calls housework the production of labour power and the dual systems account calls it patriarchy's tax, the autonomists called it what it materially is from capital's side of the ledger: work that produces the most essential commodity in the whole system, performed without a wage precisely so that the wage can appear to be the price of one person's labour while purchasing the product of two. From that analysis followed the demand that scandalised the rest of feminism, wages for housework, organised internationally from 1972 in the campaign associated with Selma James, and given philosophical depth by Silvia Federici, whose pamphlet Wages Against Housework appeared in 1975 and whose later work on the witch hunts gave the position a history of primitive accumulation written on women's bodies. The demand was widely misunderstood as a proposal to price domesticity. Its authors intended a lever: to name housework as work for capital, to demand its wage, was to make visible the exploitation the wage form conceals and to organise refusal from the kitchen as from the factory floor. The position's treatment in this series continues in the article on [federici wages for housework](/2016/10/15/federici-wages-for-housework/).

### Why did the wages for housework demand scandalise other feminists?

Because it appeared to baptise the very confinement feminists were escaping: a wage for staying in the kitchen. Its authors meant the opposite, a demand whose function was exposure and refusal, but liberal feminists heard a counsel of domesticity and many Marxist feminists heard a theoretical error, the confusion of producing labour power with producing value.

Between the three positions, the agreements are as instructive as the quarrels. All three treat the wage as a form that conceals as much as it pays. All three refuse the idea that the household is a private haven outside social analysis. All three inherit Engels's premise that women's subordination is historical, made by arrangements and therefore unmakeable. And all three were formed in argument with the same provocation, the women's liberation movement's discovery, around 1969 and 1970, that the revolutionary left of that moment reproduced in its own organisations the subordination it promised to abolish in society: women typing the leaflets, making the tea, and being talked over in the meetings of movements dedicated to universal emancipation. Margaret Benston's essay of 1969, The Political Economy of Women's Liberation, and Juliet Mitchell's Women: The Longest Revolution of 1966, stand at the head of the academic phase precisely because they posed, before the positions crystallised, the question the positions answer: what is the material basis of women's oppression in a society of formally equal citizens? The debate that then consumed a decade, over whether housework produces value, surplus value, or only use values, is the technical form of that question, and it belongs to its own article on the [domestic labour debate](/2016/10/01/domestic-labour-debate/) rather than to this pillar.

## The strongest objection: the unhappy marriage

A series rule, observed throughout this guide, is that an objection must be stated at the strength its proponents gave it, attributed to them, before any answer is attempted. The objection to Marxist feminism's unitary ambition has a named author and a famous title. Heidi Hartmann, in The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism, published in the journal Capital and Class in 1979, argued that the marriage of Marxism and feminism has been like the marriage of husband and wife depicted in English common law: the two are one, and that one is Marxism. Marxist method, she granted, is indispensable for analysing capital; but its categories are sex-blind, developed to explain the relation between capital and labour, and when the analysis turns to women it can say why capital employs women cheaply, reserves them, and expels them, while remaining silent on the prior question of why women, rather than some other group, stand in a subordinate relation to men in every sphere, including spheres capital does not organise. An analysis that cannot say why the subordinated sex is female has not explained women's oppression. It has explained the labour market and assumed the rest.

The objection then adds its historical evidence, and Hartmann's formulation of it deserves to be given as the general dual systems case rather than as one essay's flourish. Male domination predates capitalism by millennia, on any account of prehistory including Engels's own: if the defeat Engels dated to the rise of private property in herding and farming societies is accepted, then patriarchy is older than every mode of production since, and a theory that explains it as a function of capitalist production has mistaken a recent host for the organism. Male domination also survived the abolition of capitalist property where that abolition was carried through: the states of the Soviet type industrialised, collectivised, drew women into employment at rates the capitalist world did not match, and still reproduced male monopoly of political power, the double burden, and, after the middle nineteen thirties, an official familialism that re-sacralised motherhood and restricted the freedoms the revolution had enacted. If the cause were capitalist property, the abolition of capitalist property should have removed the effect. The effect persisted, in societies whose rulers had every ideological reason to abolish it and decades in which to try. Therefore, the objection concludes, patriarchy is a system with its own material base, located in men's control over women's labour in the household and their exclusion of women from the resources and occupations that confer independence, and it must be analysed and fought as a system in its own right. Hartmann's positive proposal followed: feminism requires its own theory, historical materialism extended to sex, and its own movement, allied with socialism but not dissolved in it.

Christine Delphy, writing in France from a different starting point, gave the same objection a second form that unitary theorists have found at least as hard to answer. In The Main Enemy and the essays around it, Delphy argued that marriage is a labour relation: the husband appropriates the wife's labour within a patriarchal mode of production that coexists with the capitalist mode and is not a shadow of it. Her evidence was chosen to embarrass the production-centred account, since the exploitation of wives occurs in households of every class, and a bourgeois wife, who produces no labour power for any capitalist employer, is exploited in the same institutional form as a worker's wife. If the mechanism of women's oppression were the reproduction of labour power for capital, the oppression should vary with the household's relation to capital; Delphy's point is that the marriage relation holds its shape across the class structure, which suggests a mechanism seated in sex and kinship themselves. Between Hartmann's history and Delphy's sociology, the dual systems objection occupies strong ground: chronological, comparative, and institutional at once.

### What is the strongest evidence for the dual systems objection?

The persistence of male domination in the societies that abolished capitalist property. Hartmann's argument from the record is that a function of capitalism should not survive capitalism's abolition intact, administered by states committed to women's emancipation; that it did survive is evidence the unitary account must explain, not wave away.

Now the answer, given as the unitary position gives it, with its concession kept at full size. The objection lands, the unitary theorists reply, against a theory that the serious unitary writers do not hold: the claim that capitalism created male domination out of nothing, or that abolishing private property in the means of production mechanically abolishes the subordination of women. Vogel's construction, and the social reproduction theory developed from it, claims something more precise. Male domination is inherited by capitalism from the class societies before it; capitalism's specific work was to reorganise that inheritance around a new and fateful separation, the removal of production from the household, which split life into a waged sphere coded male and an unwaged sphere of reproduction coded female, and bound women's subordination to the renewal of labour power in the privatised home. On that account, the persistence of male domination after the abolition of capitalist property in the means of production is grim but not inexplicable: the Soviet-type states abolished one property form while retaining, and in the nineteen thirties deliberately restoring, the privatised household, the separation of production from reproduction, and the assignment of the second to women. The mechanism the unitary theory names was left standing, so the effect it predicts also stood. The answer also returns a question to the dual systems side: a patriarchy that is one system across slave, feudal, capitalist, and Soviet societies, changing its form in each while remaining itself, risks becoming a name for the fact to be explained rather than an explanation of it, and the interaction between the two systems, on which everything concrete depends, is exactly what the dual systems literature describes least mechanically.

The concession, which this guide does not soften: the objection's strongest evidence is the historical record itself, and the unitary answer to it is a reinterpretation, not a refutation. It is true, and the unitary position must carry the weight of it, that male domination persisted in states that had abolished capitalist property, ruled for generations by parties professing women's emancipation. A reader deciding between the positions should notice what each side's best argument does. Hartmann's argument from the record shows that property abolition is not sufficient for women's emancipation, which refutes the crude version of the tradition and embarrasses its official custodians. It does not by itself show that a second system, rather than a surviving organisation of reproduction inside the first, does the explanatory work; that further step is where the argument between the positions genuinely lives, and it remains, after four decades, an argument. The grid above records the standoff honestly: the unitary position's main criticism is Hartmann's, and the dual systems position's main criticism is the rejoinder's.

## The record of the socialist states

Theory in this tradition was never only theory, and the guide now turns to the evidence the theorists themselves argued from: what happened to women's position in the states that claimed Marxist authority. The record has three chapters, and fairness requires all three at comparable length. The first is the early Soviet transformation, which remains, on its legal side, the most sweeping emancipation any government had enacted to that date. The second is the reversal that began in the middle nineteen thirties, which dismantled much of the first chapter's work while employment of women continued to expand. The third is the long postwar settlement across the socialist world, in which women worked outside the home at rates unmatched elsewhere, carried the household nearly as before, and almost never governed. The tradition's internal argument, between those who read the record as a betrayal of the theory and those who read it as the theory's refutation, is unintelligible without all three chapters in view.

The Bolsheviks came to power in a country where a married woman's legal personality was absorbed in her husband's, where divorce was an ecclesiastical privilege, where children born outside marriage had no claim on their fathers, and where the peasant household governed most women's lives through customary authority. Within three years the new state had replaced that order with the most advanced family legislation in the world. Decrees of late 1917 established civil marriage as the only legally recognised form and made divorce available at the request of either spouse. The Family Code of 1918 carried the logic through: marriage as a civil contract between equals, the abolition of the legal category of illegitimacy and of the disabilities attached to it, the end of adoption as a property transaction, and the removal of the church from the law of persons. In 1920 Soviet Russia became the first state in the world to legalise abortion on request, on the stated ground that prohibition drove the practice underground and killed the women it claimed to protect. No capitalist state matched that package for a generation; several did not match parts of it for half a century. Whatever verdict a reader reaches on the Soviet experiment as a whole, the legal transformation of 1917 to 1920 was real, it was driven by the tradition this guide describes, and Kollontai and her comrades in the women's department were among its authors.

### What did the early Soviet decrees change in law?

They made marriage a civil contract dissolvable at either spouse's request, abolished the legal distinction between children born in and out of marriage, removed the church from personal law, and, in 1920, made abortion legal on request, a world first. Women's formal legal personality, absorbed in the husband's under the old order, became their own.

Law was the quick part, and the Bolshevik women knew it, which is why the Zhenotdel's work mattered more than the decrees to the women it reached. A decree in Moscow did not teach a peasant woman in Tambov to read, staff a nursery in a textile town, or persuade a village soviet that a wife's complaint of beating was a public matter. The department's delegate meetings, which drew working and peasant women into elected gatherings that heard reports and passed demands upward, were an attempt to manufacture a constituency for the new law among the women it concerned, and by the middle nineteen twenties the meetings involved women in numbers no other institution of the state could claim among that population. The communal services were the programme's material edge: public dining rooms, laundries, and nurseries, built in the civil war's ruins and expanded unevenly thereafter, were meant to begin the transfer of housework from the private kitchen to social provision that Bebel's book had promised and Engels's had theorised. In Central Asia the department's successor campaigns went further and met the tradition's hardest test, confronting the veil, bride price, and child marriage in societies where the woman question was entangled with colonial domination and religious law; the unveiling campaign of 1927 and the violence that answered it, including murders of unveiled women, mark the point where emancipation from above met the limits that the dual systems theorists would later theorise from a different direction.

The February Revolution itself belongs in this chapter, because the movement phase made it. The revolution that destroyed the tsarist order began, on the day the socialist movement had set aside for women, with women textile workers in Petrograd coming out on strike for bread and against the war, against the advice of male organisers who counselled patience, and pulling the metalworkers and then the city after them. The detail is not decorative. The tradition's women had spent two decades building the observance, the networks, and the habit of public action that the day expressed; when the autocracy fell, it fell first to the constituency the socialist women's movement had organised. A reader who wants the pre-history correction in a single image can take that one: the most consequential revolution of the century opened with an action of the movement that standard histories of feminism omit.

## The reversal and the long settlement

The second chapter opens in 1930, when the women's department was abolished on the ground that the woman question stood resolved by the construction of socialism, its work folded into general party departments that promptly let it drop. Six years later the state reversed the legal core of the first chapter. The decree of 1936 prohibited abortion except on narrow grounds, made divorce progressively expensive and procedurally humiliating, and opened a campaign of official celebration of maternity and the stable family that the legislation of 1944 extended, with medals for mothers of many children and a refusal to recognise unregistered unions that left women in them without claims. The stated reasons were demographic and disciplinary; the theorists of the tradition, including the ageing Kollontai in diplomatic exile, were not consulted. What the reversal shows, and what this guide states without varnish, is that the emancipation of 1917 to 1920 had been granted by a state for reasons of state, and could be revised by the state when its reasons changed, because the independent women's movement that might have defended it had been absorbed into the apparatus that abolished its department. The movement phase had built its institutions inside the party; the party, become the state, dissolved them at will. That structural fact is the hinge between the record and the theory, and both sides of the tradition's internal argument fasten on it: dual systems theorists read it as proof that women's emancipation requires a movement no party controls, while the tradition's defenders read it as a lesson about bureaucracy rather than about the theory of reproduction.

### What was reversed in the nineteen thirties?

The direction of family policy. The women's department was abolished in 1930 as the question was declared resolved; abortion, legal since 1920, was prohibited again in 1936; divorce was made costly and shaming; and official culture turned to celebrating maternity and the enduring family, a settlement extended by the family legislation of the following decade.

The third chapter, the postwar settlement across the socialist states, resists both celebration and dismissal because its elements point in opposite directions at once. On one side: employment. The states of Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union drew women into waged work, including into technical and professional occupations, at rates and in proportions the capitalist world reached only decades later or not at all; women engineers, physicians, and judges were unremarkable facts of those societies when they remained novelties elsewhere, and the infrastructure of nurseries and kindergartens, canteens and maternity provision, however drab, embodied a public commitment to the socialisation of reproduction that no Western state of the period attempted. On the other side: the household. Time-use research conducted inside those societies themselves documented what their women already knew, that the second shift survived the first chapter and the reversal alike; the employed woman of the socialist states carried a double burden of full-time waged work and nearly undiminished housework, her husband's contribution small, the communal services that were to have abolished the burden never built at the promised scale. And above both sides: power. Women were present in legislatures in proportions Western parliaments did not match, in bodies that ratified decisions made elsewhere; in the party committees and councils where decisions were made, women were scarce to vanishing point, decade after decade, in every state of the bloc without exception.

The pattern was not confined to Europe. China's revolution carried its own version of the first chapter: the Marriage Law of 1950, the new state's first major statute, abolished arranged and mercenary marriage, concubinage, and the sale of women, and proclaimed free choice of partners and equal rights within marriage, a legal revolution in the lives of half a billion people, enforced through campaigns that met the same resistance, sometimes murderous, as the Central Asian campaigns a generation earlier. Cuba's Family Code of 1975 went where no other socialist state went, imposing on spouses a legal duty to share housework and childcare, an attempt to legislate the second shift out of existence that remains the record's most interesting exhibit: the law was real, its enforcement was widely evaded, and its existence proves at least that the tradition's own analysis of the household had reached, in one state, the statute book. Across all these cases the guide applies the protocol its brief imposes: legal changes and their direction are described, participation is characterised without invented precision, and the verdict is left where the evidence leaves it, double-edged. A tradition that promised to emancipate women through transforming reproduction delivered the transformation of their legal status and their employment, defaulted on the transformation of the household, and excluded them from command. Its theorists, who had predicted that partial emancipation would take exactly distorted forms, and its critics, who had predicted that male domination would outlast the property change, could each find their paragraph in that sentence, which is why the record settles nothing by itself and this guide does not pretend it does.

## The academic phase: the question posed again

When the argument resumed in the late nineteen sixties, it resumed in a new institution, the university, and under a new pressure, the women's liberation movement's confrontation with the New Left. The sequence is worth stating carefully because the positions of the previous section are unintelligible without it. Juliet Mitchell's essay Women: The Longest Revolution, published in New Left Review in 1966, is conventionally taken as the academic phase's founding document, and its scheme explains why. Mitchell proposed that women's condition be analysed through four structures: production, reproduction, sexuality, and the socialisation of children. The scheme's power was its refusal of a single key. A society might transform one structure while leaving the others standing: women might enter production en masse while reproduction remained privatised and sexuality unliberated, which Mitchell noted was approximately the Soviet outcome. The four structures gave the revived tradition a way to describe partial emancipation as a structural fact rather than a mystery, and the subsequent positions can be read as arguments about how the four structures connect: whether one of them, reproduction, is the hinge on which the others turn, as the unitary position holds; or whether the structures obey two logics at once, as dual systems theory insists.

Margaret Benston, a Canadian chemist writing in 1969, supplied the academic phase's other founding move, the redefinition of women's work as a material category. In The Political Economy of Women's Liberation she argued that women constitute a group defined by their relation to production of a particular kind: in a money economy, women perform the labour that produces use values in the home, goods and services consumed directly rather than exchanged, and this shared material condition, not a shared psychology or a shared culture, is the root of women's common subordination. The essay's significance for the tradition is its austerity. Benston did not appeal to male attitudes, to socialisation, or to the mysteries of femininity; she pointed at a measurable arrangement, who produces what in what form, and derived the politics from it: women would be emancipated when the work of the household was reorganised as social production, the Bebel programme restated in the vocabulary of political economy. Between Mitchell's four structures and Benston's material redefinition, the academic phase had its charter. The decade that followed, in which the domestic labour debate consumed the tradition's energies in an argument about whether housework produces surplus value that this guide deliberately leaves to its own article, was the attempt to give Benston's intuition a rigorous form inside Marx's categories, and the three positions are what survived when the attempt had run its course.

### What changed when the argument moved into the universities?

Its audience, its evidence, and its stakes. The movement phase had argued in newspapers for organisers who could act on the argument the following week; the academic phase argued in journals for readers who could cite it. Transmission became durable and narrow at once: the texts survived, indexed and taught, and the mass public that had bought Bebel did not return.

The academic phase also changed the tradition's geography. The movement phase had been centred in Germany and Russia, with its arguments carried by party organisation; the academic phase was Anglophone and Italian, carried by journals, women's studies programmes, and the publishing of small presses, and its characteristic product was not the newspaper or the congress resolution but the essay in a journal of the left and the edited collection. Something was gained in rigour what was lost in reach, and the tradition's writers knew it: the wages for housework campaign, alone among the academic phase's products, attempted to rebuild a movement practice out of a theoretical claim, organising around the demand in the streets and welfare offices rather than the seminar room, and its friction with the rest of the tradition was partly the friction of that attempt. The guide's assessment section returns to the balance sheet. Here it is enough to fix the point that the three positions are academic-phase constructions built from movement-phase materials: Engels and Bebel supplied the premises, Kollontai the questions about intimate life, the Soviet record the evidence, and the nineteen seventies the institutional setting in which the argument could be conducted as scholarship.

## Two mechanisms: segregation and the family wage

Abstract positions persuade; mechanisms convince. The academic phase's most durable empirical work concerned two mechanisms through which, on its analysis, women's subordination is continuously manufactured inside formally egalitarian labour markets, and a pillar guide should state them because they show the tradition doing something other than metaphysics. The first mechanism is job segregation by sex. Hartmann's essay of 1976 on capital, patriarchy, and job segregation argued that the labour market's division into men's jobs and women's jobs, with the second paid and graded below the first, is not a survival from pre-capitalist custom that competition should have dissolved, but an arrangement actively maintained because it serves two interests at once: employers gain a divided workforce and a pool of labour available at low wages, and male workers gain a monopoly of the better-paid occupations and a wife's unpaid services at home. Her historical evidence was the long campaign by organised male workers, across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, to exclude women from trades, restrict their hours, and reserve skilled work to men, campaigns conducted against the immediate interests of capital, which would often have preferred the cheaper labour. That evidence is the dual systems theory with its sleeves rolled up: here are the two systems, visible in a single archive, cooperating and conflicting in proportions the historian can weigh.

The second mechanism is the family wage, the demand, carried by male trade unionists through the nineteenth century and conceded in many industries, that a man's wage should suffice to maintain a wife and children at home. To the unions that won it, the family wage was a protection of working-class life against the factory's appetite for every member of the family. To the Marxist feminist analysis, it was also the hinge on which women's dependence turned: a wage structure that pays the man for the household makes the wife's unpaid labour the condition of the wage and her own earnings, where she works, a supplement by construction, priced accordingly. The tradition's writers divided over the demand as they divided over everything, the dual systems side stressing the male interest it served, the unitary side stressing capital's interest in reproducing labour power cheaply through the privatised household, the autonomists noting that the family wage is the point where the wage for one worker silently purchases the labour of two. But all three read the family wage as an institution, not a sentiment, and the reading is characteristic: where other feminisms saw an ideology of domesticity, this tradition saw a wage bargain with identifiable parties, interests, and effects, which could therefore be fought as bargains are fought.

### Why did male trade unionists defend the family wage?

On the tradition's analysis, for reasons that mixed protection and monopoly: it limited the factory's claim on wives and children, raised the price of the labour men sold, and reserved the better-paid trades to men by pricing women as supplementary earners. The bargain defended the working-class household and entrenched the wife's dependence inside it at the same time.

## Criticism from within: whose woman?

The most consequential criticism the academic phase received came not from its opponents but from women who shared much of its politics and found its subject too narrow. The Marxist feminism of the nineteen seventies, for all its talk of women as a group with a common material condition, had generalised from the situation of white, metropolitan, largely professional women, and Black feminists in the United States said so with precision. The Combahee River Collective, a Boston organisation of Black feminists many of whom identified as socialists, published its statement in 1977: it affirmed a commitment to struggling against racial, sexual, heterosexual, and class oppression as interlocking systems, and it coined, in that context, the term identity politics that later decades would use for other purposes. The statement's importance for this guide is double. It accepted the materialist premise, that oppression is organised and not merely attitudinal, while rejecting any hierarchy that made one system the base and the others decoration; and it observed, of the women's movement and the left alike, that a feminism which does not centre the women who face all the systems at once will free, at most, the women who face only one.

Angela Davis gave the criticism its historical depth in Women, Race and Class, published in 1981. Davis, a philosopher formed inside the Marxist tradition rather than outside it, rewrote the American record from the standpoint of enslaved women, for whom the household had never been a haven of unpaid labour set against the factory: enslaved women worked as field labourers under the lash, and the Black household under and after slavery was shaped by that violence and by women's waged work outside it from emancipation onward. Against any account in which women's oppression is located essentially in domestic labour for a husband, Davis set a record in which the salient facts were forced production, sexual violence as an instrument of racial domination, and a suffrage movement that had repeatedly traded Black women's enfranchisement for white women's advantage. The book is not an abandonment of the tradition but a demand that it generalise honestly, and the tradition's subsequent development, including the stream of work gathered under the heading of intersectionality, whose relation to class analysis has its own article in this series at [intersectionality and class](/2017/01/01/intersectionality-and-class/), is in part the working out of that demand. A pillar guide records the criticism at this length because it changed the tradition it criticised: after Davis and Combahee, the unitary position's labour power, the dual systems' patriarchy, and the autonomists' housewife all had to be specified by race and by history before they could be asserted of women in general, and the positions that survived the specification are stronger for it.

### What did the Combahee River Collective add to the argument?

A refusal, from inside socialist feminism, to rank the systems. Writing as Black feminists in 1977, the Collective held racial, sexual, and class oppression to be interlocking, each real in the lives of the women who faced them together, and made the women at the intersection the test of any theory that claimed to explain the whole.

## Kollontai on the family, love, and the new morality

Kollontai earns a section of her own in a pillar guide because she is the tradition's only major theorist of intimate life, and because the questions she posed in the early nineteen twenties remain the ones on which the tradition is most often caricatured. Her starting point was Engels's, carried further than Engels carried it. If the monogamous family is an economic institution, the form in which property is transmitted and labour power reproduced, then its emotional contents, jealousy, possessiveness, the treatment of a spouse as an owned thing, are not eternal features of love but the psychology of a property relation, and a society that socialises production while leaving the household's economics untouched will keep reproducing the old psychology in new flats. Her essays on the family argued that the working class was already, in practice, dissolving the old family's functions: the state and the collective were taking over child-rearing support, communal feeding, and the care of the old, and marriage was becoming a union of comrades that would last while it served both parties and end without catastrophe when it did not. Her fiction, the novella of Vasilisa Malygina and the stories of the collection usually Englished as Love of Worker Bees, worked the same seam: heroines torn between revolutionary work and lovers who demand the old exclusive possession, choosing, at cost, the work and the collective.

Her most debated text is the letter to working youth of 1923, Make Way for Winged Eros, which distinguished the wingless eros of mere sexual appetite and bourgeois possessiveness from a winged eros she imagined for a collectivist society: love as a many-sided comradely feeling, integrated with rather than set against a person's obligations to the collective, and free of the double standard that excused in men what it ruined in women. The essay is routinely misquoted as a manifesto for promiscuity, the notorious glass of water theory, according to which sexual need under communism would be satisfied as casually as thirst. Kollontai rejected that theory explicitly and repeatedly; the glass of water jibe was aimed at her by critics, and Lenin himself, in a conversation Clara Zetkin recorded in her reminiscences, attacked the theory as un-Marxist and unworthy of the revolution, without Kollontai holding it. The episode is a tidy specimen of how this tradition's record gets garbled in transmission, and it matters for the pillar's fairness rule: the theorist of sexual emancipation in the founding generation was a theorist of comradeship and against casualness, arguing that a society of equals would take love more seriously, not less, because it would no longer be entangled with property, maintenance, and escape from loneliness imposed by the isolated household.

### Why was Kollontai moved out of domestic politics?

Because she had become doubly inconvenient: a leading voice of the Workers' Opposition, which challenged the party's bureaucratisation from the left in 1921, and a theorist whose writings on the family and sexuality embarrassed a leadership turning toward respectability. Diplomacy solved both problems at once, posting her abilities abroad and her questions out of earshot.

The longer significance of Kollontai's intimate writings is that they exposed, from inside the founding generation, the limit the dual systems objection later formalised. Kollontai insisted that economic transformation would not automatically remake relations between the sexes, that new norms had to be argued for, modelled, and fought for against the old morality's tenacity in the minds of revolutionaries themselves. That is, in substance, the admission that the tradition's strongest internal critics would later press: the sexual and emotional order has a relative independence of the property order, and a movement that treats it as a reflex will be surprised by its survival. Where Hartmann concluded that a second system must be theorised, Kollontai concluded that a second struggle must be waged, inside the same movement and the same lifetime. The difference between those conclusions is the difference between the academic phase's theory and the movement phase's practice, and the pillar records it without pretending the difference has been resolved.

## The founders' own writings, and their limits

A pillar guide should be exact about how much of this tradition the founders actually wrote, because both devotees and detractors inflate the answer in opposite directions. Marx wrote no treatise on the woman question. What he left is a set of fragments, scattered across forty years, from which the tradition was built by other hands. In The German Ideology of the eighteen forties, the young Marx and Engels treated the family as the first social relation and remarked that wife and children are, within it, the husband's slaves, a germ of the later argument that the household contains a class relation in embryo. In Capital, the analysis of machinery describes capital drawing women and children into the factory and thereby, in Marx's ambivalent judgement, dissolving the old family economy while creating, amid the horror of the mills, the material premise of a higher form of the family, since collective labour by both sexes outside the home undermined the economic basis of male monopoly. His private judgements could be warmer than his theory: writing to Ludwig Kugelmann in 1868, he declared that great social changes are impossible without the feminine ferment, and that social progress can be measured by the social position of women. The fragments point consistently in one direction, toward women's subordination as a historical arrangement bound to property and production, and they stop well short of a theory. Engels's book of 1884, assembled from Marx's notebooks, was the attempt to supply the theory the fragments promised; Bebel's bestseller, five years earlier, had already supplied the programme.

The charge that classical Marxism neglected gender, that the founders subordinated the woman question to class and bequeathed the subordination to their movement, is a serious one with a serious literature, and it belongs to its own article in this series, on [did marxism neglect gender](/2016/12/15/did-marxism-neglect-gender/), where the indictment and the defence are set out at the length they require. The pillar's duty is only to mark what kind of evidence the charge and the defence trade in, so that the reader meets the later argument prepared. The indictment rests on the fragments' scarcity, on passages where the founders treated the family as a natural division of labour, and on the movement's documented habit of deferring women's demands. The defence rests on the direction of the fragments, on the fact that the tradition's founding programme was written in the founders' lifetimes and parties, and on the behaviour of the movement's women, who did not experience the theory as a gag: Zetkin and Kollontai wielded Marx's method against the movement's own male leadership, in the movement's own name. Both bodies of evidence are real, which is why the question has an article of its own.

One further fragment deserves rescue from footnote status because it shows the founders' household from inside. Marx's daughters, Eleanor above all, were not ornaments of the movement but workers in it, and the founder's household depended, as the theory would predict, on the unpaid labour of women: of Jenny Marx, who copied the manuscripts and managed the creditors, and of Helene Demuth, the family's housekeeper, whose labour sustained the production of Capital in the most literal sense the tradition affords. A guide to Marxist feminism is the right place to note that the theory of reproductive labour was first enacted, unexamined, in the theorist's own kitchen, since the observation was made by the tradition's feminist historians themselves and belongs to the self-critical thread this guide has followed throughout. The tradition's strength, at its best, has been its willingness to turn its method on its own house.

## The vocabulary: five terms and their loads

Reference guides owe readers a map of the contested vocabulary, since in this tradition the terms are themselves positions. Five terms carry most of the load. The first is patriarchy, a word the tradition uses in two senses that the three positions keep apart. In the dual systems sense, patriarchy is a system, a structure of relations among men with a material base, standing beside capitalism in the compound capitalist patriarchy. In the unitary usage, the word survives, when it survives at all, as an adjective for the inherited forms of male dominance that each mode of production reorganises, not as the name of a second system; Vogel used it sparingly for exactly that reason. A reader who knows which sense an author intends can predict the author's politics with fair accuracy, which is why the word's ambiguity is not carelessness but a map of the dispute itself.

The second term is domestic labour, the work of the household, cooking, cleaning, laundry, shopping, and the management of daily life, performed without a wage. The third is reproductive labour, broader than the second: it includes domestic labour and adds the bearing and rearing of children and the tending of the old and sick, everything that produces and maintains people rather than things. The fourth term, social reproduction, names not the work but the whole institutional field in which the work is organised: households, schools, hospitals, welfare systems, and the labour markets they feed, considered as one process by which a society reproduces its people and its labour power. The fifth pair is the double burden and the second shift, the first term the socialist world's own name, the second Arlie Hochschild's later coinage from American sociology, for the same documented arrangement: a full day of waged work followed by a second, unwaged working day at home, carried overwhelmingly by women in societies of every type. The terms came from different camps and decades; their convergence on a single measurable arrangement, established by time-use diaries rather than by polemic, is among the tradition's sturdier achievements, since the arrangement was named by its theorists before the statisticians of several governments confirmed it in their own tables.

A warning about one more term completes the map. The woman question, the phrase the movement phase used for its whole subject, sounds to later ears like a euphemism, and in the mouths of party leaderships it often functioned as one, a file to be deferred. In the mouths of the movement's women it named a fighting programme: the question of how women's subordination is produced and how it ends. The academic phase mostly replaced the phrase with the vocabulary above, gaining precision and losing the note of demand. Readers meeting the older phrase in Bebel, Zetkin, or Kollontai should hear it in its fighting sense, as the title of an unfinished argument rather than a bureaucratic category.

## The names: Marxist, socialist, materialist

No question in the tradition's mailbag is more persistent than the request to distinguish its labels, and the confusion is understandable, because the labels were weapons before they were categories and have been used loosely ever since. The settled usage this guide follows, which matches the usage of the major reference works and of the writers themselves when they were being careful, is this. Marxist feminism is the genus: the analysis of women's oppression through the joint arrangement of work and renewal, in any of the three positions. Socialist feminism, as the term hardened in the nineteen seventies, is a species: it names the dual systems position and the politics built on it, the claim that capitalism and patriarchy are two systems and that a feminism adequate to both must be simultaneously socialist and feminist, neither term dissolving into the other. The socialist feminists of that decade chose the label precisely to mark their distance from what they called Marxist feminism in the narrow sense, by which they meant the unitary confidence that a sufficiently deep analysis of capital would reach women's oppression without remainder. When a bookseller's category or a course title uses the two labels as synonyms, it erases the very dispute the labels were coined to conduct.

### How do the names Marxist and socialist feminism come to point at different theories?

Through the dispute of the nineteen seventies. Writers who concluded that a feminist theory needed its own account of patriarchy adopted the socialist feminist label to mark the two-systems commitment, leaving Marxist feminism, in careful usage, for the genus and, in polemic, for the unitary position they were arguing against. The same words do different work in different mouths.

Materialist feminism, the third label, is a neighbouring genus rather than a species. Associated with the French school gathered around the journal Questions féministes, founded in 1977 with Simone de Beauvoir's sponsorship and Christine Delphy as its driving theorist, it shares the Marxist feminist method, follow the material relations and distrust the appeal to nature, while rejecting the Marxist feminist conclusion about where the material relation sits. For Delphy and her collaborators, women are a class in a strict sense, exploited by men as a class through the marriage contract, within a patriarchal mode of production that is not capitalism's creature. The French school thus radicalised Hartmann's objection into a system of its own, and its writers were correspondingly sharp about the Marxist label, which they declined. Colette Guillaumin extended the school's method to race, arguing that the appropriation of women's bodies and labour, which she called sexage, had its analogue in the appropriation practised on racialised groups, an extension that fed, by a French route, into the same questions the Combahee statement posed from Boston. A reader who keeps the three labels apart, Marxist for the genus of production-and-reproduction analysis, socialist for the dual systems species, materialist for the French neighbour that locates exploitation in marriage itself, holds the map the tradition's participants drew, and can navigate their arguments without the static the loose usage generates.

## The argument about equality

The full comparison of the Marxist, radical, and liberal feminisms is conducted in its own article of this cluster, at [marxist radical liberal feminism](/2016/11/15/marxist-radical-liberal-feminism/), and a pillar guide must not poach it. But the pillar owes the reader the core of the disagreement with liberal feminism, since the definition section's claim, that women's oppression is not fundamentally a problem of legal exclusion, is the tradition's founding quarrel with its oldest rival and cannot be left as a bare assertion. The liberal tradition, running from Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication through John Stuart Mill's Subjection of Women to the suffrage movements, located women's subordination in law and custom that excluded them from the education, property, professions, and franchise men enjoyed, and it prescribed the remedy of equal admission. The remedy worked, on its own terms, across the century and a half that followed: the exclusions fell, one by one, in most of the world. The Marxist feminist rejoinder is not that the victories were worthless; the movement phase fought for the vote as fiercely as any liberal, and Zetkin's quarrel was with a restricted franchise, not with enfranchisement. The rejoinder is that the victories' incompleteness is diagnostic. Where formal equality arrived and substantive subordination persisted, in the wage, in the household's division of labour, in the occupancy of power, the residue is exactly what the liberal theory said should not exist, and exactly what the reproduction analysis predicts, since the law can admit a woman to the labour market on equal terms while the organisation of reproduction loads her with a second job the market prices against her.

The disagreement with radical feminism is the mirror image, and the pillar states it in the form the tradition's writers used. Radical feminism, in the work of writers such as Kate Millett and Shulamith Firestone, named patriarchy as the primary system and male domination as the model of all domination; Firestone's book of 1970, The Dialectic of Sex, attempted the boldest synthesis of the period, rewriting historical materialism with sex class in place of economic class and locating women's oppression in the biology of reproduction itself, to be overcome by technology's liberation of women from childbearing. The Marxist feminist answer, given by critics across its three positions, was that the radical account named the fact of male domination with unmatched vividness and explained its reproduction hardly at all: a system asserted across all history, rooted in biology or in male psychology, cannot explain why women's position varies so dramatically across societies and centuries, nor why it changes when the organisation of production and reproduction changes. Hartmann's dual systems work is best read as the attempt to keep the radical insight, male domination is real and not a reflex, inside a materialist frame that could still explain its variations, which is one more reason the objection section of this guide treated her argument at full strength. The three feminisms thus form a triangle of mutual criticism that the comparison article maps in full: the liberal is charged with formalism, the radical with ahistoricism, and the Marxist, by both, with the economism the dual systems debate argues about from inside.

## The material programme: houses without kitchens

Every tradition in this series is entitled to be judged partly by what it proposed to build, and Marxist feminism's constructive programme is the most concrete in the whole of feminist thought: the socialisation of housework, drawn up at various times as communal kitchens, collective laundries, public nurseries, and, at its most architectural, the kitchenless house. The programme's ancestry is wider than Marxism itself. In the eighteen sixties the American writer Melusina Fay Peirce organised cooperative housekeeping in Cambridge, Massachusetts, proposing that neighbourhood women run cooking, laundry, and sewing as paid cooperative services, freeing each household from duplicating the same labour in isolation; the experiment failed commercially and survives as the first draft of an idea. The historian Dolores Hayden, in The Grand Domestic Revolution of 1981, recovered the whole current she called material feminism: generations of campaigners, many of them socialists, who demanded the redesign of domestic space itself, apartments without private kitchens served by central dining halls, cooperative nurseries in the block, laundries as utilities. Hayden's recovery matters to this guide because it dissolves a lazy assumption, that the socialisation programme was a Soviet eccentricity. It was an international programme of the socialist and feminist movements for a century, argued in Boston and Stockholm as well as Moscow, and the Soviet communal experiments were its largest test rather than its invention.

The tests, where they came, were instructive in both directions. The Soviet communal houses of the nineteen twenties, of which the Narkomfin building in Moscow became the famous example, were designed as transitional machines for living: small private sleeping cells, collective dining, laundry, gymnasium, and nursery in the shared block, the architecture intended to make the old household economy impossible and the new one habitual. Sweden's collective houses of the nineteen thirties, promoted by Alva Myrdal and realised in Stockholm with central kitchens delivering meals by dumbwaiter to private flats, tested the programme in a social democratic rather than revolutionary key, and drew the same discovering: residents, offered liberation from the kitchen, divided between gratitude and a stubborn preference for cooking their own food in their own rooms. The kibbutz movement carried the programme furthest in daily practice, with communal dining halls, collective laundries, and children's houses in which the young slept apart from their parents; and the kibbutz record, studied by sympathetic and critical observers alike, supplies the programme's most honest footnote, since in many communities the women members pressed, over the years, for the return of children to family sleeping quarters and for a larger family kitchen, and the collective forms retreated under pressure from the people they were meant to emancipate.

A fair pillar guide draws the lesson the tradition's own best writers drew, which is not that the programme was fraudulent. The services half of the programme, public childcare, school meals, laundries, canteens, elder care, was everywhere it was tried the difference between women's employment as a double sentence and women's employment as a liberation, and the states that built those services at scale changed their women's days more than any statute did. The architectural half, the abolition of the private kitchen and the family dwelling, mistook a preference for a mere habit: the household turned out to be not only a site of labour but a site of chosen intimacy, and a programme that treated every attachment to it as false consciousness repeated, in bricks, the error the dual systems theorists identified in theory, the error of explaining a stubborn human arrangement entirely by its function for a system. The mature tradition absorbed the correction. Social reproduction theory in its developed form argues for the socialisation of the work, generously and publicly provided, and is silent, as its predecessors were not, about the abolition of the home. Kollontai, who had watched the communal kitchens of the civil war serve cabbage soup to a starving city, might have recognised the distinction: the point was never the kitchen. The point was who was condemned to stand in it, alone, unpaid, and unrelieved.

## The childcare test: war, mobilisation, and the peace

The tradition's method, follow the reproductive labour and the pattern explains itself, can be watched working on the evidence of the two world wars, which between them ran the experiment the theorists would have designed. In both wars the belligerent states, desperate for labour, drew women into munitions, engineering, transport, and agriculture on a scale peacetime ideology had declared impossible or improper, and discovered each time that the obstacle had never been women's capacity. The obstacle was childcare. A mother of young children cannot staff a shell lathe on a twelve-hour shift unless someone minds the children, and the wartime states, Britain and the United States prominently among them, therefore built in haste the thing their peacetime politics had refused: public nurseries, workplace crèches, communal restaurants, extended school meals, the socialisation of reproduction undertaken not from feminist conviction but from military necessity. In the United States, childcare centres funded under the Lanham Act served the children of war workers across the country, the nearest that republic has come to a universal public childcare system. The experiment's second half is the more instructive. With the peace, the same states that had organised the nurseries closed them, in the American case almost completely and against the protests of the mothers using them, while official culture rediscovered the sanctity of the full-time mother and the returning soldier's claim to the job and the wage. The women who had made the shells were reclassified as temporary labour and ushered home.

The tradition reads that sequence as a controlled demonstration of its central claim, and the reading is strong. Nothing about women changed between 1943 and 1946; everything about the organisation of reproduction changed, twice, in opposite directions, by state decision, in response to the state's need for labour in the factory or for the restoration of the pre-war household. A theory that locates women's position in nature, in psychology, or in an immemorial patriarchy unaided by institutions has to treat the speed of those reversals as a puzzle. A theory that locates it in how production and reproduction are arranged treats the sequence as the expected behaviour of the system, which expands and contracts the socialisation of care as the demand for waged labour expands and contracts, and prices the whole arrangement to the women who staff both shifts. The same reading organises the tradition's account of the later twentieth century's great labour market transformation, the movement of married women into permanent employment across the capitalist world, which proceeded not as ideology liberalised but as the service economy's demand for labour met the falling real value of the single male wage, and which left the second shift standing where the wartime nurseries had briefly lifted it. Where the childcare went, on this analysis, is the single most informative question a citizen can ask about any society's settlement between the sexes, more informative than its statutes on equality, because the statutes are cheap and the childcare is not.

### What does the wartime nursery episode prove, on the tradition's reading?

That the barrier to women's employment was never capacity or nature but the organisation of care. States that needed women's labour built public childcare in months and dismantled it when the emergency passed, showing the arrangement to be a policy variable, set by the demand for labour and the politics of the household, not a fate.

## Women's waged work: cheap, reserved, and segregated

The pillar has so far approached women's subordination from the household outward, as the tradition itself does, but the tradition also carries an account of women's waged work that deserves its own statement, since the two accounts interlock. Industrial capitalism was built, in its first great sector, on women's and children's labour: the cotton mills of Lancashire and New England staffed their spinning and weaving sheds predominantly with women and the young, at wages set by the assumption that a woman's earnings supplemented a man's. The assumption survived the industry. As clerical work expanded with the corporation and the state, it feminised, and as it feminised its pay and grading sank relative to the male crafts it had once neighboured, a sequence the tradition reads as the labour market pricing the worker's sex rather than the work's content: occupations do not become low-paid because women enter them, the strong version of the claim runs, so much as women are channelled into occupations whose pay the presence of a dependent workforce then ratifies. The mechanism section above, on segregation and the family wage, describes the same machine from the union side; from the employer's side, the machine's product is a workforce divided against itself, its cheaper half available to discipline the dearer.

Marx's category of the reserve army of labour, the pool of the unemployed and partially employed whose existence presses on wages, gave the academic phase its third account of women's waged position. On that account, married women constituted a latent reserve, held in the household between the economy's expansions, drawn in when labour ran short, in wars and booms and the service economy's long growth, and expelled or marginalised when it slackened, their attachment to employment treated by employers and statisticians alike as secondary because the household stood behind them as an alternative support. The account explains features of women's employment that a pure discrimination model handles awkwardly: its cyclicality, its concentration in sectors with flat hierarchies and interrupted careers priced into the wage, and the statistical habit, abandoned only slowly, of counting the housewife as economically inactive while counting the labour that reproduced the entire workforce as no labour at all. Critics inside the tradition have pressed the reserve account where it is weakest, noting that women's employment in the later twentieth century became permanent rather than cyclical, a structural feature of the service economy and not a reserve at all, and the position's defenders have answered that permanence changed the reserve's form rather than abolishing it, into part-time, temporary, and agency work disproportionately staffed by women and priced as supplementary. The argument continues inside the journals; the pillar records its existence and its terms, since a reader who can state it can follow most of what the tradition's labour economists publish.

## The welfare state and the breadwinner settlement

The tradition's analysis extends to the institution that much of the twentieth century regarded as the alternative to both market and revolution, the welfare state, and its reading of that institution is among its most influential exports. The classic postwar welfare settlements were built on an assumption their architects stated openly: the male breadwinner, in continuous full-time employment, supporting a dependent wife whose unpaid labour maintained him and the children, with social insurance designed around his contribution record and her claims derived through him. William Beveridge's plan for Britain made the assumption explicit, treating married women's security as a matter of their husbands' insurance and housewives as a class whose work the scheme honoured precisely by not insuring it. The Marxist feminist analysis of that settlement, developed by writers working in and around the tradition, pointed out what the architecture concealed. The welfare state had not abolished the privatised organisation of reproduction; it had underwritten it, paying for the household's work indirectly through the man's wage and benefits while keeping the work itself unpaid, uncounted, and assigned to women. Where the state did employ carers directly, in hospitals, schools, and nurseries, it staffed the caring professions overwhelmingly with women, at wages depressed by the same assumption that had priced the housewife's labour at nothing: that caring is what women do by nature, and nature is not a cost.

The Scandinavian settlements supplied the tradition with its contrasting case and its standing puzzle. The social democratic states of northern Europe socialised more of reproduction than any others in the capitalist world, public childcare of high quality, parental leave as a social right, elder care as a public service, and they did so partly under pressure from organised women inside their labour movements, a late flowering of exactly the politics the movement phase had practised. Women's employment there became the norm rather than the exception, and the double burden measurably lightened where the services were thickest. Yet the same societies reproduced, in softened form, the pattern the tradition predicts from the organisation that remained: women clustered in the public caring professions, part-time work feminised, the commanding heights of both state and capital staffed predominantly by men. The tradition's writers read Scandinavia neither as refutation nor as vindication but as the experiment that isolates the variables: socialise the services and the burden lightens; leave the labour market's hierarchies and the household's defaults standing, and subordination persists in the milder currency of segregation and the part-time track. A reader equipped with that reading can approach any welfare settlement, of whatever flag, with the tradition's one diagnostic question ready: who, in this arrangement, does the caring, and what are they paid?

## The correction pushed further: roots before the movement

The pre-history correction, pressed to its limit, reaches behind the organised movement to the early socialists who first joined the two halves of the tradition's claim: that women's subordination is a social arrangement, and that its mechanism is material. The earliest systematic statement in English is the Appeal of One Half the Human Race, published in 1825 by the Irish economist William Thompson, working in close collaboration with Anna Wheeler, a campaigner whose ideas run through the book and whose name its honest historians attach to it. The Appeal took the utilitarian standard, the greatest happiness, and applied it without the customary exception: a society that preached the calculus and practised the legal non-existence of wives stood convicted by its own arithmetic. Thompson, an Owenite socialist, located the mechanism in competition and property: men monopolised the means of independence and then called the resulting dependence nature. The book answered James Mill's dismissal of women's claim to the franchise and was read in the Owenite movement, where women lecturers carried socialist and feminist argument together through the industrial districts a generation before Marx published anything.

Flora Tristán, the French-Peruvian writer whose Workers' Union appeared in 1843, the year before she died of the exertions of a speaking tour through industrial France, joined the same two claims from the other side of the Channel. Tristán proposed a universal union of workers, men and women, as the instrument of emancipation, and she insisted, against the male socialists who would found her union, that the emancipation of women was not a garnish on the workers' cause but its foundation, since the most oppressed male worker still had, in his wife, someone over whom he exercised the despotism the system exercised over him. Her formulation anticipates, in a sentence, the argument Hartmann would formalise a hundred and thirty years later: domination reproduces itself inside the household of the oppressed, and a movement that ignores it will carry it into the new society unexamined. The Saint-Simonian women of the eighteen thirties, who published their own journal in Paris under the editorship of working women and preached the rehabilitation of the flesh and the couple-priest, belong to the same stratum, socialists before Marxism who treated the woman question as a first question. A pillar guide records this stratum for one reason: it corrects the last residue of the origin myth, the idea that the junction of socialism and feminism was a marriage of two finished doctrines in the nineteen seventies. The junction is as old as socialism. What Marxism added was not the wedding but the mechanism, the account of labour power and its reproduction that turned a moral junction into an analytical one.

## The suffrage fight and the strategy of the whole loaf

The movement phase's strategic signature can be read in a single controversy, its handling of the vote. Bourgeois feminist organisations across Europe campaigned, on the whole, for women's suffrage on the same terms as men's, which in most states meant a property or tax qualification that would have enfranchised a minority of propertied women and nobody else. The socialist women's movement, from the eighteen nineties and formally at its first international conference at Stuttgart in 1907, committed itself to the opposite line: universal, equal, direct suffrage for all adults, demanded whole, with no alliance that traded the general demand for a limited instalment. The Stuttgart resolution instructed socialist parties to fight for women's suffrage as part of the struggle for universal suffrage, and forbade the dilution of the demand in deference to liberal campaigns. The line was not purism for its own sake. A property-qualified women's vote would, on the movement's analysis, have added voters drawn from the possessing classes while leaving working women voteless beside their husbands, strengthening every parliament against the labour movement and splitting the women's cause along the class line the socialist women regarded as fundamental.

The strategy's costs and vindications both belong in the record. It cost the socialist women the alliance of wealthy and well-connected feminist organisations, and it exposed them, inside their own parties, to the standing accusation that the woman question divided the movement; Zetkin's career was a long negotiation of that accusation. Its vindication came in the settlements after the First World War, when the states that enfranchised women did so, in the main, universally rather than by property, in Germany and Russia as part of the revolutionary settlement itself, and the socialist women could point to two decades of agitation for exactly that outcome. Finland, the first European country to grant women both the vote and eligibility in 1906, elected the world's first women parliamentarians the following year, a striking share of them socialists formed in the labour movement's women's organisations. The episode matters to the pillar because it exhibits the tradition's political method at its most characteristic: an institutional demand, analysed in terms of which class of women it would empower, pursued through the movement's own autonomous women's organisation, against both the liberal rival and the impatience of its own side. Feminist historiography that begins in the nineteen seventies inherits the method's results without the argument that produced them, which is the pre-history correction in its political form.

## The unconscious: the tradition's psychoanalytic turn

One development of the academic phase sits awkwardly with everything this guide has described, and a complete pillar must include it, because it records the tradition discovering a gap in its own method. The method of following the labour explains the organisation of subordination; it does not, by itself, explain subordination's tenacity inside the minds of the subordinated and the subordinators, the desires and identifications through which each generation learns its place in the household before it can spell. Juliet Mitchell, whose 1966 essay had opened the academic phase with its four structures, addressed the gap in Psychoanalysis and Feminism, published in 1974, and the book landed among feminists as a provocation, since its subject was Freud, a figure the women's movement had filed among the patriarchs. Mitchell's argument was that Freud should be read not as prescribing women's subordination but as describing, with unmatched precision, the psychology of the patriarchal family that produces it: the Oedipus complex as the mechanism by which the child internalises the law of the father, femininity as a position constructed inside that drama rather than a biological datum. On that reading, psychoanalysis was not feminism's enemy but its missing volume, the account of how the structures Mitchell had listed in 1966, reproduction and sexuality and the socialisation of children, get inside the head.

The turn had consequences the pillar should register without exaggerating. It founded a durable sub-tradition, strongest in Britain and in film and literary studies, in which Marxist feminism and psychoanalysis were read together, the journal m/f being its characteristic organ, and in which the analysis of ideology shifted from the criticism of ideas to the construction of subjects. It also drew the tradition's materialist wing into a fresh internal argument, since writers of the dual systems and social reproduction camps objected that the psychoanalytic turn dissolved the measurable arrangements, the wages and the housework, into a drama of the psyche that no policy could reach and no evidence could test. Both judgements capture something. The turn to the unconscious answered a real explanatory failure, the failure to say why women defend arrangements that burden them and why men experience those arrangements as identity rather than interest. And the objection stands, that an explanation pitched at the level of psychic structure travels poorly back into the world of childcare budgets and grading disputes where the tradition began. The pillar leaves the argument where the tradition left it, open, and notes the pattern it completes: each of the tradition's internal divisions, over systems, over value, over the psyche, is a division over the same underlying question of where, exactly, the machinery of women's subordination is located, and what would count as having found it.

## The evidence of the diaries: time use

Reference guides in this series state the evidence standard a tradition's claims can meet, and Marxist feminism's central empirical claim, the double burden, happens to be one of the best-evidenced propositions in twentieth-century social science, because it can be measured with a stopwatch rather than inferred from a doctrine. The instrument is the time diary, in which samples of respondents record their activities across the day, and its landmark application was the multinational time-budget study directed by the Hungarian sociologist Alexander Szalai in the middle nineteen sixties, which collected comparable diaries across a dozen countries on both sides of the European divide. The study's findings, repeatedly confirmed by national time-use surveys since, were of the kind that reorganise a debate. Employed women in every country studied worked a total day, waged hours plus housework, longer than employed men's; the housework component fell on women in proportions that varied by country far less than the rhetoric of the systems differed; and the socialist countries, whose employed women were the most numerous, showed the pattern in its developed form, high waged hours laid over a housework burden that the promised socialisation had reduced only at the margins. The tradition did not commission the study and could not have dictated its results, which is what makes it valuable to cite: the diaries were kept by thousands of ordinary respondents, and they recorded, in the neutral medium of minutes, the second shift the theorists had named.

The diaries also disciplined the tradition's argument in a way polemic never had, by giving the dual systems objection and the unitary answer a shared measurement to fight over. Hartmann's side could point to the burden's persistence across property systems as the fact of the matter; the unitary side could point to the burden's variation with the thickness of public services, lighter where childcare and canteens were real, heavier where they were slogans, as evidence that the organisation of reproduction, and not an unchanging second system, set the dial. Both readings survive contact with the numbers because the numbers support each in part, and the pillar's assessment below takes the standoff as its text. What the diaries settled beyond argument is the proposition with which the academic phase began, Benston's proposition: that the labour performed in households is labour, measurable in hours, distributed by sex with a regularity no theory of free individual choice has ever explained, and large enough, in aggregate, to stand comparison with the waged economy it sustains. Economists of a later generation, working far from this tradition, would eventually build satellite accounts estimating the value of unpaid household production for national statistics. The tradition's historians are entitled to note that the measurement programme began as a feminist argument about what counts as work, and that the argument was won so completely it is now conducted by statistical offices.

## Equal pay and the grading machine

The tradition's mechanisms are easiest to see at the moment they jam, and the canonical jam, in the British record the academic phase grew up alongside, occurred at the Ford works at Dagenham in 1968, when the plant's women sewing machinists, who made the seat covers for the cars, struck against their grading. The women's complaint was not, in the first instance, about the headline rate. It was about classification: their skilled work had been graded as unskilled, in a lower category than the comparable work of men, so that the inequality arrived dressed as a technical judgement about the job rather than as a decision about the sex. The strike, small in numbers and enormous in consequence, is credited in the standard histories with forcing the question that produced Britain's Equal Pay Act of 1970. The tradition's writers seized on the episode because it displayed, in miniature, the whole machine this guide has described: segregation assigning women to a job family, grading ratifying the assignment as skill's neutral verdict, the family wage assumption pricing the woman's earnings as supplementary, and the women themselves, organised, identifying the classification as the point where the system could be fought. Equal pay for the same work, the demand the Act answered, left the deeper machinery standing, since men and women seldom did the same work inside a segregated plant; the successor demand, equal pay for work of equal value, which European law later adopted, is the grading machine's critics pursuing it into its last redoubt, the definition of skill itself. A tradition that teaches its readers to ask who graded the job, and by what standard the caring and sewing skills of women came out light, has given them a question that no subsequent labour market has made obsolete.

## Care across borders: the chain the tradition predicted

The tradition's framework found, in the decades after its academic crystallisation, a phenomenon it had not named but had unmistakably predicted: the international transfer of reproductive labour from poorer households to richer ones, organised as a chain. As women's employment in the wealthy economies became permanent and the public provision of care lagged behind it, the second shift was increasingly not abolished, redistributed to men, or socialised, but outsourced, to migrant women who crossed borders to clean, nurse, and mind children in other women's houses, while their own children were minded, in the countries they left, by grandmothers, by elder daughters, or by poorer women still, in a descending sequence the literature calls the global care chain. The analysis writes itself in the tradition's vocabulary, which is the point. The chain is the privatised organisation of reproduction solving its contradiction in the wealthy household by displacing it onto a poorer one, the cost carried, as the theory would lead one to expect, by the women at the chain's far end, whose own reproductive labour is performed by substitutes or left undone across a distance maintained by remittances and telephone calls. Sociologists of migration documented the chain's human texture, the professional woman in Manila or Colombo raising an employer's child in Rome or Los Angeles while her own children grew up in her mother's house; the tradition supplied the frame that makes the texture a structure rather than a series of private sorrows.

The episode carries a methodological moral the pillar should draw, because it bears on the assessment that follows. A theory earns its keep by the phenomena it renders intelligible before they are named. The reproduction framework, built to explain the housewife of the industrial city, turned out to explain the Filipina nurse's aide and the Ukrainian carer in an Italian apartment with no adjustment to its machinery: follow the care work, ask who performs it, on what terms, and whose labour power its performance renews, and the global pattern assembles itself. Critics of the tradition reply, fairly, that intelligibility is not the same as remedy, and that the chain's existence embarrassed the tradition's politics as much as it vindicated its analysis, since the beneficiaries of the outsourced second shift include precisely the professional women whose emancipation the movement existed to win. The Combahee and Davis criticisms return here in global form: a feminism whose victories are staffed by other women's subordination has solved the problem for a class, not for a sex. The tradition's serious writers have not dodged that charge, and the developed form of social reproduction theory treats the chain, and the racialised hierarchies it runs along, as central cases rather than embarrassments. The pillar records the development as the tradition's pattern at its best: a framework strong enough to be turned, by its own inheritors, on the arrangements its earlier victories produced.

## Why a technical debate carried the politics

Readers of the academic phase's history sometimes ask why the tradition spent a decade on a question as apparently scholastic as whether housework produces surplus value, and the pillar, which has referred the debate itself to its own article, owes an answer about the stakes rather than the arguments. The question was technical in form and constitutional in effect, because each answer reorganised the politics. If housework produces surplus value, as the autonomists held, then the housewife stands inside the central relation of capitalism, exploited by capital directly, and her struggle is a front of the class struggle with the same standing as the factory's, a conclusion with immediate consequences for how movements should organise and whom they should address. If housework produces labour power but not value, the unitary answer in its careful form, then the housewife's exploitation is real but routed through the wage of another, and her emancipation requires the reorganisation of reproduction as a social system rather than a wage demand addressed to an employer she does not have. If housework stands in a separate patriarchal economy, the dual systems answer, then the housewife's antagonist is in the first instance her husband, and feminism's autonomous organisation follows as surely as the other conclusions followed from theirs. Three answers to an examination question in value theory, three different movements. The debate's ferocity, which outsiders mistook for scholasticism, was the ferocity of people who understood that the classification decided the strategy, and the tradition's habit of conducting its politics in the vocabulary of categories, so exasperating to its neighbours, is the deposit of that decade. The reader who grasps why the technical question mattered holds the key to the tradition's style in every later dispute, including the ones this guide has staged: the categories are never only categories, because in a materialist tradition what a thing is decides what can be done about it.

## Demography: the state as manager of reproduction

No evidence tests the tradition's framework more uncomfortably, for every party, than the demographic policies of the twentieth-century states, because those policies treated reproduction, in the open, as a resource to be managed for national purposes, and they did so under every flag. The Soviet reversal of 1936 was demographic in motive, a state anxious about its birth rate purchasing maternity with medals and abortion's prohibition. Fascist Italy and Germany ran the same policy in a louder key, taxing bachelors, decorating prolific mothers, and preaching the cradle as a battlefield. Romania under Ceaușescu carried the logic to its terminus after 1966, prohibiting abortion and contraception together, policing women's pregnancies through the workplace, and reaping the consequences the tradition would have predicted from treating women as a demographic instrument: a generation of children warehoused in state orphanages whose conditions shamed the regime that had mandated their birth, and women dying of clandestine abortions in numbers the state concealed. China, from the other direction, administered the century's most ambitious anti-natalist programme, the one-child policy maintained for over three decades, in which the same premise, that reproduction is a legitimate object of state planning, justified the opposite target; its enforcement bore, as enforcement of such policies always bears, overwhelmingly on women's bodies, and its relaxation, when the state's demographic anxiety reversed direction toward ageing and shortage, bore the same signature as the Soviet turn of 1936, the woman question subordinated to the population the state calculates it needs.

The tradition's writers drew from this grim comparative record a conclusion that cuts against simple versions of their own side, and the pillar states it as they did. If states of capitalist, fascist, and socialist type alike manage reproduction as a resource, then the management cannot be explained by any one property system, a point the dual systems theorists file with Hartmann's argument from the record. What the unitary tradition answers, and the answer has force, is that the common element is not a trans-historical patriarchy but a trans-systemic fact: every state in the century confronted the reproduction of its population and its labour force as a problem of supply, and reached for the instrument nearest to hand, the regulation of women, because the organisation of reproduction in the privatised household made women the point of application. On that reading, demographic policy is the state admitting, in its brutal way, the truth of the tradition's founding claim: that reproduction is not a private sideshow but a central process of social life, which powers that ignore it in theory will manage it in practice, at women's expense. The feminist demand that runs through the tradition's best work, from Kollontai's insistence that maternity be honoured as a social function without women being conscripted into it, to the developed theory's defence of reproductive autonomy as a material right rather than a lifestyle preference, is the attempt to imagine a politics in which that central process is organised for the people who perform it. The demographic century is the record of the alternative.

## Development: the argument goes south

The tradition's framework was built from European and North American materials, and its encounter with the economics of development forced the generalisations this guide has already watched Davis and Combahee demand. The encounter's founding text came from outside the tradition: Ester Boserup's Women's Role in Economic Development, published in 1970, a Danish economist's demonstration, from field evidence across Asia and Africa, that development as practised was making women worse off, marginalising them from the cash economy as colonial and postcolonial agriculture commercialised men's crops, mechanised men's tasks, and trained men for the new techniques while women's subsistence production, the food the cities ate, was classified as backward and left to stagnate. Boserup's book founded the field that became women in development, and its evidence posed the tradition a double question. The marginalisation she documented was being wrought by capitalist development, which suited the tradition's account; but it was being wrought on women whose oppression plainly predated the development that worsened it, in kinship systems whose analysis required more than the reproduction of labour power for a wage economy most of them barely touched.

The tradition's response, across the following decades, was to extend its machinery rather than abandon it, and the extension is among its more honourable chapters. Its writers in the development field argued that the integration of women into production, the remedy the development agencies took from Boserup, reproduced in the plantation and the export factory the pattern the theory knew from Manchester: women drawn in as the cheapest labour, their wages priced by the assumption of a supporting household, their subsistence work at home continuing uncounted beneath the new employment. The export processing zones of the world economy, staffed predominantly by young women recruited from the countryside, became the literature's standing exhibit, and the comparison article on the global organisation of that labour belongs elsewhere in this series; the pillar's point is narrower. A framework that began with the Lancashire mill and the Petrograd textile strike proved capable of organising evidence from the Sri Lankan free trade zone and the Mexican maquiladora without changing its questions, which is what theories are for. Where it strained, its critics inside and outside the tradition said, was at the same joint as always: the kinship systems of the societies concerned, in which women's subordination was organised through lineage, bridewealth, and religion in forms that the vocabulary of labour power reaches awkwardly. The strain is recorded here, as the tradition's own journals recorded it, as an open problem rather than a settled defeat, and as one more instance of the pattern this guide has followed from the founding text onward: a method strong enough to travel, an inheritance of answers that must be rebuilt at every border.

## Assessment: what the tradition explains, and where it strains

A pillar guide ends its analytical work with a verdict, and the conventions of this series require the verdict to be earned by the steelmanning that precedes it, which the objection section and the record have attempted. The tradition's explanatory successes can be stated compactly, because they share a shape: each is a case where an arrangement that looked natural, psychological, or merely customary turned out, under the tradition's questioning, to be an organisation of labour with identifiable beneficiaries. The invisibility of housework was the first success and remains the largest: the proposition that the unpaid production and care performed in households is work, economically necessary and massively large, began as this tradition's polemic and is now the premise of statistical offices, development agencies, and an entire economics of care that seldom cites its source. The double burden was the second: named by the tradition's writers from inside the socialist states and confirmed by the time diaries of every system, it stands as the best-documented single fact about women's lives in the industrial world. The mechanisms were the third: segregation, the family wage, the grading of skill, and the reserve of labour are tools that labour historians and sociologists of every allegiance reach for, because they convert the large word oppression into processes a researcher can find in an archive or a wage book. And the record-reading was the fourth: the tradition taught its readers, against both Cold War propagandas, to judge emancipations by the organisation of daily life rather than by the statute book or the parade, a discipline that the wartime nursery episode and the demographic century reward every time they are examined.

The strains are equally statable, and the tradition's own critics have stated most of them in these pages already. The first is the strain of the second system: the unitary account's handling of male domination before capitalism and beyond it requires a machinery of inheritance and reorganisation that its critics find asserted more often than demonstrated, and the argument with Hartmann remains, after four decades, an argument. The second is the strain of the universal subject: the housewife of the classical texts, reproducing labour power in a privatised household for a waged husband, describes a specific historical arrangement, and the tradition's categories had to be rebuilt, under pressure from Black feminists and from the evidence of the developing world, for enslaved women, for colonised women, for the migrant carer whose household is divided by an ocean. The third is the strain of remedy: the tradition diagnoses the privatised organisation of reproduction with great power and has been markedly less successful at the politics of its transformation, its constructive programme having retreated from the communal house to the defensible ground of public services, and its movement, the mass organisation that once carried the argument, having no present equivalent inside or outside the universities. A verdict that respects the evidence holds the two lists together. As an explanation of how women's subordination is organised and reproduced, this tradition remains the most powerful yet constructed, the one whose questions the other feminisms have repeatedly had to answer. As a politics, it stands where its own pre-history left it: possessed of a programme whose services half has been vindicated everywhere it was tried, and still awaiting the organisation that can carry it.

One further assessment belongs to the pillar because it concerns the tradition's relation to its parent. Marxism gave this feminism its method and its founding problem, and received in return the most damaging internal criticism it has ever sustained, the demonstration, from its own premises, that its classical categories were sex-blind at the centre: that the labourer of Capital arrives at the factory gate already fed, clothed, rested, and replaced, by labour the theory did not count, performed by people the theory did not see. A parent tradition is not often handed so precise an account of its own blind spot by its own children. That the criticism was made from inside, with the parent's tools, by writers who mostly refused to leave, is the strongest evidence for the claim this guide made at the outset: that Marxist feminism is not Marxism with women added, nor feminism with class added, but a distinct tradition with its own founding problem, the reproduction of labour power, which neither parent could state and which, once stated, reorganised the questions of both.

## An earlier bridge: Reich and the sexual question

Mitchell's turn to Freud had a predecessor inside the Marxist movement itself, and the pillar's completeness requires his difficult name. Wilhelm Reich, an Austrian psychoanalyst and communist of the nineteen twenties and thirties, built in Vienna and Berlin a network of sexual-political clinics offering working-class patients, and above all working-class youth, contraception, abortion referral, and an analysis that joined their sexual misery to their housing, their wages, and the authoritarian family he took to be the factory of submissive character. Reich's wager was the inverse of the one his party made. Where the party deferred the questions of intimate life until after the revolution, Reich argued that the deferral was the counter-revolution's best ally, since a population trained in the patriarchal family to obey, to fear its own desires, and to police others would reproduce authority under any flag. His reward was expulsion from the communist movement and from the psychoanalytic association alike, and his later career descended into the orgone enthusiasms that destroyed his reputation; the tradition that cites Mitchell seldom cites him. Yet the pillar should record that the question Mitchell posed in 1974, how the structures of women's subordination get inside the subject, was posed inside Marxism forty years earlier, with clinics attached, and that the movement's answer then was the same institutional answer the Zhenotdel received: abolition, exile, and the question declared resolved by the parties it embarrassed. The sexual question is the woman question's most persistent form, and its history inside the tradition is a history of the tradition's own institutions declining to hear it, a pattern the guide has now documented at three separate points, in the party of 1907, the state of 1930, and the movement of 1932, and which a reader may weigh when the assessment speaks of politics.

## The day the reproduction stopped

The tradition's central claim can be put as a thought experiment: if the unpaid labour of households were withdrawn for a single day, the society that treats it as a natural background would discover, by evening, that it was an economy. In October 1975 the women of Iceland ran the experiment. On a Friday designated a women's day off, the great majority of the country's women withdrew both kinds of labour at once, the waged and the unwaged: offices and factories emptied of their women, schools and nurseries closed for want of staff, and in the houses the cooking, cleaning, and childminding stopped, leaving fathers to cope, in the famous reports, with children brought to offices, sausages to cook, and a sudden education in the volume of the invisible working day. The action was not this tradition's creature; it was built by a broad coalition of Icelandic women's organisations, and its demands were the liberal ones of equal pay and equal representation. But its form was the tradition's analysis enacted. By withdrawing reproductive labour together with waged labour, and by treating the two as one working day that could be struck as one, the Icelandic women demonstrated the premise on which every position in this guide rests, that the household's work and the economy's work are a single system of labour, divisible in analysis and inseparable in life.

The aftermath belongs in the pillar because it illustrates, in a small and legible society, the sequence the tradition's theory predicts for victories. The day off entered Icelandic political folklore as a founding moment; within five years Iceland had elected, by popular vote, the world's first woman president of a republic, and its women's representation in public life climbed over the following decades to levels the rest of the world cited as wonders. The tradition's writers, citing the case, drew the moral in both directions. The demonstration of the claim could hardly have been cleaner: a society shown its own reproductive machinery by the simple device of switching it off. And the demands the action won were the formal ones, representation and equal pay law, while the organisation of care in the Icelandic household, though softened by generous public provision, continued to distribute its hours by sex in the pattern the time diaries know. Iceland is the childcare test and the equality argument in one small country: the tradition's analysis vindicated as diagnosis, its remedy still pending as politics, and a national memory that honours the day the women stopped as the day the country saw itself whole.

## The record's German coda: two states, one question

Postwar Germany ran the record section's experiment in its purest form, dividing one people, one language, and one pre-war women's movement between the two systems, and the comparison was a staple of the tradition's literature for forty years. The eastern state built the settlement described in the third chapter in its most complete version: women's employment treated as the norm from school onward, childcare provision expanded toward a public guarantee, vocational training opened across the technical trades, and, in 1972, a step no other socialist state of the period took, the restoration of abortion on request in the first trimester, the position of 1920 recovered half a century later in the state that claimed the tradition of Bebel and Zetkin as its own ancestry. The western state built the breadwinner settlement of the welfare section in its most doctrinal version, a tax and insurance architecture organised around the single male wage, married women's employment officially regarded as a detour, and abortion law markedly more restrictive than the east's. East German women worked more, were trained more, and were cared for, in the statistical sense, more collectively; they also carried the double burden the time diaries recorded, shopped in the shortage economy's queues as a second unwaged occupation of its own, and governed nothing.

Reunification then performed the experiment's final, unplanned movement, merging the settlements by absorbing the eastern one into the western, and the woman question was among the merger's live wires. Eastern women faced the closure of the childcare infrastructure their employment presupposed, an abortion regime tightened toward the western standard after a constitutional struggle, and a labour market that reclassified their continuous working lives as an anomaly to be corrected toward the western pattern of interruption. The debates of that moment, in which eastern women defended arrangements they had criticised for decades against a settlement they experienced as a demotion, supply the record's most human footnote and the pillar's last word on the subject. Judged by the tradition's own standard, the organisation of daily life rather than the statute or the slogan, the eastern settlement had given its women real goods that its politics had not let them govern, and the western settlement offered political forms whose daily organisation priced their labour as supplementary. Neither half of the divided country had solved the problem the tradition named. The divided country had merely demonstrated, with experimental clarity no theorist could have commissioned, that the problem survives both of the century's great answers to it, which is the record section's conclusion and the reason the theoretical argument of this guide remains open.

## The recovery: how the academic phase found its ancestors

The pre-history correction was not a gift of the archives; it was made, deliberately, by the academic phase's historians, and the making deserves a place in the pillar since it is the tradition studying itself into existence. When Sheila Rowbotham published Hidden from History in 1973, subtitled a rediscovery of women in history from the seventeenth century to the present, the book's method was its argument: three hundred years of women's resistance, much of it inside the socialist and labour movements, recovered from pamphlets, union records, and the memoirs the official histories had not cited, demonstrating by accumulation that the feminist past had not been empty between the suffrage campaigns but full of exactly the junction this guide has described, women arguing, inside movements of the left, that the emancipation of labour and the emancipation of women were one argument or neither. Rowbotham, a historian formed in the New Left and the women's liberation movement together, embodied the junction she documented; her companion volume of the same year, Woman's Consciousness, Man's World, attempted the theory to match the history, an account of how women's subordination is lived as consciousness inside a world organised by and for men. Around her work grew the infrastructure of recovery: women's history as a field, the reprinting of the movement's texts, Zetkin and Kollontai returned to print in translation, Bebel's bestseller reissued for the classrooms of the movement that had forgotten it.

The recovery changed the tradition it recovered, in a way the pillar should mark as its final twist on the pre-history theme. The academic phase had begun, in 1966 and 1969, believing itself a beginning, an understandable error given the destruction of the transmission lines described earlier. The historians' work of the nineteen seventies converted that error into the tradition's self-knowledge: the second phase learned that it was a second phase, argued thereafter with the first phase's texts on the desk, and drew from the recovered record both its best evidence and its sharpest reproach, since the movement phase had achieved, with fewer theories, the organisation the academic phase lacked. A tradition that knows its own history argues differently with itself, and the mature form of Marxist feminism, in which Vogel's treatise of 1983 engages Engels and the Soviet record in a single construction, is the recovery's product. The guide's opening claim can now be read in its full sense. The pre-history correction is not a remark about dates. It is the tradition's own historiographical achievement, the recovery of a movement by its inheritors, and the standing demonstration that what a movement forgets about itself, it will have to re-derive at the price of decades.

## A neighbouring synthesis: the sex and gender system

One essay of the academic phase stands at the tradition's border and illuminates it from outside, and a complete guide should place it. Gayle Rubin's The Traffic in Women, published in 1975, set out to find the machinery by which societies turn female biology into subordinated womanhood, and it built its answer from the tradition's own materials used in a new combination: Marx's analysis of how a system reproduces itself, Lévi-Strauss's account of kinship as the exchange of women between groups of men, and Freud read, as Mitchell had read him, as the anatomist of the family's psychic law. Rubin's name for the machinery, the sex and gender system, the set of arrangements by which a society transforms biological sexuality into products of human activity, passed into the language of several disciplines, and her essay's candour about its sources makes it the academic phase's most instructive border crossing. She took from Marxism its method, the insistence that the arrangements are arrangements, historical and changeable, and she declined its answer, locating the system's motor in kinship and the psyche rather than in the reproduction of labour power, and remarking that a Marxist account of women's oppression that begins with capital arrives too late, since the traffic in women is older than the wage.

The tradition's response to Rubin rehearsed its permanent argument in miniature, which is why the pillar stations her here. The dual systems side welcomed the essay as a sophisticated ally: here was the second system, given an anthropologist's depth of field. The unitary side answered that kinship systems do not float above the material organisation of work and renewal but are its historical forms, and that an exchange of women explains the form of women's subordination in the societies Lévi-Strauss studied better than its persistence in societies where kinship no longer organises production. The argument was never settled, and its unsettlement is the tradition's map drawn once more: one system or two, the motor in the household's economics or in the kinship order that precedes and survives it. A reader who has followed this guide can now locate any new contribution to the theory of women's oppression on that map within a paragraph of its opening, which is the competence a pillar article exists to give.

## Five misreadings this guide corrects

The tradition is ill served by its summaries, and the errors recur with enough regularity to deserve a section of their own, since each is a place where a reader's map can be set right in a paragraph. The first and commonest misreading holds that Marxist feminism claims class matters more than gender, a league table of oppressions with class at the top. The tradition's actual claim is not a ranking but a mechanism: it says where the reproduction of subordination is organised, in the division of labour between production and reproduction, and the claim is about machinery, not about whose suffering counts most; its writers, from Kollontai arguing with her own party to Hartmann arguing with Marxism itself, spent their careers resisting the ranking when their own side attempted it. The second misreading dates the tradition from the nineteen seventies, and the whole of this guide's pre-history is its correction: a movement with theorists, institutions, a bestseller, and a legislative record flourished from the eighteen eighties, and the academic phase is a rediscovery, brilliant and partial, of an inheritance it had first to recover.

The third misreading treats Marxist feminism and socialist feminism as interchangeable labels, and the names section has given the correction: in careful usage the second names the dual systems species of the first, and the conflation erases the dispute that defines the field. The fourth misreading attributes to the tradition the belief that women's oppression dissolves automatically with the abolition of private property, the revolution as solvent. Some officials of some states asserted exactly that, in 1930, while abolishing the women's department; the tradition's theorists, from Kollontai's insistence that intimate life must be transformed deliberately to Vogel's account of the mechanisms that regrow subordination inside any property form that leaves reproduction privatised, denied it in print, and the record of the states is the refutation of the officials, not of the theorists. The fifth misreading is the premise this guide warned against at its threshold: that the tradition is one position. It is three, contradicting one another on the foundational question, and any summary that reports a single Marxist feminist view of housework, of patriarchy, or of the state has selected one position and suppressed the argument that constitutes the tradition's life. A reader who avoids the five errors holds the map correctly; the rest of the subject is detail, and the detail has its articles.

## The tradition's continuing work

The brief for this pillar requires the tradition's present activity to be described in structural terms, without naming living writers or organisations, and the discipline is a fair one, since the structures are the durable facts. The activity runs along three channels. The first is the university, where the developed unitary position, social reproduction theory, organises the largest share of the scholarly work: courses and reading groups built around the reproduction framework, journals of the Marxist and feminist left carrying the argument with its critics, and a steady traffic between the framework and neighbouring fields, geography, sociology, and the study of racial capitalism among them, as other disciplines discover that their objects, cities, migrations, welfare systems, cannot be described without an account of how life is made and remade outside the wage. The second channel is the labour movement's own education, where the tradition's materials circulate in the form the movement phase would recognise: reading groups on the founding texts, union women's committees arguing the childcare question in the vocabulary of the second shift, campaigns for public care provision that reproduce, whether or not they cite it, the services half of the old programme. The third channel is the archive itself, the continuing work of recovery: the movement phase's newspapers and pamphlets digitised and taught, the theorists' papers edited, the record of the socialist states re-examined as their archives opened, each addition re-arming the pre-history correction against the next forgetting.

The balance among the channels states the tradition's condition as plainly as its theory states its subject. The first channel is strong and growing more specialised; the second is thin beside the movement phase's mass organisation; the third is the bridge between them, the wager that a tradition which keeps its own record can be rejoined to a public when conditions turn. Whether that wager pays is a question about the future of more than this tradition, and the pillar, whose business is the record and the argument, leaves it open, noting only that the tradition has survived one complete destruction of its transmission lines and rebuilt itself from the printed residue, which is more than most traditions in this series have had to do.

## Where to start reading

The tradition's literature is large, scattered across a century and a half and several kinds of writing, and the reader who wants a route rather than a bibliography should take the route the guide itself has walked. Begin with Bebel, whose book survives its century because it was written for the workers' households that bought it: the argument entire, property, the family, the programme of socialised housework, in prose of a clarity the academic phase rarely matched. Read Engels's Origin of the Family beside it, for the theory Bebel presupposed, and read it with the guide's warning in hand, that its anthropology is a period piece and its questions are the inheritance. From the founding pair, go to Kollontai, whose essays on the family and whose fiction give the tradition its unmatched account of what emancipation was supposed to feel like from inside, and whose fate teaches the record's lesson in biographical form. Zetkin's speeches and the selections from her work supply the strategist's voice, the movement arguing with itself about the franchise and the party.

For the academic phase, the order of this guide is the order of reading. Mitchell's essay of 1966 opens the questions; Benston's of 1969 states the materialist charter in a few pages; Hartmann's unhappy marriage essay of 1979 gives the strongest objection in its author's own terms and should be read before any summary of it, including this one; Davis's Women, Race and Class corrects the field's subject from inside; and Vogel's treatise of 1983, the hardest book on this list and the most rewarding, assembles the unitary position whole. The wages for housework pamphlets, Dalla Costa and Federici above all, are short, fierce, and best read as the interventions they were. A reader who has walked that route holds the tradition's spine; the articles of this cluster supply the ribs, on the founding text, on social reproduction theory, on the domestic labour debate, on the comparison of the feminisms, and on the charge of neglect, each linked from the section of this guide where its question arose.

## An ancestry in arms: the women of the Commune

The movement phase claimed ancestors as movements do, and its favourite stood at the barricades of the Paris Commune of 1871, a decade before Bebel's book. The Commune's women organised through the Union of Women for the Defence of Paris and the Care of the Wounded, founded by Elisabeth Dmitrieff, a young Russian socialist who had come to Paris from the circles around Marx in London and reported to him on the revolution she was helping to make; the Union demanded women's right to work, equal pay, and the organisation of workshops, and its members staffed the ambulances, the canteens, and, in the final week, the barricades themselves. Louise Michel, schoolteacher, ambulance driver, and the Commune's most famous woman defendant, faced its military court with a defiance that made her a legend of the movement she later served as an anarchist rather than a Marxist, a distinction the socialist women's movement, claiming her memory, tended to blur. Marx, in The Civil War in France, praised the working women of Paris in the warmest terms he used for any actors in that tragedy. The pillar records the ancestry for the same reason the movement claimed it: the socialist women's movement understood itself, from its first conferences, as the heir of women who had fought for a social revolution and demanded, inside it, their own emancipation as a condition and not a consequence. Dmitrieff's Union, with its programme joining the right to work to equal pay in 1871, is the pre-history correction's earliest document, and the distance from its demands to the Stuttgart resolution of 1907 is shorter than the distance from Stuttgart to the seminar rooms where the tradition was later said to begin.

## The machine in the kitchen: technology's broken promise

One finding from outside the tradition's ranks has become part of its standard equipment, because it disposes of the most comfortable objection to the whole analysis, the objection that housework is a shrinking residue that technology will finish off without politics. The historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan, in More Work for Mother, published in 1983, examined the industrialisation of the American home across a century of appliances, running water, vacuum cleaners, washing machines, and prepared foods, and found the paradox her title announces: the hours of the full-time housewife's labour did not fall across the very decades in which her tools were transformed. What fell was the labour of others, the servants, laundresses, and daughters whose work the machines absorbed, and what rose were the standards, cleanliness demanded more frequently, meals expected more varied, laundry done at a tempo the washboard had made unthinkable, childcare reconceived as an intensive maternal profession. The housewife of the machine age worked the hours of her grandmother with better tools and higher expectations, alone in a privatised workplace whose product was consumed as fast as it was produced. The tradition absorbed Cowan's finding as the empirical seal on its oldest proposition, Bebel's and Engels's proposition that the problem of housework is not its technique but its organisation: labour performed privately, for love as the saying goes, without measure or limit, expands to the standard the society sets, and only a change in the organisation, the socialisation of the work or its redistribution, changes the hours. Every generation's new promise, the servant-keeping suburb, the appliance, the meal kit and the app that summons a cleaner, has arrived since as a fresh test of the proposition, and the time diaries continue to return the verdict Cowan documented: the machine in the kitchen serves the woman who can summon another woman's labour, and leaves the woman who cannot exactly where the theory found her.

## Standpoint: the method turned on knowledge itself

The academic phase's most ambitious methodological claim grew directly from this tradition's soil, and the pillar should register it because it changed how several disciplines argue. Nancy Hartsock, in work gathered in Money, Sex and Power of 1983, proposed the feminist standpoint, built by explicit analogy with Marx's account of the proletariat. Marx had held that the worker's position in production affords, potentially, a truer view of the society than the capitalist's, since the worker experiences the system's costs in his body and his day, while the master's view is organised by the need not to see them. Hartsock argued that women's assigned labour, the production of use values in the household, the work of bodies and care that the money economy treats as nature, affords an analogous standpoint: a view of the social order from the activity that sustains life itself, against the abstract, exchange-mediated view of the marketplace and the boardroom. The claim was not that women are automatically wiser; a standpoint, in the Marxist original and in Hartsock's version alike, is achieved through analysis and struggle, not conferred by birth. It was that the tradition's subject matter, reproductive labour, is an epistemological position as well as an economic one, a place from which the society's real dependencies are visible.

Standpoint theory travelled far beyond its source, into sociology, philosophy of science, and the study of race, where it was taken up, criticised, and rebuilt, often by writers with no allegiance to the tradition that minted it. Its career illustrates the pattern the assessment recorded: this tradition's exports are frequently more successful than its institutions. It also attracted the tradition's own internal cautions, since a standpoint claimed for women in general ran into the Combahee and Davis objection in epistemological form, the question of which women's labour, in which household, under which racial and colonial order, affords the view claimed. The developed versions of the theory answered by pluralising the standpoint and grounding it in specific labours rather than in sex as such, a repair conducted, characteristically, by returning to the method's source: Marx's proletariat was never a birthright either, but a position in a process, and the feminist standpoint survived its critics by remembering that it had been built as an analogy to a position, not as a compliment to a sex.

## Housewifisation: the argument at world scale

The development section of this guide noted the strain the tradition's categories felt at the world's borders; the German sociologist Maria Mies turned that strain into a system in Patriarchy and Accumulation on a World Scale, published in 1986, and her central coinage earns a paragraph in any complete account. Mies argued that the housewife, the woman defined as a dependent non-worker whose labour is love, was not a survival from pre-capitalist society but a product of capitalist accumulation itself, manufactured wherever the system expanded: the same process that proletarianised men in the colonies and the metropoles housewifised women, defining their subsistence and craft production out of the category of work and their persons into dependence on a male wage, because a workforce reproduced at no cost to capital, and a reserve of labour available at any price, required the housewife as its condition. Her evidence ranged from the lace makers of Narsapur in India, integrated into world markets as supposedly leisured housewives whose piece rates rested on the definition, to the witch hunts of early modern Europe, which Federici was independently reading as the violent founding of the same settlement. Mies's synthesis, which fed the ecofeminist current she helped to found, is the autonomist inheritance at world scale: the insistence that the household's definition is an act of power with a price attached, extended from the industrial city to the planet. Its critics, including Marxist feminists of the other positions, objected that the synthesis purchases its sweep by flattening the differences among the societies it traverses, the mirror image of the objection to Hartmann's trans-historical patriarchy. The pillar leaves the symmetry visible, since it is the tradition's signature: every attempt to state the whole of women's subordination in one mechanism has been answered, from inside the tradition, by the demand to specify the mechanism's local form, and the argument between the sweep and the specification is the tradition's engine, in 1986 as in 1884.

## Protection or exclusion: the movement argues with itself

No internal debate of the movement phase better displays its quality than the quarrel over protective legislation, the laws limiting women's hours, prohibiting their night work, and barring them from specified trades and processes. The case for protection wrote itself from the factory reports: women's bodies were being consumed by fourteen-hour days in the mills and by lead and phosphorus in the match and pottery trades, and the socialist women's movement, Zetkin prominently, defended special legal protection for women workers as a working-class demand, a limit on capital's appetite where the appetite fed hardest. The case against came from two directions at once, and the movement had to answer both. From the equality feminists came the charge that protection was a cage: every trade closed to women at night was a trade whose wages women could not earn, and the protected woman was being protected out of the better-paid work into the custody of the family wage. From inside the movement's own ranks came the harder observation, that male trade unionists supported protective laws with an enthusiasm that had less to do with women's lungs than with women's competition, and that a law could serve the factory inspector's conscience and the craftsman's monopoly in the same clause.

The debate was never settled by the movement phase, and its unsettlement is instructive because it is the equality argument of this guide in legislative form. If women's difference from men in the labour market is an artefact of the organisation of reproduction, then laws treating women as a class apart entrench the artefact; if the difference is a present material fact, of bodies loaded with the double burden and targeted by the worst processes, then identical treatment under formal equality abandons actual women to the market's tender mercies. The socialist women's conferences argued the question repeatedly and landed, characteristically, on a both-sided resolution: protection as a floor for all workers was the goal, sex-specific protection a weapon to be used where the alternative was the unregulated mill, and the whole question subordinate to the organisation that alone could enforce any law, the union of the women themselves. Later feminism inherited the dilemma entire, in the controversies over maternity provisions, night work directives, and the sameness-versus-difference jurisprudence of the following century, usually without knowing that the socialist women of 1900 had stated both horns, in conference, with the factory reports on the table. The pillar records the debate as the movement phase's certificate of seriousness: a tradition that could argue with itself, at that level, about the instruments of its own programme was not the caricature of class reductionism that its later summaries describe.

## The third international of women

The movement phase had a second international flowering after the war split its first one, and its brevity is part of the record. The Communist International, founded in 1919, built a women's apparatus on the Bolshevik model: an international women's secretariat in Moscow, a journal, The Communist Women's International, edited under Zetkin's direction, and conferences of communist women that attempted to coordinate the work across the new communist parties as the Stuttgart and Copenhagen conferences had coordinated it across the old socialist ones. The theses on the woman question adopted in those years stated the programme at its most uncompromising: no special women's demands separate from the revolutionary programme, and no revolutionary programme that did not carry the liberation of women as a condition, with the party obligated to build women's organisations, press, and campaigns as a permanent part of its structure rather than an electoral auxiliary. For a few years the apparatus functioned as the tradition's second centre, training women organisers from a dozen countries in Moscow and distributing the movement's literature across the language frontier the war had closed.

Its decline followed the pattern this guide has documented twice already, at the scale of the Russian party and at the scale of the German. As the Communist International subordinated its national sections to the turns of Soviet policy, the women's work was progressively reduced from a programme to an instrument, its conferences rarer, its secretariat absorbed, its journal extinguished by the end of the decade; the German party's women's organisation, the largest in the West, was directed increasingly from outside and dissolved with everything else in the catastrophe of 1933. Zetkin herself, by then the movement's living monument, spent her last years in Moscow as an honoured critic whose warnings about fascism her own side's leadership was refusing, a trajectory the next section closes. The pillar draws from the episode the structural moral its participants drew, and which the feminist movement of a later generation would adopt as an axiom without knowing its source: that women's organisation inside a larger movement survives only where it commands its own press, its own funds, and its own right of assembly, and that every one of those autonomies will be reclaimed by the centre the moment the centre finds them inconvenient, unless the women organised can defend them. The dual systems theory's political conclusion, the autonomous feminist movement in alliance rather than in absorption, is that moral stated as doctrine, learned by the tradition's women in the institutions of 1907, 1919, and 1930 before it was argued in the journals of 1979.

## The destruction, and a speech

The movement phase ends, as a matter of dates, in 1933, and its ending explains the forgetting that the pre-history correction exists to repair. The German socialist women's movement was the tradition's centre of gravity: its newspaper, its theorists, its mass membership, its archive. National Socialism destroyed it in a season, dissolving its organisations, scattering its cadres into exile, imprisonment, and the camps, and burning its literature in the famous fires. The Soviet centre that might have preserved the inheritance was simultaneously completing the reversal documented above, its women's work abolished, its theorist of the family in diplomatic exile, its official doctrine proclaiming the question resolved. Within a few years the tradition had lost, at the same moment, its western mass base and its eastern state sponsor, and what survived the double catastrophe survived as books: Bebel in the exile libraries, Kollontai in a Stockholm embassy, the conference reports in the archives the historians of 1973 would reopen. The guide's earlier statement, that the tradition's interruption was a loss of transmission lines rather than of arguments, rests on this double event, and a reader who wants the whole of the pre-history in one judgement can take it here: the academic phase did not fail to remember the movement phase. The movement phase was murdered, and its murderers and its false friends between them buried the obituary.

One scene concentrates the ending. In August 1932 the newly elected Reichstag convened in Berlin, with the National Socialists its largest party, and by the chamber's custom its oldest member presided over the opening. The oldest member was Clara Zetkin, seventy-five years old, ill, nearly blind, returned from Moscow for the purpose. She used the chair's address, which custom expected to be ceremonial, to deliver a call for a united front of the working class against fascism, naming the danger in terms the subsequent decade vindicated in full, before handing the chamber to the presiding officer the Nazi party supplied. The woman who had proposed International Women's Day twenty-two years earlier, who had built the socialist women's movement's newspaper and its international, thus closed the movement phase from the rostrum of its parliament, months before the parliament itself was extinguished. She died in exile the following summer. The pillar ends the movement's story with her because she joins its ends: the organiser of 1889 and the prophet of 1932 are one person, and a tradition that produced her, and produced the audience that understood her, is misdescribed, to the point of falsehood, as a tendency invented in the universities forty years after her death.

## Emancipation sold back: the consumer turn

The tradition's account of the interwar decades in the capitalist West contains a chapter its summaries omit, on the form emancipation took when the market rather than the movement supplied it. The woman of the nineteen twenties in the metropolitan countries won the vote, entered the offices and the universities in growing numbers, and was addressed, with an intensity no previous generation of women had experienced, as a consumer: the department store, the cosmetics industry, the cigarette companies, and the new advertising profession discovered the female purchaser and built the modern machinery of persuasion partly upon her. The tradition's writers watched the discovery with a characteristic double vision. The famous emblem is the cigarette campaign of 1929 in which the publicist Edward Bernays, advised by a psychoanalyst on the symbolism, staged young women smoking in New York's Easter Parade as torches of freedom, converting a feminist vocabulary into a sales device before the cameras of the press. To liberal eyes, then and since, the episode is an irony. To this tradition it is a diagnosis: a society that organises reproduction privately and sells its products publicly will happily sell women the insignia of liberation, the cigarette, the lipstick, the labour-saving tin, while leaving the organisation that burdens them exactly where it stood, since the insignia are profitable and the organisation is not. The flapper's freedom was real as far as it went, in dress, in manners, in the refusal of chaperonage; the tradition's point is about where it stopped, at the door of the household economy its heroines would enter on the old terms, their wages supplementary by design, their afternoons spoken for. Every subsequent wave of commercial feminism, each generation's marketing of empowerment as a purchasing style, has been processed by the tradition's writers through the template Bernays supplied in 1929, and the template has worn well enough to count as one of the framework's confirmed predictions: where emancipation can be sold, it will be, in the form that leaves the seller's world intact.

## Using the framework: four questions for any arrangement

A pillar guide should leave its readers with an instrument, not only an exposition, and the tradition's instrument can be carried in four questions, each of which this article has watched at work. The first question is the founding one: who performs the reproductive labour here, and on what terms? Asked of a factory, a university, a hospital, or a tax code, it surfaces the hidden subsidy, the wives, mothers, grandmothers, and migrant carers whose unpaid or underpaid hours the arrangement presupposes. The second question follows the wage: whose earnings are treated as the household's foundation and whose as a supplement, and what in the grading, the hours, and the career structure enforces the distinction? The third question is Kollontai's: what happens to intimate life inside this arrangement, what does it teach the people formed in it to expect from one another, and does the teaching match the arrangement's public account of itself? The fourth question is the record section's: who governs, and has the emancipation on offer reached the rooms where the decisions are made, or does it stop, as the socialist states' settlement stopped, at employment and the statute book?

### What separates a Marxist feminist explanation from a merely sympathetic one?

The mechanism. A sympathetic account deplores women's burdens and praises their capacities; a Marxist feminist account shows the arrangement that produces the burden, names who benefits from it and how, and identifies the point where changing the organisation of reproduction would change the outcome. Explanation, in this tradition, is the politics in analytical form.

The questions travel because the arrangement does. Applied to a profession's partnership track, they find the second shift priced into the promotion timetable; applied to a platform economy's cheerful flexibility, they find the care work re-privatised onto the worker's own household and the risk re-privatised with it; applied to a government's family policy, they find the demographic hand described in this guide, managing reproduction for the state's purposes while speaking the language of choice. The tradition does not own the questions, and its writers would be the first to note that other feminisms ask versions of them; its claim is the historical one this guide has documented, that it asked them first, in print, with a movement attached, and that the century since has been largely the story of the questions outliving the answers their first askers gave.

Watch the instrument work on a single institution, a hospital, chosen because hospitals concentrate the arrangement. Who performs the reproductive labour here? The nursing, cleaning, feeding, and comforting on which the medicine floats, done overwhelmingly by women, much of it graded below the procedures it makes possible, and behind the staff the second workforce at home, the wives and daughters nursing the discharged patient through a convalescence the shortened stay has transferred to the household without payment or record. Whose wage is foundational? The consultant's, whose career timetable presupposes a household run by someone else; the nurse's wage is set, in the labour market's quiet arithmetic, as a supplement, and her interrupted service is priced accordingly. What does the arrangement teach? That care is a disposition of certain persons rather than a cost of the institution, a lesson the patients absorb with their medicine. And who governs? The trust's board and the medical hierarchy, in proportions the record section's third chapter would recognise at a glance. Four questions, one building, and the whole of this guide's argument stands visible in a corridor: the separations of production from reproduction, of the foundational wage from the supplementary one, of care's performance from care's command. The tradition's claim for its method is exactly this portability, that a framework built on the nineteenth century's mills and kitchens reads the twenty-first century's hospital without translation, because the arrangement it describes was never a feature of one industry's technology. It is a feature of how the society organises the making of life, and it persists, in new uniforms, wherever that organisation is left unexamined.

## The other side of the bargain: men in the arrangement

The tradition's analysis is of an arrangement, not of a sex's villainy, and its completeness shows in its account of what the arrangement does to men, who are its beneficiaries in the household and its raw material in the market. The family wage bargain priced a man's worth by his wage and made his claim to domestic service depend on delivering it; the man who failed in the market failed, by the arrangement's logic, as a man, and the literature of every industrial society records the wreckage of that equation in the lives of the unemployed, the injured, and the retired. The bargain also fenced men out of the reproductive sphere it assigned to women, with consequences the tradition's writers noted early and the later study of masculinity documented at length: fathers estranged from the daily knowledge of their children, emotional life conducted under a code of provision and silence, and a dependence on women's unpaid care, in sickness and old age, as total as the dependence it imposed in the other direction. Kollontai, characteristically, saw the point before the literature existed, arguing that the new morality she wanted would liberate men from the proprietor's psychology as surely as it liberated women from the proprietor's household, and Bebel's bestseller had addressed its male readers as parties imprisoned by the same house.

The point matters to the pillar for the fairness of its central dispute. When the dual systems theorists speak of patriarchy's material base in men's interests, the claim is precise about the level at which the interest operates: men as a group receive services, leisure, and authority from the arrangement, and defend them, in the union campaigns and the legislative record this guide has cited, with their eyes open. It does not follow, and the serious writers on every side denied, that the arrangement serves men's flourishing, or that its beneficiaries experience it as a gift rather than as the price of admission to a recognised life. The Nordic experiments with reserved parental leave for fathers, in which a portion of the leave is lost to the household if the father does not take it, are the policy form of the tradition's insight: the organisation of reproduction changes when its assignment is changed by design rather than exhorted by sermon, and the fathers who took the leave, in the studies that followed them, reported the discovery the theory predicts, that the work of care is work, and that having done it rearranges a man's account of the household he returns to. An arrangement, not an essence: the sentence is the tradition's whole position in miniature, and it applies to the sex that staffs the offices as to the sex that staffs the kitchens.

## The last shift: age and the care of the old

The tradition's framework began with the reproduction of labour power, the making of workers, and its logic extends without alteration to the other end of life, where the same privatised arrangement waits. A society that assigns the care of children to mothers as a natural duty assigns the care of the old, when it comes, by the same reflex and to the same hands, one generation on: to daughters and daughters-in-law in the middle of their own working lives, whose withdrawal from employment to nurse a parent is recorded in the statistics, when it is recorded at all, as economic inactivity. The developed welfare states socialised this shift unevenly, as they had the nursery shift before it; the residential and domiciliary services of the northern settlements stand at one end of the range, and at the other stands the family obligation enforced by law or by the simple absence of any alternative, with the market's solution, the paid carer, recruited along the care chain this guide described from the poorer households of the world. The demographic turn of the wealthy societies, ageing populations and shrinking cohorts of the young, has made the last shift the fastest-growing part of the whole reproductive economy, and the tradition's writers have not been slow to notice that the century's argument is repeating itself in a new ward: a vast quantity of socially necessary labour, performed overwhelmingly by women, counted as love or as nothing, and priced, where it is priced at all, at the bottom of the wage structure.

The pillar draws the thread into its assessment deliberately, because the ageing settlement is the test on which the tradition's competing heirs and critics will be judged by readers who come after this guide. The unitary framework predicts that the care of the old will be organised as the care of children was, privatised where politics permits, socialised where labour's demand or women's organisation forces the issue, and staffed in every case by the sex and the class the arrangement designates. The dual systems warning predicts that the designation will survive changes of regime and of rhetoric, as it survived 1917 and 1949 and the welfare settlements between. Both predictions are being run, in the nursing homes and the spare bedrooms of every ageing society, at a scale the founding generation could not have imagined, by populations who have never heard the tradition's name. That is the condition in which this guide leaves its subject: an analysis whose object has outgrown every institution built to carry the analysis, which is either the definition of a classic or the definition of an unfinished one, and which this tradition, characteristically, would insist are the same thing.

## Bern: the movement against the war

The movement phase's finest institutional hour came at its parent movement's worst. When the First World War split the socialist parties of Europe in 1914, the men's internationals collapsed into national war efforts, and the socialist women's organisation, alone among the Second International's structures, convened internationally against the war: at Bern in March 1915, delegates from the belligerent and neutral countries, brought together under Zetkin's leadership of the women's secretariat, adopted a manifesto addressed to the women of the warring nations, declaring the war a product of the same order that exploited their labour and calling on working women to refuse the role assigned them, of cheering the slaughter and replacing the slaughtered in the mills at lower wages. Russian delegates carried the sharper politics of the Bolshevik emigration into the hall, and the argument between revolutionary defeatism and pacifist appeal ran through the conference as it ran through the whole anti-war left; what matters for the pillar is the institutional fact. The women's international, built in the years of the suffrage fight, had nerves its parent body lacked, because it had been built partly against the parent's indifference and had kept its own secretariat, its own newspaper, and its own habit of assembly. The Bern conference is the autonomy argument of the dual systems theorists, demonstrated in 1915 by the movement's practice: when the general movement failed its own principles, the women's organisation, possessing the means of independent action, acted. The tradition's historians cite Bern beside Petrograd in 1917, where the women's day demonstration opened the revolution, as the twin exhibits of the movement phase at its best, and the pillar joins them: the two episodes, two years apart, are what the pre-history correction is finally about, not the recovery of names but the recovery of a standard, the standard of an organised women's politics that the later tradition, for all its theoretical superiority, has yet to match in institutions.

## Practice in the second phase: a union of their own

The academic phase was not all journals, and its most instructive organisation deserves the record the movement phase's institutions received. The Chicago Women's Liberation Union, founded in 1969 and dissolved in 1977, was the American socialist feminist movement's model institution: a membership organisation with dues, elected leadership, and work groups that combined the two halves of the dual systems politics in brick and mortar. Its health work included the abortion counselling service known as Jane, which began by referring women to practitioners and ended, in the years before the law changed, by providing abortions itself, an illegal service run on feminist principles at prices women could pay, and one of the famous underground institutions of the period. Its other work groups ran a school that taught women the theory of their own movement alongside practical skills, organised around employment and childcare campaigns, and published a newspaper and a celebrated pamphlet literature. The Union's premise, stated in its founding documents, was the one this guide has followed through two centuries: that women needed an organisation of their own, neither a caucus inside someone else's movement nor a service agency without a politics, in which the analysis of capitalism and the analysis of male domination could be conducted by the same people at the same table.

The Union's dissolution, after eight years, in disagreements over structure and direction that its historians have documented candidly, belongs in the pillar beside the Zhenotdel's abolition and for a different reason. The Russian department was killed by its parent; the Chicago union exhausted itself, as much of the second phase's organisational inheritance did, in the difficulty of sustaining a membership institution without a mass base, in years when the movements that had carried it were receding. Between them, the two endings frame the tradition's organisational problem in its permanent form: absorbed by a stronger movement, the women's organisation is dissolved by decision; independent without a mass movement behind it, it is dissolved by attrition. The movement phase solved the problem for a generation, at the price of the subordination the Bern conference's autonomy protested. No later phase has solved it durably, and the tradition's continuing work, described above in the universities and the unions' committees, proceeds inside that unsolved problem, which a complete guide should end by naming rather than decorating.

## The correction from the field: Leacock and the egalitarian foragers

The founding text section of this guide recorded that Morgan's anthropology was superseded, and honesty about a tradition includes naming the scholar who did most to rebuild its prehistory on evidence. Eleanor Leacock, an American anthropologist formed in the Marxist tradition and in the field, spent her career on the question Engels had posed with inadequate materials: what were relations between the sexes in societies without private property in the modern sense, and what happened to those relations when property and the market arrived? Her fieldwork among the Montagnais-Naskapi people of Labrador, and her study of the records the fur trade left behind, supported a disciplined version of the founding claim. In the band societies she described, production was communal, decisions were made by consensus in which women stood as autonomous persons, and the subordination of women was not a feature of the social order; the Jesuit relations and traders' accounts from the seventeenth century onward record, from the colonisers' own pens, the pressure to remake those arrangements, to teach Native men to govern their wives and Native families the habits of private property and female obedience, as the trade bound the hunters to the market. Leacock's edition of Engels's Origin, published in 1972 with an introduction that remains a standard orientation, defended the book's direction while discarding its ladder of stages, and her essays, collected in Myths of Male Dominance in 1981, extended the argument against the claim that male dominance is a human universal written in nature.

The pillar stations her here because her work states, in the tradition's most empirical register, what its theory predicts and what its critics must answer. If women's subordination were natural or immemorial, the ethnographic record should show it everywhere in roughly its familiar form; what the record assembled by Leacock and the feminist anthropology that followed her shows instead is variation that tracks material organisation, autonomy where production is communal and kinship undifferentiated by property, subordination arriving with the institutions, trade, missions, property law, whose business was to install it. The founding text's ladder is gone beyond recall, and the specific sequence Engels borrowed from Morgan with it; the claim that survived the fieldwork is the one Bebel had made without Morgan's help, that the subordination of women is a historical product, younger than the species and younger than society, and therefore not a sentence. A tradition is entitled to be judged by the fate of its corrigible parts as well as its dogmas, and on that standard the founding text's career is the opposite of an embarrassment: it sent researchers into the field with a question sharp enough to be corrected, and the correction brought back evidence for the claim the question had been built to test.

## The household as battleground: rent strikes and bread queues

The tradition's theory holds that the household is a site of political economy, and the First World War supplied the demonstration in the cities of the belligerents, where the politics of reproduction, rent, food, and prices, became mass politics conducted overwhelmingly by women. In Glasgow in 1915, with the men at the front and munitions workers flooding into the shipyard districts, landlords moved to raise rents on the tenements the war workers' families occupied, and the women of the districts answered with the weapon the household commands: a rent strike, organised street by street through the women's housing associations, defended against eviction by crowds that humiliated the sheriff's officers and held the stairs. Mary Barbour, a socialist organiser from Govan, became the strike's emblematic leader, and the agitation, spreading across the Clyde and into other British cities, forced the government, in the middle of a war it could not afford to complicate, to pass the Rent Restriction Act of 1915, freezing working-class rents at their pre-war level for the duration. The episode is a staple of the tradition's teaching because its analysis is written on its face. The strikers were not, in the main, factory workers withdrawing their labour from production; they were housewives withdrawing their cooperation from the reproduction of the workforce on the landlord's terms, and they won, against property, in wartime, because the state needed the shipyards' labour peace more than it needed the landlords' increases.

The pattern repeated across the belligerent capitals in the war's last years, in the food queues and bread protests of Petrograd, Vienna, and Berlin, where the politics of the household's provisioning, women's politics by the division of labour the century had imposed, repeatedly supplied the spark and the crowd for crises that ended governments. The February Revolution's opening demonstration has been given its place in this guide's record; the Glasgow strike belongs beside it as the western exhibit, and the two together state the movement phase's practical discovery, the one its theory had prepared it to recognise: that in an industrial society the organisation of reproduction is not a private prelude to politics but one of politics' principal terrains, and that the women assigned to that terrain, organised through the networks the socialist women's movement had spent two decades building, could act on it with an effect no contemporary institution of the left could match. The academic phase's theory of social reproduction is, among other things, the attempt to state in categories what the rent strikers of Govan had stated in practice.

## The junction in one life: Sylvia Pankhurst

British readers meet the tradition's central tension in a single biography, and the pillar closes its historical arc with her. Sylvia Pankhurst was raised inside the suffrage movement's most famous family and broke with it over exactly the question this guide has followed: her mother and sister's organisation, turning toward militancy and a single demand, expelled her in 1914 for the offence of building, in London's East End, a suffragette federation of working women that joined the vote to the price of milk, to nurseries, to the cost of the war to the households that bore it. The East London Federation of Suffragettes, and its newspaper, the Women's Dreadnought, practised the junction as an organisation: cost-price restaurants and a nursery and a toy factory in Bow, alongside the suffrage campaign, on the principle Pankhurst shared with Zetkin's line across the Channel, that working women would not be emancipated by a franchise that ignored the kitchen, nor fed by a socialism that postponed the franchise. She welcomed the Russian Revolution, argued with Lenin in print and in person as an ultra-left critic he answered by name, and spent her later decades in the anti-colonial cause of Ethiopia, a trajectory that carried her out of every organisation she had built and kept her, to the end, at the junction her family had expelled her for occupying. The pillar does not claim her for a card she did not carry; it records her as the British form of the figure this guide has met in Germany and Russia, the woman for whom the suffrage movement was too narrow and the socialist movement too patient, and whose existence, in the decade the textbooks allot to the suffragettes alone, is the pre-history correction wearing a single face.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk on this topic is not ignorance but compression: the answer that treats Marxist feminism as one doctrine, dates it from the nineteen seventies, and credits it with the view that class outranks gender will be marked as a summary of summaries, because each of those is a named error this guide corrects. Build your answers on the separations. Separate the three positions and attribute each claim to its holder, Vogel for the unitary construction, Hartmann for dual systems, Dalla Costa and Federici for the autonomist claim, since markers reward attribution as the visible form of understanding. Separate the movement phase from the academic phase, and be able to deploy the pre-history correction in a sentence: an organised socialist women's movement, with Bebel's bestseller, Zetkin's newspaper and conferences, and Kollontai in government, preceded the academic rediscovery by the better part of a century. Separate the record into its three chapters, legal transformation, reversal, and the long settlement of employment without power, and refuse any question's invitation to collapse them into a verdict about whether communism was good or bad for women. The dedicated treatment of how these materials are examined, including model structures for the comparison and record questions, is the cluster's own [marxist feminism exam answers](/2017/01/15/marxist-feminism-exam-answers/). For revision, the three positions repay being learned as a table before they are learned as essays: the [VaultBook study notes prepared for this series](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) carry a card on the three positions grid, its claims, theorists, and criticisms in the form in which an examiner's question will ask you to reproduce them, and working from the card to this article's prose and back is the fastest route to answers that separate what the summaries merge.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The pedagogical problem with this material is that students arrive with the flattened version installed, feminism as a sequence of waves and Marxist feminism as the wave's class-conscious tributary, and the seminar's first task is demolition, for which the pre-history is the efficient instrument: a single session on Bebel's editions, Zetkin's newspaper circulation, and the Bern conference rearranges the room's assumptions more thoroughly than a term of theory. Sequence the teaching as this guide is built. Establish the definition and the reproduction mechanism before the positions, since the positions are answers to the mechanism's problems; give Hartmann's objection a full session in its author's terms before any reply is permitted, since students who meet the objection first as a refutation to be defeated learn neither it nor the reply; and use the record as the seminar's evidence exercise, setting the legal transformation, the reversal, and the time-use findings side by side and requiring each student to state which position each document supports and which it embarrasses. The assessment your students will actually face on this material is comparative, and the cluster's article on [marxist radical liberal feminism](/2016/11/15/marxist-radical-liberal-feminism/) is built for exactly that assessment, mapping the three feminisms' accounts of the same phenomena in the form an examination rewards. For discussion, the two questions that have never failed to divide a room are the ones this guide leaves open: whether male domination's survival beyond the abolition of capitalist property refutes the unitary theory or merely its crude version, and whether the communal house was a liberation refused or a preference misread. Both are documented above at the length a seminar needs.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this tradition is archival and quantitative in roughly equal measure, and this guide's apparatus is designed to point at both. The movement phase remains under-worked relative to its documentation: the socialist women's press, above all the files of Zetkin's newspaper and the conference reports of the internationals, survives in quantity and has been mined by the recovery generation for narrative more than for the sociology of the movement itself, its membership, its finances, its readership's geography. The Zhenotdel's records, and the delegate meeting protocols through which peasant and working women addressed the Soviet state, are the other great under-used source, the rare archive in which the objects of an emancipation policy speak in their own recorded voices. On the quantitative side, the time-use tradition that Szalai founded now extends across decades and most of the world's statistical systems, and the tradition's central propositions, the double burden's persistence and its sensitivity to public provision, are testable against that series by any researcher willing to build the comparisons the theorists asserted from fragments. Journalists covering the care economy, the demographic anxieties of ageing states, or the politics of childcare are working this tradition's territory whether they cite it or not, and the guide's four questions, who does the reproductive labour, whose wage is foundational, what does the arrangement teach, and who governs, will organise a story on any of those subjects faster than the press releases will. The founding text's modern scholarly treatment, with its sources and its critics in one place, is the series reader's guide to Engels on the family, linked from the founding text section above, and the theoretical frame within which the whole tradition sits is the series pillar on the [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxist feminism?

It is the tradition that explains women's subordination through the way societies organise making a living and making life itself: the production of goods and the reproduction of people. Its distinctive claim is that the unpaid work of households, which feeds, raises, and restores the workforce, is part of the economic system rather than a private background to it, and that women's position follows from being assigned that work. The tradition begins in the socialist movement of the nineteenth century, rests on Engels's account of the family's history, and divides into three rival positions over whether one system or two produces the subordination it describes.

### Q: How does Marxist feminism differ from other feminisms?

The liberal schools locate the problem in exclusion, in rights, professions, and offices closed to women, and prescribe equal admission. The radical schools locate it in male domination as a system standing on its own. This tradition locates it in the joining of the two great spheres of labour: a society that separates waged production from the unwaged work of renewal, and staffs the second sphere with women, manufactures subordination afresh each generation whatever its laws proclaim. Its remedy is correspondingly organisational rather than juridical: change how the work of renewal is distributed and supported, and the equality that statutes announce becomes a description instead of an aspiration.

### Q: Who were the early socialist women theorists?

The movement's first theorists worked inside the socialist parties of the generation before the First World War. Clara Zetkin edited the German movement's women's newspaper for three decades and set the strategy of an autonomous women's organisation fighting for universal suffrage. Alexandra Kollontai wrote the period's deepest work on the family and on sexual relations before administering welfare in the revolutionary Russian government. Rosa Luxemburg contributed a celebrated intervention on women's suffrage and class struggle. Eleanor Marx, the founder's daughter, organised women workers in Britain and co-wrote an early pamphlet on the woman question. Inessa Armand built the first Soviet women's department. Behind them stood August Bebel, whose 1879 book gave the movement its shared argument.

### Q: What is the difference between Marxist and socialist feminism?

In careful usage the first term is the wider one, naming every analysis that explains women's oppression by the arrangement of production and reproduction. The second term settled, during the nineteen seventies, onto one position inside that field: the dual systems view, which holds that capitalism and patriarchy are separate systems, each needing its own theory and its own movement, joined in alliance. Writers adopted the socialist feminist label precisely to mark their distance from the unitary position, which trusts a single-system analysis to reach the whole problem. Popular usage blurs the distinction constantly, which is unfortunate, because the two labels encode the field's central theoretical quarrel.

### Q: What are the main theoretical positions within Marxist feminism?

Three positions divide the field, and they contradict one another on the basic question. The unitary position, whose developed name is social reproduction theory and whose classic statement is Lise Vogel's, holds that one system organises production and reproduction together, and that women's oppression is continually regenerated by the assignment of labour power's renewal to women in privatised households. The dual systems position, associated with Heidi Hartmann, holds that patriarchy is a second system with its own material base in men's control of women's labour, irreducible to capital's requirements. The autonomist position, founded by Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James, holds that household labour produces value for capital directly, and built the wages for housework demand on that claim.

### Q: What did the Bolsheviks change for women?

In law, almost everything, and at a speed no other government of the period approached. Between 1917 and 1920 the new Russian state replaced church marriage with a civil contract dissoluble at either partner's request, abolished the legal category of the illegitimate child and the disabilities attached to it, ended the absorption of a wife's legal personality in her husband's, and made abortion available on request, the first state in the world to do so. Beyond the codes, the party's women's department ran literacy campaigns, delegate meetings, nurseries, and communal dining and laundry services meant to begin transferring housework from the private kitchen to public provision. Much of the legal edifice was reversed from the middle nineteen thirties.

### Q: Who was Alexandra Kollontai?

She was the Russian revolutionary who became the tradition's foremost theorist of the family and of intimate life, and its only major figure to administer her programme in government. An agitator among women workers before 1917, she took the welfare portfolio in the revolutionary government, a cabinet post no woman in Europe had previously held, and helped build the women's department that carried the new family legislation into daily life. Her writings argued that transforming property would not by itself transform relations between the sexes, that new forms of comradely love had to displace the possessiveness of the property family. Her part in the Workers' Opposition and her unfashionable questions made her inconvenient, and the party posted her to diplomatic missions abroad for most of her remaining life.

### Q: Which socialist book on women sold most widely?

August Bebel's Woman and Socialism, first published in 1879, outsold every other theoretical work the socialist movement produced on the subject and, by the usual historical judgement, on most subjects. Bebel, a co-founder of German social democracy, argued that women's subordination arrived with private property, that emancipation required women to enter social production on equal terms and the private household's labour to pass into collective hands, and that a socialism postponing that programme had not understood its own argument. The book was prosecuted and periodically banned, and it sold through the bans, running to dozens of editions in its author's lifetime and translation across Europe and beyond, reaching working-class households, very many of them women's, that owned few other books.

### Q: Is Marxist feminism still active?

As an organised mass movement of the kind the nineteenth-century parties sustained, no; that form belongs to the tradition's history. As an analysis, it is active in three durable settings. In the universities, social reproduction theory in its mature form organises a large share of the scholarly work on gender and political economy. In the labour movement, its vocabulary of the second shift and its childcare politics circulate through unions' women's structures and campaigns for public care. And in the archive, the recovery of the movement phase continues, as the newspapers, pamphlets, and records of the socialist women's organisations are edited and taught. The questions the tradition poses about unpaid care structure public argument even where its name goes unmentioned.

### Q: Where should you start reading Marxist feminism?

Begin where the movement began, with Bebel's Woman and Socialism, whose clarity about property, the family, and the programme of collective housework survives its century, with Engels's Origin of the Family read alongside for the theory, keeping in mind that its anthropology belongs to its period. Move to Kollontai's essays on the family for the tradition's account of intimate life and to Zetkin's speeches for its strategy. For the modern argument, read Mitchell's 1966 essay and Benston's of 1969 as the academic phase's charter, then Hartmann's essay on the unhappy marriage for the strongest objection in its author's words, Davis's Women, Race and Class for the correction of the field's subject, and Vogel's treatise of 1983 for the unitary position whole.

### Q: How does the pre-history correction change where the tradition's story begins?

It moves the beginning back by the better part of a century and changes the kind of thing the tradition is taken to be. The received story opens in the nineteen seventies, with essays in journals, and presents the field as an academic speciality that arose when feminists encountered Marxism. The corrected story opens in the eighteen eighties, with a mass movement possessing its own theorists, newspapers, conferences, and, after 1917, a share in state power and a legislative record. On the corrected account the nineteen seventies become a second phase, a rediscovery conducted after the movement's institutions and transmission lines had been destroyed, and the questions change accordingly: not only what the theory claims, but what its movement built, what its governments did, and why the inheritance was lost.

### Q: What should a student separate between the tradition's feminist inheritance and its own contribution?

Three separations earn their keep in an examination. First, separate what the tradition took from the wider women's movement, the demands for suffrage, education, legal personality, and equal pay, which it shared and fought for, from what it added: the explanation of why those victories, when won, left subordination standing. Second, separate the inheritance from classical Marxism, the method of explaining a social arrangement by the labour that sustains it, from the correction the tradition administered to that inheritance, the demonstration that the labour which reproduces the workforce had gone uncounted by the founders. Third, separate the positions from one another, since an answer that attributes the dual systems claim to the unitary school, or the wages demand to either, announces that the candidate has read the summaries and not the argument.

### Q: Why did reproduction move to the centre of the analysis?

Because the older explanations left the central fact untouched. Accounts in terms of prejudice could not say why subordination regenerated itself in each generation among people who disavowed it; accounts in terms of law could not say why formal equality left it standing. The theorists who moved reproduction to the centre were following the one process that demonstrably renewed the arrangement daily: the production and renewal of labour power in households, assigned to women, unpaid, and presupposed by every wage ever paid. A mechanism that operates every day explains a pattern that recurs every generation, which attitudes and statutes, changing at their own pace, do not. Once that process was named, the tradition's other questions, about segregation, the family wage, and the state, arranged themselves around it.

### Q: How does the three positions grid order the schools?

The grid orders the schools by the question on which they divide: how many systems produce women's oppression, and what follows for politics. Its rows are the unitary position, which answers one system and seats the mechanism in labour power's renewal; the dual systems position, which answers two, capitalism and patriarchy in interaction; and the autonomist position, which answers one system read from the household, holding domestic labour directly productive for capital. Its columns force each row to declare the same five things: the claim about capitalism and gender, the principal theorists, what reproductive labour is said to produce, the political conclusion drawn, and the main criticism the position attracts. Read down a column, the grid compares; read across a row, it summarises a whole theory in a line.

### Q: What separates a Marxist feminist explanation from a dual systems explanation?

The number of mechanisms each is willing to posit, and the evidence each treats as decisive. A Marxist feminist explanation in the unitary sense traces women's subordination to the organisation of reproduction within a single system: change how labour power is renewed, and the subordination loses its engine, whatever older forms it inherited. A dual systems explanation insists that the evidence, male domination before capitalism and its survival after capitalist property was abolished, requires a second mechanism with its own base in men's control of women's labour, which no reorganisation of production alone reaches. The separation is therefore empirical as much as logical: the two explanations part company over what the socialist states' record proves, and a student who can state that parting precisely understands both.
