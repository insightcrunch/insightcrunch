A car rolls along a straight road while its speedometer needle wanders, rising on the downhill stretches and sagging on the climbs. Ask how far the car traveled during one hour of that uneven driving and the arithmetic of earlier courses falls silent, because the speed refuses to sit still long enough for a single multiplication to describe it. Calculus AB answers the question with an idea so sturdy that it carries the entire second half of the course: chop the hour into thin slices, pretend the speed holds steady inside each slice, multiply, add, and then let the slices shrink until the pretense becomes exact. That chopping and adding is a Riemann sum, and the theorem that certifies the limit of the process is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

Students often meet integration first as a hunt for antiderivatives, a reverse gear for differentiation, and that first meeting quietly distorts everything that follows. Antidifferentiation is a technique, one tool among several, while integration itself is a measurement: the measurement of accumulated change. A definite integral reports how much of something piled up while a rate varied, whether that something is distance, water, population, cost, or area under a curve. Riemann sums keep that meaning visible because every rectangle in the sum stands for a small, understandable piece of the pile. The Fundamental Theorem then connects the pile to the rate in both directions, and fluency with those two directions is precisely what the AP exam rewards.

This guide works the whole arc in one place. It builds Riemann sums from tables, graphs, and formulas, compares left, right, midpoint, and trapezoidal estimates and their errors, walks one accumulation problem end to end, states and uses both parts of the Fundamental Theorem, and finishes with accumulation functions, average value, net change, and the habits that separate strong exam answers from near misses. Readers who want the full course map first can start with the [AP Calculus AB complete guide](/2008/08/10/ap-calculus-ab-complete-guide/), and those who need the limit and derivative foundations refreshed will find them in the companion treatment of [limits, derivatives, and basic integrals](/2008/08/05/ap-calculus-ab-limits-derivatives-integrals/).

## Accumulation: the Idea Under Everything

Every problem in this territory begins with a rate and a question about a total. A rate describes how fast something changes at an instant: miles per hour, gallons per minute, dollars per item, people per year. A total describes how much accumulated across an interval of time or across a stretch of the axis. The two ideas pair through multiplication whenever the rate stays constant, since a pump delivering exactly 6 gallons per minute for 10 minutes delivers 60 gallons, and a car holding 50 miles per hour for 2 hours covers 100 miles. Constant rates make the pairing trivial, and real rates are almost never constant, which is exactly why the subject exists.

Varying the rate breaks the single multiplication without breaking the idea behind it. Over a very short interval, even a wandering rate barely changes, so the product of the rate and the interval length approximates the small accumulation across that interval with tiny error. Slice the whole interval into many short pieces, form that product on each piece, and add the products: the sum approximates the total accumulation, and the approximation improves as the slices thin out. Nothing in that sentence depends on the quantity involved. The same slicing argument estimates distance from a velocity graph, volume pumped from a flow record, revenue from a marginal revenue curve, and area from the formula of a function.

Area enters the story because a graph turns the slicing argument into a picture. Plot the rate against time and each short product becomes the area of a thin rectangle whose height is the rate and whose width is the time slice. Adding the products means adding rectangle areas, and the grand total approximates the area trapped between the curve and the axis. When the rectangles grow thin enough, their union hugs the curve so closely that the sum of their areas and the true area under the curve become indistinguishable. The definite integral is the name given to that limiting total, and it therefore carries a double meaning from birth: accumulated change, and signed area under a graph.

Signed area deserves its own sentence early, because it governs interpretation everywhere later. When a rate graph dips below the axis, the corresponding accumulation runs backward: water leaves the tank, the car moves in reverse, the population shrinks. The integral keeps the sign, subtracting those stretches from the total, so an integral reports net accumulation rather than gross accumulation. Exam questions exploit the distinction relentlessly, asking sometimes for net change, sometimes for total distance or total volume, and the difference between the two answers is exactly the handling of the negative portions.

## Rectangles Under a Curve: Building a Sum

Fix an interval from a to b and a function f defined on it. A partition cuts the interval into n subintervals, and on each subinterval the construction chooses one sample point and reads the function height there. The rectangle over that subinterval takes the chosen height and the subinterval width, and its signed area is the product of the two. The Riemann sum is the sum of those n products, and every named approximation scheme in the course is nothing more than a rule for choosing the sample points.

Equal widths dominate the exam because they simplify the arithmetic and the reading of tables. When the cuts are evenly spaced, each subinterval shares the width delta x, computed as (b minus a) divided by n. Choosing the left endpoint of every subinterval yields the left Riemann sum, choosing the right endpoint yields the right sum, and choosing the center yields the midpoint sum. Unequal partitions appear too, most often when a table reports values at irregular times, and then each rectangle carries its own width, read straight from the gaps between consecutive table entries.

The symbols compress all of this into a compact line. A general Riemann sum is written as the sum, running over the subintervals, of f evaluated at the sample point of each subinterval, multiplied by that subinterval's width. Sigma notation packages the instruction: an index counts the rectangles, the function value supplies each height, and the width factor converts height into a small area. Nothing about the notation changes the picture, and students who keep the picture in mind rarely misread the symbols.

### What does delta x measure in a Riemann sum?

Delta x measures the width of one subinterval when an interval from a to b is divided into n equal parts, computed as (b minus a) divided by n. Every rectangle in the sum inherits that width, so delta x converts a sampled height into a small area contribution and controls how finely the sum resolves the curve.

A concrete pass through the machinery fixes the vocabulary. Take f(x) = x squared on the interval from 0 to 2, split into four equal subintervals, so delta x equals 0.5 and the cut points are 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2. The function values at those points are 0, 0.25, 1, 2.25, and 4. A left sum reads heights at 0, 0.5, 1, and 1.5, whose total is 0 plus 0.25 plus 1 plus 2.25, equal to 3.5, and multiplying by the width 0.5 gives 1.75. A right sum reads heights at 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2, totaling 7.5, and the same width gives 3.75. Already the two sums bracket the truth, because this function increases across the whole interval, a fact later sections turn into a general rule.

Midpoints sample the centers of the subintervals: 0.25, 0.75, 1.25, and 1.75. Their squares are 0.0625, 0.5625, 1.5625, and 3.0625, which add to 5.25, and the midpoint sum is 0.5 times 5.25, or 2.625. The true value of the integral, confirmed later through the Fundamental Theorem, is 8/3, about 2.667. Three cheap computations already reveal the personality of each scheme: the left sum fell short by about 0.92, the right sum overshot by about 1.08, and the midpoint sum missed by barely 0.04. That spread in accuracy is not an accident of this example, and understanding its causes is worth real points.


## The Trapezoidal Rule: Slanted Tops Instead of Flat Ones

Rectangles force a curve to be summarized by flat tops, and the mismatch along slanted stretches is where their error lives. The trapezoidal rule softens the mismatch by letting each strip follow the curve's endpoints: over one subinterval, the region is approximated by a trapezoid whose two parallel sides are the function values at the left and right edges and whose width is the subinterval width. The area of that trapezoid is the width times the average of the two heights, a formula worth memorizing in words as well as symbols: width times half the sum of the endpoint values.

On the x squared example with four subintervals, the trapezoid areas are 0.5 times the average of 0 and 0.25, then 0.5 times the average of 0.25 and 1, then 0.5 times the average of 1 and 2.25, then 0.5 times the average of 2.25 and 4. Those four products are 0.0625, 0.3125, 0.8125, and 1.5625, and they total 2.75. A faster route notices that the trapezoidal estimate always equals the average of the corresponding left and right sums, strip by strip: half of 1.75 plus 3.75 is 2.75, matching the strip-by-strip computation exactly. That identity gives exam takers a genuine shortcut, since many problems hand over a table from which both endpoint sums are quick.

Unequal widths break the shortcut but not the method. Each strip still contributes its own width times the average of its two endpoint heights, and the sum of those contributions is the estimate. Tables with irregular spacing are a signature exam setting precisely because they test whether the student computes strip by strip rather than reaching for a single global width. The walkthrough later in this guide runs exactly such a table through every scheme, so the arithmetic stays visible.

Accuracy separates the methods in a predictable order on smooth curves. Doubling the number of subintervals on the x squared example, from four to eight, pulls the trapezoidal estimate to 2.6875, cutting its error from about 0.083 to about 0.021, roughly a factor of four. Midpoint sums improve at a similar rate, while endpoint sums improve more slowly, their error shrinking roughly by a factor of two when n doubles. Finer partitions always help, yet the ranking of methods at a fixed n depends on the shape of the curve, and the next section explains the shape rules that the exam loves to probe.

## Overestimates, Underestimates, and the Testimony of the Graph

Monotonicity settles the endpoint sums with a picture anyone can draw. On an interval where the function increases, every left rectangle stops at the lower left corner of its strip and leaves a small curvy triangle of area unfilled, so the left sum underestimates. The right rectangles on the same strips overshoot into empty space above the curve, so the right sum overestimates. Reverse the direction for a decreasing function: left sums overestimate, right sums underestimate. When a graph changes direction inside the interval, the sums inherit both behaviors on different stretches, and the exam expects the student to argue piece by piece rather than pronounce a single verdict.

Concavity governs the trapezoidal and midpoint verdicts. Where a curve bends upward, each trapezoid's slanted top cuts across the bend and encloses a sliver of extra area, so the trapezoidal rule overestimates; where the curve bends downward, the slanted top slices below the arc and underestimates. Midpoint rectangles obey the opposite pattern on the same shapes: an upward bend lets the tangent-like midline height leave small corners unfilled, producing an underestimate, and a downward bend produces an overestimate. On the x squared example the curve bends upward everywhere, and the numbers obeyed the rules exactly: trapezoid 2.75 above the true 2.667, midpoint 2.625 below it.

Bounds are the practical prize. Whenever a left and right sum disagree on an increasing stretch, the true integral lies between them, and a midpoint or trapezoidal value can be bracketed the same way when concavity is known across the interval. Exam questions phrased as "which of the following could be the value of the integral" are usually bracketing questions in disguise, and the student who knows which estimates bound the truth can eliminate options without any further computation. Where neither monotonicity nor concavity holds across the whole interval, no scheme earns a guaranteed verdict, and saying so, with the reason named, is itself a scored justification on free-response parts.

### Why does the trapezoidal rule average the two endpoint heights?

A trapezoid over one subinterval has parallel sides equal to the function values at the two edges, and the area of a trapezoid equals its width times the average of its parallel sides. Averaging the endpoint heights therefore gives the exact area of the approximating trapezoid, which hugs a slanted curve more closely than either flat-topped rectangle can.

## The InsightCrunch Accumulation Walkthrough

One complete problem, carried from raw data to interpreted answer, teaches more than five summaries. Picture a tank that starts a shift holding 40 gallons of water while a sensor records the inflow rate at irregular moments, because the pump cycles on and off and the readings follow the cycling. The recorded rates, in gallons per minute, appear in the table that anchors this walkthrough, and the questions are the ones the exam asks in rotation: estimate the total inflow over twelve minutes, state the method, judge whether the estimate runs high or low, and report the amount in the tank at the end.

| Time (minutes) | 0 | 1 | 3 | 6 | 10 | 12 |
| Rate (gallons per minute) | 4 | 6 | 5 | 8 | 7 | 9 |
| Width of strip to the right (minutes) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 2 | none |
| Left height product (gallons) | 4 | 12 | 15 | 32 | 14 | none |

The strips have widths 1, 2, 3, 4, and 2 minutes, read from the gaps between consecutive times, and no single delta x exists for this table. A left Riemann sum uses the rate at the left edge of each strip, so its products are 1 times 4, 2 times 6, 3 times 5, 4 times 8, and 2 times 7, which give 4, 12, 15, 32, and 14 gallons. Adding those products yields 77 gallons. A right Riemann sum shifts every height one entry to the right: 1 times 6, 2 times 5, 3 times 8, 4 times 7, and 2 times 9 produce 6, 10, 24, 28, and 18, totaling 86 gallons. The two endpoint sums already disagree by 9 gallons, and that gap measures the uncertainty the data leave unresolved.

Trapezoids split the difference strip by strip. The five trapezoid areas are 1 times the average of 4 and 6, 2 times the average of 6 and 5, 3 times the average of 5 and 8, 4 times the average of 8 and 7, and 2 times the average of 7 and 9. Numerically those are 5, 11, 19.5, 30, and 16, and their sum is 81.5 gallons. That total is exactly the average of 77 and 86, and the equality is no coincidence: each trapezoid area equals the average of the left and right rectangle areas on its own strip, so the trapezoidal sum always averages the two endpoint sums. What dies with unequal widths is only the lazy habit of factoring out one common width before adding.

Interpreting the estimate matters as much as producing it. The trapezoidal value says about 81.5 gallons flowed in during the twelve minutes, on top of the 40 gallons present at the start, so the tank ends near 121.5 gallons, with the word "about" doing honest work because the rate between readings is unknown. If a follow-up asks whether 81.5 overstates or understates the inflow, the data alone cannot say: the recorded rates rise, fall, and rise again, so neither a monotone nor a concave pattern covers the whole interval, and the defensible answer names that obstruction instead of guessing. A different part might ask for the average inflow rate across the shift, which is the accumulated total divided by 12 minutes, about 6.79 gallons per minute, a first glimpse of the average value machinery built later in this guide.

Free-response scoring on such problems separates the setup from the arithmetic. The setup, widths times heights with heights traced to named table entries, earns the structural points; the arithmetic earns one accuracy point; the interpretation with units earns the final point. Students who write only a final number forfeit most of the value even when the number is right, a theme the strategy section near the end develops in full. For timed drills on exactly this pattern, the [free AP math and science practice questions](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ap-math-science-practice.html) supply table-based sets that mirror the exam's phrasing.

## Sums from Graphs: Reading Heights Instead of Formulas

Half of the approximation work on the exam arrives as a picture rather than a table or a formula. A graph problem hands over the curve, a marked partition, and a named scheme, and the student's task is to read heights off the picture at the correct sample points. The discipline is bookkeeping: mark each subinterval, note where its sample point sits, read the height with care at exactly that horizontal position, and only then multiply. Errors cluster at the sampling step, particularly on midpoint problems, where the sample point is the center of the strip rather than an endpoint the eye naturally visits.

Curves assembled from straight segments and circular arcs make graph problems friendlier than they look, because the limiting integral often equals a combination of triangle, rectangle, and semicircle areas. When a question asks for a Riemann sum, though, the student must resist the geometric shortcut and actually form rectangles, since the sum is defined by its samples and not by the true area. When the same graph returns later in a question asking for an exact integral or an accumulation value, geometry becomes the intended tool, and recognizing which mode a part demands is a core skill.

Grid counting underlies many multiple-choice graph items. Each grid square carries a known area, width times height in axis units, and a sum over a partition amounts to counting covered squares weighted by the sampling rule. Care with axis scales prevents the classic blunder of treating one square as one unit when the axes say otherwise. A graph whose vertical scale runs in twos doubles every height, and the students who annotate the scale on their scratch paper before computing rarely pay for that mistake.

## From Sums to the Definite Integral: The Limit Step

Shrinking the widths and multiplying the count of rectangles turns approximation into definition. The definite integral of f from a to b is defined as the limit of Riemann sums as the number of subintervals grows without bound and the widest width shrinks to zero. Any consistent sampling rule converges to the same limit for the continuous functions this course handles, which is why left, right, midpoint, and trapezoidal estimates all close in on one number. The integral sign itself is a stretched letter S for sum, and the dx that closes the notation is the ghost of delta x after the shrinking, a record of the widths that produced the total.

Writing a limit of sums in the other direction is a tested skill with its own patterns. Given an integral, students should be able to express it as a limit of right-endpoint sums: delta x equals (b minus a) over n, the sample points march as a plus i times delta x for i running from 1 to n, and the sum of f at those points times delta x approaches the integral as n grows. Given a limit of sums, students run the identification backward, reading off the interval and the function from the pattern of the terms. The sample expression sum of the squares of (2 plus 3i over n), times 3 over n, with i from 1 to n, decodes to the integral from 2 to 5 of x squared dx: the 3 over n is delta x for an interval of length 3, the inner expression starts at 2, and the integrand is the square of the whole sample point.

One computation shows the limit machinery working rather than merely being described. For f(x) = x squared on the interval from 0 to 2 with n equal subintervals, delta x is 2 over n and the right endpoints are 2i over n. The right sum becomes the sum over i of (2i over n) squared times (2 over n), which simplifies to 8 over n cubed times the sum of i squared from 1 to n. The closed form for that sum of squares, n times (n plus 1) times (2n plus 1) over 6, turns the whole expression into 8 times n(n plus 1)(2n plus 1) over 6n cubed. As n grows, the ratio n(n plus 1)(2n plus 1) over n cubed approaches 2, so the sum approaches 8 times 2 over 6, which is 8/3. The limit lands exactly where the earlier bracketing said it must, between 1.75 and 3.75 and close to the midpoint and trapezoidal guesses.

### How do you recognize the function hidden inside a limit of sums?

Read the width factor first: delta x is the fraction or term multiplying the whole sum, and its numerator reveals the interval length. Then inspect the sample point inside the function: its constant term is the left endpoint a, and whatever operation surrounds the sample point is the integrand itself, with the sample point playing the role of x.


## The Fundamental Theorem, Part Two: Evaluating Integrals Exactly

Approximation has a ceiling, and the Fundamental Theorem breaks through it. The evaluation part of the theorem says that if F is any antiderivative of a continuous function f, then the integral of f from a to b equals F(b) minus F(a). Two substitutions and one subtraction replace the infinite labor of the limit, provided an antiderivative can be found. The notation F evaluated from a to b, written with a vertical bar, packages the subtraction, and the constant of integration cancels in the difference, which is why any antiderivative serves.

Run the theorem on the example that has anchored this guide: the integral from 0 to 2 of x squared dx. An antiderivative of x squared is x cubed over 3, so the value is 8/3 minus 0, exactly 8/3, confirming the target the sums kept approaching. A second evaluation with more moving parts: the integral from 1 to 3 of (3x squared minus 2x plus 1) dx uses the antiderivative x cubed minus x squared plus x. At 3 that antiderivative gives 27 minus 9 plus 3, which is 21; at 1 it gives 1 minus 1 plus 1, which is 1; the difference is 20. Showing both substitutions before the subtraction is the format graders reward, because a sign slip in one substitution is then visible and partly forgivable, while a bare answer hides everything.

Trigonometric and root examples round out the standard repertoire. With sin x as integrand, the integral from 0 to pi uses the antiderivative negative cos x: at pi it gives 1, at 0 it gives negative 1, and the difference is 2, a clean number that matches the symmetric arch the sine curve traces. For the square root of x integrated from 0 to 9, rewriting the root as x to the one-half power gives the antiderivative (2/3) x to the three-halves power; at 9 that is (2/3) times 27, or 18, and at 0 it is 0, so the integral equals 18. Each evaluation follows the same liturgy: antiderivative, substitution at the top, substitution at the bottom, subtraction.

Why the theorem works deserves one intuitive pass, because the exam probes understanding as well as execution. Fix the left endpoint and let the right endpoint move: the accumulating area becomes a function of position, and its rate of change at any point equals the height of the curve there, since a thin added slice contributes roughly height times a tiny width. An accumulation function therefore grows at the rate f, which means it is an antiderivative of f, and evaluating the total accumulation across the whole interval is the difference of that antiderivative's values. The two parts of the theorem are this single observation read in opposite directions.

## The Fundamental Theorem, Part One: Differentiating an Integral

Read forward, the first part of the theorem says that the accumulation function A(x), defined as the integral from a constant a to a variable x of f(t) dt, has derivative A prime of x equal to f(x). The derivative of the accumulated area is the height of the curve at the moving edge, exactly the slice argument from the previous section made precise. The dummy variable t inside the integral exists so that the limit x stays free, and confusing the two letters is a notational error the exam quietly punishes.

Chain rule variations supply the standard twists. When the upper limit is a function of x rather than x itself, the derivative picks up the derivative of that limit as a factor. The derivative of the integral from 1 to x cubed of 1 over (1 plus t squared) dt is 3x squared divided by (1 plus x to the sixth), because the integrand evaluated at x cubed gives 1 over (1 plus x to the sixth) and the chain rule multiplies by the derivative of x cubed, namely 3x squared. When the variable sits in the lower limit instead, a sign flip joins the computation, since swapping limits negates an integral: the derivative of the integral from 2x to 7 of a function of t dt equals negative 2 times the integrand evaluated at 2x. When both limits move, split the integral at any constant and apply both rules to the pieces.

Variable limits also hide inside accumulation questions that never say the word derivative. A part that asks where an accumulation function increases, decreases, or peaks is asking for the sign and zeros of its derivative, which Part One hands over as the integrand itself. The whole analysis of accumulation graphs in the next section runs on that single identification: A prime equals f, A double prime equals f prime, and the graph of f becomes a complete weather report for the graph of A.

### What changes when the upper limit of an integral is x cubed instead of x?

The derivative of the accumulation gains a chain rule factor. Part One of the theorem evaluates the integrand at the upper limit, and then the answer is multiplied by the derivative of that limit, 3x squared, because the boundary now moves three x squared times as fast as x itself moves along the axis.


## Accumulation Functions and Their Graphs

An accumulation function packages an integral with a moving right edge: A(x) is the integral from a fixed start a to the variable endpoint x of f(t) dt. Values of A answer "how much so far" questions. If a problem supplies an initial amount, the total amount at position x is the initial amount plus A(x), exactly as the tank walkthrough added 40 starting gallons to the estimated inflow. Forgetting the initial amount is among the most common lost points in the whole unit, and circling it in the problem statement is a cheap defense.

Graphs turn accumulation into area bookkeeping. Consider a function f drawn as a line segment from (0, 2) descending to (4, negative 2), then running flat at height negative 2 from x = 4 to x = 6. Let A(x) be the integral of f from 0 to x. On the opening stretch the line crosses the axis at x = 2, carving a triangle above the axis with base 2 and height 2, so A(2) equals 2. From x = 2 to x = 4 the mirror triangle sits below the axis, contributing negative 2, so A(4) equals 0. The flat tail adds a rectangle of width 2 and height negative 2, contributing negative 4, so A(6) equals negative 4. Three geometric computations, no antiderivative anywhere, produce exact values of the accumulation at the marked points.

Behavior questions ride on Part One of the theorem. Since the derivative of A is f itself, A increases exactly where f sits above the axis and decreases where f dips below, so in the example A climbs on the interval from 0 to 2 and falls from 2 to 6, reaching its maximum at x = 2. Concavity comes from the derivative of f: where f decreases, A bends downward; where f increases, A bends upward; where f holds constant, A runs straight. In the example A is concave down across the whole descending stretch from 0 to 4 and linear along the flat tail. An inflection point of A appears wherever f changes direction, at a peak or valley of the rate graph.

Extremes of the quantity itself, rather than of A as a function, follow the same sign logic with a physical accent. A tank fed by a rate that turns negative is fullest at the moment the rate crosses zero heading down, because accumulation stops growing and starts shrinking there. Exam questions about the greatest amount of water, the highest population, or the moment a particle sits farthest from its start all reduce to locating that sign change of the integrand and justifying it with the derivative identification. The justification sentence matters: graders award the point for reasoning that names the sign change of f, not for the bare location.

Second derivative questions complete the set. Asking where A is concave up is asking where f increases; asking for A double prime at a point is asking for the slope of the graph of f there. Students who build a small translation table in their heads, A from area, A prime from height, A double prime from slope, stop mixing the levels, and graph-based free-response questions become transcription exercises rather than puzzles.

## Signed Area, Displacement, and Total Distance

Integrals add with signs, and motion problems make the stakes concrete. A particle moving with velocity v(t) = t squared minus 4 on the interval from 0 to 3 travels backward while the velocity is negative, from t = 0 to t = 2, and forward afterward. Its displacement is the integral from 0 to 3 of (t squared minus 4) dt, which the evaluation theorem computes through the antiderivative t cubed over 3 minus 4t: at 3 the value is 9 minus 12, at 0 it is 0, so the displacement is negative 3. The particle ends three units behind its starting point, a statement about endpoints only.

Total distance demands the unsigned tally. Split the integral where the velocity changes sign, at t = 2, and add the absolute values of the pieces. On the first piece the integral of (t squared minus 4) from 0 to 2 equals 8/3 minus 8, which is negative 16/3, contributing 16/3 of distance. On the second piece, from 2 to 3, the same antiderivative gives negative 3 minus negative 16/3, which is 7/3. The total distance is 16/3 plus 7/3, equal to 23/3, about 7.67 units. Note the arithmetic relationship: displacement is the difference of the two magnitudes, 7/3 minus 16/3 equals negative 3, while distance is their sum, and that relationship is a reliable check on both computations.

The same signed logic governs every rate context. Net change of a population is births minus deaths accumulated with signs; a bank balance integral nets deposits against withdrawals; a tank integral nets inflow against outflow when the rate records the difference. Questions that ask for a total amount of something consumed, produced, or traveled almost always want the unsigned version, computed by splitting at sign changes, while questions that ask for the amount at a later time want the signed integral added to the initial amount. Reading the question's noun, change versus total, settles which machine to run.

Area between the curve and the axis obeys the same convention. The integral from a to b of f gives the signed area, regions above the axis counted positive and regions below counted negative. A question asking for actual geometric area wants the integral of the absolute value, split wherever the graph crosses the axis. Symmetric graphs offer relief: an odd function integrated across a symmetric interval gives zero, and spotting that symmetry converts a page of work into a sentence of justification.

## Average Value: The Height of the Equivalent Rectangle

Average value answers a flattening question: what constant height, spread over the same interval, would accumulate the same total? Average value is defined for f on the interval from a to b as 1 over (b minus a), multiplied by the integral of f(x) across the whole interval. Geometrically the formula picks the height of a rectangle over the interval whose area equals the signed area under the curve, so some rectangle computations double as average value computations when the area is known by geometry.

Two worked values fix the pattern. For x squared on the interval from 0 to 3, the average value is one third of the integral, and the integral is 27 over 3, which is 9, so the average value is 3. Somewhere in the interval the function must actually attain that average, by the mean value theorem for integrals, and here x squared equals 3 at x equal to the square root of 3, a point safely inside the interval. For sin x on the interval from 0 to pi, the computation gives 1 over pi times the integral 2, hence 2 over pi, about 0.637, a sensible flattening of an arch that peaks at 1.

Average rate contexts give the formula its exam weight. The average value of a rate over a time interval is the total accumulation divided by the elapsed time, which is how the tank walkthrough produced an average inflow near 6.79 gallons per minute from the trapezoidal total. Parts that ask for average velocity, average temperature, or average population all reduce to the same division, and the integral in the numerator is frequently one the problem already asked the student to estimate in an earlier part. Recognizing the recycling saves both time and arithmetic.

### When does an accumulation function reach its maximum?

An accumulation function A(x) reaches an interior maximum where its integrand f changes sign from positive to negative, because Part One of the Fundamental Theorem identifies f as the derivative of A. Before the crossing, positive area is still being added; after it, area is subtracted, so the accumulated total peaks exactly at the sign change.


## The Net Change Theorem and the Discipline of Units

The net change theorem is the evaluation part of the Fundamental Theorem wearing a context: the integral of a rate of change over an interval equals the net change of the quantity over that interval. If F prime records the rate at which a quantity F changes, then the integral of F prime from a to b equals F(b) minus F(a). Every accumulation problem in this guide is an instance, and naming the theorem in a justification tells the grader the student knows why an integral belongs in the solution at all.

Units police the whole family of problems. A rate carries units of quantity per unit of input: gallons per minute, meters per second, dollars per unit produced. Multiplying by the input variable's units, minutes or seconds, cancels the denominator and leaves pure quantity, which is why an integral of a rate yields an amount. An answer stated in gallons per minute for a question asking how much water entered is dimensionally wrong even when the number came out right, and free-response rubrics carry a dedicated unit point on many problems. Average value restores the rate's units, since dividing the accumulated quantity by the interval length rebuilds quantity per unit, a consistency check worth running on every average value answer.

Marginal analysis in economics contexts follows the same grammar. Marginal cost, in dollars per item, integrates to the change in total cost across a production run, and adding the fixed cost produces the total cost figure. The initial value step is the fixed cost there, just as it was the starting gallons in the tank. Population models, drug concentration problems, and leaking-container problems all repeat the skeleton: rate given, integrate for change, add the start, report with units.

## Properties of Definite Integrals Worth Having on Command

A handful of algebraic properties convert awkward integrals into easy ones, and the exam tests each of them directly. Integrating from a point to itself gives zero, since no width means no accumulation. Swapping the limits negates the value, which is the property behind the lower-limit derivative rule. An integral across adjacent intervals splits at the shared point, so an integral from a to c plus its companion from c to b equals the full integral across the span, for any middle point c, even one outside the original interval. Constants pull out front, and sums and differences of functions integrate term by term.

Comparison properties support estimation questions. If one function stays above another across an interval, its integral is at least as large, and if a function is trapped between a minimum m and a maximum M on an interval of length (b minus a), its integral is trapped between m times (b minus a) and M times (b minus a). Those bounds turn a rough graph reading into a defensible inequality, the same spirit as the bracketing that endpoint sums provided earlier. Together with the overestimate and underestimate rules, they form the estimation half of the topic; the evaluation half rests on antiderivatives, and the differentiation half on Part One.

## Midpoint Sums in Depth: the Quiet Overachiever

The midpoint rule samples each strip at its center, and a geometric coincidence explains its strength. Draw the tangent line to the curve at the midpoint of a subinterval and build the rectangle whose height is the tangent's value there: on a curve of steady concavity, the tangent rectangle trades area with the true region almost perfectly, gaining on one side of the center roughly what it surrenders on the other. The midpoint rectangle shares that height, so it inherits the cancellation, and its error on smooth curves lands near half the trapezoidal error with the opposite sign. Graders rarely ask students to prove any of this, but MCQ writers love asking which estimate sits closest to the truth, and the midpoint's quiet advantage is the intended insight.

Numbers from the running example make the relationship tangible. With eight subintervals on x squared from 0 to 2, the midpoints are 0.125, 0.375, 0.625, 0.875, 1.125, 1.375, 1.625, and 1.875, their squares add to 10.625, and the midpoint sum is 0.25 times that total, or 2.65625. Against the true value 8/3, the error is about 0.0104, almost exactly half the trapezoidal error of 0.0208 at the same resolution and on the opposite side, just as the concavity rules predict: the curve bends upward, so the midpoint sum underestimates while the trapezoid overestimates. Averaging those two estimates would land within about 0.005 of the truth, a bonus insight for estimation questions that present both.

Doubling behavior completes the picture. Each method's error shrinks as the partition refines, but at different rates: endpoint sums gain roughly a factor of two in accuracy per doubling, while midpoint and trapezoidal sums gain roughly a factor of four. A question that shows a trapezoidal estimate with four subintervals and another with eight and asks how the second compares is testing that factor of four. A question that asks which single computation with a stated n deserves the most trust is usually pointing at the midpoint sum for a smooth, single-concavity curve.

## A Second Curve: Decreasing, Bending Upward

Variety in examples prevents rule memorization from hardening into superstition, so run the full battery on f(x) = 1 over x on the interval from 1 to 3 with four subintervals. The width is 0.5, the cut points are 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3, and the heights are 1, about 0.667, 0.5, 0.4, and about 0.333. The function decreases throughout, so the left sum should overestimate and the right sum underestimate. The left sum multiplies the height total 1 plus 0.667 plus 0.5 plus 0.4, which is 2.567, by the width 0.5, giving about 1.283. The right sum multiplies its height total 0.667 plus 0.5 plus 0.4 plus 0.333, which is 1.9, by the same width, giving about 0.95. Bracket established: the integral sits between 0.95 and 1.283 before any finer method is tried.

The finer methods respect the concavity verdicts. The graph of 1 over x bends upward everywhere on this interval, so the trapezoidal rule should overestimate and the midpoint rule underestimate. The trapezoidal estimate averages the endpoint sums: half of 1.283 plus 0.95 is about 1.117. The midpoint samples are 1.25, 1.75, 2.25, and 2.75, with heights 0.8, about 0.571, about 0.444, and about 0.364, summing to about 2.180, so the midpoint estimate is 0.5 times that, about 1.090. The exact value, delivered later among the evaluations, is the natural logarithm of 3, about 1.0986, and it obeys every prediction: inside the endpoint bracket, below the trapezoidal guess, above the midpoint guess. Four methods, four verdicts, one curve, and the pattern of agreement is the real lesson: shape knowledge converts arithmetic into knowledge about the arithmetic.

Estimates from data inherit the same logic with weaker guarantees. A table of rates that falls steadily supports the left-over, right-under reading for the sum over that data, and a table that rises and falls supports no global claim at all. The exam's favorite follow-up asks students to identify a subinterval where a claim holds even when the whole interval refuses one, rewarding those who read shape locally instead of globally.

## A Velocity Table, Worked Four Ways

Tables of motion data are the most common approximation setting on the exam, and one clean pass builds the reflexes. A cyclist's velocity, in feet per second, is recorded every three seconds: at times 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 the readings are 10, 14, 18, 15, and 11. The width of each strip is 3 seconds. The left sum uses the first four readings, whose heights total 10 plus 14 plus 18 plus 15, equal to 57, and 3 times 57 gives 171 feet. The right sum uses the last four readings, totaling 14 plus 18 plus 15 plus 11, equal to 58, and 3 times 58 gives 174 feet. The trapezoidal estimate averages them: 172.5 feet. A midpoint sum cannot be formed from this table at all, since no readings exist at the strip centers, and naming that impossibility is sometimes exactly what a question asks.

Interpretation sharpens the exercise. The cyclist sped up and then slowed down, so neither endpoint sum bounds the distance across the whole ride; the left sum underestimates on the speeding stretch and overestimates on the slowing stretch. The trapezoidal figure of 172.5 feet remains a reasonable estimate, reported with the word "approximately" and the unit feet, but no inequality claim attaches to it without more shape information. Notice also how close the two endpoint sums sit to each other: when left and right sums nearly agree, every reasonable estimate in between inherits that confidence, and the exam's answer choices tend to cluster around the shared neighborhood.

## More Evaluations: Exponentials, Reciprocals, Absolute Values

The evaluation theorem rewards a stocked antiderivative shelf, and a few entries do outsized work. Exponentials evaluate just as cleanly: from 0 to 1, e to the x integrates to e minus 1, since e to the x is its own antiderivative and e to the 0 is 1. Reciprocals answer with logarithms: from 1 to 3, 1 over x integrates to the natural logarithm of 3 minus the natural logarithm of 1, which is ln 3, the exact truth behind the 1.0986 the approximations kept circling. From 0 to pi over 3, secant squared x integrates to tan evaluated across the interval, giving the square root of 3, because the derivative of tangent is secant squared. Each of these is a one-line evaluation once the antiderivative is recognized, and recognition speed is a trainable asset.

Algebra sometimes has to precede antidifferentiation. The integral from 1 to 2 of (x cubed minus 4x) over x dx simplifies first, dividing term by term to get x squared minus 4, and then evaluates through x cubed over 3 minus 4x: at 2 that is 8/3 minus 8, at 1 it is 1/3 minus 4, and the difference is (8/3 minus 8) minus (1/3 minus 4), which equals 7/3 minus 4, or negative 5/3. Presenting the simplification as its own line protects the work, because an antiderivative attempted on the undivided form invites errors that no partial credit can rescue.

Absolute value integrands demand a split at the sign change, the same move distance problems use. The integral from negative 1 to 2 of the absolute value of x dx breaks at 0: on the left piece the integrand is negative x, on the right piece it is x. The left piece contributes 1/2, the right piece contributes 2, and the total is 5/2, which the geometry confirms as two triangles of areas 1/2 and 2 sitting on the axis. Skipping the split and antidifferentiating blindly is the classic way to lose an otherwise easy point.

Solving an accumulation equation reverses the usual direction. Suppose a tank drains at the rate r(t) = 2t plus 1 gallons per minute and the question asks when 24 gallons have drained. The accumulated drainage by time T is the integral from 0 to T of (2t plus 1) dt, which equals T squared plus T. Setting T squared plus T equal to 24 gives T squared plus T minus 24 equals 0, and the quadratic formula yields T equal to (negative 1 plus the square root of 97) over 2, about 4.42 minutes; the negative root is discarded because time runs forward. Accumulation equations that resist algebra, usually because the rate comes from a graph or table, are solved numerically or graphically on the calculator sections, by finding where the computed accumulation crosses the target.

## Anatomy of a Free-Response Accumulation Problem

The flagship free-response question in this unit follows a durable four-part architecture, and rehearsing the architecture once, on a concrete reservoir, converts exam-day surprises into recognition. A reservoir holds 500 thousand gallons at time t = 0, and water flows in at the rate R(t) = 30 plus 4 sin(t over 2) thousand gallons per hour, for t between 0 and 8 hours. Water also leaves at a constant 20 thousand gallons per hour. The four classic parts ask, in some order, for the amount at a later time, the times when the amount is extremal, an average rate, and an interpretation of a derivative or integral expression.

The amount part is bookkeeping with two integrals. Inflow by time 8 is the integral from 0 to 8 of R(t) dt; an antiderivative of 30 plus 4 sin(t over 2) is 30t minus 8 cos(t over 2), since the derivative of cos(t over 2) carries a factor of one half that the 8 absorbs. Evaluated from 0 to 8, inflow equals (240 minus 8 cos 4) minus (0 minus 8), which is 248 minus 8 cos 4; with cos 4 about negative 0.654, inflow is about 253.2 thousand gallons. Outflow is a mere rectangle: 20 times 8 equals 160. The amount at t = 8 is therefore 500 plus 253.2 minus 160, about 593.2 thousand gallons. The structure, start plus in minus out, matters more than the arithmetic, and writing that structure in words before computing earns the setup point even if a keystroke fails later.

The extremum part runs on the net rate. The amount changes at the rate R(t) minus 20, which is 10 plus 4 sin(t over 2), and since sin never falls below negative 1, that net rate never falls below 6, so the amount increases throughout the eight hours and its minimum sits at t = 0 with 500 thousand gallons and its maximum at t = 8. A variant in which the outflow exceeds the inflow partway would instead ask for the crossing time where R(t) equals the outflow rate, found numerically, and the fullest moment would be that crossing. The justification template is identical either way: the amount's derivative is the net rate, its sign decides monotonicity, and its zeros locate the candidates.

The average rate part divides. The average inflow rate across the eight hours is the inflow integral divided by 8, about 253.2 over 8, about 31.65 thousand gallons per hour. The interpretation part might hand over the expression for the derivative of the amount at t = 3 and ask its meaning and units: the net rate of change of the water volume at that instant, in thousand gallons per hour. Each part reuses the same model with a different lens, which is why the strongest preparation drills whole questions rather than isolated skills. Timed rehearsal of full prompts, with attention to the phrasing graders expect, is the focus of the companion guide to [Calculus AB free-response strategy](/2008/08/01/ap-calculus-ab-frq-strategy/).

Differential equations grow directly out of this soil. A problem that states a rate of change as an expression involving the quantity itself, then asks for the quantity later, is an accumulation problem whose integrand depends on the answer, and the separation techniques for those problems build on the same start-plus-accumulated-change skeleton. Readers continuing forward will meet that machinery in the treatment of [AP Calculus AB differential equations](/2006/09/03/ap-calculus-ab-differential-equations/), where slope fields visualize exactly the accumulation this guide computes numerically and symbolically.

## Calculator Craft and No-Calculator Discipline

The exam splits into sections where a graphing calculator is allowed and sections where it is forbidden, and the topic divides its labor accordingly. Calculator sections host the ugly integrals: rates given by messy formulas, accumulation equations solved by intersection, definite integrals of functions with no elementary antiderivative. The numerical integration command evaluates a definite integral directly, and the craft lies in setup rather than keystrokes: enter the integrand exactly as printed, set the limits in the right order, and store intermediate values in memory rather than transcribing rounded decimals into later parts. Rounding discipline is scored: intermediate values should be carried unrounded, with final answers reported to three decimal places when a decimal is expected.

Graphing the integrand before integrating catches the errors keystrokes hide. A quick plot reveals sign changes that demand splitting, reveals whether an accumulation should come out positive or negative, and reveals scale, so a computed value wildly off the visible scale flags a data-entry mistake. When a part asks for the time at which an accumulation reaches a target, graphing the accumulation function, or the difference between the accumulation and the target, turns the solve into an intersection reading. When the rate is defined by a table, the calculator steps aside and the sums of the earlier sections take over, which is why both toolsets must stay warm.

No-calculator sections invert the aesthetic: every integral there is designed to yield to a familiar antiderivative or a geometric reading, and every limit of sums is designed to decode by the width-and-sample-point method. Answers belong in exact form, pi and roots and logarithms left symbolic, because a decimal approximation of ln 3 earns nothing where ln 3 was the point. Students who practice the no-calculator repertoire separately, mental antiderivatives, geometric area, symmetry spotting, build the pattern library that makes those sections feel short.

## The Error Catalogue: Where Points Actually Leak

Certain mistakes recur so reliably in this unit that naming them in advance functions as armor. The first is sampling drift: computing a right sum with left heights, or a midpoint sum with endpoints, because the plan was never stated. Writing the sample points in a row before any multiplication prevents it, and the row takes five seconds. The second is width blindness on uneven tables, where a student multiplies every height by the most common gap or by the total span divided by the count of readings, ignoring that five readings make four strips, not five. The number of strips is always one less than the number of table entries, and checking that count aloud is a free safeguard.

The third leak is the missing initial value: an accumulation computed flawlessly and then reported as the amount, when the problem gave a starting amount pages earlier in the prompt. The fourth is sign negligence, reporting a negative total distance, a negative volume, or a negative count of people because the integral came out negative and nobody asked whether the question wanted a net or a total. The fifth is the unit drop, an answer of "81.5 minutes" for gallons accumulated, which the walkthrough's phrasing habits are designed to prevent. The sixth is justification vacuum on extremum parts: a correct time with no sign argument earns the answer point and loses the reasoning point, and the reasoning point is usually the one the part was built to test.

Subtler leaks deserve mention too. Differentiating an accumulation and forgetting the chain rule factor when the limit is composite is a perennial. Evaluating an antiderivative at the lower limit and adding instead of subtracting appears in every grading cohort. Approximating when the part says "exact" and evaluating exactly when the rate exists only as data are mirror-image misreadings of the prompt's data type. And the trapezoidal shortcut of averaging endpoint sums tempts students to average a left sum from one table with a right sum from a different partition, an invalid mixture, since the identity only holds when both sums share the same strips.

## Reading an Integral Expression Like a Sentence

Exam prompts frequently hand over a finished integral expression and ask what it means, a skill distinct from computing anything. The expression the integral from 0 to 5 of r(t) dt, where r measures the rate at which water enters a tank in gallons per minute and t counts minutes, means the total gallons that entered during the first five minutes. Three ingredients build the interpretation: the accumulated quantity (gallons of water entering), the interval (the first five minutes), and the direction implied by the rate's definition (entering, not leaving). An interpretation that omits the interval or the direction is incomplete, and rubrics say so.

Slightly disguised expressions test whether the reading skill transfers. The quantity A(9) minus A(4), where A is an accumulation function for a rate, means the amount accumulated between times 4 and 9, not the amount at 9 and not the amount at 4. The expression 1 over 12 times the integral from 0 to 12 of a temperature function means the average temperature across twelve hours, the division by the interval length being the fingerprint of every average value statement. The expression for the integral of a velocity from 1 to 4 set equal to zero means the particle returned to its starting position at time 4, whatever distance it covered in between. Each reading is a small translation exercise, and fluent translation is half of free-response success in this unit.

Units complete every interpretation. If the rate runs in people per year and time runs in years, the integral counts people. If a marginal cost in dollars per item is integrated across items produced, the result is dollars. Interpretation parts carry unit points with unusual frequency, because the College Board treats unit sense as evidence that the calculus means something to the student. A durable habit: before computing, state silently what the answer will count and in what units, then check the finished number against that prediction. A mismatch is a smoke alarm for a setup error.

The same reading skill runs backward into modeling. A prompt describing a quantity that grows by an integral must be translated into an accumulation function with the right variable limit and the right starting value, and the translation choices, integrand, lower limit, initial amount, are the scored decisions. Students who practice narrating expressions in plain sentences find the modeling direction far easier, because both directions exercise one bilingual fluency between symbols and situations.

## Geometry as an Evaluation Method

Some definite integrals surrender instantly to area formulas, and the no-calculator section stocks them deliberately. From 0 to 2, the square root of (4 minus x squared) traces a quarter circle of radius 2, so its integral is pi, the quarter circle's area. Across the symmetric interval from negative 3 to 3, the square root of (9 minus x squared) traces the upper half of a circle of radius 3, and its integral is that semicircle's area, 9 pi over 2. An integrand built from line segments over a marked graph evaluates as a sum of triangle and trapezoid areas, with pieces below the axis subtracted. Recognizing a circle equation inside a square root is the trigger; the integrand y equals the square root of (r squared minus x squared) is always the top half of a circle centered at the origin.

Geometry also referees symbolic work. An antiderivative evaluation that contradicts the visible area, wrong sign, wrong order of magnitude, announces its own error. A student who sketches even a rough graph before computing gains that referee for free. Symmetry is geometry's shortcut wing: an even integrand over a symmetric interval doubles the half integral, an odd integrand over a symmetric interval vanishes, and a periodic integrand over a whole number of periods multiplies one period's accumulation. Each of these observations converts a computation into a recognition, and recognition is faster and safer under time pressure.

Piecewise-defined functions on the exam are geometry in disguise more often than not. A function defined by one formula on the left of a breakpoint and another on the right integrates by splitting at the breakpoint, and each piece usually evaluates to a triangle, a trapezoid, or a circular segment rather than to an antiderivative exercise. The split itself is the property of adjacent intervals from the properties section, applied with intent.

## Two Rates at Once: Net Accumulation and Catching Up

Many flagship problems run two rates against each other: inflow against outflow, production against consumption, births against deaths. The net accumulation is the integral of the difference of the rates, and the sign of that difference function decides growth or decline at every moment. A reservoir gaining water at R(t) and losing it at a constant 20 has a net rate R(t) minus 20; the amount function is the starting amount plus the integral of that net rate. Splitting the work into in minus out, as the anatomy section did, or integrating the difference directly, gives the same result, and the difference view makes extremum analysis cleaner because one sign chart governs everything.

Comparison questions between two accumulating quantities follow a parallel script. Two tanks start with different amounts and fill at different rates; the moment one catches the other is the moment their amount functions agree, which is the moment the integral of the rate difference closes the initial gap. Setting the accumulated difference equal to the initial difference and solving, numerically when the rates are complicated, is the whole method. Questions about which tank holds more at a stated time compare the same accumulated difference against the same initial gap, no equality solving required. The skeleton never changes: gap, accumulation of difference, comparison.

Rates defined by graphs make these problems visual. The accumulated difference between two curves over an interval is the signed area between them, and a crossing of the graphs flips which quantity is winning. A quantity that starts behind can end ahead if the area where its rate dominates outweighs the area where it trails, and area estimation by counting grid regions between the curves is a legitimate exam technique on the appropriate sections. This is also where the unit reaches forward to the area-between-curves work later in the course: integrating a difference of heights is exactly accumulating a gap.

## Differentiating Accumulations: a Drill Set in Prose

Fluency with Part One grows from varied reps, so work several shapes in a row. Start plain: if A(x) is the integral from 2 to x of (t cubed minus 5t) dt, then A prime of x is x cubed minus 5x, the integrand with t relabeled, and A prime of 2 is negative 2. Delay the evaluation habit: derivative first as a formula, values second. Next, the accumulation from x to 9 of a function of t has derivative equal to the negative of the integrand at x, from the limit swap. An accumulation from 4 to x squared of sin(t) dt differentiates to sin(x squared) times 2x, the chain rule factor being the derivative of x squared. An accumulation from x to x cubed of a function of t splits at a constant, say 1: the integral from x to 1 plus the integral from 1 to x cubed, differentiating to negative f(x) plus 3x squared times f(x cubed), two rule applications in one line.

Two traps guard this territory. The first is an integrand that itself contains x: the integral from 1 to 4 of (x t plus t squared) dt cannot be differentiated by Part One as it stands, because the theorem governs integrals over t of a function of t alone; here x is a parameter that factors out of the integration, giving x times the integral of t plus the integral of t squared, and the work proceeds by ordinary antidifferentiation with x treated as constant. The second trap is differentiating an accumulation whose value is requested instead: a part asking for A(5) wants area bookkeeping, and a part asking for A prime of 5 wants the height f(5), and swapping the two responses is a classic unforced error. Reading the prime symbol, or its absence, is a two-second check that rescues whole parts.

Estimation questions complete the drill family. Given the graph of f, A double prime at a point is the slope of that graph at the corresponding position, estimated by a tangent line's rise over run. Given a table of f, A prime at a table entry is simply the entry, while A prime between entries can only be estimated, perhaps by the trend of neighboring entries, and a careful answer says so. Precision about what data can and cannot deliver is itself scored behavior in this unit.

## Multiple-Choice Tactics for This Unit

Multiple-choice items in this territory fall into families, and each family has a fast lane. Approximation items that ask which value could equal an integral are bracketing items: compute the two endpoint sums, or recall which named estimate must lie above and which below, and eliminate every choice outside the fence. Estimation items with a graph reward counting and scaling over formula hunting. Items that show a limit of sums are decoding items, solved by the width-first read described earlier, and the choices typically differ in exactly the interval endpoints or the integrand, so careful decoding settles them without any limit computation.

Units eliminate choices in interpretation items: an option stated in gallons cannot answer a question about gallons per minute. Sign eliminates choices in accumulation items: an amount that only grows cannot be matched by a decreasing expression among the options. Reasonableness eliminates choices in table items: an accumulation estimate wildly larger than the largest rate times the whole interval length is impossible, since no accumulation can exceed the product of the maximum rate and the span. These screens take seconds and routinely cut five options to two before any real computation begins.

Time allocation deserves one rule: the computation-heavy approximation items and the one-line recognition items carry identical weight, so harvest recognition items first. A Part One differentiation with a composite limit is twenty seconds for a prepared student; a four-strip midpoint computation is two minutes. Banking the fast points early also builds the calm that the slower items demand. ## A Study Routine That Builds the Whole Skill Set

Preparation for this unit divides naturally into four drill families, rotated in short sessions rather than massed in one sitting. The first family is computation: endpoint, midpoint, and trapezoidal sums from tables and formulas, with deliberate inclusion of uneven tables, until the strip-count check and the sample-point row become automatic. The second family is evaluation: antiderivative fluency drills, geometric integrals, absolute value splits, and the simplification-first integrands, all done without a calculator to keep the no-calculator sections honest. The third family is translation: narrating given integral expressions in words with units, and modeling described situations as accumulation functions, trading direction each session. The fourth family is whole-question rehearsal: full free-response prompts under time, graded against the habit list of setup, arithmetic, interpretation, and justification.

An error log multiplies the value of every drill. Each mistake gets filed under its species, sampling drift, width blindness, missing initial value, sign negligence, unit drop, justification vacuum, and the log's tallies tell the student which armor to wear on exam day. Students are often surprised that their leaks concentrate in two species rather than spreading evenly; the concentration is good news, because two habits are fixable in a week. Reviewing the log before each practice set, and again on the morning of the exam, converts past errors into present points with unusual efficiency.

Spacing retrieval across days matters as much as volume. The overestimate rules, the Part One variants, and the average value formula should be recalled cold, from memory, at widening intervals, because exam conditions offer no notes and no warm-up. A final self-test worth taking: given any rate graph, produce an estimate with bounds, an exact value where geometry allows, the accumulation's increasing and decreasing intervals, its maximum location, and the average rate, all in under ten minutes. A student who can run that gauntlet owns the unit in the form the exam measures.

## A Third Curve: Increasing but Bending Downward

One more full battery, on f(x) equal to the square root of x across the interval from 0 to 4 with four subintervals of width 1, confirms that the verdict rules track shape rather than habit. The heights at the cut points 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 are 0, 1, about 1.414, about 1.732, and 2. The function increases, so the left sum underestimates and the right sum overestimates: the left sum is 0 plus 1 plus 1.414 plus 1.732, about 4.146, and the right sum is 1 plus 1.414 plus 1.732 plus 2, about 6.146. The curve bends downward throughout, so the trapezoidal rule should underestimate and the midpoint rule overestimate, completing the prediction grid before any of their arithmetic runs.

The computations honor the grid. The midpoint samples are 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, and 3.5, with heights about 0.707, 1.225, 1.581, and 1.871, summing to about 5.384, so the midpoint estimate is about 5.384. The trapezoidal estimate averages the endpoint sums: half of 4.146 plus 6.146 is about 5.146. The exact integral, through the antiderivative (2/3) x to the three-halves, is (2/3) times 8, or 16/3, about 5.333. The ordering comes out exactly as the shape rules promised: left 4.146 at the bottom, trapezoidal 5.146 above it, the truth 5.333 next, midpoint 5.384 just over the line, and right 6.146 at the top. Three curves and three orderings now stand in the guide, each dictated by its monotonicity and concavity pairing, and a student who can generate the ordering before computing has converted this topic from arithmetic into reading.

Notice also how the bracket quality differs across examples. On x squared the endpoint sums bracketed the truth within a window of width 2; on 1 over x the window was about 0.33; here the window is 2 again, driven by the steep opening stretch where the square root shoots upward with a vertical tangent. Steep stretches widen endpoint disagreement, and the exam's choice of functions for estimation items tends to feature one dramatic stretch precisely so that the verdict reasoning, not the raw totals, does the discriminating.

## Accumulation as an Antiderivative Machine: Initial Value Problems

Every antiderivative can be manufactured by an accumulation function, and the initial value problem is the manufacturing process stated as a question. Given the derivative of a quantity and the quantity's value at one point, the quantity everywhere else is that known value plus the accumulated change since: F(x) equals F(a) plus the integral from a to x of the rate. The formula is the net change theorem solved for the final amount, and it explains why the arbitrary constant in antidifferentiation is pinned down by any single known value.

A clean instance: a function y satisfies dy over dx equal to 3x squared minus 2, with y equal to 5 when x is 1. The accumulation form gives y(x) equal to 5 plus the integral from 1 to x of (3t squared minus 2) dt. Evaluating the integral through the antiderivative t cubed minus 2t gives (x cubed minus 2x) minus (1 minus 2), which is x cubed minus 2x plus 1, so y(x) equals x cubed minus 2x plus 6. Checking the initial condition, y at 1 is 1 minus 2 plus 6, which is 5, as required. The standard route, antidifferentiate and solve for the constant, produces the same function, but the accumulation route never loses track of why the constant is 6: it is the starting value adjusted by the reference point of the integral.

This framing clarifies a persistent confusion about the two parts of the theorem. Part Two evaluates a completed accumulation using any convenient antiderivative. Part One reveals that accumulation itself constructs an antiderivative, indeed the specific antiderivative that starts at zero at the lower limit. A course could, in principle, define the natural logarithm as the accumulation of 1 over t from 1 to x, and some developments do exactly that, because the integral definition guarantees the derivative property that makes logarithms work. The exam does not demand that depth, yet students who glimpse it stop treating the two parts as unrelated twins.

## A Context Gallery: Costs, Temperatures, Populations

Marginal cost supplies the economics dialect of accumulation. A factory's marginal cost, the cost of producing one more unit at output x, is modeled in dollars per unit by MC(x) equal to 0.003 x squared minus 0.2 x plus 40. The variable cost of raising production from 0 to 100 units is the integral of marginal cost across that range: an antiderivative is 0.001 x cubed minus 0.1 x squared plus 40x, which at 100 gives 1000 minus 1000 plus 4000, a total of 4000 dollars. Adding a fixed cost of, say, 12,000 dollars yields the total cost of the run, 16,000 dollars, the initial value step again wearing a business suit. Notice how the cubic and quadratic terms cancel at this output, a reminder that accumulation totals depend on the whole path of the rate, not on its endpoint value.

Temperature supplies the periodic dialect. Suppose the temperature over a day follows T(t) equal to 70 plus 10 sin(pi t over 12) degrees, with t in hours from midnight. The average temperature across the full day is 1 over 24 times the integral from 0 to 24 of T(t) dt. The sine term integrates across exactly one full period, since its cycle spans the whole 24 hours, and its total accumulation is zero: peaks and troughs cancel in the signed accounting. The integral therefore equals 70 times 24, and the average is exactly 70 degrees, matching intuition about a symmetric daily cycle. Over a partial day the cancellation fails and the integral must actually be computed, which is the version the exam prefers.

Population models supply the two-rate dialect in its purest form. A town gains residents through births and arrivals recorded by one rate and loses them through deaths and departures recorded by another; the population at a future year is the census figure plus the integral of the difference of the rates. When the problem supplies the rates as graphs on the same axes, the net accumulation is the area between the curves with sign, and the census figure is again the initial value that careless solutions drop. Across all three dialects the skeleton is identical, and fluency is nothing more than recognizing the skeleton through the costume.

## Squeezing More from Two Estimates: an Extrapolation

Two trapezoidal estimates at different resolutions can be combined into a sharper one, a trick worth knowing even though the course never requires it. On x squared from 0 to 2, the four-strip trapezoidal estimate was 2.75 and the eight-strip estimate was 2.6875. Because the trapezoidal error on a quadratic shrinks by exactly a factor of four when the width halves, the eight-strip error is one quarter of the four-strip error, and the combination (4 times the finer estimate minus the coarser estimate) divided by 3 cancels the shared error pattern entirely: 4 times 2.6875 is 10.75, minus 2.75 is 8, divided by 3 is 8/3, the exact integral. The maneuver is called Richardson extrapolation, and on general smooth curves it improves accuracy dramatically even when it cannot deliver exactness.

The deeper point of the trick is what it reveals about error structure. Approximation error in this unit is not fog; it has shape, sign, and scaling laws, all readable from monotonicity, concavity, and refinement behavior. A student who internalizes that stance reads an estimate the way an engineer reads a gauge, with a built-in sense of which side of the truth it sits on and how far it might stray. That stance, more than any single formula, is the durable asset this topic deposits.

## Partitions in Full Generality

The equal-width case dominates the course, yet the definition tolerates far more. A general partition places cut points at arbitrary positions, each subinterval carrying its own width, and the quality of the partition is measured by its norm, the width of its widest subinterval. A Riemann sum over such a partition samples one point per subinterval, anywhere inside it, and the definite integral is the number those sums approach as the norm shrinks to zero, regardless of where the cuts fall or where the samples sit. That indifference is what licenses every practical scheme: samples can chase convenience, at endpoints, centers, table entries, because the limit forgives all of it.

Monotone functions admit two celebrity sums inside this generality. On an increasing function, sampling every subinterval at its left endpoint produces the smallest possible rectangles that fit under the curve, the inscribed or lower sum, while right-endpoint sampling produces the circumscribed or upper sum that covers the region with the least excess. The true area is trapped between any lower and upper sum, and refining the partition squeezes the trap from both sides. The endpoint sums from the earliest examples were exactly these bounds in disguise, which is why their bracketing behavior felt so reliable.

Non-monotone functions lose the tidy trapping, since a single subinterval can hide a peak or a valley that no endpoint sample reports honestly. The midpoint sample guards against some of that hiding by sampling the interior, one reason the midpoint rule behaves well on wavy curves. Exam graphs with a hump inside a wide strip test precisely this awareness: a left sum across a strip containing the hump can miss the hump entirely, and the defense is either a finer partition or a sample rule that visits the interior.

## Structural Moves: Solving for Integrals Without Integrating

A family of exam items provides a few integral values and asks for another, testing the algebra of the properties section rather than antidifferentiation. Given that the integral of f from 0 to 5 equals 7 and the integral of f from 0 to 3 equals 2, the integral from 3 to 5 is 7 minus 2, which is 5, by splitting at 3. Given additionally that the integral of a second function g from 0 to 5 equals negative 4, the integral of (2f minus 3g) from 0 to 5 is 2 times 7 minus 3 times negative 4, which is 14 plus 12, equal to 26, by pulling constants out and splitting the sum. The moves are the whole solution; reaching for an antiderivative here is a category error, since no formula for f exists anywhere in the problem.

Swapped limits and symmetry extend the family. If the integral of f from 2 to 6 equals 10, then the integral from 6 to 2 equals negative 10, and the integral from 2 to 2 equals 0. If f is even and the integral from 0 to 3 equals 6, the integral from negative 3 to 3 equals 12. If f is odd and the integral from 0 to 3 equals 6, the full symmetric integral equals 0, and the integral from negative 3 to 0 equals negative 6. Each answer is one property deep, and the items are gifts to students who keep the property list warm.

Comparison items close the family. If a function h satisfies 2 less than or equal to h(x), itself less than or equal to 5, across the interval from 1 to 4, then its integral over that interval lies between 2 times 3 and 5 times 3, that is, between 6 and 15. Any answer choice outside that fence is impossible, and combining the fence with a midpoint estimate often isolates the answer completely. Structural fluency of this kind is cheap to build and pays across both exam sections.

## A Second Graph, Read Completely

Graphs built from a triangle and a semicircle give the accumulation machinery a second full workout. Define f by a tent and a dip: the graph rises in a straight line from (0, 0) to the peak (2, 2), descends in a straight line to (4, 0), and then traces the lower half of a circle of radius 1 centered at (5, 0), dipping to (5, negative 1) before returning to (6, 0). Let A(x) be the integral of f from 0 to x, and interrogate A the way an exam would.

Values first. A(2) is the area of the opening triangle, base 2 and height 2, which is 2. A(4) adds the descending triangle, another 2, for a total of 4. A(6) subtracts the semicircular dip, whose area is pi times 1 squared over 2, that is pi over 2, so A(6) equals 4 minus pi over 2, about 2.43. The accumulation at integer waypoints is exact, and each value came from geometry rather than from any formula for f, which is the entire point of such a graph.

Behavior next, all of it read off f through Part One. Since f is positive on the stretch from 0 to 4, A increases there; since f is negative from 4 to 6, A decreases there; the maximum of A on the whole interval sits at x = 4, where the integrand changes sign from positive to negative, with value 4. Concavity follows the slope of f: A is concave up from 0 to 2 where f climbs, concave down from 2 to 5 where f descends first along the tent and then along the first half of the dip, and concave up again from 5 to 6 where the dip recovers. Inflection points of A appear at x = 2 and x = 5, exactly where f reverses direction.

Derivative values finish the interrogation. A prime at 3 is f at 3, which is 1, read off the descending tent line halfway down. A double prime at 3 is the slope of f there, which is negative 1, the slope of the segment from (2, 2) to (4, 0). A prime at 5 is f at 5, which is negative 1, the bottom of the dip. A student fluent in the translation table, A from area, A prime from height, A double prime from slope, answers all of these by inspection, and that fluency is the single highest-yield skill in the unit's graph work.

## Justification Language: Sentences That Earn the Point

Free-response rubrics in this unit award reasoning points for sentences of recognizable shapes, and stocking those shapes in advance is legitimate preparation. For an extremum: "The amount is greatest at t = 4 because the net rate changes from positive to negative there, so accumulation stops growing and begins to fall." For an increasing claim: "Since R(t) minus 20 is positive throughout the interval, the amount is increasing on the whole interval." For an approximation verdict: "The trapezoidal estimate overestimates the integral because the graph of the rate is concave down on the interval, so each trapezoid covers extra area."

For an interpretation: "The integral from 0 to 5 of r(t) dt is the total number of gallons that entered the tank during the first five minutes." For an average value: "Dividing the accumulated change by the length of the interval gives the average rate of change of the amount over that period." Each model sentence pairs the claim with its mechanism and its units, which is the anatomy graders reward. Students who draft these sentences during practice, rather than improvising them under pressure, write solutions that read as arguments instead of calculations, and the score difference follows.

## A Short History of the Ideas

The slicing idea is ancient. Greek geometers estimated areas by exhausting them with polygons, and Archimedes pinned down the area of a parabolic segment by summing a geometric series of triangles, a Riemann sum in spirit two millennia before Riemann. The seventeenth century turned the trick into a system: Newton and Leibniz, working independently, recognized that the accumulation problem and the tangent problem undo each other, which is the Fundamental Theorem in embryo, and their notation war bequeathed the integral sign from Leibniz and much of the vocabulary from Newton. Bernhard Riemann, in the nineteenth century, finally gave the sum definition rigorous form, specifying exactly which functions possess an integral and proving that the sampling choices do not matter. The theorem the exam treats as a tool was, for two thousand years, a frontier.

Knowing the lineage clarifies the course's design choices. The curriculum defines the integral through sums before evaluating through antiderivatives because the definition came first historically and carries the meaning; evaluation is the shortcut discovered later. Students who sense that order stop asking why both machines exist: sums say what an integral is, the theorem says how to get one quickly, and Part One says why the shortcut had to exist. History rarely appears on the exam, but the understanding it organizes appears on every page of it.

## Simpson's Rule and a Glance Beyond the Course

One classical scheme outperforms everything in this guide at equal effort, and enrichment reading often meets it. Simpson's rule fits parabolas instead of lines across pairs of strips, producing a weighted sum with coefficients 1, 4, 2, 4, and so on, scaled by delta x over 3, and it requires an even number of subintervals. On the x squared example with four strips, Simpson's rule gives (0.5 over 3) times the quantity 0 plus 4 times 0.25 plus 2 times 1 plus 4 times 2.25 plus 4, which is (1/6) times the quantity 0 plus 1 plus 2 plus 9 plus 4, equal to 16/6, or 8/3: the exact integral, because Simpson's rule integrates every quadratic, and indeed every cubic, without error. The AP course does not test Simpson's rule, yet meeting it once explains the family resemblance among all the schemes: better local fits, better global accuracy.

Calculus BC extends this territory in directions worth previewing. Improper integrals stretch accumulation across infinite intervals and ask whether the total converges. Euler methods and logistic models extend the accumulation story into differential equations with numerical and qualitative tools. Series in BC are, at heart, accumulation with discrete steps, and the integral test for convergence compares a series to an integral precisely because sums and integrals are siblings. The student who owns the AB accumulation picture carries most of the intuition those topics require.

## Piecewise Rates and Split Integrals

Rates that change their formula mid-stream force the split property into service, and one worked specimen covers most exam variants. A pump delivers at the rate r(t) equal to t gallons per minute for the first two minutes, then at the rate 4 minus t for the next two minutes, tapering as the tank fills. Total delivery is the integral from 0 to 4, split at the breakpoint: the integral from 0 to 2 of t dt plus the integral from 2 to 4 of (4 minus t) dt. The first piece, through the antiderivative t squared over 2, equals 2. The second piece, through 4t minus t squared over 2, evaluates to (16 minus 8) minus (8 minus 2), which is 8 minus 6, equal to 2. The total is 4 gallons, confirmed instantly by geometry: the rate graph is a triangle of base 4 and height 2, area 4. Formula and picture agreeing is the signature of a correct split.

Piecewise accumulation functions arise when a later part asks for the amount at a time inside the second piece: the accumulated total up to the breakpoint becomes the initial value for the next piece, and the running amount is built by adding the second integral to that subtotal. Students who build the subtotal explicitly, labeling it as the amount at the breakpoint, rarely tangle the pieces. The same architecture governs tax-bracket style cost problems, tiered pricing, and any rate table whose regime changes, making the split one of the most transferable moves in the unit.

## Five Multiple-Choice Archetypes, Decoded

Beyond tactics, the recurring item designs themselves can be memorized. The first archetype shows a function's graph with a shaded partition and asks for the sum type that a drawn set of rectangles represents; the answer turns on whether the rectangles touch the curve at left edges, right edges, centers, or corners above and below. The second archetype gives a small table and a named sum, and the distractors are precisely the other sums of the same table, so computing the named one carefully, and only it, is the discipline. The third archetype presents a limit of sums with the terms scrambled just enough that the width factor masquerades as part of the integrand; the width-first read defeats it.

The fourth archetype states an accumulation function built on an unfamiliar graph and asks for a derivative value, an increasing interval, or a maximum location; every variant is the translation table applied once. The fifth archetype states two or three integral values and asks for a combination, the structural family, and its distractors correspond to sign slips and split errors, so slow, property-by-property work beats speed. Recognizing the archetype within five seconds of reading an item tells the student which tool to lift, and that recognition speed is what timed practice actually trains.

## Fences from Data: Strip-Wise Bounds

A point estimate invites the question of how wrong it might be, and tables support a disciplined answer. Treat each strip separately and bound the rate on that strip by the smaller and larger of its recorded endpoint values, an assumption that holds whenever the rate moves monotonically between readings. On the walkthrough table, the strip minima are 4, 5, 5, 7, and 7, and the strip maxima are 6, 6, 8, 8, and 9. The lower fence multiplies minima by widths: 1 times 4, plus 2 times 5, plus 3 times 5, plus 4 times 7, plus 2 times 7, giving 4 plus 10 plus 15 plus 28 plus 14, which is 71 gallons. The upper fence gives 1 times 6, plus 2 times 6, plus 3 times 8, plus 4 times 8, plus 2 times 9, totaling 6 plus 12 plus 24 plus 32 plus 18, which is 92 gallons. The trapezoidal estimate of 81.5 sits comfortably inside the fence from 71 to 92, and the fence width of 21 gallons honestly quantifies what the sparse readings leave unknown.

A cruder fence uses the global extremes: a rate trapped between 4 and 9 gallons per minute across 12 minutes accumulates between 48 and 108 gallons, true but nearly useless. Refinement is the whole game: narrower strips and strip-wise extremes tighten fences, which is the data version of refining a partition. Exam items occasionally ask for the best possible bounds from a table, and the strip-wise construction is the intended method, with the monotonic-between-readings caveat worth stating in the justification. Where the rate is known to be increasing across the whole table, the endpoint sums themselves are the fences, and the strip-wise method collapses to the left and right sums, as it should.

## Average Value in Depth

The mean value theorem for integrals guarantees that a continuous function attains its average value somewhere inside the interval, and locating that point is a standard task. For x squared on the interval from 0 to 3, the average value was 3, attained where x squared equals 3, at the square root of 3. For sin x on the interval from 0 to pi, the average value is 2 over pi, about 0.637, attained where sin x equals 2 over pi, which happens twice inside the interval, near x equal to 0.69 and again near x equal to pi minus 0.69, about 2.45, by the symmetry of the arch. Non-uniqueness is normal and worth expecting rather than fearing.

Distinguish average value from its cousins. The average rate of change of a quantity over an interval is the difference of endpoint values divided by the interval length, a slope; the average value of the quantity's derivative over the same interval, by the net change theorem, equals exactly that slope, which is why the two notions blur in students' minds. The average value of the quantity itself is a different animal entirely, computed by integrating the quantity rather than its rate. Keeping the integrand straight, rate versus amount, resolves most confusion in average value items: read what the problem hands over, integrate that, divide by the span.

Averages of rates answer "typical intensity" questions: average current over a cycle, average rainfall rate during a storm, average traffic flow during a rush. Averages of amounts answer "typical level" questions: average water height in a tank over a day, average balance across a billing period. The formula never changes; the integrand's identity does, and units expose it every time, since the average keeps the integrand's units.

## Modeling from a Cold Start

Accumulation modeling reduces to four decisions, taken in order. Name the quantity and its units. Identify the rate and check that its units are the quantity per unit of the input variable. Fix the starting value and the moment it describes. Then write the amount at a general time as the starting value plus the integral of the rate from the start time to that time. A scenario worked cold shows the decisions interlocking. A pipe begins leaking at a rate that grows as the crack widens: L(t) equal to 5 plus 2t liters per hour, with t hours since the leak was noticed. The quantity is leaked volume in liters; the rate's units check out; the starting volume leaked at time zero is zero; the volume by hour h is the integral from 0 to h of (5 plus 2t) dt, which is 5h plus h squared.

Questions then answer themselves. By hour 6 the leak has lost 30 plus 36, which is 66 liters. The hour at which the cumulative loss reaches 100 liters solves h squared plus 5h equal to 100, and the quadratic formula gives (negative 5 plus the square root of 425) over 2, about 7.81 hours, the positive root only. The instantaneous severity at hour 6 is the rate itself, 17 liters per hour, while the average loss rate across the first six hours is 66 divided by 6, or 11 liters per hour, and the contrast between those last two numbers, point rate versus average rate, is a distinction graders probe deliberately.

Real prompts complicate the skeleton with outflows, thresholds, and piecewise regimes, but the four decisions never change, and writing them as a header before computing is the modeling equivalent of the sample-point row: cheap, fast, and protective. A model written in symbols can be interrogated, differentiated for rate questions, evaluated for amount questions, and inverted for time questions, which is why so many free-response prompts begin by asking for exactly such a model.

## What Grading Reports Teach About This Unit

Chief reader reports on past administrations circle the same findings, and a student can harvest them without sitting the exam. Readers report that approximation setups earn more credit than approximation arithmetic, and that students who show widths times heights, with heights visibly sourced from the table or graph, collect points even when a product slips. Readers report that interpretation parts are the most frequently skipped and the most cheaply earned, since the sentence templates are learnable in an afternoon. Readers report that extremum justifications fail most often by asserting a maximum where a derivative is zero without checking the sign change, a hole the model sentences in this guide are built to plug.

The reports also praise what strong papers do with units and labels: amounts labeled with their quantity and time, rates labeled with their compound units, variables defined before use. None of this is decoration; on a nine-point question spread across four parts, presentation discipline is worth a point or two by itself, which at the margin is a full score band. Preparation that includes presentation, not merely computation, is the reports' unanimous advice, echoed by every section of this guide.

## Negative Rates Inside a Sum

Sums digest negative heights without complaint, and one table with a reversal shows the bookkeeping. A shuttle's velocity in miles per hour is recorded hourly across four hours as 8, 3, negative 2, negative 5, and negative 1: the shuttle slows, stops, and backs up. With strip width 1 hour, the left sum is 1 times the quantity 8 plus 3 plus negative 2 plus negative 5, which is 4 miles of net displacement. The right sum is 1 times the quantity 3 plus negative 2 plus negative 5 plus negative 1, which is negative 5 miles. The trapezoidal estimate averages them: negative 0.5 miles, a small net backward displacement, plausible for a ride that spent its second half in reverse.

None of those figures is the distance traveled, which would require the speed, the absolute value of velocity, whose samples are 8, 3, 2, 5, and 1. A left sum of speed gives 1 times (8 plus 3 plus 2 plus 5), which is 18 miles; a right sum gives 1 times (3 plus 2 plus 5 plus 1), which is 11 miles; the trapezoidal estimate of distance is 14.5 miles. Presenting both tallies side by side exposes the conceptual fault line in its sharpest form: same table, same widths, same schemes, and the signed and unsigned versions diverge completely because the phenomenon reversed. Questions that ask for distance while supplying velocity are testing whether the student notices the reversal at all, and the tell is always a negative entry in the data or a graph crossing the axis.

Accumulation functions with endpoints before the start obey the same sign logic through the swapped-limits property. The accumulation from 5 back to 2 of a rate is the negative of the accumulation from 2 to 5, interpretable as undoing three hours of change. Amounts at times earlier than a known census value are recovered by subtracting the accumulation between, a maneuver that appears whenever a problem gives a later value and asks about an earlier one.

## Where the Unit Leads: Areas, Volumes, and Work

The accumulation pattern reappears across the back half of the course wearing new costumes, and recognizing it early pays compound interest. Area between two curves is the accumulation of the vertical gap between them: the integral of (top minus bottom) adds up thin vertical rectangles, each a tiny Riemann rectangle whose height is a difference of rates in disguise. Volumes by cross sections accumulate area the same way: a solid whose cross-sectional area at position x is A(x) has volume equal to the integral of A(x), since each thin slab contributes A(x) times its thickness. A pyramid-shaped solid with square cross sections of side x at distance x from the apex, over a height of 2, has volume the integral from 0 to 2 of x squared dx, which is 8/3, the running example of this whole guide reappearing as a volume. Volumes of revolution replace the cross-sectional area with pi times a squared radius, but the skeleton, accumulate slices, is unchanged.

Work problems in physics-flavored prompts accumulate force across distance, and probability-flavored contexts accumulate density across intervals, though those sit at the course's edge. The durable lesson is that the integral sign always announces the same story: a quantity built from many small products of a varying intensity and a small span. A student who reads every new integral as that story has a permanent head start on each new application the course introduces.

## Capstone: One Velocity, Every Tool in the Unit

A single velocity function, interrogated from every angle the unit owns, shows the tools interlocking. Let a particle move with velocity v(t) equal to t squared minus 3t plus 2 on the interval from 0 to 3, starting at position 0. The velocity factors as (t minus 1)(t minus 2), so the particle moves forward on the first hour, backward on the second, and forward again on the third, an itinerary that will punish every unsigned shortcut.

Approximations first, with three strips of width 1. The velocity values at the integers are v(0) equal to 2, v(1) equal to 0, v(2) equal to 0, and v(3) equal to 2. The left sum is 1 times (2 plus 0 plus 0), which is 2. The right sum is 1 times (0 plus 0 plus 2), which is also 2, and the trapezoidal estimate inherits 2 as their average. All three agree, and all three are wrong in the same direction, because every strip sample misses the backward dip between the integers where the velocity goes negative. The midpoint samples catch the dip: v at 0.5 is 0.75, v at 1.5 is negative 0.25, and v at 2.5 is 0.75, summing to 1.25, so the midpoint estimate of displacement is 1.25. Agreement among endpoint methods is no guarantee of accuracy when the action hides between samples, a lesson worth the whole example.

Exact work settles everything. The antiderivative of the velocity is t cubed over 3, minus 3t squared over 2, plus 2t. Displacement across the full interval is that antiderivative at 3 minus at 0: 9 minus 13.5 plus 6, which is 1.5. Piece by piece, the antiderivative at 1 is 1/3 minus 3/2 plus 2, which is 5/6; at 2 it is 8/3 minus 6 plus 4, which is 2/3. The three signed pieces are therefore 5/6, then 2/3 minus 5/6, which is negative 1/6, then 3/2 minus 2/3, which is 5/6. Total distance adds the absolute values: 5/6 plus 1/6 plus 5/6, equal to 11/6, about 1.83, while the displacement 3/2 is their signed sum, and the two figures check against each other exactly.

The remaining standard questions fall in line. The average velocity across the three hours is the displacement divided by 3, which is 0.5, and it equals the average value of the velocity function, as it must. The particle's position rises to 5/6 at t equal to 1, dips to 2/3 at t equal to 2, and ends at 3/2, so on this interval the greatest position is the endpoint value 3/2, while the greatest distance from the start in the backward direction never materializes because the dip stays shallow. An accumulation function S(x) defined as the integral of the velocity from 0 to x has derivative v, so S bends downward wherever the velocity decreases, on the stretch from 0 to 1.5, and upward afterward. Every question type in the unit has now been asked and answered on one small quadratic.

## Moving Between Representations

The course's deepest habit is translation among four representations of the same mathematics: verbal, symbolic, graphical, and tabular. This unit exercises all twelve directions of translation. Verbal to symbolic is modeling: a described tank becomes an accumulation function. Symbolic to graphical is sketching an accumulation from its integrand's graph. Graphical to tabular is reading heights into a table before summing. Tabular to verbal is interpreting a computed sum in a sentence with units. Exam questions rarely stay inside one representation for long, and the students who stall are usually stuck at a border crossing rather than at a computation.

Notation is the passport for those crossings, and reading symbols aloud in full sentences builds the fluency. The integral from a to b of f(x) dx reads as "the accumulated value of f across the interval, built from products of heights and widths." The bracket notation F(x) evaluated from a to b reads as "the antiderivative at the top minus the antiderivative at the bottom." Sigma notation reads as "add these terms, one per index value." A student who can narrate each symbol string as a process rarely sets up the wrong process, because the narration is the setup, spoken in advance.

One translation deserves special drilling because it inverts the usual direction: given a quantity's accumulation graph, recover statements about the rate. The height of the accumulation graph is an amount; its slope is the rate; its steepest point is where the rate peaked; its flat spots are where the rate vanished. Students habitually read the height as the rate, and the error survives because the graphs look similar. The translation table from the accumulation section, value from area, derivative from height, second derivative from slope, applied in reverse, is the corrective: on an accumulation graph, ask what is area, what is height, what is slope, in that order, before answering anything.

## Running Totals: Building an Accumulation Table from a Rate Table

Approximation questions sometimes ask not for one total but for the accumulation at several moments, and the efficient method is the running total: compute each strip once, then keep a cumulative sum. Return to the walkthrough's tank, with rates 4, 6, 5, 8, 7, and 9 gallons per minute at times 0, 1, 3, 6, 10, and 12, and use trapezoidal strips of 5, 11, 19.5, 30, and 16 gallons. Starting from zero accumulated inflow at time 0, the running totals after each strip are 5 gallons at time 1, 16 at time 3, 35.5 at time 6, 65.5 at time 10, and 81.5 at time 12. Adding the 40 gallons initially in the tank converts those into estimated amounts: 45, 56, 75.5, 105.5, and 121.5 gallons at the five recorded moments after the start.

A table of running totals answers questions that a single total cannot. Which recorded moment first shows the tank above 100 gallons? Time 10, at an estimated 105.5 gallons, since the time 6 estimate of 75.5 still falls short. When during the shift did the tank cross 100 gallons? The crossing sits inside the strip from time 6 to time 10, where 30 gallons arrive across 4 minutes; the tank needs 24.5 of those gallons to reach 100, a fraction 24.5 over 30, about 0.817 of the strip, so a linear interpolation places the crossing near time 6 plus 4 times 0.817, about 9.27 minutes. Interpolation inside a strip assumes the rate behaves evenly there, an assumption to state, but it converts a table into a timeline, which is often the real request.

Running totals also expose the accumulation function's shape in data form. The increments per strip, 5, 11, 19.5, 30, and 16, first grow and then shrink, hinting that the underlying rate rose through the middle of the shift and eased near the end, even though the raw rates end at their highest reading. Width matters: the final strip contributes less than the fourth strip despite a higher rate, because it spans only 2 minutes against 4. Reading increments rather than rates is the tabular version of reading an accumulation graph's steepness, and it is the correct instinct whenever a question asks when accumulation was fastest: compare strip totals per unit width, that is, the average rate per strip, which for these strips runs 5, 5.5, 6.5, 7.5, and 8 gallons per minute, a steadily rising intensity that the strip totals alone disguised.

The method generalizes to any scheme: running left sums, running right sums, and running trapezoidal sums each produce an estimated accumulation function defined at the table's moments. Problems that ask for an estimate of the amount at an intermediate recorded time are running-total problems in disguise, and solving them strip by strip, once, beats re-deriving a fresh sum for every part.

## Ten-Second Sanity Checks

Finished answers deserve a ten-second interrogation before the page turns, and four checks cover most catastrophes. The bound check asks whether the answer fits inside the crudest fence: no accumulation over an interval can exceed the largest rate magnitude times the interval length in absolute value, so an estimated inflow of 300 gallons from rates bounded by 9 gallons per minute across 12 minutes is impossible on its face, since the fence is 108. The sign check asks whether the answer's sign matches the graph's dominant side of the axis. The unit check reads the answer's units aloud and compares them with the question's request. The magnitude check compares the answer with the average-value intuition: an accumulation should look like a plausible typical rate times the span, and a result ten times that size usually hides a width error, most often a delta x taken from the count of readings instead of the span divided by the count of strips. Ten seconds per part is a trivial tax against the points these checks rescue across a full exam.

A fifth check, the agreement check, applies whenever two independent routes reach the same quantity. An amount computed by an antiderivative should sit inside the fences that endpoint sums build from the same data; a distance computed by splitting at sign changes should exceed the displacement in absolute value and match it in the all-one-direction case; an average rate multiplied by the span should reproduce the accumulated change. Disagreement between two routes is not a coin flip to resolve by preference: it is a located error, and the cheaper route, usually the geometric or the bracketing one, is the referee to recompute first. Students who build the habit of manufacturing a second route, even a crude one, convert single guesses into verified answers, which is the closest thing to insurance this unit sells.

## Choosing a Method Under Pressure

Exam problems rarely announce their method, so a decision habit helps: interrogate the data type first. A formula for the integrand invites antidifferentiation or a numerical integral, depending on the section and the formula's friendliness. A table forbids antidifferentiation entirely and points to sums, running totals, and fences. A graph points to geometry for exact values and to height reading for sums and derivative values. A verbal description points to modeling: name the quantity, find the rate, add the start. Four data types, four toolboxes, and the first five seconds of any problem should be spent identifying which toolbox the problem opened.

Within the formula toolbox, a second interrogation follows: does the question ask for a value, a derivative, or a behavior? Values belong to Part Two or to geometry; derivatives of accumulations belong to Part One; behavior belongs to sign charts built from the integrand. Within the table toolbox, ask whether the requested output is one total, several running totals, a verdict, or a rate: each has its own machinery, all rehearsed in the walkthrough sections. Within the graph toolbox, ask whether the target is an area, a height, or a slope, and the translation table answers. These micro-decisions, practiced until they are reflexes, are what experienced teachers mean by problem recognition, and they convert the unit's many techniques from a list into a flowchart the mind runs automatically.

Pacing follows from the same flowchart. Recognition items, a Part One derivative, a structural combination, a geometric evaluation, should be taken on sight and banked. Construction items, a four-strip midpoint sum, a split distance computation, deserve a written sample row or split point before arithmetic begins. Modeling items deserve the four-decision header. Spending the first moments on classification rather than computation feels slower and finishes faster, because nearly every expensive error in this unit is a classification error: a sum computed where geometry was wanted, a net reported where a total was asked, an antiderivative hunted where only data existed. Students preparing for the exam can train the flowchart directly by sorting a mixed problem set by method before solving any of it, naming the toolbox and the target quantity for each item in a single phrase. Sorting twenty problems takes minutes, and the fluency it builds transfers to every unit that follows, because the habit of asking what kind of object a problem wants precedes all technique.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are Riemann sums on AP Calculus AB?

A Riemann sum is an approximation of a definite integral built by slicing the interval into subintervals, choosing a sample point in each one, and adding the products of function height and subinterval width. Each product is the signed area of one rectangle, so the sum estimates the accumulated change and the signed area under the curve at once. The named versions correspond to sampling rules: left endpoints, right endpoints, midpoints, or the trapezoidal variant that averages endpoint heights. As the number of subintervals grows and the widths shrink, every consistent sampling rule converges to the same limit, which is the definite integral itself. On the exam, Riemann sums appear with formulas, tables, and graphs, and they carry units whenever the function is a rate.

### Q: How do you use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on AP Calculus AB?

The theorem is used in two directions. The evaluation direction computes a definite integral exactly: find an antiderivative F of the integrand, then subtract F evaluated at the lower limit from F evaluated at the upper limit. The differentiation direction handles accumulation functions: the derivative of the integral from a constant to x of f(t) dt is simply f(x), with chain rule adjustments when the upper limit is a composite expression and a sign change when the variable occupies the lower limit. Most exam problems announce which direction they want through their data: a formula for the integrand suggests evaluation, a variable limit demands differentiation, and a graph or table routes the work back to sums and area bookkeeping instead.

### Q: How do you approximate area with Riemann sums on AP Calculus AB?

Divide the interval under the curve into subintervals, and on each one choose a height: the function value at the left edge, the right edge, or the center. Multiply each height by the subinterval width to get one rectangle's signed area, then add the products. With equal widths, factor the common delta x, computed as (b minus a) over n, out of the sum. With a table, take each width from the gaps between consecutive entries and pair it with the height the named rule selects. The result approximates the signed area between the curve and the axis; regions below the axis enter negatively, so a request for total geometric area requires splitting the computation at every axis crossing and adding absolute values.

### Q: What is the difference between left, right, and midpoint Riemann sums on AP Calculus AB?

The three sums share the same partition and widths and differ only in where each rectangle reads its height. A left sum samples the left endpoint of every subinterval, a right sum samples the right endpoint, and a midpoint sum samples the center. On an increasing function the left sum underestimates and the right sum overestimates, with the pattern reversing on a decreasing function, while the midpoint sum is governed by concavity and is usually the most accurate of the three at a given resolution. On a table, the midpoint sum may be impossible when no readings exist at strip centers. The trapezoidal estimate, a close relative, equals the average of the left and right sums on the same partition.

### Q: How is the trapezoidal rule tested on AP Calculus AB?

Testing usually presents a table of values, often with unequal spacing, and asks for a trapezoidal approximation of an integral or an accumulated quantity, sometimes followed by an interpretation or an overestimate question. Each strip contributes its width times the average of its two endpoint values, and the contributions are added. With equal widths the estimate equals the average of the left and right Riemann sums, a shortcut worth using. The verdict on accuracy follows concavity: the rule overestimates where the graph bends upward and underestimates where it bends downward. Graders award most credit for the visible setup, widths times averaged heights with values traced to the table, so writing each strip's computation protects the score even if an arithmetic slip follows.

### Q: How do you evaluate a definite integral using the Fundamental Theorem on AP Calculus AB?

Find an antiderivative of the integrand, then compute the difference of its values at the endpoints: the integral from a to b of f(x) dx equals F(b) minus F(a). Work the liturgy in the open: write the antiderivative, substitute the upper limit, substitute the lower limit, subtract. For the integral from 1 to 3 of (3x squared minus 2x plus 1) dx, the antiderivative is x cubed minus x squared plus x, giving 21 at the top and 1 at the bottom, for a value of 20. Prepare the integrand first when needed: divide through by a common factor, split absolute values at their sign changes, or rewrite roots as fractional powers before antidifferentiating. Exact answers stay in exact form on no-calculator sections.

### Q: What is an accumulation function on AP Calculus AB?

An accumulation function is a function defined by an integral with a variable endpoint: A(x) equals the integral from a fixed start a to x of f(t) dt. Its value at any point is the net amount accumulated between the start and that point, so when f is a rate, A measures how much of the quantity has piled up, been spent, or been traveled. If the quantity began with an initial amount, the total at x is the initial amount plus A(x), a step students lose points by omitting. Accumulation functions are studied through their values, computed by area or antidifferentiation, and through their behavior, which the first part of the Fundamental Theorem supplies by identifying the integrand as the derivative of A.

### Q: How do you differentiate an integral using the Fundamental Theorem on AP Calculus AB?

Apply the first part of the theorem: differentiating the integral from a constant to x of f(t) dt returns f(x), the integrand with the dummy variable replaced by the limit. Adjust for the limit's form. If the upper limit is a function u(x), multiply by u prime of x, so the derivative of the integral from 1 to x cubed of 1 over (1 plus t squared) dt is 3x squared over (1 plus x to the sixth). If the variable is the lower limit, swap the limits mentally and attach a negative sign along with the chain rule factor. If both limits move, split the integral at a constant and treat each piece by its own rule.

### Q: How do Riemann sums connect to the definite integral on AP Calculus AB?

The definite integral is defined as the limit of Riemann sums. As a partition is refined and the widest subinterval width shrinks toward zero, the sums built from any consistent sampling rule approach one number, and that number is the integral. The connection runs in both directions on the exam: a sum with finitely many strips approximates an integral, with accuracy improving as strips thin, and an expression written as a limit of sums must be recognized as an integral by reading its width factor and sample points. The integral sign itself memorializes the connection, being a stretched S for sum, while the dx records the vanished widths that produced the total.

### Q: How do you interpret area under a curve on AP Calculus AB?

Area under a curve is interpreted according to what the axes measure. Purely geometric problems read it as signed area: regions above the axis count positive, regions below count negative, and the integral reports the balance. Rate problems read it as accumulated change: when the horizontal axis is time and the vertical axis is a rate, the area equals the net change of the quantity over the interval, in the quantity's own units, because rate units times time units cancel to pure amount. A velocity graph's area gives displacement; a flow graph's area gives volume transferred. When a question wants a total rather than a net figure, distance traveled or water moved regardless of direction, the negative regions must be flipped positive before adding.

### Q: How do you compute a Riemann sum from a table on AP Calculus AB?

Read the widths from the gaps between consecutive input entries, remembering that the number of strips is one less than the number of entries. Pair each width with the height the named rule selects: left endpoints for a left sum, right endpoints for a right sum, averaged endpoint pairs for a trapezoidal sum. Multiply width by height strip by strip and add the products, keeping each strip's computation visible for setup credit. A table of rates at times 0, 1, 3, 6, 10, and 12 minutes, for instance, has widths 1, 2, 3, 4, and 2, and no single delta x exists. If the named rule is midpoint and the table has no center readings, say so: that impossibility is sometimes the tested insight.

### Q: What is the average value of a function on AP Calculus AB?

Defined for f on the interval from a to b, the average value is 1 over (b minus a), times the integral from a to b of f(x) dx. It is the constant height whose rectangle over the interval accumulates exactly the same signed area as the curve, so it flattens the graph while preserving the total. The average value of x squared on the interval from 0 to 3 is one third of the integral 9, which is 3, and the function attains that average at the square root of 3, as the mean value theorem for integrals promises. When the integrand is a rate, the average value is the average rate over the interval, total change divided by elapsed span, reported in the rate's own units.

### Q: How do you apply the second part of the Fundamental Theorem on AP Calculus AB?

The second part is the evaluation engine: to find the integral of f from a to b, take any antiderivative F and compute F(b) minus F(a). Apply it whenever the integrand has a recognizable antiderivative: polynomials by the power rule, sine and cosine with their sign care, exponentials unchanged, 1 over x becoming natural logarithm of absolute x, roots rewritten as powers. The constant of integration cancels in the subtraction, so the simplest antiderivative suffices. Verify plausibility against the graph whenever possible, since an evaluation that contradicts the visible signed area is wrong regardless of how clean the algebra looked. On calculator sections the same theorem justifies evaluating numerically when no elementary antiderivative exists.

### Q: How do you approximate from a graph using Riemann sums on AP Calculus AB?

Mark the partition on the graph first, then read each rectangle's height at the exact sample point the named rule requires: left edge, right edge, or strip center. Multiply each height by the strip width, respecting the axis scales, since a vertical scale in twos doubles every height and a horizontal scale in halves halves every width. Add the products for the estimate. Grid paper makes each square a known area, width times height in axis units, and counting weighted squares is a legitimate computation. Resist the urge to compute the true area by geometry when the question asks for a sum, because the sum is defined by its samples; geometry becomes the right tool only when a later part asks for the exact integral or an accumulation value.

### Q: How do accumulation and rate connect on AP Calculus AB?

Rate and accumulation are the two faces of the Fundamental Theorem. Integrating a rate over an interval produces the accumulated change in the quantity: distance from velocity, volume from flow, cost change from marginal cost. Differentiating an accumulation returns the rate: carry out the differentiation on an integral running from a to x, and the rate function itself comes back. This duality drives the exam's signature questions, which hand over a rate and ask about amounts, maxima of amounts, and average rates, or hand over an accumulation graph and ask where the rate was positive, largest, or zero. Units seal the connection: a rate's units are quantity per input unit, and multiplying by input units through integration yields pure quantity.

### Q: How do you write a Riemann sum in sigma notation on AP Calculus AB?

Sigma notation names the index, its range, and the general term. For n equal subintervals of an interval from a to b, set delta x equal to (b minus a) over n. A right sum is the sum from i equals 1 to n of f of (a plus i delta x), times delta x; a left sum runs i from 0 to n minus 1 with sample points a plus i delta x; a midpoint sum samples a plus (i minus one half) delta x. Reading a given sigma expression backward identifies the interval and integrand: the factor outside the function is delta x, its numerator is the interval length, and the constant inside the sample point is the left endpoint.

### Q: How do you tell whether a Riemann sum overestimates or underestimates on AP Calculus AB?

Read the shape of the function on the interval. For endpoint sums, monotonicity decides: on an increasing function the left sum underestimates and the right sum overestimates, and both verdicts reverse on a decreasing function. For the trapezoidal rule, concavity decides: it overestimates where the graph bends upward and underestimates where the graph bends downward. For midpoint sums, concavity decides with the opposite verdicts from the trapezoid. When the function changes direction or bending inside the interval, no single verdict covers the whole sum, and the correct response argues strip by strip or names the obstruction. Bracketing is the practical reward: opposing verdicts from two methods fence the true integral between their values.

### Q: How does the net change theorem appear on AP Calculus AB?

The net change theorem states that the integral of a rate of change over an interval equals the net change of the quantity: the integral from a to b of F prime equals F(b) minus F(a). It appears whenever a problem gives a rate, whether a formula, table, or graph, and asks for an amount at a later time, since the amount is the starting value plus the integral of the rate. It also appears in interpretation parts that ask what an integral expression means, and in average rate parts, where dividing the net change by the interval length yields the average rate. Recognizing the theorem underneath a word problem is the modeling step on which every other part of the problem depends.

### Q: How do units work in an accumulation problem on AP Calculus AB?

Units multiply and cancel like fractions. A rate carries compound units, quantity per unit of input: gallons per minute, meters per second, dollars per item. Integrating multiplies by the input variable, minutes or seconds or items, so the denominator cancels and the integral carries pure quantity units: gallons, meters, dollars. An approximation inherits the same units, since each rectangle is a rate value times a width. Average value divides the accumulated quantity by the interval length, restoring the rate's original units. Graders award a dedicated point for correct units on many free-response parts, and an answer whose units mismatch the question, gallons per minute where gallons were requested, signals a setup error worth hunting before time expires.

### Q: How do you compare the accuracy of different approximations on AP Calculus AB?

Compare each estimate against the truth when the truth is known, or against the fences the shape rules provide when it is not. On smooth curves with a single concavity, midpoint sums typically err least, trapezoidal sums err about twice as much in the opposite direction, and endpoint sums err most, with their gap halving only as resolution doubles, while midpoint and trapezoidal errors shrink roughly by a factor of four per doubling. On the integral of x squared from 0 to 2 with four strips, the errors were about 0.92 for the left sum, 1.08 for the right, 0.083 for the trapezoid, and 0.042 for the midpoint, against a truth of 8/3. Verdict knowledge converts comparisons into certainty: the estimate known to overestimate and the one known to underestimate bracket the answer, and the bracket midpoint is usually the safest single guess available.
