Every reader who opens Romeo and Juliet reads two texts at once. The first is the play the early printers left behind: the short first quarto of 1597, the long second quarto of 1599, and the Folio text of 1623 that descended from them. The second text is the one laid over those witnesses by a century of editors, from Nicholas Rowe in 1709 to Edmond Malone in 1790, who modernized the spelling, repunctuated the verse, amplified the stage directions, demoted lines they distrusted, restored lines they admired, and gradually built the apparatus of evidence underneath the page. The familiar play, the one quoted in classrooms and printed in paperback, is the second text wearing the first one's clothes. Editing Romeo and Juliet from Rowe to Malone is therefore not a footnote to the play's history. It is the history of how the play became readable at all.

The verdict argued through this article is double, and both halves matter. The eighteenth-century editors saved the play as a reading text: without Rowe's intelligible page, Pope's pointing, Theobald's method, Capell's collations, and Malone's documents, the tragedy would have remained a printers' artefact, legible mainly to specialists. The same editors also interposed themselves between the play and its readers, sometimes by necessity and sometimes by vanity, and a portion of what they interposed was imposition dressed as improvement. The tradition's direction of travel, however, runs unmistakably from taste toward evidence. Rowe edited by theatrical instinct, Pope by aesthetic judgement, Warburton by conjecture, and Malone by record and register. Each generation corrected the last, and the modern text is the sediment of those corrections. To read the play attentively is to read the editors too, whether their names appear on the page or not.

That sediment is easy to demonstrate. The act and scene numbers in every modern edition descend through the Folio line that Rowe regularized and furnished with locations. The stage directions that tell an actor when to enter, fight, or die carry Rowe's amplifying hand somewhere in their ancestry. The punctuation that tells a silent reader where Juliet's sentences turn is a modernized system whose modernization began in earnest in 1709. Even the confidence with which a modern editor prints one reading and footnotes another rests on habits of proof that Theobald announced, Capell practised in silence, and Malone made documentary. The cliche imagines an authorless modern text, printed clean from the author's manuscript. No such manuscript survives for this play, and no such text exists. Restoring the editors to view restores the hands that shaped the words.

## The Text Before the Editors: What Rowe Inherited

The inheritance was narrower than the play's modern abundance suggests. Between the last early quarto and Rowe's edition, the tragedy lived in the Folios: the First Folio of 1623, set from a quarto descendant, and its reprints of 1632, 1663, and 1685. The Fourth Folio of 1685, the last of the line, was the copy from which Rowe worked, and it was a degraded instrument. Nearly a century of reprinting had accumulated compositorial error, modernized some forms while leaving others archaic, and thinned the already sparse stage directions of the early texts. The quartos, which preserved the fuller and earlier state of the play, sat in a handful of libraries and private collections, consulted by almost nobody. An educated reader of 1700 who wished to read the tragedy of the Veronese lovers read the Fourth Folio's version of it, in old spelling, with erratic punctuation, without a list of characters, without locations for the scenes, and without any note explaining a hard word or a darker passage.

The consequences show in the play's most famous cruxes of presentation. The second quarto, the substantive early text, prints parts of the dialogue with the loose, rhetorical pointing of the period, in which commas mark breath and emphasis rather than grammar. Its stage directions are famously incomplete: fights begin without warning, characters drift on and off, and the great death scene in the Capel vault assembles its bodies with minimal instruction. The Folio tradition regularized some of this and neglected the rest. A reader meeting "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2) in the Fourth Folio met it inside a page that offered no help with the scene's geography, no balcony named, no indication of who stood where. The words were present. The page was mute about everything else.

No profession of editor yet existed to answer that muteness. The seventeenth century had produced commendatory verses, promptbooks, and reprints, but nobody had undertaken to prepare Shakespeare's text for readers as an act of scholarship or even of systematic care. The early editors of classical authors, the tradition of commentary descending from the Renaissance humanists, offered a model; so did the editing of the English poets, where John Dryden and others had presented older work in modern dress. Shakespeare awaited the same treatment because the conditions for it had only just assembled: a London book trade wealthy enough to finance a large collected edition, a copyright in the plays worth defending, a reading public that bought polite literature in quantity, and a theatrical culture, Rowe's own, that kept the plays in performance and made their texts a living concern. Jacob Tonson, the most enterprising publisher of the age, held the largest share of the Shakespeare copyright and stood ready to exploit it. What Tonson needed was a name to put on the title page, and the name he chose belonged to a working playwright.

The stage had kept the play alive through the barren print years, though on the Restoration and early Augustan stage the tragedy circulated in forms its first audiences would scarcely have known, cut, altered, and in the 1680s even furnished by Thomas Otway's Roman adaptation with a rival afterlife of its own. Performance preserved the story while print neglected the text, and the divorce between the two is the precondition of Rowe's edition: by 1709 the play existed as a theatrical memory and a mouldering Folio page, each ignorant of the other. Rowe, a playwright editing for a publisher, was the first man positioned to remarry them, and his edition's theatrical good sense, its care for who stands where and who speaks to whom, is the stage re-entering the text through the editor's study door. The century that followed widened the divorce even as it healed it, the scholars retreating ever deeper into libraries while the playhouses adapted ever more freely; but the founding gesture of 1709, print learning from performance, remained the tradition's buried foundation, visible whenever an editor's direction clarifies a piece of business the quartos left dark.

One further fact about the inheritance deserves emphasis, because the whole century's work turns on it. The editors did not begin from a vacuum of authority; they began from a hierarchy of print that later scholarship reversed. To Rowe and Pope, the Folio was simply the works, the collected and canonical form, and the quartos were curiosities where they were known at all. The gradual discovery that the quartos, and above all the second quarto, carried an authority the Folio lacked is the century's deepest editorial story, deeper than any single emendation. Theobald gestured toward it, Capell acted on it, Malone documented it. When the story opens in 1709, however, the Folio's descendant ruled unchallenged, and the first editor's task was not to recover a lost original but to make a inherited page presentable.

## The Business of the Book: Tonson, Copyright, and the Editor's Pay

The succession of editors was also a succession of employees, and the employer was a copyright. The right to print Shakespeare's plays had descended through the seventeenth-century stationers to a consortium in which Jacob Tonson, the dominant literary publisher of the age, held the commanding share. Tonson's interest in the plays was proprietary before it was literary: a collected edition, handsomely produced and aggressively marketed, converted an old property into a new revenue stream, and each successive edition refreshed the property for another generation of buyers. The editors worked inside that commercial logic. Rowe was a Tonson author presenting a Tonson property. Pope's subscription edition of 1725 was a financial event as much as a scholarly one, its editor rewarded on a scale that announced how valuable the property had become. Theobald, Hanmer, Warburton, Johnson, and Malone all published into the same structure of ownership, rivalry, and resale.

Copyright shaped the texts in ways readers seldom suspect. Because the proprietors' asset was the plays as a going concern, editions were expected to look new without becoming strange: innovation had to be visible enough to justify a purchase and conservative enough not to devalue the property's familiarity. Rowe's modernizations fit the brief exactly, a visibly improved page over substantially received readings. Pope's typographical drama, the demotions and the marked beauties, likewise advertised novelty on the page itself. The mid-century litigation over literary property, in which the Tonson interest defended a perpetual copyright in the classics against Scottish reprinters, forms the legal backdrop to Johnson's delayed edition: while the lawyers argued over who owned Shakespeare, the scholars waited to edit him. When the House of Lords finally refused perpetual copyright in 1774, the plays became common property, editions multiplied, and the variorum enterprise of Steevens, Reed, and Malone unfolded in a competitive market where scholarship itself had become the selling point.

The commercial frame explains a puzzle in the succession's rhythm: why method, announced by Theobald in 1726, took sixty years to conquer. Collation is slow, expensive, and invisible on the page; a proprietor gains little from an editor's years among the quartos if the buyer cannot see the difference. Taste, by contrast, is visible at a glance, in prefaces, demotions, and handsome paper. The market therefore rewarded Pope and Hanmer before it rewarded Capell, and it rewarded Capell hardly at all, because his revolution was procedural. Only when a reading public educated by the variorum's bulk had learned to value documentation did Malone's archives become a commercial asset rather than a private obsession. Andrew Murphy's history of Shakespeare in print insists on this material frame against any purely intellectual history of editing, and the insistence is just: the ideas won when the market learned to pay for them.

Subscription publishing added a further twist to the editor's incentives. Pope's edition was sold by subscription, its buyers committing in advance on the strength of the editor's name, and the subscription list printed in the volumes reads like a roll of the political and literary nation. An editor so financed owed his subscribers a performance: the preface's brilliance, the demotions' confidence, the beauties' marginal flags are all, among other things, deliverables in a contract of display. Theobald, by contrast, worked for booksellers at trade rates, and his edition's sobriety reflects a different contract, value delivered as accuracy rather than as spectacle. Neither contract corrupts in itself; both shape the page. The variorum's editors worked increasingly for a market of institutions and collectors, where bulk itself certified value, and Malone's ten volumes addressed a readership for whom documentation had become a luxury good. Reading the succession with its contracts in view does not reduce the editors to their paymasters. It explains why the same intelligence, differently financed, produced editions of such different character, and why the documentary method's triumph waited for a market that would pay for patience.

None of this diminishes the editors' achievements; it locates them. Theobald's method was a scholarly programme and a bid for the property's future. Capell's collations were a life's devotion and, in the market's cold arithmetic, a gift to successors who monetized them. Malone's documentary turn succeeded intellectually and commercially at once, because by 1790 documentation had become what discriminating buyers wanted a Shakespeare to display. The tradition from Rowe to Malone is a history of scholarship learning to survive inside a business, and the modern scholarly edition, university-press bound and apparatus-laden, is the business's descendant as surely as it is the scholarship's.

## Nicholas Rowe and the First Edited Romeo and Juliet

Nicholas Rowe came to the plays as a man of the theatre. A successful dramatist, later Poet Laureate, he edited the Works of Mr. William Shakespear for Tonson in 1709, in six volumes, and the edition's virtues are a playwright's virtues: it asks, page by page, what a reader needs in order to imagine a performance. Rowe modernized the spelling and punctuation throughout, regularized the act and scene divisions he inherited from the Folio, prefixed to each scene a statement of its place, supplied lists of the dramatis personae, and fleshed out the skeletal stage directions of his copy with the entrances, exits, and business that a reader requires to follow the action. For Romeo and Juliet the effect is immediate and permanent. Verona acquires streets, chambers, a cell, a vault. The lovers' meetings acquire doors and directions. The play becomes, for the first time in print, a text that stages itself in the reader's mind.

Rowe's text of the tragedy rested on the Fourth Folio, and its readings therefore descend from the Folio line, with Rowe's own corrections and modernizations worked over them. His punctuation repays attention because it set the pattern for the century: where the early texts point rhetorically, Rowe points grammatically, clarifying syntax at the cost of the older pointing's flexibility. When Juliet cries "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (1.5), the exclamatory clarity of the modern page, the sense of a sentence shaped for a reader's eye, begins with the habits Rowe normalized. Multiply that intervention by every speech in the play and the scale of his quiet authorship becomes clear. Rowe did not merely present the text. He re-voiced it for silent reading, and every subsequent editor worked either with his grain or against it.

He also wrote the first biography, the account of Shakespeare's life prefixed to the edition, assembled from inquiries made through the actor Thomas Betterton, who had known men who had known the Restoration stage's memory of the previous century. The Life is thin, anecdotal, and partly wrong, yet its existence inside an edition of the plays declared a new idea: that the text had an author with a history, and that the history belonged with the text. Malone's documentary scholarship at the century's end is the Life's remote descendant, grown rigorous. Between Rowe's anecdotes and Malone's registers lies the whole education of English editing.

### What did Rowe add to the page besides the words?

Rowe added the furniture of the readable play: a list of the persons before the text, a named place at the head of every scene, entrances and exits spelled out where his copy left them implicit, and spelling and punctuation recast for a modern eye. His reader always knows where the action stands and who is on stage.

The limits of Rowe's work are as instructive as its innovations. He collated nothing. The quartos lay outside his method, which was to present the received Folio text intelligibly, correcting obvious corruption by sense and theatrical instinct. Where the Fourth Folio erred, Rowe frequently erred with it, and where the Folio lacked what the quartos preserved, Rowe lacked it too. His stage directions, for all their usefulness, are inventions of a reasonable kind, composed by a dramatist imagining the scene rather than recovered from any early witness; later editors, printing them without brackets or note, allowed Rowe's imagination to harden into the play's apparent facts. The pattern recurs through the century: an editor supplies a deficiency so gracefully that the supply is forgotten, and the text gains a feature its documents never contained.

Yet the judgement of posterity on Rowe has been kinder than the judgement on most of his successors, and justly. Marcus Walsh, the historian of eighteenth-century editing whose account frames much modern discussion, treats Rowe's edition as the moment Shakespeare became an edited author in the modern marketplace, the point at which the plays entered the condition every classic text requires: a prepared page, an accountable presenter, a public invited to read rather than merely to possess. Rowe prepared that page for this play with a practitioner's tact. The tragedy that Garrick's audiences admired and that the century's readers wept over was, in its verbal surface, substantially Rowe's.


## Pope's Edition: Taste, Demotion, and the Beauties

Alexander Pope's edition of 1725 changed the editor's job description. Rowe had presented the received text; Pope judged it. Working for the same Tonson interest and paid on a scale that made the edition one of the most lucrative literary undertakings of the age, Pope announced in his preface a theory of the text that justified judgement: Shakespeare's works had reached print through the carelessness of actors and the ignorance of printers, stuffed with interpolations foisted in by players and mangled by the press. From that theory followed the editor's licence. Passages that struck Pope as unworthy of Shakespeare could be suspected as another hand's work, and the suspicion could be printed into the page. Across the canon Pope degraded well over a thousand lines to the foot of the page, in smaller type, as probably spurious. He also marked the passages he admired, the shining beauties, with inverted commas in the margin, so that the edition taught its readers not only what to doubt but what to admire. Editing became criticism with typographical powers.

For this play the Popean method cut both ways. Pope consulted early copies more widely than Rowe had done, and his text of the tragedy absorbed quarto readings at a number of points, an instinct toward the earlier witnesses that later scholarship ratified. His ear for verse was the finest any editor of the century brought to the text, and his regularization of metre, though it sometimes sandpapered a deliberate roughness into smoothness, often recovered the line the compositors had blurred. When the Friar warns that "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder" (2.6), the clean pentameters of the modern page owe something to a tradition of metrical attention that Pope, more than any predecessor, made an editor's duty.

The cost stood in the demotions and in the confidence behind them. Pope's criterion was taste, and taste is a witness that cannot be cross-examined. Lines in this play that offended Augustan decorum, the bawdy fencing of the servants, Mercutio's wilder flights, the Nurse's prolixity, stood under a suspicion that had nothing to do with documents. Mercutio's prose wit, which the second quarto gives in full sail, embarrassed an editor for whom the author of the great tragedies ought not to stoop. The demoted lines were not deleted, and Pope's defenders note that degradation to the foot of the page preserved what it doubted; but the typography instructed the reader's trust, and generations read the play with Pope's suspicions built into its layout. Simon Jarvis, in his revisionist history of the period's editing, asks readers to take Pope's project seriously as a coherent poetics of editing, an attempt to give the public the Shakespeare it could admire. The defence is genuine, and it convicts as much as it acquits: an edition designed to produce admiration will always trim the awkward, the excessive, and the strange, which in this play are load-bearing qualities.

### Did Pope edit the play or judge it?

Pope judged it, and printed the judgement as the text. His demotions set the lines he distrusted in smaller type at the foot of the page, and his marginal marks singled out the beauties he approved, so readers absorbed his verdicts on authorship and worth before forming opinions unaided.

Pope's preface, however, performed one service that outlasted his demotions. It told the reading public, in famous prose, that the text of Shakespeare had a history of damage, that the printed page was not a transparent window onto the author's manuscript. That idea, once planted, could not be unplanted, and it created the audience for everything Theobald, Capell, and Malone would later do. Pope taught readers to suspect the text. His misfortune was that the first reader to practise the suspicion systematically practised it on Pope.

Pope's claim to have restored the text from the old copies deserves a final scrutiny, because its truth and its exaggeration together explain his edition's strange double legacy. He did consult quartos, a diligence Rowe had not attempted, and at scattered points his text acknowledges their authority. But the consultation served the judgement rather than governing it: where an old reading pleased his ear, it entered; where it displeased, the Folio line or his own sense prevailed, and no apparatus existed by which a reader could reconstruct his choices or contest them. The edition's form concealed its method, and the concealment was the point at issue between Pope and the scholar who exposed him. Editing, as Theobald was about to insist, is not the result but the reasoning; an edition that hides its reasoning asks to be believed, and belief is what the eighteenth century was learning, painfully, to stop granting its editors. Pope's Romeo and Juliet remains eminently readable, pointed with a poet's intelligence, and it is read, when it is read at all, as a document in the history of taste. Theobald's is read as a stage in the history of the text. The distinction between those two afterlives is the distinction the whole century was working to draw.

## Theobald and the Invention of Editorial Method

Lewis Theobald was a professional man of letters, a translator and playwright of modest success, who in 1726 published Shakespeare Restored, a specimen of the many errors in Pope's edition with an account of the method by which they might be corrected. The book is the founding document of modern Shakespeare editing, and its method matters more than its spite. Theobald argued that an editor must know the author's hand and habits, must collate the early editions rather than trust the newest reprint, must understand the sources from which Shakespeare worked, must command the grammar and metre of the period, and must emend only where the transmitted text fails and a correction restores sense, idiom, and metre together. Emendation, in Theobald's hands, stopped being a display of taste and became a disciplined inference from evidence.

His celebrated correction in another play states the method in miniature. Where the Folio's Henry V makes the dying Falstaff's hostess say that his nose was as sharp as a pen "and a Table of greene fields," Theobald read the copy behind the misprint and restored "and a' babbled of green fields," a dying man's babble recovered from a compositor's nonsense by knowledge of idiom, of the scene's pathos, and of how such words deform in print. The emendation has never been displaced. Its importance for Romeo and Juliet is indirect and total: the same discipline, turned on this play in Theobald's edition of the works in 1733, produced the first text of the tragedy prepared by collating the early quartos and the Folio, weighing their readings, and correcting the received page where the witnesses and the sense together condemned it. Readings that descend from Theobald's labour entered the tradition so quietly that their origin vanished; the modern text is full of his repairs, unattributed because they persuaded.

Theobald's attention to the play's language shows the method at work on small change. The opening scene's fencing with words, in which Sampson and Gregory trade on coals, colliers, choler, and collar, depends on spellings and senses that the Folio tradition had left cloudy; Theobald's generation of editors, armed with glossarial learning, began the work of annotation that made such passages teachable. "Gregory, on my word, we'll not carry coals" (1.1) is a joke about dishonour wearing the dress of a pun on coal-bearing, and the scene's wit survives in the modern classroom because editors from Theobald onward glossed what the centuries had darkened. Peter Seary's biography of Theobald, the standard modern account, restores the scale of this achievement against the ridicule that buried it: the first editor to treat Shakespeare's text as a historical problem requiring historical evidence.

The ridicule deserves its paragraph, because it shaped the tradition's politics. Pope answered Shakespeare Restored not with argument but with apotheosis in reverse: the Dunciad of 1728 enthroned Theobald, spelled Tibbald, as king of the dunces, the patron hero of pedantic dullness. The satire was magnificent and unjust, and for decades it fixed Theobald's public character as a grubbing corrector of other men's genius. The feud had a structural cause beneath the personal one. Pope's edition embodied the gentleman-editor, the poet whose taste licensed his text; Theobald embodied the scholar-editor, whose evidence licensed his. The Tonson copyright made both men rivals for the same property, and the quarrel over method was also a quarrel over who owned Shakespeare: the wits or the scholars. Posterity's verdict split the prize. Pope kept the fame. Theobald kept the text. Every editor since has worked in Theobald's discipline, including the editors who never read him, because his discipline became the definition of the job.

Theobald also understood, earlier than any rival, that editing this play meant editing its documents in their plurality. The second quarto's authority impressed itself on his practice, and his edition's respect for the early texts, imperfectly theorized but consistently felt, pointed the way Capell would systematize and Malone would document. If Rowe made the play readable and Pope made it judged, Theobald made it corrigible: a text whose errors could be identified, argued, and repaired in the open, with reasons given. That openness, the obligation to show the evidence, is the thread that runs from 1733 to the modern scholarly edition, and it begins as one hack writer's refusal to let a great poet's taste stand in for proof.

Theobald's edition of 1733 enjoyed the long reign its method deserved. Reprinted and absorbed into the trade's stock of texts, it underlies the mid-century editions that claimed other pedigrees: Hanmer's compositors worked within a textual world Theobald had already corrected, and Warburton's conjectures were offered, tacitly, as improvements on a Theobaldian base. The pattern is characteristic of methodological revolutions, which succeed by becoming invisible. A reader of 1750 who bought the tragedy in any respectable edition read a text whose worst Folio errors had been silently repaired a generation earlier, and the repairer, ridiculed in the Dunciad and dead since 1744, collected no royalty on the improvement. The tradition's memory is unjust in both directions: it remembers the quarrels, which changed little, and forgets the repairs, which changed everything. Recovering the proportion between them is one purpose of the timeline artifact printed later in this article, where Theobald's row, plain as it looks, carries the heaviest entry in the table: the method of modern editing itself.

The reign also had a limit that the sequel exposed. Theobald's collation, pioneering as it was, remained selective, guided by the passages where corruption announced itself; he did not yet possess Capell's conviction that every line must be verified against the witnesses, nor Malone's archives for dating what the witnesses said. Method in 1733 was a discipline of intervention at the points of failure. The century's later work converted it into a discipline of total verification, and the difference between the two is the difference between a corrected text and a documented one. Both are Theobald's posterity. The second is also his reproach, delivered by successors who honoured him chiefly by finishing what he began.

## The Editor's Toolkit: Collation, Emendation, and the Gloss

The succession's quarrels become intelligible once the editor's four instruments are distinguished, because each editor in the line is best understood by the instrument he trusted. The first is collation: the systematic comparison of copies, word by word, to establish what the witnesses actually read. Collation is unglamorous and foundational; before any judgement can be exercised, somebody must know what the documents say, and the century's progress can be charted by the growth of collation from Rowe's absence of it, through Theobald's selective practice, to Capell's exhaustiveness and Malone's documented completeness. The second instrument is emendation, the correction of a transmitted reading judged corrupt. Emendation divides into the conservative kind, which restores a reading attested in another witness, and the conjectural kind, which prints what no witness contains because the editor believes the author must have written it. The century's drama is largely the story of conjecture's rise under Pope and Warburton and its disciplining under Johnson, Capell, and Malone.

The third instrument is the gloss, the explanatory note, and its history is the quietest revolution of the four. Early editions glossed almost nothing; the hard words, dead customs, and buried allusions of the play stood unexplained for a century of readers. Theobald's generation began systematic annotation, Johnson made the note a form of criticism, and Steevens made it an archive. For this play the gloss is not decoration. The fencing vocabulary of the duelling scenes, the apothecary's poisons and the Friar's herbs, the Italian street customs, the proverb lore inside the Nurse's speeches: all reach the modern reader through a chain of explanation whose first links the eighteenth century forged. The fourth instrument is presentation, the page itself: spelling, punctuation, lineation, stage directions, lists of persons, scene locations. Presentation is the instrument readers notice least and obey most, because it shapes the experience of the text before any word is weighed. Rowe revolutionized presentation; his successors mostly inherited his revolution and refined it.

Each instrument carries its characteristic temptation, and the complication the brief demands, the shading of improvement into imposition, maps neatly onto the four. Collation's temptation is timidity, the refusal to correct a document even when the document errs. Emendation's temptation is vanity, the conjecture that displays the editor. The gloss's temptation is pedantry, the note that buries the line it serves. Presentation's temptation is invisibility, the silent alteration that no reader can detect or contest. A great editor is one who feels all four temptations and yields to none habitually; the century produced no editor of whom that can be said without qualification, which is precisely why the succession, rather than any individual, deserves the credit for the modern text. The instruments corrected one another across the generations: Capell's collations checked the conjectures of men dead before he began, Malone's records checked Capell's assumptions, and the variorum's accumulation made every editor's choices auditable by every later reader. Method, in the end, is the name for temptations institutionalized into checks.

The toolkit also explains the tradition's treatment of this play's peculiar textual situation. Romeo and Juliet survives in two early states of very different character, a short quarto that looks like a performance's shadow and a long quarto that looks like an author's draft, with a Folio text derived at a remove. An editor armed chiefly with taste, like Pope, confronts that plurality as an embarrassment and smooths it. An editor armed with collation confronts it as evidence and preserves its tensions in the apparatus. The movement from Rowe to Malone is, in this light, the movement from smoothing to preserving, from the text as a performance to be enjoyed to the text as a dossier to be studied. Modern editions, which print the long quarto's play and annotate the short one's variants, are the institutional form of the second attitude; their existence is the toolkit's final vindication.

## Hanmer and Warburton: The Middle of the Century

The generation after Theobald demonstrates how slowly method defeats money and rank. Sir Thomas Hanmer's edition, printed at Oxford in 1743 and 1744, was the most beautiful Shakespeare of the century: large paper, fine type, and a suite of engravings designed by Francis Hayman, the painter who also supplied scenes for the London stage. As an object it announced that Shakespeare had become a classic fit for a gentleman's library. As a text of this play it announced very little. Hanmer, a country baronet editing as an amateur's pastime, worked chiefly from Pope's edition, seasoning it with conjectures of his own and with suggestions drawn from Warburton, whose learning he admired. The plays gained a glossarial apparatus and a handsome dress; the tragedy gained scarcely a reading. Hanmer's Romeo and Juliet is Pope in better clothes, and its historical function is to prove that by the 1740s the Shakespeare edition had become a piece of furniture whose value could rise while its scholarship stood still.

William Warburton's edition of 1747 presents the opposite spectacle: furniture of no particular beauty animated by ferocious intelligence. Warburton, a clergyman of formidable learning and later Bishop of Gloucester, had assisted Theobald and received from him, in the years of their alliance, a body of notes and conjectures. When Warburton published his own edition, the alliance had curdled; his preface treated Theobald with contempt, and Theobald's friends charged that the bishop's text was built on borrowed materials its author affected to despise. Whatever the rights of that quarrel, the edition itself practised emendation as blood sport. Warburton emended boldly and often, defending conjecture as the editor's highest faculty, and his pages swarm with ingenuity unballasted by the collation Theobald had preached. Where the evidence ran out, Warburton's confidence did not.

For this play the Warburtonian manner meant a text repeatedly adjusted to what a learned divine thought Shakespeare ought to have written, the very procedure Shakespeare Restored had been written to abolish. The episode matters because it clarified the stakes for everyone watching. An editor's conjecture, Johnson would soon insist, is a confession that the text has defeated him, to be admitted only when every witness has failed; Warburton treated it as a triumph. The mid-century thus offered the reading public two false models in succession, the edition as ornament and the edition as ego, and the tradition's health can be measured by the speed with which it repudiated both. Warburton's notes were mined for a generation by editors who used his learning and discarded his text, the most damning afterlife an edition can have.

The quarrel over Theobald's notes deserves a closer look, because it exposed the young profession's weakest joint: its economics of credit. Warburton had moved in Theobald's circle when the older man was the only practising textual scholar of Shakespeare in England, and the assistance he received, by letter and conversation, belonged to a republic of letters that ran on reciprocal obligation. When his own edition appeared in 1747 with a preface that sneered at the man whose materials it used, the offence registered beyond the parties. Editing depends on trust in transmission: an editor who misrepresents his debts to a predecessor teaches readers to doubt his reports of the documents, since both are testimony. The scandal therefore damaged Warburton's text as well as his name, and it helps explain the care with which Johnson, Capell, and Malone documented their obligations and their evidence. A discipline that lives by citation cannot survive editors who forge the citations of their own lives. The mid-century's disrepute was, in this respect, instructive: the tradition learned professional ethics from a bishop's failure to practise them, and Malone's scrupulous acknowledgements, like his registers, belong to the reformation that followed.

### Why did the middle of the century stall?

The stall was institutional before it was intellectual. The Tonson copyright made Shakespeare a property to be dressed and resold rather than a problem to be solved, and the mid-century editions served the property: Hanmer's sold beauty, Warburton's a famous name's combativeness. Nobody required collation of the quartos, and nobody supplied it.

Underneath the stall, however, the ground was being prepared. Theobald's edition remained in print and in use; the study of Shakespeare's language advanced in the hands of scholars such as John Upton, whose critical observations of 1746 defended the text's difficulties against its improvers; and a young Edward Capell was spending the 1740s and 1750s in libraries, doing in silence the work the famous editions had skipped. The middle of the century is therefore best read not as an empty interval but as the delay between a method announced and a method resourced. Editing had learned what it ought to do. It had not yet found the conditions, the patience, and the documentary appetite to do it.

## Johnson the Editor: Discipline Over Conjecture

Samuel Johnson's edition of 1765 is the century's most famous Shakespeare and, textually, among its most conservative, and the conjunction defines the man. Johnson had proposed an edition in 1756; litigation over the Shakespeare copyright and his own famous deliberateness delayed it for nine years. When the eight volumes appeared, the preface prefixed to them took its place at once as the greatest piece of Shakespeare criticism in English, a judgement about Johnson the critic that later articles in this series take up in its own right. Johnson the editor, the subject here, worked by rules almost opposite to Warburton's. He collated early copies, as he said, with diligence that found less reward than he expected; he believed the old editions were the only authentic sources of the text; he emended sparingly, on the principle that a reading which makes sense, however strange, outranks a conjecture which makes elegance; and he reserved his energy for the notes, where his task was not to alter the play but to explain it.

Applied to this tragedy, Johnsonian discipline looks like restraint at exactly the points where his predecessors had reached for the pen. The hard places of the text, the compressed syntax of the lovers, Juliet's "Wilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day" (3.5) with its aubade logic inverted and re-inverted, Mercutio's tumbling prose, stood in Johnson's text substantially as the witnesses delivered them, with the notes doing the work of rescue. Where Shakespeare's meaning darkened, Johnson glossed; where a predecessor's conjecture glittered, Johnson suspected. His edition taught its century that an editor's first duty is custody. The lesson may sound negative, and Johnson's text indeed breaks little new ground; but custody, after Warburton, was a reform.

Johnson also changed what a note could be. The explanatory note in his hands became an essay in miniature: lexical, historical, and judgemental at once, written in prose that readers bought the edition to read. Steevens would inherit the form and multiply it into the variorum's ocean of illustration. For Romeo and Juliet, Johnson's notes modelled a way of standing beside the text that respected its difficulty without surrendering to it, and his scepticism about the whole editorial enterprise, his confession that he had collated the copies and been disappointed in the profit, carried an authority that quieted the conjecturers more effectively than any polemic. An editor who announces the limits of editing disarms the vanity of editors.

### Why did Johnson trust the old copies more than the editors?

Johnson's trust followed from a theory of error. A text, he held, degrades as it passes through hands that do not understand it, each generation of printing and editing adding its own confident misunderstanding; the earliest copies therefore stood nearest the author, and a modern conjecture furthest from him.

The limits of Johnson's editing are real and were visible to his contemporaries. His collation was intermittent, his text eclectic without a stated principle, and his scorn for pedantry sometimes excused him from labours the text needed. The scholars who followed, Capell before him in time though later in influence, Steevens beside him, and Malone after him, all surpassed Johnson as textual workmen. What Johnson supplied that none of them could was the philosophy of the office, stated once and permanently: the editor as the author's custodian and the reader's advocate, forbidden to substitute his own composition for either's. The tradition from Rowe to Malone is often told as a progress of technique. Johnson is the reminder that it was also a progress of conscience, and that technique without conscience had already produced Warburton.

## Capell's Quiet Collation of the Quartos

Edward Capell spent more than twenty years preparing an edition that almost nobody celebrated when it appeared in 1767 and 1768, and that scholars later recognized as the most methodologically advanced Shakespeare of the century. Capell was a civil servant with a scholar's monomania: he assembled a working library of the early editions, quarto by quarto, and collated them against the Folio and against each other with a thoroughness no predecessor had attempted. His principle inverted the inheritance Rowe had accepted. Authority, Capell held, flows from the earliest substantive witnesses, and an editor's text should be built from the old copies themselves, with every departure argued. For Romeo and Juliet the principle had a specific consequence of the first importance: Capell's text leaned on the second quarto, the fullest early state of the play, treating the Folio tradition that Rowe and Pope had followed as a derived and often corrupted descendant.

The consequences ran beyond individual readings. Capell attended to features of the text that taste-based editing had trampled: the evidence of the stage directions, which in the quartos preserve the playhouse's own traces; the integrity of passages that Augustan editors had suspected because they were rough, bawdy, or strange; and the discipline of recording variants, so that a reader could audit the editor's choices. His later Notes and Various Readings, published between 1779 and 1783, presented the collations in a form that anticipated the modern apparatus, and his School of Shakespeare printed the sources and analogues from which the plays grew, extending Theobald's insight that an editor must know what Shakespeare read. Capell working on this play could set the lovers' story beside Brooke's poem in his own library, because he had built the library for that purpose.

Capell's manner was the opposite of Warburton's in every particular. He advanced no theory of his own genius, founded no school in his lifetime, and wrote with a dryness that invited neglect. The neglect duly arrived: his edition sold modestly, the reviewers shrugged, and the variorum line that dominated the following decades absorbed his results while crediting his more sociable contemporaries. The recovery of his reputation belongs to later bibliographers, who found in his collations work they had assumed undone and in his principles the draft of their own. Fredson Bowers, the twentieth century's most influential theorist of editing, worked in a discipline whose founding practices, the authority of the earliest witness and the duty of full collation, Capell had already embodied without fanfare.

Capell's handling of this play's double inheritance shows the procedure in action. The long second quarto gave him his foundation, but the short first quarto, which the Folio tradition had effectively buried, entered his deliberations as a witness with rights: where the long text is mute about stage business, the short text often speaks, preserving the shape of entrances, fights, and deaths as a playing company knew them. An editor formed by taste would have found the short quarto merely corrupt, a botched report unworthy of attention. An editor formed by collation found it evidence of a different kind, documentary rather than authorial, and Capell's willingness to let a despised witness testify on the matters it knew best marks the maturity of his method. The modern practice of printing the long text while mining the short one for directions and occasional readings descends from discriminations of exactly this kind, first systematized in Capell's quiet volumes.

Capell's library deserves record as the method's material base. He had spent decades and a considerable fortune assembling the early editions, buying quartos when the market thought them curiosities, until his collection stood among the finest of the age; at his death it passed to Trinity College, Cambridge, where the Capell collection remains a working resource for the discipline he helped to found. The bequest is the perfect emblem of his editorial character. Rowe left a readable page, Pope a famous quarrel, Warburton a warning; Capell left the tools, shelved and catalogued, for strangers to continue the work. His collations of this play's quartos, made when the copies were rarities in private hands, preserved evidence that later editors, working from the same library, could verify and extend. The tradition's memory, which the heroic narrative assigns to great names, in Capell's case took the humbler and more durable form of a bequest.

### Why did Capell's edition make so little noise?

Capell offered labour where the market wanted liveliness. His edition appeared without a fighting preface, without a famous name to promote it, and without the anecdote and judgement that made Johnson's volumes readable; its novelties were procedural, visible only to readers comparing texts line by line.

For the history traced here, Capell is the hinge. Before him, respect for the early texts was an instinct, felt by Theobald and confessed by Johnson, but practised fitfully and defended by taste. After him, it was a procedure with a literature: collate everything, print from the best early witness, record the variants, let the documents argue. The procedure needed one more ingredient before it could govern the tradition, and the ingredient was external evidence, facts about the plays' world drawn from records rather than from the texts themselves. That ingredient was Malone's contribution, prepared in the same decades by the last of Capell's great contemporaries, the annotator who taught the notes to swallow the library.

## Steevens and the Annotation of Everything

George Steevens entered Shakespeare editing as a collector, reprinting in 1766 twenty of the plays from their early quarto texts, an act of documentary piety that put the witnesses themselves into circulation. With Johnson he produced the edition of 1773, ten volumes in which the text stood above an accumulating mass of commentary, and after Johnson's death he revised the joint work in 1778, 1785, and finally in his own fifteen-volume edition of 1793. The line of Johnson and Steevens, continued by Isaac Reed and crowned by Malone's posthumous variorum of 1821, established the form in which the nineteenth century read Shakespeare: a text stabilized by an editor, surrounded and defended by the notes of every predecessor worth quoting. The variorum edition, the edition of the various notes, is the eighteenth century's most durable invention after the edited text itself.

Steevens's own gift was illustration. Where Johnson's notes judge, Steevens's notes document: parallel passages from Elizabethan and Jacobean literature, explanations of customs, weapons, games, plants, and proverbs, the whole material culture inside which the plays' words had their first meanings. For Romeo and Juliet the method bore fruit in the glossarial thickets where readers most need help. The apothecary's shop in Mantua, the herbs of the Friar's soliloquy, the fencing terms of the duelling scenes, the street customs of an Italian city as London imagined it: Steevens and his collaborators in annotation ransacked contemporary books for the parallels that make such passages legible, and modern editions live on the capital of that ransacking still. His notes on the play's sources, drawing the comparison with Brooke's poem and Painter's prose, taught readers to see the tragedy as a made thing with materials, an editorial lesson continuous with Theobald's and Capell's.

The variorum's vice grew with its virtue. Commentary attracted commentary; an editor's duty to completeness became an excuse for accumulation, and Steevens himself, a man of brilliant malice, sometimes annotated to wound a rival or display a rarity rather than to help a reader. The stage directions of the modern text, the glosses, the source parallels, all descend through this apparatus, and so does a certain deformation: the play as an occasion for learning about everything except the play. Later centuries would rebel, stripping the notes back in acting editions and school texts; but the rebellion presupposed the variorum's achievement, a text so surrounded by evidence that an editor could choose what to omit. Omitting from abundance is a luxury Rowe's readers, starved of any note at all, could not have imagined.

Steevens also hardened the tradition's textual conservatism in a useful direction. Working daily among the quartos he reprinted, he treated their authority as a fact of handling rather than a theorem, and the Johnson-Steevens text, for all its eclecticism, rests visibly closer to the early copies than the texts of the century's first half. The succession Rowe, Pope, Theobald, Hanmer, Warburton, Johnson, Capell, Steevens looks, from the vantage of 1793, like a slow convergence on a single standard: the text is what the earliest documents, critically sifted, say it is. What remained was to give that standard its archives, its chronology, and its name. Edmond Malone, an Irish lawyer turned man of letters who had contributed notes to Steevens's editions and quarrelled with Steevens over their credit, stood ready to supply all three.

## Decorum and the Bawdy Play: What the Editors Blushed At

No pressure tested the succession's methods like the play's indecorum. Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy threaded with bawdy: the servants' opening exchange trades in sexual menace, Mercutio's wit runs on double meanings, the Nurse's reminiscences are cheerfully obscene, and the lovers' own language carries an erotic charge that Augustan politeness found difficult to print without flinching. The early texts print it all without ceremony. The eighteenth-century editors, preparing the play for the parlour, faced in acute form the choice that defines their century: to transmit what the documents contain or to protect the reader the market had promised them. Their successive answers chart the tradition's moral education as faithfully as their collations chart its scholarship.

Rowe's generation handled the difficulty chiefly by silence and smoothing, letting modernization of the surface carry some of the sting away and trusting readers to pass over what remained. Pope's method made the discomfort structural: passages that offended his sense of the author could be suspected of interpolation, and suspicion, in his typography, meant demotion. The criterion was indefensible as evidence and entirely explicable as decorum, since the lines a polite age wished away and the lines a taste-led editor doubted were suspiciously often the same lines. Theobald's method offered the first principled refuge for the bawdy text: if the witnesses attest a passage and the idiom of the period sustains its sense, the editor's embarrassment is not an argument. Steevens completed the rescue by scholarly means, surrounding the double meanings with parallels from contemporary literature that converted impropriety into information. A joke glossed with six Elizabethan parallels has been, in effect, naturalized; the note is a fig leaf made of learning, and it worked, because it allowed the full text to stand in respectable editions.

The episode matters beyond its comedy. Decorum was the point at which the market's demands and the documents' claims collided most openly, and the tradition's eventual settlement, print everything and explain it, required editors to believe that evidence outranked embarrassment. That belief is Theobald's and Capell's and Malone's bequest, and the modern unexpurgated text, bawdy and all, is its monument. The expurgated Shakespeares of the following century, prepared for family reading by other hands, show by contrast what the scholarly tradition had refused: the editors from Rowe to Malone, for all their smoothing, never made suppression their system, and the play survived their century entire.

The decorum question also exposes the editors' differing relations to their century's stage. The playhouse of Garrick's era altered the tragedy far more radically than any editor dared, cutting the bawdy, softening the catastrophe, and regularizing the lovers into models of sensibility. Against that background the printed tradition's timidities shrink to scale. Rowe's smoothing and Pope's demotions were the mild end of a culture-wide negotiation between the play Shakespeare wrote and the play the age could receive, and the editors, measured against the managers, were the conservatives of the pair. The point will matter when the stage's own textual history is weighed elsewhere in this series; for the editing tradition it suffices that print, having begun the century as performance's servant, ended it as the document's guardian, and that the guardianship was learned partly by watching what the playhouse did when nobody guarded anything.

## Bell's Acting Edition and the Play's Two Publics

In 1773 and 1774, while Johnson and Steevens were assembling the variorum, the London bookseller John Bell issued a rival Shakespeare on an opposite principle: the plays as performed. Bell's edition printed the texts used at the Theatres Royal, taken from the promptbooks of Drury Lane and Covent Garden, in cheap small volumes meant for playgoers and the trade beyond the scholarly market. For this tragedy the performed text meant David Garrick's version, the adaptation that had ruled the stage since 1748, in which the story was tightened, Rosaline dismissed early, and the lovers granted the famous alteration of the tomb scene, Juliet waking before Romeo's poison finishes its work, so that the pair die in each other's knowledge rather than in sequence. Bell's readers bought the play London applauded. Steevens's readers bought the play the documents attested. The same decade thus offered the public two Romeo and Juliets under one author's name, each with an editor's warrant, each claiming to be the thing itself.

The rivalry clarified the scholarly tradition by giving it an antagonist it could define itself against. The variorum editors treated the promptbook text as the enemy's country: a tissue of actors' alterations, managers' cuts, and accumulated stage habit, exactly the corruption Pope's preface had theorized and Theobald's method existed to purge. Bell's defenders could reply that the stage's text carried its own authority, the authority of performance continuously renewed since the Restoration, and that a tragedy exists in the playing as much as in the printing. The quarrel was never settled in the eighteenth century because it cannot be settled by evidence alone; it is a quarrel about what a play is, of the same family as the quarrels surveyed in the section on the century's changing answer. But its practical outcome is written in the tradition's subsequent history. The scholarly line won the libraries. Malone's documentary edition of 1790 addressed a public that had learned to want the attested text, and the acting edition retreated to its own durable niche, the theatre's working copies, where promptbook descent continues in every production's rehearsal script.

For the history of editing the episode carries a corrective moral. The succession from Rowe to Malone is often told as the text's rescue from the stage, and the acting edition shows the rescue's price: the scholarly tradition, in securing the documents, severed the printed play from the performed one for two centuries, and the modern movement to reunite them, in editions that record performance variants and in theatres that advertise uncut texts, is a negotiation between Bell's inheritance and Malone's. The tomb scene states the difference in a single image. In the documents, Romeo dies believing Juliet dead, and she wakes to find him gone; the pathos is ironic and solitary. In Garrick's performed text, the warrant of Bell's edition, she wakes in time for recognition and farewell; the pathos is mutual and theatrical. Every editor of the scholarly line prints the documents' version and footnotes the stage's. The footnote is the scar where the two publics were joined, and it descends, like so much else on the page, from decisions the eighteenth century made about which authority an editor serves.

## The Readers Who Bought the Edited Play

Behind the editors stood the buyers, and the buyers changed as the century turned. Rowe's six octavo volumes addressed the new reading class of the Augustan town: professionals, merchants, clergy, and the women readers whose presence in the market for polite literature publishers had begun to court. For such readers the tragedy arrived as literature, encountered in the parlour rather than the playhouse, and Rowe's presentational reforms answer their needs precisely: a named place for every scene, a list of persons to consult, directions that replace the imagination's guesswork. The play's extraordinary afterlife as a love story, the property of young readers and especially of young women, begins in this domestication of the text. Juliet became a heroine of the page in the century that made the page habitable.

Pope's subscribers bought a monument; Hanmer's buyers bought furniture; Johnson's bought a philosophy with plays attached. Each edition thus recruited the tragedy to a different idea of what reading Shakespeare was for, and the text adjusted to its recruitment. The demotions that puzzle modern readers made sense to buyers who wanted their admiration pre-sorted. The variorum's bulk made sense to buyers for whom owning Shakespeare meant owning the conversation about Shakespeare. By the time Malone published, the discriminating buyer had been trained by eighty years of editions to expect evidence: an apparatus, a chronology, a life built from records. Malone's triumph was to satisfy the expectation the tradition itself had created, a neat demonstration that editors educate their own market.

The readers also included the theatre's, and the traffic between page and stage ran in both directions all century. Actors performed from texts descended from the edited tradition, promptbooks absorbed the editors' divisions and directions, and the stage's needs flowed back into print in the form of acting editions that trimmed and regularized for performance. The tragedy on the eighteenth-century stage was often heavily adapted, Garrick's version chief among the adaptations, and the page's editors watched the stage's liberties with a scholar's horror that usefully sharpened their sense of custody. The printed text became, partly in reaction, the place where the play was kept entire against the playhouse's economies. When modern readers prize the full text, all five acts uncut, they are Malone's heirs in attitude even when they read a paperback: the conviction that the play whole, documented and explained, is the play true, is an eighteenth-century conviction, won by editors against both the printers' decay and the theatre's convenience.

## Malone and the Documentary Turn in Editing Romeo and Juliet

Edmond Malone's edition of 1790, The Plays and Poems of William Shakspeare in ten volumes, closed the century by changing the kind of thing an editor was. Malone was trained in the law, and he brought a lawyer's respect for records into a field that had run on taste, wit, and collation. His Shakespeare rests on archives: the Stationers' Register entries that date the early editions, the papers of the theatrical manager Philip Henslowe, parish registers, wills, court records, the documentary sediment of the Elizabethan theatre. Where his predecessors had argued from the text outward, Malone argued from the record inward, and the text of every play in his edition stands on a foundation of dated, cited, checkable fact. His Attempt to Ascertain the Order in Which the Plays Attributed to Shakspeare Were Written, first published in Steevens's edition of 1778 and revised for his own of 1790, made chronology an editorial instrument; in Malone's ordering the tragedy of the Veronese lovers stands in 1596, placed there by an argument from the early editions, the contemporary allusions, and the records of the stage.

For this play the documentary turn had concrete results. The early quartos ceased to be curiosities and became dated legal objects with printers, publishers, and registrations that could be investigated; the question of what the first and second quartos were, which the following centuries would argue at length, could not even be properly asked before Malone's generation fixed the bibliographical facts. His text of the tragedy, eclectic like his predecessors' but argued from a fuller collation and a stricter respect for the early witnesses, displaced the Johnson-Steevens text as the scholarly standard, and his notes absorbed and corrected the variorum accumulation with a lawyer's instinct for what counted as evidence. Later editors quibbled with his readings for a century without displacing his method. Even his errors were documentary errors, falsifiable in principle, and that falsifiability is the signature of the new regime: an editor could now be wrong in a way that could be proved.

Malone also redrew the editor's relationship to the author's life. Rowe's anecdotal Life, compiled from Betterton's hearsay, had stood at the head of the tradition; Malone replaced anecdote with record, pursuing Shakespeare through Stratford documents and London archives and printing what the evidence supported, no more. The biography and the text underwent the same discipline together, and the pairing is the deepest statement of the documentary turn: both the words and the life were to be recovered from witnesses, not composed by wits. Malone died in 1812 with a larger edition unfinished; his papers passed to James Boswell the younger, whose twenty-one volume variorum of 1821, the third variorum, carried Malone's text and notes, augmented from his manuscripts, into the nineteenth century as its standard Shakespeare. The tradition from Rowe to Malone thus ends not with a full stop but with an institution, the scholarly edition as an accumulating public record, which the following centuries inherited entire.

Malone's treatment of the play's chronology shows the documentary method earning its keep on a question taste could not settle. Earlier editors had floated the tragedy vaguely in the author's career, guided by its manner. Malone assembled the fixed points: the registrations and printings of the early quartos at the century's end, the contemporary references to the lovers' story on the stage, the internal evidence of style weighed against the dated plays around it. His placement of the play in 1596, refined by his successors to the middle of that decade, still frames the discussion, and the manner of its reaching matters more than the year: a date argued from records can be corrected by records, while a date asserted from sensibility can only be contradicted by another sensibility. The same discipline governed his handling of the early texts' descent. Where Rowe had accepted the last Folio as the natural copy and Pope had rummaged the quartos for beauties, Malone treated each edition as a witness with a pedigree to be established before its testimony could be weighed, the legal habit of examining a document's provenance before admitting its evidence.

The contrast with the tradition's opening could hardly be sharper. Rowe's edition asked the reader to trust a dramatist's tact. Malone's asked the reader to check a lawyer's citations. Between those two requests lies the entire professionalization of editing, and the play's text is its most legible record, because the play's witnesses are so diverse that every editor's method left distinguishable marks upon it. A reader who compares the tragedy in Rowe, in Pope, in Johnson, and in Malone compares four theories of knowledge wearing the same words.

Malone's editorial character completes the century's arc of temperaments. Rowe had been a practitioner, Pope a judge, Theobald a methodizer, Warburton a combatant, Johnson a custodian, Capell a collator, Steevens an illustrator. Malone was an investigator, and investigation proved to be the temperament the office finally required, because it subordinated the editor's personality to the evidence most completely. The point is not that Malone lacked personality; his feuds, including the rupture with Steevens over borrowed credit, were as warm as any in the century. The point is that his edition's authority does not depend on his personality surviving inspection. It depends on registers that any reader may consult, and an authority so founded can be inherited, corrected, and extended by strangers. That inheritability is what makes 1790 a terminus. After Malone, editing Romeo and Juliet is a profession with archives. Before him, it was a succession of remarkable men.

## What Counts as the Play: The Century's Changing Answer

Underneath the quarrels about readings runs a deeper quarrel about what the play is, and the succession can be reread as a sequence of answers to that single question. For Rowe, the play was the received repertory text: the words as the theatrical and Folio tradition delivered them, to be made presentable. His answer located the work in transmission, in what had come down, and it licensed presentation while forbidding archaeology. For Pope, the play was the author's genius as recoverable by taste: the work existed underneath its transmission, corrupted by players and printers, and the editor's judgement was the instrument of its recovery. That answer licensed demotion and conjecture, and its weakness is written across Pope's page, the impossibility of distinguishing the genius recovered from the preference exercised.

For Theobald, the play was a historical document problem: the work existed in its witnesses, plural and disagreeing, and editing meant reasoning from their evidence under rules that other scholars could inspect. For Johnson, the play was a classic under custody: the work existed in the old copies, and the editor's honour lay in transmitting it with explanation rather than improvement. For Capell, the play was its earliest substantive state: the work stood nearest its origin in the witnesses closest to the author's papers and the first performances, and editing meant building the text up from those witnesses alone. For Malone, the play was a documented event in theatrical and publishing history: the work included its dates, its registrations, its performances, its records, and an edition that suppressed the documentation suppressed part of the play.

Each answer survives inside the modern edition, layered like the text itself. The modern editor prints a single reading text, Rowe's presentational heir; exercises judgement among variants, Pope's heir under discipline; argues from the witnesses, Theobald's heir; glosses rather than alters, Johnson's heir; builds on the early quarto, Capell's heir; and surrounds the whole with dated documentary apparatus, Malone's heir. The plurality that embarrassed the century's beginning became, by its end, the definition of completeness. Romeo and Juliet is an unusually pure case of the transformation, because its two early states differ so markedly that no editor could pretend the documents agreed. The play forced the question what counts as the play, and the century's editors, answering it in turn, invented the modern discipline by their disagreements.

That invention had one more stage to run inside the period itself, and Malone supplied it: the admission that the editor's answer is provisional. Rowe's page admits no doubt; Pope's admits nothing but his own. Malone's edition, by contrast, builds its doubts into its structure, printing the evidence on which a successor might reverse him. The scholarly edition as an open system, corrigible by design, is the form in which the nineteenth and twentieth centuries received the play, and the form in which a reader still receives it whenever an apparatus records the reading not chosen. The tragedy of the Veronese lovers thus became the site of a second, quieter drama, the drama of its own transmission, and the editors from Rowe to Malone are that drama's cast.

The layering has a practical consequence every serious reader eventually meets. When two modern editions disagree about a word in the balcony scene or a direction in the tomb, the disagreement is seldom new: it is the succession arguing with itself across three centuries, Capell's witness against Pope's ear, Malone's record against Johnson's scruple, with the modern editors as counsel for parties long dead. Knowing the parties transforms the reading of any apparatus from a chore into a narrative, and the narrative, once learned, cannot be unlearned. The play acquires a second plot, running beneath the feud of the Montagues and Capulets: the feud of the editors over who may speak for the text, a feud with its own alliances, betrayals, and exhausted peaces, ending, provisionally, in the treaty the modern page embodies.

## What the Eighteenth Century Left in the Modern Text

Open a modern scholarly edition, Brian Gibbons's Arden text or Jill Levenson's Oxford text, and the eighteenth century is visible on every page, most of it unsigned. The words stand in modern spelling because Rowe's generation decided that the author's accidentals were the printers' business and the reader's comfort the editor's. The scenes arrive numbered, located, and populated by directions that tell the story of entrances and deaths, a presentational system Rowe founded and his successors filled. The punctuation guides the eye grammatically, by a logic the century imposed on the rhetorical pointing of the quartos. The text rests on the second quarto because Capell's principle, ratified by Malone's documents, demoted the Folio line that Rowe had trusted. The notes explain the hard words because Theobald, Johnson, and Steevens made explanation an editor's trade. Even the disagreements are inherited: when modern editors dispute a reading, they dispute it with collations and records, in the courtroom Theobald designed and Malone furnished. A full account of the inheritors belongs to the comparison of [Modern Editions Compared: Arden, Oxford, Folger](/2026/06/12/modern-editions-compared-arden-oxford/), and the Oxford case in particular to the study of [Jill Levenson and the Oxford Edition](/2027/06/18/jill-levenson-oxford-edition/); the point here is the depth of the debt those editions acknowledge.

The debt includes specific habits of presentation that readers mistake for the play itself. Editorial stage directions, printed in brackets by scrupulous modern editors and without brackets by careless ones, descend from Rowe's amplifications: the early texts seldom say who draws, who dies where, who exits in whose arms, and the choreography familiar from the page is a reconstruction begun in 1709. The division of the play into its numbered scenes, whose own history is traced in the account of [Who Divided the Play Into Acts and Scenes?](/2026/05/29/act-and-scene-divisions-history/), reached modern readers through the Folio line Rowe regularized, and the text beneath those divisions reached them through the quarrel of the quartos and Folio that the century slowly settled, a settlement unintelligible without the study of [Romeo and Juliet in the First Folio (1623)](/2026/04/17/romeo-and-juliet-first-folio-1623/). Keeping the century's variants straight is the sort of collation labour for which a structured research vault such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists, since the argument of this article depends on holding a dozen editions in view at once.

What the century left can be set out most compactly as a timeline of the succession itself, which is the article's findable artifact.

No single editor made the modern play. The succession made it, by correction and counter-correction, and the timeline's value lies in showing the corrections as a sequence rather than a blur.

### What did the editors leave for the next century to undo?

They left their own invisibility. The next century inherited texts in which Rowe's directions, Capell's choices, and a hundred absorbed emendations sat unmarked inside the play's apparent facts. The work left undone was exposure: printing the evidence openly, separating witness from editor on the page, so readers could see the hidden hands.

## What the Editors Said They Were Doing: The Prefaces

The succession can be read in its prefaces, where each editor announced a theory of his office and, usually, an indictment of his predecessor. Rowe's prefatory matter is modest, a publisher's presentation rather than a manifesto, and its modesty is itself a position: the editor as a servant of the author's fame, offering the works decently dressed. Pope's preface of 1725 is the century's first great editorial performance, a brilliant essay that made the corruption of the text its premise and the editor's taste its remedy, arguing that an author so injured by actors and printers could be recovered only by a judgement equal to his own. The confidence is the content. Pope's preface taught the public to want an editor who was himself a poet, and the lesson cost the tradition thirty years.

Theobald's critical writings answer with a counter-theory stated as method: the editor as a scholar among documents, bound to show his evidence. Johnson's Preface of 1765, the most celebrated document in the whole history, contains the office's mature philosophy alongside its literary criticism. Johnson apologized for the necessity of notes with the aphorism that notes are necessary evils, a confession that commentary is scaffolding rather than building; he described his collations with candour about their disappointments; and he fixed the editor's duty as the recovery and explanation of what the author wrote, under a standing suspicion of the editor's own ingenuity. Capell, characteristically, let the work argue for itself, his prefatory statements dry and procedural beside Johnson's eloquence, as if to demonstrate that an edition founded on collation needs no rhetoric. Malone's prefatory and biographical writings complete the sequence by changing the subject: the editor as investigator reports what the records contain, and the report's authority lies in its citations rather than its style.

Read in sequence, the prefaces trace the same migration the timeline table displays, from authority claimed by status, through authority claimed by taste, to authority claimed by evidence. They also reveal the tradition's engine of change, which was generational disrespect. Nearly every preface in the line is an act of patricide: Pope against the players and printers, Theobald against Pope, Warburton against Theobald, Johnson against the conjecturers, Malone against anecdote itself. The disrespect was productive because the documents survived each editor to judge him. A tradition in which the newest preface routinely convicts the last is a tradition with a built-in correction mechanism, and the mechanism, more than any single editor's virtue, accounts for the direction of travel from 1709 to 1790. The modern edition's introduction, with its ritual account of copy-text, collations, and departures from predecessors, is the preface's descendant, still performing the same double act of homage and patricide in politer prose.

One further continuity deserves note. Every preface in the succession addresses two audiences at once, the readers who will use the text and the scholars who will judge it, and the balance between the addresses shifts measurably across the century. Rowe writes almost wholly for readers; Malone writes almost wholly for the record, trusting readers to follow. The modern scholarly edition inherits Malone's balance and Rowe's ambition simultaneously, which is why its front matter has swollen into the textual essay, a genre that must seduce the common reader and satisfy the specialist in the same pages. The difficulty of that double address is the eighteenth century's parting gift to its successors, still unsolved, still generative.

## How the Story Gets Told: The Historians' Quarrel

The succession has been narrated in two competing ways, and the disagreement between the narratives is itself part of the article's subject, because each narrative quietly instructs its readers in what editing is. The older narrative, which the editors' own descendants in the variorum tradition first composed and which Peter Seary's scholarship on Theobald refined rather than replaced, tells a story of emancipation: darkness under the unedited Folios, a dawn of presentation under Rowe, a false noon of taste under Pope, and then the long victory of method through Theobald, Capell, and Malone, with Warburton as the cautionary villain and Johnson as the conscience. On this telling, the history of editing is the history of error yielding to evidence, and the modern scholarly edition is its justified conclusion.

The revisionist narrative, associated above all with Simon Jarvis's Scholars and Gentlemen and with Marcus Walsh's study of eighteenth-century literary editing, distrusts the emancipation plot. Jarvis reads the gentleman-editors, Pope and Hanmer and Warburton, as practitioners of a coherent alternative enterprise: editing as a branch of polite letters, in which the editor's taste, learning, and judgement were the instruments a reading culture actually wanted applied to its classics. Walsh complicates the hero side of the story as well, showing how much of Theobald's method had roots in the scholarly editing of classical and Miltonic texts, and how the period's editors of every party shared assumptions the emancipation narrative hides. Sonia Massai presses the correction further back in time, arguing that editorial agency in Shakespeare's texts began in the print shop long before Rowe, among compositors, correctors, and publishers, so that a history organized around famous editors mistakes the visible peaks for the range. Andrew Murphy's account adds the material frame this article adopted earlier: the story is unintelligible without the copyright, the publishers, and the market that paid for each successive Shakespeare.

The disagreement is genuine and consequential, and it adjudicates, on inspection, into domains. About the text of this play, the emancipation narrative is substantially right: the readings a modern edition prints descend through the victory of collation and documentary method, and no rehabilitation of Pope's taste will make his demotions defensible as textual criticism, because they proceeded from a criterion, admiration, that cannot distinguish the author's line from the editor's preference. About the page, the revisionists win: Rowe's presentational settlement and Pope's pointing formed the conditions under which the play could be read at all, and the method party quietly kept those gifts while denouncing their givers. About the profession, Massai and Murphy win: the solitary editor was always a figurehead for a trade, and Malone's archives were the trade's collective memory organized by one tireless man. The verdict distributes the honours because the evidence does. What neither narrative may claim is the whole succession, which is why the timeline artifact matters: the table shows a migration of authority among copy, taste, and record, not a coronation of any single principle.

Jill Levenson, editing this play for the Oxford series at the end of the twentieth century, embodies the distributed verdict in practice. Her text rests on the second quarto as Capell's principle directs; her apparatus records the witnesses as Malone's example teaches; her page is as readable as Rowe's reforms made possible; and her introduction treats the editorial tradition itself as part of the play's meaning, a layer of the work's existence in the world. The modern editor does not stand outside the succession Rowe began. She is its latest term, and her edition's authority rests on showing the succession's work rather than concealing it, which may be the final lesson the eighteenth century teaches: an editor earns trust by displaying the evidence, the choices, and the debts, all three, in the open.

## Improvement or Imposition: The Verdict on Rowe to Malone

The brief's complication, the place where editorial improvement shades into imposition, can now be located precisely, because the century exhibits both in pure forms. Imposition is Pope degrading lines his taste rejected, Warburton rewriting by conjecture, Hanmer dressing a derivative text in authority's clothes, Rowe's invented directions hardening into fact. Improvement is Theobald restoring sense by knowledge of the author's idiom, Capell returning the text to its earliest witnesses, Johnson refusing to let elegance outvote evidence, Malone replacing anecdote with record. The same office produced both, sometimes in the same edition, and the historian's task is not to sort the editors into heroes and villains but to mark the criterion by which their acts divide. The criterion the tradition itself discovered is simple to state: an editorial act is improvement when it submits to the documents and imposition when it submits the documents to the editor.

On that criterion the older heroic narrative, in which Theobald and Malone rescue the text from Pope and Warburton, survives in amended form. Peter Seary's Theobald, the maligned founder of method, withstands the revisionists' scrutiny where the text is concerned: the collation standard won, and the modern edition is its monument. But Simon Jarvis and Marcus Walsh are right about the losers. Pope's edition was not vandalism; it was a coherent attempt to give a reading public a Shakespeare it could use, and its pointing, its metrical care, and its provocative theory of corruption all fed the tradition that displaced it. Sonia Massai's work on the print origins of editing adds the final correction, that much of what the heroic narrative credits to editors began as compositors' and publishers' practice, so that the succession of great men is partly a story the book trade told about itself. The verdict that accommodates all the evidence is distributional: the documents won the text, the readers' editors won the page, and the modern Romeo and Juliet is the treaty between those victories.

One measure of the treaty's stability is that no succeeding century has repealed it. The nineteenth century's Cambridge editors built their text on the collations the eighteenth had pioneered; the twentieth century's bibliographers, Greg and Bowers among them, theorized Capell's and Malone's practice into doctrine; the twenty-first century's editions print a text whose debts to 1709 and 1790 are matters of record in their apparatus. The play that readers love, Juliet at her window crying "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2) to a world that has repeated the line ever since, reaches those readers down a channel dug by nine editors and their quarrels. To know the channel is not to love the play less. It is to know what kind of object a classic text is: not a relic but a rescue, renewed by every generation that finds the documents worth arguing over, and argues better than the generation before.

A last measure of the settlement is the fate of the play's cruxes of presentation in the modern classroom. A student assigned the tragedy reads a text whose scene locations, directions, and pointing descend from Rowe, whose foundation is the quarto Capell enthroned, whose notes descend from Steevens's archive and Johnson's example, and whose disputed readings are disputed in Malone's evidentiary court. The student is not told most of this, because the modern edition's success is measured by how completely the mediation disappears. The editors from Rowe to Malone aimed, at their best, at exactly that disappearance: to make the play present to a reader as if unmediated. This article has argued that the disappearance should be resisted, not from antiquarian piety but because the mediation is part of the work's meaning in the world. A text that nine generations of editors quarrelled over, demoted, restored, collated, and documented is a different cultural object from a text that descended untouched, and the difference is not a defect in the object. It is the object's biography, written in the only ink that survives: the words on the page, in the order, spelling, and company that the succession chose for them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch editors timeline, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch editors timeline is the table in this article setting the succession from Rowe in 1709 to Boswell's Malone variorum of 1821 side by side: each editor's edition, copy and method, and the mark left on this play. The pattern it exposes is a migration of authority. The century opens with the newest Folio copy trusted because it was newest, passes through a phase in which the editor's taste decided what Shakespeare could have written, and closes with authority relocated to the earliest documents and the public records. Every stage of the migration left a permanent feature in the text readers inherit, from Rowe's readable page to Malone's documentary apparatus, so the table doubles as a map of where the modern play came from.

### Q: Who was Nicholas Rowe, and why does his name open the history of editing the play?

Nicholas Rowe was a successful dramatist, later Poet Laureate, whose Works of Mr. William Shakespear of 1709 was the first edition of the plays prepared by a named editor for readers. His name opens the history because everything later editors did presupposes his founding acts: he modernized the spelling and punctuation, regularized the act and scene divisions, named each scene's place, listed the dramatis personae, and amplified the stage directions, converting a printers' text into a readable play. He also prefixed the first biography of Shakespeare. Before Rowe there were reprints; from Rowe there is editing, a continuous tradition of accountable presentation in which each successor corrected the last.

### Q: What copy did Rowe print the play from in 1709?

Rowe printed the play from the Fourth Folio of 1685, the last and most corrupted reprint in the Folio line. He did not collate the early quartos, which in 1709 were rare copies in few hands and carried no special authority in the trade's estimation. His text therefore descends from the Folio tradition descending from 1623, with his modernizations and corrections worked over that base. The choice mattered for a century: the readings of the Folio line, rather than the fuller second quarto of 1599, formed the starting text that Pope judged, Theobald began to correct against the quartos, and Capell finally displaced by returning the play to its earliest substantive witness.

### Q: What did Alexander Pope do to the lines he thought Shakespeare could not have written?

Pope degraded them to the foot of the page, printed in smaller type as probably spurious, while marking the passages he admired with inverted commas as beauties. His preface supplied the theory: the plays had been corrupted by actors' interpolations and printers' ignorance, so an editor of taste could distinguish the author's gold from the players' dross. Across the canon the demotions ran to well over a thousand lines. In this play the suspicion fell heaviest on the bawdy, the prolix, and the excessive, qualities Augustan decorum distrusted. The lines survived, demoted rather than deleted, but the typography taught generations of readers to distrust whatever Pope had doubted.

### Q: Why did Pope make Theobald the hero of the Dunciad?

Pope made Theobald king of the dunces in the Dunciad of 1728 because Theobald's Shakespeare Restored of 1726 had exposed, in patient detail, the errors of Pope's edition of 1725. The satire answered scholarship with ridicule, enthroning the corrector as the patron of dullness, and for decades it fixed Theobald's public reputation. Beneath the personalities ran a structural rivalry: Pope embodied the gentleman-editor whose taste licensed his text, Theobald the scholar-editor whose evidence licensed his, and both men competed within the same Tonson copyright. Posterity divided the spoils with some justice. Pope kept the fame; Theobald's method became the definition of editing itself.

### Q: What was Lewis Theobald's Shakespeare Restored, and what did it prove?

Shakespeare Restored, published in 1726, was Theobald's specimen of the errors in Pope's edition, offered as a demonstration of the method by which such errors should be corrected. It proved that editing could be a discipline with rules: know the author's idiom and sources, collate the early editions instead of trusting the newest reprint, respect grammar and metre as evidence, and emend only where the transmitted text fails and the correction restores all three at once. The book's immediate effect was a famous feud. Its permanent effect was larger. When Theobald's own edition followed in 1733, the first text of the plays prepared on those principles, the programme announced in the pamphlet became the profession's charter.

### Q: What method did Theobald practise that earlier editors had not?

Theobald practised systematic collation joined to argued emendation. Rowe had presented the last Folio intelligibly; Pope had judged by taste. Theobald compared the early quartos and the Folio reading by reading, weighed their authority, and corrected the received text where the witnesses and the sense together condemned it, giving reasons that other scholars could inspect and dispute. He brought to the task knowledge of Shakespeare's sources, of period grammar, and of how copy deforms in a printing house. The method's emblem is his restoration, in Henry V, of the dying Falstaff who "babbled of green fields" from a Folio nonsense reading, a correction no editor has displaced since.

### Q: Why is Hanmer's edition remembered for its pictures rather than its text?

Hanmer's Oxford edition of 1743 and 1744 is remembered for its pictures because its pictures were its achievement. Sir Thomas Hanmer, a baronet editing as a gentleman's pastime, produced the most beautiful Shakespeare of the century, large paper, fine type, and engravings after Francis Hayman's designs, while his text rested chiefly on Pope's, seasoned with conjectures of his own and suggestions borrowed from Warburton. For this play scarcely a reading originates with Hanmer. The edition's historical meaning is therefore diagnostic: by the 1740s a Shakespeare could rise in price and prestige while its scholarship stood still, because the market valued the classic as an object for the library shelf.

### Q: What went wrong with Warburton's edition of 1747?

Warburton's edition practised conjecture as a display of genius, and genius unchecked by collation corrupted the text it claimed to restore. William Warburton, a clergyman of vast learning, had received notes from Theobald during their earlier alliance; his preface then treated his benefactor with contempt while his text absorbed the benefits, and Theobald's friends charged appropriation. The deeper failure was methodological. Where Theobald had taught that emendation is a disciplined inference from evidence, Warburton emended boldly and often, confident that learning licensed invention. Later editors mined his notes for a generation while discarding his text, the clearest verdict the tradition delivers on an edition.

### Q: What did Johnson change as an editor, and what did he refuse to change?

Johnson changed the philosophy of the office more than the words of the text. His edition of 1765 rested on the principle that the old copies are the only authentic sources, that a reading which makes tolerable sense outranks any elegant conjecture, and that the editor's energy belongs in the notes, where difficulties are explained rather than abolished. He refused, accordingly, to emend on suspicion, to regularize what he could interpret, or to let his own celebrated taste overrule a witness. For this tragedy the result was custody: the hard places stood as the documents delivered them, rescued by commentary. After Warburton's fireworks, Johnsonian restraint amounted to a reform of editorial conscience.

### Q: What did Edward Capell do with the old quartos that no predecessor had done systematically?

Capell collated them exhaustively and built his text from them. Over more than twenty years he assembled a working library of the early editions and compared them copy against copy, concluding that authority flows from the earliest substantive witnesses rather than from the newest reprint. For this play the consequence was decisive: his edition of 1767 and 1768 leaned on the second quarto of 1599, demoting the Folio line that Rowe and Pope had followed. He also recorded variants in a form that anticipated the modern apparatus and attended to the stage directions as evidence. His later Notes and Various Readings and his bequest of the collection to Trinity College, Cambridge, turned a private discipline into a public resource.

### Q: How did George Steevens change what a note beneath the text was for?

Steevens made the note documentary. Johnson's notes judge and explain; Steevens's notes illustrate, surrounding a hard word or custom with parallel passages drawn from the literature and material culture of Shakespeare's age. In the Johnson-Steevens editions of 1773 onward, and in Steevens's own revisions through 1793, annotation became an archive: fencing terms, herbs, poisons, street customs, and proverbs all glossed from contemporary evidence, much of it gathered by ransacking books no editor had opened for the purpose. For this play, whose Mantuan apothecary, duelling vocabulary, and Italian colouring depend on such illustration, the change was permanent. Modern notes live on capital Steevens's generation accumulated, even where his accumulation swelled into excess.

### Q: What new kind of evidence did Edmond Malone bring to editing?

Malone brought the archive. Trained in the law, he founded his edition of 1790 on records rather than impressions: the Stationers' Register entries dating the early editions, Philip Henslowe's theatrical papers, parish registers, wills, and court documents, the checkable sediment of the Elizabethan stage. His Attempt to Ascertain the Order in which the plays were written, first published in 1778 and revised in 1790, made chronology an editorial instrument, placing this tragedy in 1596 by argument from documents. The novelty was falsifiability. A predecessor's taste could only be contradicted; Malone's evidence could be examined, corrected, and extended, and editing after him became a cumulative public science rather than a succession of performances.

### Q: What was Malone's edition of 1790, and why did later editors start from it?

Malone's Plays and Poems of William Shakspeare of 1790, in ten volumes, was the first edition of the works built throughout on documentary scholarship: texts argued from full collation and strict respect for the early witnesses, a chronology of the plays, a biography rebuilt from records, and notes that absorbed the variorum tradition while correcting it by evidence. Later editors started from it because retreat was impossible. Its facts could be refined but not unlearned, and its text displaced the Johnson-Steevens standard for a century. James Boswell the younger completed Malone's papers as the twenty-one volume variorum of 1821, carrying his text and method into the next century as its scholarly foundation.

### Q: Who owned the right to print Shakespeare in the eighteenth century, and why does the ownership matter?

The right descended through the stationers who had held the early copy to a consortium dominated by Jacob Tonson and his heirs, the publishers behind Rowe, Pope, and their successors. The ownership matters because it set the conditions under which editing developed. The proprietors' asset was a familiar classic, so editions had to look new without growing strange, a pressure that rewarded visible novelty, handsome presentation, demotions, marked beauties, over invisible collation. Litigation over the claimed perpetual copyright overshadowed Johnson's edition and ended only with the Lords' refusal of perpetuity in 1774, after which editions multiplied and scholarship itself became the selling point that Malone's archives satisfied.

### Q: What is a variorum edition, and where does the line from Johnson and Steevens lead?

A variorum edition prints a stabilized text together with the accumulated notes of many editors, so that the commentary of the whole tradition stands beneath the lines it interprets. The form takes its name from the Latin phrase for an edition with the notes of various scholars. Johnson and Steevens's edition of 1773 first embodied it for Shakespeare at scale; Isaac Reed continued it; Steevens revised it through 1793; and it culminated in the twenty-one volume edition of 1821 that James Boswell built from Malone's papers, the variorum through which the nineteenth century read the plays. The form's virtue is audit: every choice an editor makes can be checked against the recorded choices of his predecessors.

### Q: Why did the century's editors modernize Shakespeare's spelling and punctuation?

The editors modernized because their readers were buying a classic to read, not a document to decipher. Rowe's generation held that spelling and punctuation were the printers' accidentals rather than the author's substance, that the old rhetorical pointing, which marks breath rather than grammar, obstructed a silent reader, and that an intelligible page served the author better than an antiquarian one. The policy conquered completely: every general edition since has printed the play in the spelling and pointing of its own day. The cost is real, since pointing interprets, and the modern reader receives sentences pre-parsed by editorial decisions; but the gain, a tragedy legible to the whole reading nation, is the foundation on which the play's later fame stands.

### Q: Where does editorial improvement shade into imposition in this history?

The shading occurs wherever an editorial act submits the documents to the editor instead of submitting to the documents. Rowe's invented stage directions, supplied so gracefully that readers mistook them for the play's facts, stand at the gentle end. Pope's demotion of lines his taste suspected, typography instructing distrust without evidence, stands in the middle. Warburton's confident conjectures stand at the far end. Against these the tradition itself evolved the test that separates the categories: an act is improvement when it can be argued from the witnesses and the records, in the open, and imposition when its only warrant is the editor's preference. Theobald's method, Capell's collation, and Malone's archives are the history of that test being built.

### Q: How much of a modern page of the play descends from the eighteenth-century editors?

Nearly everything except the words' ultimate source descends from the eighteenth century in some degree. The modern spelling and grammatical pointing continue the modernization Rowe began. The numbered scenes, their locations, the lists of persons, and much of the stage choreography descend through the presentational settlement Rowe founded. The choice of the second quarto as the text's foundation ratifies Capell's principle. The notes explaining hard words begin with Theobald, Johnson, and Steevens. Even the reader's confidence that disputes about readings can be settled by evidence is Malone's inheritance. The words are the early printers'; the page that delivers them, in form, foundation, and explanation, is the eighteenth century's construction.

### Q: Why does no modern editor print Rowe's or Malone's text, if so much of their work survives?

No modern editor prints their texts because the tradition's method forbids inheriting any predecessor's results without re-arguing them from the witnesses. Rowe's readings descend from the Fourth Folio, a base Capell and Malone discredited; Malone's eclecticism, though documentary in spirit, predates the bibliographical analysis that later centuries brought to the quartos. What survives is not their letter but their functions: Rowe's readable page, Theobald's method, Capell's copy principle, Malone's evidentiary standard, all re-earned by each modern edition from the documents afresh. The succession's final lesson is that an editor's authority is never inherited. It is rebuilt, on the foundations the dead editors laid, by every generation that takes the documents down from the shelf.
