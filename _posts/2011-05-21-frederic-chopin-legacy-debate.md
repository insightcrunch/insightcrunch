## Four charges, all of them true

Begin with the measurements by which Frederic Chopin is usually found wanting, stated as plainly as his critics state them. He wrote no symphony. He wrote no opera. He wrote almost nothing that does not require a piano, and the exceptions can be listed in a breath: two concertos from his teens, a cello sonata, a piano trio, a set of Polish songs. Few of his works run longer than ten minutes, and most run far shorter, the length of a song or a dance. A composer is brought before these four measurements, and on every one of them he is found deficient. That is the whole of the standard case against him, and it has been repeated, in roughly this form, for more than a century and a half.

![Frederic Chopin legacy debate, the standard charges against his standing tested against the criteria behind them - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-20.webp)

The first thing to say about the charges is that they are true. This article will not dispute a single one of them, because none of them can be disputed. The catalogue is closed and has been closed since 1849. It contains some two hundred and thirty works, and a reader who searches it for a symphony will not find one, any more than a reader who searches it for an opera, a string quartet or a Mass. The concertos date from 1829 and 1830, when their composer was nineteen and twenty, and after them the orchestra disappears from his output almost entirely. The longest works, the three piano sonatas, the four ballades, the Fantasia in F minor and the Polonaise-Fantaisie, are the exceptions in a catalogue built from nocturnes, mazurkas, waltzes, preludes, études and polonaises. Any defence of Chopin that begins by denying the shape of his output begins by losing, and the honest argument has to start somewhere else.

It starts with a question about the measurements themselves. Every charge laid against Chopin, that he was a miniaturist, that he wrote for one instrument only, that his music is sentimental, rests on criteria built from the German symphonic tradition, and the serious question is not whether he meets those criteria but whether a tradition gets to set the terms on which outsiders are judged. That is the thesis of this article, and everything in it is arranged to test the thesis rather than to decorate it. Because the four charges are factually accurate, the argument cannot be about facts. It has to be about criteria: where they came from, whose interests they served, what they measure well and what they cannot measure at all. A verdict that follows automatically from a yardstick tells us about the yardstick before it tells us about the thing measured.

One question opens here and stays open for two more sections. Who decided that a symphony was the test? Nobody voted on it. No congress of musicians ratified the hierarchy that places a forty-minute orchestral argument at the summit of the art and a four-minute mazurka near its base. The hierarchy arrived gradually, carried by identifiable writers, institutions and habits of thought, and it can be traced. Until it has been traced, the charges hang in the air as if they were laws of nature rather than the customs of one tradition. Section three answers the question with names and dates. First the criteria need to be looked at directly, because they are usually applied without ever being stated, and an unstated criterion cannot be argued with.

## Where the criteria come from

The criteria behind the four charges amount to a single picture of what a serious composer is. A serious composer commands the large public forms, above all the symphony. A serious composer writes for the full range of available media, orchestra, chorus, stage and chamber ensemble, and proves the command of each. A serious composer builds works of substantial duration in which themes are stated, developed and reconciled, because development is where musical thought is held to happen. And a serious composer maintains a certain distance from mere feeling: the music argues, it does not confess. Chopin fails the picture at every point, which is exactly why the picture deserves scrutiny. A test that one tradition's central figures pass by definition and an outsider fails by definition is not a discovery about the outsider. It is a description of the tradition, written in the grammar of a judgment.

The debate can be laid out in one view, and the table below does that. Each charge is given with its factual status, the criterion it silently assumes, the place that criterion comes from and the thing the criterion is structurally unable to measure. The rest of the article is an expansion of these four rows.

The genealogy matters because the criteria present themselves as timeless. They are not. In the eighteenth century the hierarchy of genres placed vocal music, opera and church music at the top, and instrumental music was the servant art, pleasant and suspect in equal measure. The reversal that put the symphony on the throne was the work of a specific generation of German writers and was bound up with the reception of one composer. When the criteria are applied to Chopin, what is really being applied is the outcome of that reversal, frozen into a standard and then mistaken for the nature of music itself. A Polish pianist working in Paris salons in the 1830s was being measured, without anyone saying so, by a settlement reached in German criticism a generation earlier, in a debate he never took part in.

Carl Dahlhaus, the most authoritative historian of the nineteenth century in the twentieth, gave that settlement its modern scholarly form. His history of the period is organised around Beethoven and the symphonic tradition that descends from him, and within its architecture the character piece, the salon genre and the piano miniature occupy the periphery by construction. Dahlhaus did not invent the hierarchy, and his own judgments were subtler than the standard use made of them, but his framework is where the criteria live in their most respectable modern dress. When a modern critic calls Chopin a miniaturist as if the word were a measurement, the authority behind the gesture, acknowledged or not, is this tradition of writing history from the symphony outward.

## The German symphonic tradition as the default standard

The question left open in section one can now be answered. The symphony became the test because a tradition made it the test, in print, in institutions and in the training of everyone who later applied it. The decisive document is early. In 1810 the writer and composer E. T. A. Hoffmann reviewed Beethoven's Fifth Symphony and argued, in terms that reversed the older hierarchy at a stroke, that instrumental music was the most romantic of the arts precisely because it had no words and no function beyond itself. The symphony, the largest instrumental form, inherited the crown that argument created. From that point the German critical tradition built outward: the symphony as the genre in which a composer demonstrated mastery of thematic development, tonal architecture and public utterance at once, and every other genre ranked by its distance from that centre.

Institutions then did what institutions do. Conservatoires taught composition as progress toward the large forms. Concert societies built their programmes around the symphony and used smaller works as relief between them. Historians of music, writing the first general histories of the nineteenth century, needed a spine for the story, and the symphony's line from Haydn through Beethoven to Brahms and beyond supplied one ready-made. A composer who stood outside that line was not refuted by such histories. He was simply placed in a side chapter, among the pianists, the salon composers, the national schools, and the placement did the work of a verdict without the inconvenience of an argument. Chopin's position in the standard histories for a hundred years was exactly that: a chapter of exquisite small things, admired and bracketed in the same gesture.

Jeffrey Kallberg, whose study of Chopin and genre remains the sharpest account of this machinery, showed that the hierarchy of genres was never a neutral ranking of sizes. In the nineteenth century the large forms were coded public, architectural and masculine, and the small forms were coded private, decorative and feminine, and the coding did ranking work that no one had to argue for openly. A nocturne did not merely happen to be short. It belonged to a class of music associated with the domestic interior, with amateur performance and with women listeners and players, and in the value system of the period each of those associations subtracted standing before a note was heard. The criteria, in other words, were loaded at the level of genre before any individual work was examined. This is the second rung of the argument, and it changes the status of everything that follows: the criteria derive from one national tradition and were applied to Chopin by critics working inside it, and some of those criteria carried a social ranking disguised as a musical one.

None of this yet shows that the criteria are wrong. A standard can have a parochial origin and still measure something real, and the case for the symphonic standard, stated at its strongest, gets its own hearing in sections five and nine, where the critical side of this debate is given the best version of itself. What the genealogy establishes is more modest and more damaging to the usual verdict: the standard is a position, not a fact of nature, and a composer who fails it has been measured, not judged. Judging requires the extra step of defending the standard itself, and that step is the one the standard case against Chopin almost never takes. The next four sections take the two largest charges in turn, miniaturism first, and give each side its full strength before any assessment is attempted.

## The miniaturist charge stated properly

The word miniaturist does quiet work in criticism of Chopin, and it is worth slowing down over what it actually claims. It does not claim that the music is bad. Critics who use it usually admire the finish of the pieces, the perfection of the surface, the rightness of every note in its place. What the word claims is that the perfection is the perfection of a small thing: exquisitely made, like a portrait miniature or a jewelled snuffbox, and bounded by the size of its container. A miniaturist, in this usage, is a master whose mastery never had to survive enlargement. The charge is therefore not about quality but about scale and the capacities scale is supposed to prove. Stated properly, it says that Chopin solved only the problems that arise inside a few pages of music for one instrument, and that the harder problems, the ones that arise across forty minutes and a hundred players, were never put to him because he never put them to himself.

### Was Chopin a miniaturist?

Yes, in the plain sense that most of his works are short and written for a single instrument played alone. The word only becomes an accusation when it assumes that size and worth rise together, which is the assumption this article examines rather than grants to either side.

The charge gains force from the company the word keeps. In the critical vocabulary of the nineteenth century, the miniature belonged to the salon and the album, to the gift culture of the souvenir and the keepsake, to music as furnishing for an evening rather than argument for a public hall. To call a composer a miniaturist was to assign him a social address as much as a formal one. Kallberg's work on genre showed how consistently this vocabulary sorted music by the rooms it was played in and the people who played it there, and how the sorting preceded the listening. The mazurka played in a drawing room by a competent amateur and the mazurka played by its composer were the same notes, but the genre label had already decided the ceiling of what could be claimed for them.

There is also a harder, more technical form of the charge that deserves to be separated from the social one. It holds that certain musical capacities can only be demonstrated at length: the management of large-scale tonal tension across a long span, the development of a theme until it becomes something its opening did not predict, the pacing of contrast so that a return feels earned rather than merely repeated. On this form of the charge, brevity is not a social failing but an evasion, a refusal of the examination every major composer is expected to sit. The next section states that case as strongly as it can be stated, because it is the strongest thing the critical side of this debate has to say, and a defence of Chopin that never faces it defends nothing.

The metaphor inside the word came from the visual arts, where the portrait miniature was a recognised and honourable craft with its own masters, its own prices and its own ceiling. Nobody called a miniaturist a failed painter of altarpieces; the insult was invented in the transfer to music, where scale had been moralised by the symphonic argument. The album leaf, the bagatelle, the impromptu, the whole economy of short piano pieces that the nineteenth century produced for its domestic market, sat under the same metaphorical cloud, and a composer whose output consisted of such pieces could be dismissed by category before any specimen was examined. It is worth keeping the metaphor in view, because metaphors in criticism are rarely audited, and this one smuggled a conclusion about worth inside a description of size for a hundred and fifty years.

## The case that small forms are a limitation

Here is the case against the small forms, made as its best advocates make it. Music is an art that unfolds in time, and the deepest difficulties of composition are difficulties of duration. Anyone with a gift for melody and harmony can fill four beautiful minutes; the phrases suggest themselves, the proportions are felt in a single span of attention, and the whole can be held in the mind at once. What separates the masters from the gifted is what happens when the span exceeds what attention can hold: when a movement must generate its own momentum across hundreds of bars, when a theme stated in the first minute must still be generating consequences in the twentieth, when a slow movement and a finale must answer an opening movement the listener can no longer hear except in memory. The symphony and the sonata are not arbitrary hurdles. They are the forms in which those difficulties are concentrated, and a composer who never attempts them leaves the central question about his powers permanently unanswered.

The case continues with the orchestra. Writing for an orchestra is not writing for a bigger piano. It requires imagining sound in combinations that cannot be tested at a desk, balancing masses of tone, giving each section of the ensemble a reason to exist, and sustaining a public rhetoric that addresses a hall rather than a room. Beethoven's development as a composer is inseparable from that discipline: the orchestra forced him outward, into argument, rhetoric and architecture. A composer who writes only for his own instrument and his own hands risks a closed circle, in which the medium's habits and the player's habits reinforce each other and nothing external ever corrects them. On this view the two concertos are the most telling works in the catalogue, not because they are weak but because they are early: the one encounter with the full apparatus of public music-making happened before he was twenty-one, and he never returned to it. The mature composer chose the room over the hall every time, and the choice, repeated for twenty years, looks less like a preference than like a boundary.

The sentiment of the case deserves respect as well as its logic. Critics who press it are often defending a real value: the belief that art should attempt the largest thing it can conceive, that ambition is a virtue in itself, and that a culture which contents itself with miniatures has lowered its sights. Dahlhaus gave this belief its scholarly dignity by treating the symphonic tradition as the place where the nineteenth century thought its deepest musical thoughts, and the implication for everyone outside the tradition was left to draw itself. If the symphony is where the century's musical thinking happened, then a composer of miniatures, however exquisite, was thinking smaller thoughts. The case does not need to insult Chopin. It only needs to rank the questions, and to hold that the questions he answered were, in the end, the lesser ones. That is the strongest form of the miniaturist charge, and no honest treatment of his standing can proceed without carrying its weight.

The examination metaphor that runs through the case was, for much of the period, an actual examination. Conservatoires set the large forms as the curriculum's summit: the student symphony, the prize cantata, the Prix de Rome's required genres ranked the young composer by his command of scale, and the teachers who set those exercises were trained in the same hierarchy that the critics applied in print. A composer who never passed through that gate, as Chopin, formed in Warsaw and launched by the salon, never did, carried the mark of the unexamined in the eyes of the examining profession. The strongest form of the case, then, is partly institutional: the large forms were where the profession certified its own, and the uncertified man's small forms were read as the work of someone the profession had never passed. That reading is precisely what the next section disputes, not by denying the profession's standards but by asking whether its certificate was ever the same thing as the music's worth.

## The case that small forms were the point

The answer begins by refusing the premise that the small forms were a refuge from difficulty. Consider what the catalogue actually shows when it is read as a working life rather than as a list of sizes. The mazurkas alone number more than fifty and span the whole career, from the composer's late teens to the final year of his life, and they behave not like repetitions of a success but like a laboratory that was never closed. The études take the most mechanical genre in piano teaching, the exercise, and turn each one into a study of a single pianistic problem raised to the level of a poem. The preludes of Opus 28 compress into a few pages a range of harmonic behaviour that other composers spread across acts. If this is miniaturism, it is miniaturism of a strange kind: not the avoidance of hard problems but the decision to solve hard problems at a scale where every note is exposed and nothing can be hidden by mass or motion.

Jim Samson, the analyst who has written most searchingly about Chopin's structures, makes the complementary point about the works that are not small in any meaningful sense. The four ballades occupy ten or twelve minutes each, but their forms are inventions: neither sonata nor rondo nor variation set, they build large spans out of two contrasting ideas whose returns are progressively transformed, and the Fourth Ballade in particular sustains an argument of a complexity that analysts are still unpacking. The Barcarolle and the Polonaise-Fantaisie, from the last years, go further, dissolving the boundary between the character piece and the large design until the categories stop describing the music. On Samson's reading, the late Chopin was converging on the large form from inside the small one, and the trajectory suggests not a composer who could not enlarge but one who was rebuilding enlargement on his own terms when his health ran out. The twenty years of small forms look, in this light, like preparation rather than limitation.

There is finally the question of what the hierarchy of sizes is for. Kallberg's genre research cuts both ways in this debate, and the critical side should notice that it does: if the ranking of large over small was produced by a social sorting of genres, by rooms and audiences and the gender of the people in them, then the ranking cannot be used as evidence about the music without arguing in a circle. The hierarchy is the thing under examination. Citing it to convict the small forms of smallness assumes precisely what the trial was called to decide. The defence does not claim that size is irrelevant, or that a nocturne does what a symphony does. It claims that the inference from short to lesser is an inference the nineteenth century built for its own reasons, that the reasons were partly social rather than musical, and that the music, examined closely, keeps failing to be as limited as the inference requires. The next section tests that claim on the genre where the experimentation is densest.

The period itself supplies a parallel that the symphonic hierarchy never absorbed comfortably. The nineteenth century was the great age of the lyric poem, the short story and the fragment, forms in which literature placed some of its highest ambitions precisely at small scale, and no critic of Goethe's lyrics or of the Romantic fragment argued that brevity was a failure to write an epic. The fragment, in the German Romantic theory that Chopin's generation breathed, was a deliberate modern form: complete in itself, pointing beyond itself, valued for its concentration. Music criticism never granted its small forms the same philosophical dignity, largely because music's hierarchy had been settled by the symphonic argument before the fragment's prestige could transfer. The double standard is a matter of record rather than of rhetoric, and it marks the small-form defence as something more than special pleading: the claim is that one art was allowed its miniatures as masterpieces and another was not, and that the difference lies in the histories, not in the works.

## What the mazurkas do that a symphony cannot

The mazurka is the right test case because it is the genre where Chopin worked longest, most privately and with the least regard for public effect. A mazurka is a stylised Polish dance in triple time whose identity lives in its rhythm: the accent falls where the dancers need it, displaced onto the second or third beat, and the phrase lengths breathe irregularly against the bar lines. A symphony cannot use this material at this scale, not because the symphony is too grand for it but because the effect depends on intimacy of timing. The displaced accent has to be felt in the body of a listener close enough to sense a hesitation of a fraction of a second. Enlarged and orchestrated, the hesitation becomes a notation; kept at the piano, under one pair of hands, it stays a gesture. The mazurkas are full of gestures of this kind that exist only at their native scale, and a criticism that counts scale against them is counting their enabling condition as their defect.

The harmony tells the same story at greater depth, and this is where the third rung of the argument lands. The harmonic experimentation that the century's history usually locates in the opera house and the symphony, the loosening of tonal certainty, the chord that refuses to declare its key, the modulation that arrives by slide rather than by argument, happens in Chopin inside the mazurkas, in pieces three minutes long that a hostile criticism had filed as dances. The Mazurka in A flat major, Opus 17 Number 4, opens with chords that withhold their tonal allegiance so successfully that analysts still describe the opening in competing ways, and it does so not in a development section under the obligations of a large form but in the first bars of a dance. The late mazurkas push further: modal inflections drawn from Polish folk practice, including the raised fourth degree that gives the Lydian colour, sit inside phrases of an ambiguity that later composers would need whole scenes to achieve. Samson's analyses of these pieces treat them as the frontier of Chopin's harmonic language, and the frontier is in the miniatures.

The mazurkas also do the one thing the symphonic tradition most prizes, and do it across a span the symphony cannot match. They develop. Not within a single piece, usually, but across the set of more than fifty, published and posthumous, that tracks one composer's thinking about rhythm, mode and phrase across twenty years. A listener who plays them in sequence hears a mind returning to the same dance and finding it changed each time, which is the experience the symphonic tradition claims for the nine symphonies of Beethoven: a genre used as an instrument of thought over a lifetime. The unit of ambition is different. In Beethoven it is the individual large work; in the mazurkas it is the genre itself, treated as a single continuing composition in fifty instalments. Whether that difference is a lesser ambition or merely a different one is the whole question of this article in miniature, and it will not be settled by measuring the instalments.

The technical record inside the genre supports the larger claim. The Mazurka in C sharp minor, Opus 50 Number 3, contains a passage of strict canon, voices imitating each other at the unison inside a dance piece, a device from the learned style of Bach transplanted into the most national and least academic of forms. The last mazurka of all, the F minor piece published after his death as Opus 68 Number 4, compresses into two pages a chromatic argument so restless that the music seems to circle its own tonic without ever being granted rest. These are not the works of a composer decorating a small space. They are the works of a composer who had discovered that the small space, worked for twenty years, could be made to hold anything he knew, including the counterpoint and the harmonic daring that the large forms were supposed to monopolise.

## The single-instrument objection

The second charge is the simplest to state and the hardest to phrase fairly. Chopin wrote for the piano. Setting aside the two early concertos, in which the piano is in any case the protagonist and the orchestra the setting, and setting aside a cello sonata, a trio and a group of songs from his youth, the entire mature output is for piano alone: one instrument, one player, ten fingers. No other composer of comparable standing confined himself so completely. The objection built on this fact holds that the confinement itself is the limitation, whatever the quality of what was produced inside it. A composer, on this view, is an inventor of sounding worlds, and the world of one instrument, however deeply explored, is a province where the art's other territories, the blended colours of the orchestra, the massed voice of the chorus, the drama of the stage, lie under a permanent interdict. The objection is not that the piano works are poor. It is that we know, and can only ever know, a fraction of what their composer might have been.

### Does writing only for piano limit a composer?

Only if command of many media is the test, which is the assumption under examination. It limits the evidence available about a composer: whole dimensions of his ability go untested. It does not by itself limit the music, whose depth is measured inside the medium he chose.

Two features of the situation complicate the objection before it is even argued. The first is that the piano of the 1830s and 1840s was not a fixed, narrow medium but the most rapidly evolving instrument of its age, the machine through which the century heard nearly all its music, in transcription, in the home, in the salon and increasingly in the concert hall. To write for the piano in 1835 was not to withdraw from the musical world; it was to write for its central distribution network and its most contested frontier of sonority. The second complication is that Chopin's piano writing is saturated with the other media he supposedly renounced. His melody is modelled on Italian opera singing, studied at first hand in the Paris theatres and urged on his pupils as the source of all phrasing. His textures quote the orchestra by implication, the horn call in a left-hand figure, the string tremor in a repeated chord. The instrument is one; the imagined sound-world behind it is not.

A third complication concerns the road not taken, because it was not refused out of ignorance. Chopin loved the opera house with a connoisseur's passion, followed the Italian seasons in Paris season by season, and counted singers among his closest artistic reference points; the man who supposedly lacked the ambition for the stage spent his evenings in it, memorising Bellini. He also knew the orchestral repertoire of his time from the inside of the audience and the salon, where symphonies were domesticated in piano transcription and judged nightly by the very musicians who wrote them. His confinement to the piano was a decision taken in full view of the alternatives, renewed year after year in a city that would have welcomed a Chopin opera with curiosity at the least. Decisions of that kind are evidence about a composer's judgment of his own gift. The objection treats the same facts as evidence about the gift's limits, and the difference between those two readings is the difference this whole debate turns on: whether a boundary drawn by the composer is to be respected as knowledge or overridden as confession.

## The case that breadth of medium is a real criterion

The strongest form of the objection deserves the same patience given to the strongest form of the miniaturist charge, because it too defends something real. Breadth of medium is not a bureaucratic requirement. It tests capacities that no single medium can test: the imagination of timbre, the construction of public rhetoric, the management of forces too large to be one person's hands, and the willingness to submit private invention to the resistance of other musicians. The history of the art gives the criterion its evidence. Beethoven's imagination was enlarged by every medium he entered; the string quartet taught him a concentration the symphony needed, and the symphony taught the quartets a scale of utterance. Schubert's songs and Schubert's symphonies correct and complete each other. A composer who works in one medium risks mistaking the medium's conveniences for the art's necessities, and the risk is not imaginary. Pianists who compose have filled the nineteenth century with fluent, idiomatic, forgettable piano music, and the fluency is exactly the problem: the instrument rewards its natives too readily, and only an external discipline separates invention from facility.

The concertos return here as evidence, and the critical side is entitled to them. Their orchestral writing has been found wanting by generation after generation of musicians, not hostile ones, and the works have been repeatedly reorchestrated, by Karl Tausig in the nineteenth century and by others since, in an effort to supply what the original scoring lacks. If the young Chopin had possessed the orchestral imagination of a Weber or a Mendelssohn, the objection runs, the concertos would show it, and the mature silence that followed them would read as a choice. Instead the record suggests a gift that found its element at the keyboard and never seriously sought another. On the criterion of breadth, then, the verdict is not merely that the evidence is missing. The one experiment that was run points the same way, and a fair judge is allowed to say so. This section has given the objection its full force because the article's answer, in the next section, is not that the criterion is worthless. It is that the criterion measures a real thing and then claims, without warrant, to be measuring everything.

Behind the criterion stands an older discipline that gives it real moral weight: the working obligation. Bach wrote cantatas because Leipzig required them weekly, Haydn wrote symphonies because Esterháza required them by the season, and the obligation forced both men outward into problems they would never have set themselves in freedom. The Kapellmeister tradition treated breadth as a duty of the office, and the office produced the art's central monuments often enough that the association of range with seriousness is not a prejudice but a summary of experience. A freelance composer in a Paris salon owed nobody a Mass, a symphony or an opera, and the critical side is entitled to wonder whether the freedom was a liberation or an exemption. The question is a genuine one, and the answer cannot be that obligations are irrelevant to achievement. It can only be the harder answer, argued next, that this particular freelancer imposed the obligation on himself in a different currency: not range of medium but depth of return, the same dances and nocturnes demanded again each year at a higher standard than the year before.

## The case that depth within one medium is a real criterion

Begin with what depth in a single medium actually consists of, because the phrase is used loosely. It is not doing the same thing beautifully for twenty years. It is the progressive discovery, inside one set of physical constraints, of resources that the medium's other masters did not find. By that test the piano after Chopin is a different instrument from the piano before him, and the difference is documentable rather than rhapsodic. He rebuilt the relationship between the hands, freeing the left from accompaniment patterns into a partner in the argument. He turned the sustaining pedal from a blurring device into an instrument of harmonic resonance, marked in his scores with a precision his editors have spent a century trying to honour. He made the piano sing in long, ornamented lines learned from Bellini and the Italian stage, and then made it think in counterpoint learned from Bach, whom he revered and taught. The twenty-seven études alone re-founded piano technique: each takes a mechanical difficulty and discovers the music hidden inside it, and every pianist since has been educated by them whether or not the debt is acknowledged.

Depth also has a social dimension that breadth lacks. The composer who writes for the largest forces writes for institutions, and institutions mediate everything: the work is performed by strangers, in public, under conditions the composer supervises at a distance. The composer of piano music in Chopin's Paris wrote for a circuit of rooms in which he himself performed, taught the pieces to pupils who carried them across Europe, and heard the results. The feedback was immediate and personal, and the refinement it produced, the sense that every piece is finished to the last semiquaver, is the audible trace of that circuit. Whether depth of this kind equals breadth in value is a genuine question, and this article does not pretend it answers itself. What can be said is that the two criteria are different instruments measuring different properties, and that a verdict which reports only one of them has not weighed the composer; it has weighed the criterion. The comparison with the many-medium composers, taken next, shows how rarely the two measurements are kept separate in practice.

The contents of the depth deserve one more paragraph of specifics, because depth is an easy word to grant and a hard one to cash. His pupils recorded the instruction that a pianist must learn to sing: attend the Italian opera, listen to the great singers, make the piano do what the voice does with breath. The melody that resulted is not decorated pianism but vocal line transferred to keys, with the ornaments functioning as a singer's graces rather than as display. At the other pole of his art stands the counterpoint. He took the Well-Tempered Clavier with him to Majorca in the winter of 1838 and 1839, began his own teaching from Bach, and filled the late works with canonic and imitative writing that a merely decorative pianist-composer has no use for. Singing line and strict counterpoint are the two disciplines least native to the salon piano, and he imported both into it deliberately. Depth within one medium, in his case, meant refusing the medium's own definition of itself and rebuilding it from the resources of two others, the voice and the organ loft, without ever writing a note for either.

## Comparing him with composers who wrote for everything

The comparison that the objection invites is usually run against Beethoven, and it is worth noticing what happens when it is run against others instead. Franz Schubert wrote for everything: symphonies, quartets, Masses, operas, and more than six hundred songs. The songs are miniatures by any measure, most of them shorter than a mazurka, and a large part of his claim on posterity rests on them. Yet Schubert is never called a miniaturist. He escapes the word because the symphonies and quartets stand in the catalogue beside the songs, certifying that the large forms were within his reach. The certification, notice, is doing the work: the songs themselves are not larger for being certified. If the identical mazurkas had been written by a composer who also produced nine symphonies, the miniaturist charge would lose its footing overnight, although not one note of the mazurkas had changed. A charge that can be dissolved by works it is not about is a charge about the catalogue's shape, not about the music in question.

The reverse comparison is equally instructive. The nineteenth century is full of composers who wrote for everything and are remembered for almost nothing: symphonies correctly built, operas competently staged, quartets in the approved forms, all of it satisfying every criterion in section two and none of it surviving except in footnotes. Breadth was within their reach and depth was not. Their existence does not refute the breadth criterion, but it fixes its value: breadth is at most a enabling condition, a widening of the field on which depth may or may not occur. What the catalogue of a Hummel or a Ries shows is that the criteria of section two can all be met by music that vanishes, and what the catalogue of Chopin shows is that they can all be failed by music that does not. A standard with that track record may still be useful. It cannot be final.

One more comparison belongs here because it disciplines both sides. Liszt, Chopin's exact contemporary and the other great pianist-composer of the age, wrote for everything Chopin avoided: symphonies in all but name, concertos, choral works, an opera in his youth, songs, organ music. By the breadth criterion he should stand unambiguously above his contemporary, and in the nineteenth century's own estimation he often did. The long verdict of the repertoire has been less certain, and the uncertainty is instructive: it suggests that breadth settles less than its advocates claim, and that the qualities listeners finally keep are distributed by laws the criteria only partly capture. What those laws might be is taken up when the greatest-composer claim is stated on explicit criteria in section twenty-seven. For now the instrument question can be left where the evidence leaves it: the objection is real, its strongest form has been heard, and it establishes a fact about the shape of a career. It does not establish the conclusion usually drawn from it.

Mendelssohn sharpens the same point from the opposite direction. He wrote for everything, symphonies, oratorios, concertos, chamber music, and also the Songs Without Words, piano miniatures for the domestic market that are the exact generic cousins of the nocturnes. The miniatures have never cost him his standing, because the oratorios and symphonies stand in the catalogue to certify the range. Yet the Songs Without Words, delightful as they are, do not contain the harmonic or formal adventure of a single Chopin mazurka book, a judgment most pianists would endorse without hesitation. The comparison isolates the variable with laboratory cleanliness: same genre, same market, same decade, one composer certified by breadth and one not, and the uncertified miniatures are the greater music. If the certification is what the ranking rewards, the ranking is measuring the certificate.

## The sentimentality charge

The third charge is the vaguest of the four and the most commonly believed. Chopin's music, it says, is sentimental: it feels too much, too directly, and it invites the listener to feel in the same unguarded way. The nocturnes sigh. The waltzes charm. The melodies, beautiful as everyone concedes them to be, are said to aim at the heart with a directness that serious music is supposed to discipline with structure. Where the miniaturist charge is about size and the instrument charge about range, this one is about tone, and it carries a moral flavour the others lack: sentimentality is not merely a limitation but a small corruption, feeling in excess of what the object earns, emotion taken on credit. A composer convicted of it is not just minor but slightly embarrassing, and the embarrassment has attached itself to his name with a persistence the other charges have not matched.

### Is Chopin's music sentimental?

The scores argue against it. Their markings are spare, their structures exact, and the pupils who studied with him described a master who demanded rhythmic steadiness. The swooning manner attached to his name belongs largely to the tradition that performed him after his death.

The charge opens a question that this article will take four sections to answer, because the answer requires separating three things that are usually merged: what the scores direct, what performers have done with the directions, and what listeners have been taught to hear. Is the sentimentality in the music or in the way it is played? The question is left open here deliberately. It closes in section fifteen, after the origins of the charge have been traced and the performance tradition has been examined as a suspect in its own right. What can be said at the outset is that the charge has a history, and the history is not flattering to the charge. Sentimentality entered the criticism of Chopin early, it travelled with the portrait of the composer as a frail and suffering invalid, and it was reinforced by a vocabulary for his music that the next sections will show was doing social work quite apart from describing sound.

The word itself deserves a moment, because its meaning drifted and the drift is part of the story. In the eighteenth century, sentimental named a valued capacity, the educated responsiveness of feeling that novels and moral philosophy prized. Across the nineteenth century the word curdled: by the time it settled into music criticism it meant feeling in excess of its object, emotion indulged rather than earned, and it carried the suspicion of the parlour, of tears too easily shed by listeners too gently raised. A charge with that history is never a pure description of a score. It is a judgment about audiences as much as about music, about who was imagined to be weeping and where. The modern afterlife of the word, in the marketing that sells this composer as an accompaniment to relaxation and romance, in the compilations of his slowest pages packaged for candlelight, shows the charge and its commercial twin still sharing one assumption: that the music's primary content is a mood. The next section traces where that assumption was manufactured.

There is a final preliminary distinction, without which the four sections that follow will blur. Sentimentality, as the charge uses it, is a property alleged of the music; expressiveness is a property nobody denies it; and the distance between the two words is the distance between feeling governed by form and feeling indulged at form's expense. Every party to this debate agrees that the music is expressive to an unusual degree, saturated with song, with dance, with the rhetoric of longing and defiance. The charge is that the expression is cheaply bought. Cheapness, however, is a claim about workmanship, and workmanship can be inspected: in the voice-leading, in the pacing of climaxes, in the relation between the ornament and the line it ornaments. The sections that follow inspect it, because a charge about workmanship that is never taken to the workbench is a charge about the manufacturer's portrait, and the portrait is a separate exhibit with its own section.

## Where the sentimentality charge originates

Follow the charge back and it separates into three sources, each identifiable. The first is the room. Chopin's Paris was the Paris of the salon, and the salon in the critical imagination of the period was a feminine space: domestic, sociable, governed by taste rather than by argument. Music written for that room inherited its coding. The nocturne, his most characteristic lyric genre, came to him from John Field and was already associated with the evening, the interior and the amateur's music desk. A genre does not carry its reputation in its notes, and the nocturne's reputation preceded every individual nocturne he wrote. When critics reached for the word sentimental, they were often describing the genre's address rather than the piece in front of them.

The second source is the portrait, the figure of the consumptive poet at the piano, which section sixteen examines in detail. A composer imagined as perpetually ill, exiled and lovesick will have his slow movements heard as confessions whether or not they were written as any. The biography, much of it manufactured after his death, taught listeners what to listen for, and what they listened for was feeling. The third source is the most concrete: the amateur market. Chopin's pieces were the glory of the domestic piano, played in thousands of homes by players of modest technique for whom the emotional surface was the accessible part. Music loved by amateurs was suspect to a professional criticism that guarded the border between the concert hall and the drawing room, and the suspicion attached itself to the composer rather than to the circumstances. None of the three sources is a property of the scores. All three were fully formed before most of the music had been heard in public at all.

The critical case, at its strongest, should be stated here as the traps of this debate require, because the charge is not only a prejudice and pretending otherwise weakens the answer. Feeling in music can be unearned. There is a kind of melody that flatters the listener's emotions without costing the composer any thought, and the nineteenth century produced it in industrial quantities for the salon market: the romance, the reverie, the tearful farewell with a title about a maiden or a grave. Critics who policed sentimentality were policing a real frontier, and some of Chopin's imitators, and some of the pieces published under the influence of his manner, stood on the wrong side of it. The question is whether the master stood there with them, or whether the frontier guards, having identified the territory by its weaker inhabitants, declined to examine the strong ones too closely because the portrait had already told them what they would find.

The trade itself blurred the frontier the guards were watching. Parisian and German publishers issued floods of salon music under titles designed to catch his public, and the name Chopin became a shelf category as much as a man: albums of morceaux in his manner, arrangements, pastiches, pieces by minor composers marketed to the buyers of the nocturnes. A critic reviewing the week's piano publications met the manner ten times for every encounter with the master, and irritation at the manner was a rational response to an irrational market. The mechanism is familiar in every art: a strong style creates a school, the school creates a flood, the flood creates a backlash, and the backlash bills the founder for the followers. Naming the mechanism does not acquit anyone. It relocates the charge from the scores to the ecology around the scores, which is where the next section's suspect, the performing tradition, also lives.

## Performance practice and manufactured sentimentality

Suppose the sentimentality is real in the experience of most listeners. It still does not follow that Chopin put it there, because between the score and the listener stands a century and a half of performance, and performance is not a transparent medium. The tradition that formed around his music in the decades after his death was built by virtuosos whose own fame rested on personality, and personality in a nineteenth century pianist meant the visible signs of feeling: the swaying, the delayed melody note stretched until the accompaniment nearly loses it, the tempo that surges and collapses with every phrase. Vladimir de Pachmann, the most famous Chopin player of the generation before the First World War, turned mannerism into a stage act, murmuring to the music and to himself while he played, and audiences learned from such performances what a Chopin piece was supposed to feel like. What they learned was a style. The style was then mistaken for the music.

The gramophone and the cinema completed the education. Twentieth century popular culture took the most melting pages of the catalogue and recoded them as the sound of romance itself. A melody from the Fantaisie-Impromptu became a popular song about chasing rainbows in 1918. The A major Polonaise, a piece of public defiance in its Polish context, was fitted with English words and sold in 1945 as a love song about waiting for a soldier's return. Film scores reached for the nocturnes whenever a scene required refined sorrow. Each adaptation was harmless in itself, and together they built a Chopin who exists independently of the scores: the composer of beautiful sadness, suitable for languor, candlelight and loss. A listener formed by that inheritance hears the music through it, and the sentimentality charge, repeated by such a listener, is evidence about the inheritance rather than about the inheritance's source.

Against the tradition stands the testimony of the people who heard the composer and studied with him, and it points the other way with unusual consistency. Karol Mikuli, his pupil and later the editor of his works, recorded that Chopin required the strictest steadiness of tempo and treated rhythmic licence as a fault to be corrected, not a charm to be cultivated. The image of rubato that emerges from the pupils' accounts is of a melody floating over an accompaniment that keeps time like a conductor, freedom in one hand purchased by discipline in the other. John Rink, whose research into the annotated copies left by Chopin's pupils has reconstructed much of this teaching in detail, shows a master annotating his students' scores with fingerings, phrasings and dynamics aimed at clarity of line and restraint of effect. The tradition that swoons is not the tradition that descends from the lessons. It descends from the stage, and it should be billed to the stage.

The deposit is not merely a matter of written testimony, because the early recording era preserved the performing tradition in sound. Pianists born in the nineteenth century, trained in the decades when the swooning manner was the height of Chopin playing, left records that let a modern listener hear exactly what the tradition supplied: the melody note delayed until the harmony aches, the tempo inhaling at every phrase joint, the climax underlined three times over. Paderewski's recordings and de Pachmann's preserve the style in its grand and its mannered forms alike. Set beside the metrical steadiness the pupils describe, the discs are the sound of a different composer, and the difference between the discs and the testimony is the size of the deposit. Listeners who formed their idea of this music from the grand tradition were not imagining the sentimentality. They were hearing it, accurately, in performances that had added it.

## What the scores ask for versus what tradition supplies

Return now to the scores themselves, because they are the one witness in this dispute that cannot be suspected of building a portrait. What do they ask for? The markings are famously exact and famously economical. Chopin wrote his dynamics, his phrasing and his pedalling with a care that makes his first editions among the most precisely specified piano texts of the century, and the character of the specification is telling: the expressive indications are brief, the climaxes are rationed, and long stretches of the most supposedly lachrymose music proceed under nothing more demonstrative than a slur and a dynamic level. The Marche funèbre, the most famous slow movement he wrote, moves at a tread that his own tempo indications keep brisk enough to walk to, and its trio offers its famous consolation once, without insistence. The swoon, where it occurs in modern performance, is an addition. It is supplied by the tradition described in the previous section, at points where the score supplies proportion instead.

The structural evidence points the same way. Sentimental music, in the strict sense the critics intended, subordinates design to feeling: the phrase goes where the emotion wants, and form is a frame for display. Chopin's scores are built the other way. The nocturnes are arias, certainly, but arias laid over bass lines of a contrapuntal independence that belongs to the study of Bach he prescribed to his pupils. The ballades bind their lyric material into arguments of return and transformation as strict as anything in the sonata literature. Even the preludes, those twenty-four fragments that look most like pure effusion, are organised as a systematic circuit of the keys, a design as cerebral as it is compact. A listener may still find the result sentimental; taste is not governed by evidence. But the charge, examined at the level of the page, describes a music that is not there. What is there is feeling under an unusually strict government, which is a different thing, and in most other composers it is called mastery.

Tempo evidence adds a measurement to the argument. His scores carry metronome marks in a number of the published works, and Rink's work on the performance tradition has drawn attention to what those marks imply: speeds consistently brisker than the funereal tread the later tradition adopted for the famous slow pieces. A nocturne taken at the marked speed cannot swoon; its phrases move with the gait of a song sung by someone walking, and the sentiment that depends on lingering evaporates. The tradition slowed the music down, and slowness, in this repertoire, is itself an expressive addition, a decision to make the feeling last longer than the composer allowed it. Few deposits on the text are as measurable as that one, or as rarely confessed by the performers who leave them.

The open question from section twelve can therefore be closed. The sentimentality attributed to Chopin is real as an experience and largely false as a description of the scores. Much of it is supplied by a performance tradition the scores do not ask for and the composer's own teaching contradicts, and the remainder is supplied by a critical inheritance that taught listeners to hear confession where the page directs song. This is the fourth rung of the article's argument, and it generalises beyond this charge: in case after case, what is being judged in the Chopin debate is not the music but a deposit left on the music by the people who transmitted it. The next three sections examine the largest of those deposits, the portrait of the invalid poet, and the language in which it was written.

## The invalid portrait and its sources

Every debate about Chopin is haunted by a picture: a slight, pale figure at the piano, coughing into a handkerchief, composing nocturnes between attacks of the illness that would kill him at thirty-nine, sustained by the devotion of a famous woman novelist and mourned by all Paris. The picture is not false in its materials. The illness was real and long; he was unwell from his teens onward, he died in Paris in October 1849 after years of chronic chest disease, and the woman was George Sand, in whose house and under whose care many of the major works were written. What the picture does, and what makes it a portrait rather than a record, is arrange those materials into a single luminous meaning: the music as the overflow of a suffering spirit, beautiful because the body producing it was failing. That arrangement had authors, and the authors can be named.

The chief of them is the biography published in 1852 under Franz Liszt's name, the first book-length life of Chopin and for decades the source from which all other accounts drew. It is treated in this article as what it is: not a source for the events of the life but one of the constructions this debate exists to examine. The book was written largely by the Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein, Liszt's partner, working from his direction and his memories, and its Chopin is a literary creation, a sylph-like, half-angelic being of Polish sorrow and Parisian refinement, described in prose of a richness that overwhelms its evidence. Its facts are unreliable where they can be checked. Its influence was enormous where they could not. The frail poet of the piano, the phrase and the figure alike, descends from this book more than from any document of the life itself, and every later account that repeats the portrait without examining it is repeating 1852.

Sand's own writings supplied the second layer: her account of the disastrous winter in Majorca in 1838 and 1839, where the illness worsened in a damp charterhouse, and her later memoir portrait of the composer as a fragile, impossible, exquisite dependent. Her evidence is that of a participant with a novel to defend and, after their rupture in 1847, a position to justify, and it is used in scholarship with the caution due to any interested witness. Around these texts grew a relic culture that fixed the image in objects: the cast taken of the composer's left hand, the death mask, the lock of hair, the slight figure in Delacroix's portrait, painted from life and endlessly reproduced. Even the medical question became part of the portrait's maintenance. The illness was read in his lifetime and after as consumption, and tuberculosis remains the standard account; in the late twentieth century an alternative, cystic fibrosis, was proposed in the medical literature and argued over without resolution. The proposal was never tested and cannot now be tested by any means scholarship would accept, and the honest statement of the matter is that the diagnosis is a retrospective inference, probable in its standard form and unproved in all forms. What matters for the debate is not which disease he had. It is that the portrait needed the disease, of whatever kind, to explain the music, and built the explanation before the evidence was in.

The nicknames that popular culture fastened on the pieces belong to the same portrait economy. The Raindrop Prelude owes its name to Sand's Majorca narrative of rain dripping through a charterhouse roof, a scene from the second layer of the portrait literature, not from the score, which bears no title at all. Once named, the piece is heard as weather and pathos forever after, and thousands of listeners have met the prelude through the nickname before meeting it through the notes. Chopin himself authorised almost no programme titles for his works. The titles the world knows were supplied by publishers, memoirists and the anecdote trade, and each one is a small deposit of the same kind the performance tradition leaves: an instruction, issued by the transmitters, about what the listener is about to feel.

## The gendered language of nineteenth century criticism of him

The vocabulary in which the nineteenth century discussed this music was not neutral, and its pattern is documented too consistently to be argued away. Critics in London, Paris and the German states described Chopin's playing and his works with a recurring set of terms drawn from sickness and from sex: the music was called effeminate, morbid, sickly, languishing, exquisite in the way of things too fine for the public world. The London Musical World under its editor James William Davison returned to the attack for years, treating the music as a kind of elegant disease in the art. Against these terms stood their opposites, reserved for the symphonic line: masculine, vigorous, architectural, healthy. The pairing was not Chopin's invention to suffer; it was the period's standard machinery for ranking, applied to him with unusual completeness because his genres, his rooms, his instrument and his portrait all fell on the same side of the divide.

Kallberg's research assembled this record and named its mechanism, and the finding stands as the shareable fact at the centre of this article. Nineteenth century criticism repeatedly described Chopin's music in feminised terms as a way of ranking it below the German symphonic repertoire, and that framing persisted in reference works well into the twentieth century. The mechanism matters more than any single insult. To call a nocturne feminine in 1845 was not to describe its sound; it was to assign it a rank in a hierarchy where the feminine named the private, the decorative and the minor, and the masculine named the public, the structural and the great. The critic did not need to argue that the music was lesser. The adjective filed the claim, and the filing was accepted as criticism because the culture outside music supplied the same ranking for everything else. This is the fifth rung of the argument: the gendered language was not decoration on the judgment of Chopin. It was one of the judgment's working parts, and it operated for generations in dictionaries, histories and programme notes whose authors may never have noticed they were repeating it.

Fairness to the dead critics requires two qualifications, and the stance of this article requires stating them. The first is that the vocabulary was the common critical currency of the period, applied to many composers and many genres, and the men who used it were working inside a language they had inherited rather than conspiring against one pianist. Their positions should be represented as they were, not caricatured: the same London press that attacked the music also recorded, sometimes with wonder, the effect of his playing on those who heard it. The second qualification cuts the other way. A vocabulary can be common and still do specific damage to a specific reputation, and in Chopin's case the damage is traceable in the repertoire itself, which is the subject of the next section. The words were general. The consequences were particular.

The vocabulary also policed the composer's champions, and the policing is part of the record. His most devoted performers in the decades after his death included many women pianists, teachers and salon players of genuine attainment, and criticism learned to use their devotion as evidence against the music: a repertoire beloved of women students could be filed, by the same adjective machinery, as a repertoire for the drawing room's softer purposes. Schumann stands as the great contemporary exception that proves the machinery's usual direction. Reviewing the polonaises, he reached for the opposite pole of the period's imagery and described the music as cannons concealed among flowers, martial force hidden in the dance. The image is famous precisely because it fought the filing system from inside its own language, insisting that the flowers had artillery in them. That the defence of this composer in 1836 already needed to borrow the masculine pole of the vocabulary tells us how completely the feminine pole had been assigned to him, and how early.

## How that language shaped the repertoire's standing

Language becomes standing through institutions, and the route can be followed. For the century after his death, Chopin's place in the concert repertoire was real but partitioned. The concertos and a handful of large pieces anchored the public claim, while the mazurkas, the nocturnes and the preludes circulated as the intimate Chopin, the encore Chopin, the Chopin of the recital's second half and the drawing room's whole evening. Histories of music gave him his chapter and his adjectives together, the poet, the invalid, the miniaturist of genius, a formula that praised the gift and fixed its ceiling in a single breath. The formula's persistence is the point: a reference work does not argue, it settles, and what it settled, well into the twentieth century, was that this was a composer of the second magnitude by the nature of his gifts, however perfect of his kind. The ranking survived the disappearance of the vocabulary that had created it, which is how such rankings usually work. The gendered words faded from respectable criticism; the shelf they had placed him on remained.

The recovery, when it came, came from two directions at once, and both are instructive about what standing is made of. From the platform, the great pianists of the recording age made the whole catalogue central again: the complete mazurkas, the complete preludes, the late works recorded as summits rather than curiosities, until a recital without Chopin became the eccentricity. From the study, the scholars named in this article rebuilt the case on new evidence: Samson on the structures, Kallberg on the genre system that had ranked them, Goldberg on the Warsaw formation that the Paris portrait had erased, Rink on the texts and the playing. Neither direction refuted the old ranking by assertion. They changed what counted as evidence about the music, and the ranking moved because the evidence moved. That is the optimistic reading, and this article endorses it with one reservation: the same machinery that demoted him for a century on social evidence could promote or demote another composer tomorrow on evidence just as external, and the defence against it is not a better verdict but the habit, which this article has tried to practise, of asking every verdict for its criteria. The nationality dispute, taken next, shows the machinery running in a different gear, with flags where the adjectives used to be.

Two quieter institutions did as much of the demoting as the critics did, and they deserve naming because they reached further than any review. The examination syllabus sorted the piano repertoire into grades for the teaching market, and for generations the nocturnes and waltzes sat in the middle grades as accomplishment pieces for the daughters of the middle class, a placement that taught every examination candidate in Europe exactly where this composer belonged in the order of things. The encyclopaedia did the settling at the other end of the culture: the early editions of Grove's dictionary and their continental counterparts handed down the portrait and the ceiling together, in prose that presented itself as reference rather than argument, and reference prose is the hardest kind to argue with because it never announces that an argument is being made. Between the grade book and the dictionary, a century of readers absorbed a ranked Chopin before they had heard a note judged on its merits.

## The nationality dispute

No question in the Chopin debate is asked more often, or settled less, than the question of what he was. Polish or French: the dispute has run for more than a century and a half, conducted by governments, institutes, competition juries, publishers and guidebooks, and it shows no sign of exhaustion, because it was never only a question about a composer. He was born in 1810 in Żelazowa Wola, a village west of Warsaw, to a Polish mother and a father who had come from Lorraine as a young man and made himself Polish by every act of his life except birth. He left Poland at twenty, weeks before the November uprising against Russian rule made his generation of Poles into exiles or prisoners, and he never saw his country again. He lived the remaining nineteen years of his life in Paris, took French citizenship, and was buried in Paris, while his heart, at his own reported wish, was carried to Warsaw and immured in a church pillar. A life could hardly be designed to give two nations a better claim each, and both nations have pressed theirs with everything they have.

### Was Chopin Polish or French?

The record supports both and compels neither. He was born and formed in Warsaw, of a Polish mother, and his national dances are Polish to the core. He lived his whole adult life in Paris, held French citizenship and lies in a Paris grave.

The article reports the two cases without resolving them, for a reason that needs stating plainly: the dispute involves living national claims, sustained by institutions that exist now and by citizens for whom the answer is a matter of identity rather than of scholarship. A verdict from this article would not be a scholarly act; it would be an enlistment. What scholarship can do, and what the next two sections do, is give each case its strongest form, on the evidence, with its advocates' best materials, and then examine the dispute itself: why it persists, whose purposes it serves, and what the commemorative machinery built on both sides reveals about how a composer's nationality is manufactured after the fact. The reader who came for a flag will leave without one. The reader who came to understand why the flag is contested will leave with the materials of the contest.

The dispute is not a modern invention projected backward; it began in his lifetime and in his presence. Parisian criticism of the 1830s claimed the brilliant newcomer for France with the easy confidence of a capital annexing a talent, while the Polish emigration, organised and vocal in the same city, claimed him back as the exiled voice of the partitioned nation, and the composer, who depended on both audiences and offended neither by declaration, let the claims run. The paperwork of the period already contains the whole argument in embryo. After the uprising's suppression, Polish emigrants in France lived under surveillance and passport disability, and the French citizenship he obtained in 1835 was, among other things, the legal instrument that let a stateless-feeling exile travel and work. Polish advocates read the passport as necessity; French advocates read it as allegiance. Both readings were available in 1835, and both are still being filed, with better footnotes, in the scholarship of the present day.

## The case that he is a Polish composer

The Polish case begins with formation, and Halina Goldberg's history of music in Chopin's Warsaw has made it unanswerable in its strong form. Warsaw in the 1810s and 1820s was not a provincial waiting room where a genius happened to be born; it was a working musical capital with an opera house, a conservatory, a publishing trade and a vigorous concert life, and the young Chopin was formed inside all of it. His teacher Józef Elsner, director of the Warsaw Conservatory, trained him in counterpoint and composition and recognised, in writing, that his pupil's gifts were of the first order. The child prodigy who performed in Warsaw salons and the student who wrote the concertos was a product of Polish institutions and Polish teaching, and the language of his musical thinking, its rhythms above all, was learned from the dances of the country he was born in. The mazurka and the polonaise are not decorations on his style. They are its foundation, and they are Polish in their metre, their accent and their social meaning, raised by him from the ballroom and the village to the level of high art without surrendering their identity.

The case continues with allegiance. He left in November 1830, days before the uprising, carrying a silver goblet of Polish earth given him by friends, and the suppression of the rising in the following year turned his journey into an exile he had not chosen and never accepted as permanent or as indifference. His letters burn with the politics of the partitioning powers. He wrote Polish songs. He formed his closest bonds in Paris within the Polish emigration, the Great Emigration of officers, poets and aristocrats who made the Hôtel Lambert a capital in exile. The polonaises were heard by Poles, in his lifetime and ever after, as the sound of the nation that had been erased from the map: the A major Polonaise as a military summons, the A flat major as a heroic epic in dance form. And at the end there is the fact that no argument has ever dissolved: his heart is in Warsaw, in the Church of the Holy Cross, carried there by his sister Ludwika because, by the account the family preserved, he asked that it should be. The full narration of the life that produced these facts is given in [the account of Chopin's life and career](/2011/03/21/frederic-chopin-life-and-career/), and the Polish case rests on it comfortably: born, formed, feeling and symbolically buried Polish, whatever his passport said.

The documentary trail of the allegiance is unusually concrete, and the Polish case is entitled to its strongest exhibits. When news reached him in Stuttgart in September 1831 that Warsaw had fallen to the Russian army, he poured his response into pages of a diary whose anguish has never been seriously doubted: rage at the partitioning powers, grief for the city, shame at his own safety. Elsner, his Warsaw teacher, put the formation's verdict in writing when his pupil left, certifying a talent he described in terms the conservatory did not use lightly, and the certificate survives as the Polish institution's own testimony. Even the goblet of earth was kept, an emigrant's relic preserved by the family as evidence of what the departure meant to the man who made it. A case built on birth and schooling can be answered; a case built also on documented anguish, on the testimony of his teachers and on the dances at the centre of the art has to be answered at a deeper level, which is what the French case, at its strongest, attempts next.

## The case that he is a French composer

The French case begins with blood and proceeds to choice. His father Nicolas was a Frenchman, born in Lorraine, who emigrated to Poland at sixteen; the name the composer used in his professional life was not Fryderyk but Frédéric, and the language of his maturity, of his business, his teaching and most of his surviving correspondence beyond the family circle, was French. He arrived in Paris in the autumn of 1831, aged twenty-one, and Paris is where the composer the world knows was actually made: the child of Warsaw became Chopin in the salons of the aristocracy and the studios of the rue du Faubourg Poissonnière, under the eyes of Liszt, Berlioz and Delacroix, published by Schlesinger and Pleyel, formed as a public artist by French institutions and French taste. The refinement that the world calls Chopinesque, the horror of exaggeration, the art that conceals art, is a Parisian discipline learned in Parisian rooms.

The case continues with the legal and material record, which is entirely French. In 1835 he became a French citizen and thereafter travelled on a French passport, a step taken by decision and never reversed; his earnings, his pupils, his publishers and his copyrights were French; the pianos he championed were Pleyels, built in Paris; the estate at Nohant where the great summer works were composed is in the Berry countryside, as French as soil can be. When he died, Paris buried him: the funeral at the Madeleine drew thousands, the grave at Père Lachaise received the body, and Auguste Clésinger, a French sculptor married into his intimate circle, made the monument that stands over it. France holds the manuscripts, the letters, the death mask and the grave. On the evidence of where the adult life was lived, under what citizenship, inside which culture and among which dead he lies, the French case is not a courtesy claim. It is a description of the biography's second and decisive half, and its advocates can fairly ask what more a nation is supposed to contribute to a composer than his language, his livelihood, his audience, his formation as an artist and his grave.

The teaching lineage makes the same point in professional form. His pupils were the flower of Parisian musical society and its serious students alike, and through pupils such as Georges Mathias, who carried the teaching into the Paris Conservatoire itself, the Chopin tradition became a French institutional inheritance, examined, certified and transmitted by the French system for a century. When he travelled to Britain in the last year of his life, in 1848, he travelled as the French citizen the passport said he was, played in London and Scotland as a Parisian artist, and returned to Paris to die. He never revisited Poland in nineteen years, though the grief of the Stuttgart pages was real; the French case reads the nineteen years as the answer the man gave with his life, whatever his heart instructed his sister to do with its remains. Two readings of one biography, each with its documents in order: that is exactly the situation the next section examines.

## Why the dispute persists and who it serves

Two cases this strong do not persist by accident, and they are not kept alive by confusion. They persist because they are useful, and because the thing they divide is genuinely indivisible. Begin with the usefulness. For Poland, Chopin is the supreme proof that a nation erased from the map for more than a century remained a civilisation: the competition founded in Warsaw in 1927 made his name a Polish institution that the whole pianistic world must visit, and the bicentenary of his birth in 2010 was proclaimed and funded as a national year, a state argument made in concerts. For France, the claim is quieter and more institutional: the grave, the manuscripts, the Conservatoire tradition that descends through his pupils, the steady presentation of Chopin as a glory of French culture in the city where he chose to live and die. Publishers, record companies, festival towns and tourist boards on both sides have budgets that depend on the attachment, and a third claimant, the Mallorcan monastery of Valldemossa, markets the cell where he spent one miserable winter, its relics disputed and its visitors undeterred. The dispute serves everyone who holds a piece of it, which is most of the reason it will never be settled by evidence.

The deeper reason is that the question is malformed, and both sides know it at their best. Nationality, as the dispute uses the word, asks for a single allegiance from a man formed by the age of exile, when the map of Europe was a palimpsest and a musician's identity was layered: language at home, language in the world, a father from one country, a mother from another, a formation in a third city and a career in a fourth. Chopin's own practice was double without strain: Polish in his letters home, his dances and his griefs; French in his manners, his business and his art's finish. The dispute persists because institutions need singular flags for singular monuments, and a double man fits no flagpole. The honest scholarship on both sides, Goldberg's Warsaw work included, increasingly describes the layering rather than awarding the prize, and the persistence of the cruder contest tells us more about the needs of commemoration than about the composer. What commemoration built, on both sides, is the subject of the next section.

The scholarly middle position, that the composer was a Polish artist formed again by Paris and fully at home in neither simplification, has been available for decades and is probably where most specialists privately stand. It serves nobody's budget. A dual plaque raises no funds, a layered identity headlines no anniversary year, and the record industry, which must file the catalogue under one nation's flag in its national series and its marketing, has no shelf for a palimpsest. Even the editions have been conscripted: the great collected edition prepared in Warsaw under Paderewski's name was a Polish national project as much as a scholarly one, and its authority was a form of custody exercised in print. The dispute's persistence is therefore overdetermined, driven by sentiment, by money and by the genuine doubleness of the evidence, and anyone who expects a documentary discovery to end it has misunderstood what kind of object it is. It is not a problem awaiting a solution. It is an institution with two branches.

## The commemorative apparatus and its claims

The dispute has a physical form, and it is the strangest fact in this entire article. The composer's body lies in Paris, in Père Lachaise, beneath Clésinger's monument of a grieving muse. His heart lies in Warsaw, sealed in a pillar of the Church of the Holy Cross beneath an inscription from the Gospel of Matthew: where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. The division was made by his family's hands in the days after his death and ratified by two nations ever since, and it states the nationality dispute more honestly than any essay: neither capital would surrender its relic, so the man was divided between them, and each side's shrine quietly testifies to the other side's claim. Visitors make both pilgrimages. The apparatus of commemoration, usually a machine for simplifying a life into a single story, here preserves the complication in stone and crystal, and deserves credit for failing to resolve what it could not.

Around the two shrines the apparatus operates at full scale. Warsaw built the institutional claim: the international piano competition that bears his name and has convened since 1927, the institute devoted to his works, the museum, the editions, the state bicentenary. Paris built the custodial claim: the grave maintained as a site of national culture, the manuscripts in the national library, the salon at Nohant preserved as a shrine of French literary and musical life. A planner laying the two programmes side by side, the Warsaw cycle against the Paris cycle, in a browser-based presentation viewer such as [the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), is looking at a single reputation administered by two states, each funding the half it holds and each, by the funding, renewing the claim. Anniversaries intensify the machine without altering its logic: 1949 brought the centenary editions, 1999 the sesquicentenary festivals, 2010 the bicentenary year, and at each recurrence the apparatus produced concerts, conferences and publications in both capitals, and the question of the flag was asked again in the press of both countries, and answered again in both directions. The apparatus does not resolve the dispute because resolution is not among its functions. Its function is custody, and custody, divided, is the truest monument the facts allow.

The Warsaw shrine carries one more layer of meaning that the apparatus did not choose and cannot remove. The Church of the Holy Cross stood in a city that the twentieth century very nearly erased: the church was damaged in the destruction of Warsaw in 1944, and the urn with the heart survived the war to be restored to its pillar in the rebuilt church. A relic that had embodied a partitioned nation's survival in 1882 came, after 1945, to embody it again in a second register, and the double survival is part of what Polish commemoration is commemorating. The Paris grave, by contrast, speaks of continuity undisturbed, the cemetery of a capital that kept its dead through every regime. Even the stones, in the end, argue the two cases: endurance against erasure on one side, unbroken custody on the other, and the composer, who belonged to the century of the first story and the city of the second, divided between them with a completeness no committee could have designed.

## The unstable text as a philosophical problem

The debate so far has assumed something that the next three sections withdraw: that there is a stable object, the music of Chopin, about which the charges and defences are arguing. For most composers of his century that assumption is safe enough. A Beethoven sonata exists in a text that editors have stabilised; performers argue about interpretation, not about which notes the composer wrote. Chopin's music does not sit still in that way. Because he published simultaneously in three countries to protect his copyrights in an age before international agreements, in Paris, in Leipzig and in London, he prepared separate manuscript copies for separate publishers, often at different moments, and the copies differ. He corrected proofs unevenly. He then taught the published pieces to pupils and altered them again in the lessons, and the pupils' scores, annotated in his presence, preserve readings that match no printed edition exactly. For a significant number of works there is no single authoritative text. There is a population of authorised variants, all descending from the composer, differing in ornaments, in voicing, sometimes in whole passages.

This opens the third and last of the article's loops, and it is the most unsettling of the three. If no authoritative text exists, what exactly are we judging? Every verdict delivered in the previous sections, miniaturist, sentimental, Polish, French, rested on the assumption that the music under judgment was a fixed thing. If the thing itself is partly fluid, if the nocturne one critic condemns for a melting phrase exists in another authorised version without that phrase, then some part of every verdict is a verdict on an editorial choice. The question is not rhetorical and it is not an invitation to despair about judging at all. It closes in section twenty-six, after the evidence about the variants has been set out and the editors' and performers' shares in the text have been weighed. What can be said now is that the problem is real, that it is unusual in its scale, and that it changes what a responsible verdict on this composer has to include: a statement of which Chopin, in which text, is being judged.

The machinery that produced the variants deserves a paragraph of its own, because it was rational at every step. There was no international copyright in the 1830s. A work published only in Paris could be reprinted in Leipzig and London without a penny reaching the composer, so the defence was simultaneous publication in all three markets, each edition establishing rights in its own territory. Simultaneity required three manuscripts, prepared under time pressure, often by copyists working from his drafts and corrected by him at different sittings in different weeks. Julian Fontana, his friend, factotum and copyist, stood at the centre of the traffic, preparing fair copies, carrying proofs, and dealing with the publishers in three cities. No step in the process aimed at variance; variance was what the process produced, as reliably as three photographs of a moving subject differ. The unstable text is not the residue of carelessness. It is the fossil of the only business model available to a composer protecting his property in a lawless market, and it is one of the prices this particular career paid for being modern early.

The scale of the resulting problem should not be understated either. For the works published in his lifetime, the source picture for a single piece can include an autograph, one or more copyists' manuscripts prepared for different publishers, three first editions that disagree, corrected proofs, and the annotated scores of several pupils, and the sources do not arrange themselves into a tidy order of authority. The autograph is not always the latest word, since he revised in the lessons after printing; the printed page is not always his last word either, since an edition could preserve the earlier reading he had already abandoned. Editors describe the situation as a network rather than a ladder, and the description is exact. A philosophical problem with that shape, where the work's identity is distributed across a network of documents, cannot be solved by appointing one document king. It can only be managed by keeping the network visible, which is what the best modern editions do and what the next sections ask the act of judgment to do as well.

## What it means to say a work is by him

Consider what the variants are, concretely, because the philosophy is only as good as the facts underneath it. The French first editions, published in Paris under the composer's eye, differ from the German editions published from separately prepared copies, and both differ at points from the English editions issued by Wessel in London. The differences are not misprints, though misprints exist too. They are alternative readings: a different ornament realised in full, a chord voiced another way, a passage of the left hand redistributed, a repeat treated differently, dynamics and pedalling specified in one source and absent in another. Pupils' copies add a third layer. Jane Stirling, his Scottish pupil, preserved a set of his works annotated during her lessons, and the annotations record the composer revising his own printed text in the act of teaching it, sometimes restoring what an edition had smoothed, sometimes trying a reading he had not printed anywhere. Rink's research and the catalogue of the first editions prepared by Christophe Grabowski and Rink have mapped this terrain in full, and the map shows a composer for whom the text was a living thing, adjusted in the presence of the music's use.

What, then, does authorship mean here? The comfortable model, one work, one final text, one act of completion, fits a composer who published once and supervised the result. Chopin's practice fits a different model, closer to a family of versions around a stable core. The core is very stable, and the trap of this subject, warned against in every serious treatment, is to let the variants swell into a claim that nothing is certain. The editions agree on the overwhelming majority of what matters: the themes, the harmonies, the forms, the sequence of events in every major work are not in dispute, and no variant turns a nocturne into a different nocturne. The instability lives at the music's surface and at certain expressive joints, in the ornamentation, the articulation, the exact voicing of a climax, the pedal wash under a coda. Those are not trivial places. In music this exposed, the surface is where the meaning is carried, and a variant at a joint can change the character of a paragraph. But proportion matters: to say a work is by Chopin is to name the stable core and its documented family of surfaces, and that is a perfectly workable definition. It is simply not the definition the rest of the repertoire taught us to expect.

The posthumous works add a final twist that any honest account of authorship here has to include. Chopin left unpublished manuscripts with instructions, attested by those around him, that they should not be published, and Fontana, after the death, published a selection of them anyway, editing as he went. The Fantaisie-Impromptu, the most famous piano piece in the world by some measures, reached the public through that act of disobedience, in a text that later scholarship, working from the autograph, has had cause to qualify. The work is by Chopin in every sense that matters to a listener, and its text is by Fontana in a sense that matters to a scholar, and the two statements have to be held together. Authorship in this catalogue is not a single fact but a small committee: the composer, his copyist, his three publishers, his pupils with their pencils, and the editors who came after. Naming the committee is not pedantry. It is the precondition of the next section's question, which is who among the committee a verdict on the music is actually judging.

## Authority, editions and the performer's share

The editors inherited the problem first, and their history is a history of two honesties in conflict. The great collected edition prepared under Ignacy Jan Paderewski's name and published from Warsaw across the middle of the twentieth century gave pianists a usable Chopin at last, and it did so, as its sources required, by choosing among variants and sometimes conflating them, often without telling the reader a choice had been made. The Polish National Edition, prepared under Jan Ekier from the 1960s onward, undertook the opposite discipline: to reconstruct each work from the full surviving source picture and to print the evidence with the text, so that the choices are visible and the reader can audit them. Between those poles the modern performer works. The scholarly position, stated by Rink among others, is that the variants are not a corruption of Chopin's intentions but a record of them, that he treated his texts as performers treat interpretations, as things renewed in use, and that the annotated pupils' copies are therefore as much evidence of the work as any printed page.

The performer's share follows from this, and it is larger for Chopin than for any comparable composer. A pianist preparing the Fantasia in F minor must decide, at documented points, between the French reading and the German, between the printed ornament and the one the composer's pupil wrote down, and every decision is audible. Serious performers keep their choices the way scholars keep citations, and the discipline is easier to maintain in a tool built for provenance: a register of which source stands behind each passage, kept in the offline encrypted notes app obtained from [the VaultBook download page](https://vaultbook.net/download/), lets a player defend a text bar by bar when a teacher or a reviewer asks. The loop opened in section twenty-four can now be closed, and its closing is the sixth and final rung of the argument. Because competing originals exist and performers must choose between them, judging him means judging a text that is partly constructed by whoever plays it, which makes the usual comparisons harder than they look. The miniaturist verdict, the sentimentality verdict, every verdict in this article, was delivered on performances and editions that silently chose their Chopin first. A debate that does not know which text it is judging has one more assumption to surrender, and it is the deepest one: that the object of judgment was ever simply there.

Institutions have begun, quietly, to concede the point by their practice. Competitions and conservatoires that once treated the printed page as a fixed law now work from source-critical editions and expect candidates to know that a text has a history; the Warsaw competition's own reference texts have changed across its history as Polish editing moved from the Paderewski conflations toward the National Edition's documentary discipline. The change matters beyond pedantry. A generation of pianists trained to ask which reading, from which source, on what evidence, is a generation inoculated against the older debate's central habit, the confident verdict on an unexamined object. The text's instability, which looked like a defect in the catalogue, turns out to be one of the catalogue's great teachers: it forces everyone who touches this music to become, in a small way, a scholar, and it makes the philosophy of the work impossible to dodge. Few composers make their performers think about what a work is. This one makes it unavoidable, fifty times a year, in the practice room, where the real verdicts on standing are prepared.

## The Chopin overrated debate in its sharpest form: the greatest-composer claim stated on criteria

Everything so far has been preparation for the question the debate always reaches in the end: where does he stand? Is Chopin one of the greatest composers, and if the question is to mean anything, greatest by what measure? The procedure of this section is borrowed from the discipline the whole article has practised. The criteria are stated in advance, one at a time, in the next three sections: harmonic originality, influence on those who followed, and structural ambition. Each is applied as evenly as the evidence allows, to Chopin and by implication to the field he is ranked in. Only after the criteria have done their work separately does section thirty-one add up where he wins and where he does not. A ranking produced any other way, by temperament, by loyalty, by the volume of one's record collection, is the disease this article was written against, and the comparison with Beethoven is instructive precisely because it was run for years without criteria, as [the parallel argument over Beethoven's standing](/2011/03/07/ludwig-van-beethoven-legacy-debate/) shows in its own way.

### Is Chopin overrated or underrated?

The question has no answer until the criteria are named. On the criteria of the symphonic tradition he ranks below the tradition's own masters. On harmonic originality and on what the piano became in his hands, the same evidence places him near the summit of his century.

One clarification before the criteria are applied. The greatest-composer question, as usually posed, smuggles in the settlement examined in sections two and three: it assumes a single scale on which all composers can be placed, and the scale, unsurprisingly, is calibrated in the symphonic tradition's units. Stating the criteria separately is a way of breaking the single scale into its components so that each can be argued on its merits. A composer might stand first on one component and tenth on another, and the honest output of the exercise is the profile, not a single number. If the reader finishes these sections with a profile rather than a rank, the exercise has succeeded in the only way it honestly can.

Rankings themselves have a history, and it is not reassuring. François-Joseph Fétis, the most powerful critic-historian of the composer's own lifetime and the compiler of the century's standard biographical dictionary, treated Chopin's harmony as a tissue of errors against the rules of counterpoint he had codified, and the dictionary's measured coolness fixed an early verdict from the summit of the profession. Later lists reversed the polarity without changing the method: the great triumvirates of the concert guides, the polls of critics, the sleeve-note hierarchies, each presented its order as a finding while smuggling its weights. Fétis at least published his rules, which is why his verdict can be argued with; the modern list usually hides them, which is why its verdicts feel like weather. The procedure of the next three sections is Fétis's honesty without Fétis's rules: criteria named, weights visible, conclusions drawn where the reader can watch them being drawn.

The modern form of the question, overrated or underrated, arrives with the magazine poll and the listicle, formats that require a verdict in a headline and have no room for a profile. Their verdicts oscillate with the fashion of the decade, and the oscillation itself is data: a standing that swings with the format of the asking is a standing held in the formats, not in the music. The three criteria that follow were chosen because they are the ones on which evidence, rather than fashion, can actually be brought: the notes can be analysed, the channels of influence can be traced in documents, and the structures can be laid out and compared. A criterion on which no evidence could ever count, greatness of soul, sublimity, the verdict of posterity unaided, has been excluded with intent. What cannot be evidenced cannot rank anyone, and excluding it is the last piece of hygiene before the weighing begins.

## Harmonic originality as the criterion

Take first the criterion on which his claim is strongest: the originality of the harmony, the actual notes and their connections, the part of music that cannot be faked by reputation. The record here is a matter of analysis rather than of opinion, and it has been assembled by scholars with no flag to fly. The young Chopin arrived at procedures that the theory of his day could not comfortably describe: chords that function by colour and by slide rather than by root logic, phrases that tonicise a distant key for two bars and withdraw, the Neapolitan and augmented sonorities used not as spice but as structure. The mazurkas, as section seven showed, carry modal harmony absorbed from Polish folk practice into the centre of art music. The Prelude in E minor builds its whole span over a harmonic descent of an austerity that analysts reach for words like radical to describe. The Fantasia in F minor opens with a harmonic procession that wanders for pages before the work declares its key with certainty, an opening without real precedent in the piano literature of 1841.

The influence of that originality is traceable in the harmony of the rest of the century, which is how originality certifies itself. Wagner's harmonic language in Tristan und Isolde has documented affinities with Chopin's procedures that scholars have traced in detail, and Liszt, who knew the music at first hand, carried its chromatic voice-leading into his own late piano works, where the line from Warsaw and Paris to the far end of the century is audible to any analyst. On this criterion the comparison class stops mattering: measured by the distance between the harmonic practice he found and the harmonic practice he left, against any composer of his generation and most of the generation after, Chopin stands at or near the head of the list. The miniaturist charge meets its limit here with particular clarity. The frontier of the century's harmony runs through pieces of three minutes, and a criterion that cannot see them cannot see the frontier.

Honesty requires the debts to be entered on the same page, because originality in harmony is never creation from nothing. He learned from Hummel's piano concertos, which he performed as a boy and whose figuration his early works absorb; from Field, who invented the nocturne as a singing piano piece before him; from the Italian opera orchestra, whose accompanimental patterns and whose sense of harmonic rhythm stand behind his textures; and from Maria Szymanowska, the Polish pianist-composer whose études and nocturnes precede his in print. The critical side is entitled to the whole list. What the list does not do is dissolve the originality, because invention in music has always meant the transformation of inheritances, and the distance between Field's nocturne and the Nocturne in C sharp minor, or between Hummel's figuration and the Fantasia's opening procession, is precisely the quantity the criterion measures. Samson's analyses make the measurement carefully, distinguishing the borrowed devices from the uses no predecessor had imagined for them, and the verdict of that careful work is that the transformation, not the borrowing, is the fact that needs explaining.

## Influence as the criterion

Influence is the criterion the debate reaches for most casually and defines least, so let it be defined before it is applied: a composer is influential to the degree that later music would be audibly different without him, in the work of named successors, through identifiable channels. By that definition Chopin's influence is enormous and unusually well documented, because it runs through the instrument he remade. Every pianist-composer after him worked in a house he had rebuilt: Schumann, whose own piano style formed itself in dialogue with the early works he had reviewed with such excitement; Liszt, whose mature piano writing absorbs the lessons even while exceeding them in scale; and then the line that runs through Fauré and Debussy, whose piano music is unthinkable without the example of pedalled resonance and of harmony as colour; through Scriabin, who began as a Chopinist outright; through Szymanowski, who carried the mazurka itself into the twentieth century as a Polish inheritance consciously claimed. The channel is rarely in doubt because the medium is single: the influence travels in the hands of pianists, from teacher to pupil, edition to edition, recital to recital.

Measured against the whole field, the profile on this criterion is distinctive rather than simply high. Beethoven's influence changed what composers attempted; Bach's, recovered and institutionalised, changed what they studied. Chopin's changed what they could do at one instrument and what listeners believed that instrument could say, and it did so continuously, without the eclipse and revival that mark the other two cases. There is no decade since 1830 in which his music was not being played, taught and absorbed. Where the criterion is influence on the largest forms and the broadest public, he is outranked, and section thirty-one will say so without flinching. Where the criterion is density of consequence inside a living practice, the case for the first rank is a serious one, and it is made of names and works rather than of adjectives.

The channels deserve one concrete paragraph, because influence claimed without channels is the vice this article exists to police. The first channel was the teaching studio: his pupils, among them Mikuli in Lemberg, Mathias in Paris and a generation of aristocratic and professional students, carried his fingerings, his phrasing and his repertoire into conservatoires across Europe, so that the influence was transmitted in the hands before it was ever written about. The second was print: the simultaneous editions and the flood of reprints made the works the common property of every musical household, and composers met them at the piano as children, the deepest channel of all. The third was emulation within the genres he had remade: once the ballade existed as a form, Brahms and Liszt and Fauré wrote ballades; once the mazurka had been raised to art, Szymanowski could claim it for a new century; once the nocturne had been transformed, Fauré's thirteen nocturnes could trace a life's arc inside it. Influence with named channels, named heirs and named works is evidence. Influence asserted in a warm paragraph is advertising, and the distinction is the whole method of this section.

## Structural ambition as the criterion

The third criterion is the one on which the critical tradition has always felt safest, because it seems to restate the miniaturist charge in positive form: structural ambition, the building of large spans whose parts are bound by necessity. The evidence, taken whole, is more interesting than either side's slogan. At one end stand the ballades, which invented a large instrumental form outside the sonata's laws and sustained it four times without repeating a solution, and the late works in which Samson finds the character piece and the large design fusing: the Barcarolle, with its single span of accumulating variation over a rocking bass, and the Polonaise-Fantaisie, which assembles fantasia, polonaise and sonata impulses into a structure that analysts describe in competing vocabularies because the work declines every ready-made one. At the other end stand the three piano sonatas, where the record is genuinely mixed: the B flat minor Sonata, with its funeral march and its finale of rushing unison octaves that the nineteenth century found baffling, is a structure whose audacity posterity has vindicated, while the charge that its movements sit together by juxtaposition rather than by logic has been made by serious analysts and never wholly answered.

The honest summary on this criterion is a split verdict, and the split is itself the finding. In the forms he invented, the structural ambition is of the first order and the invention is the ambition: the ballade is a solution to the problem of the large piano form that the sonata could not supply for his purposes. In the inherited large forms, the sonata above all, he is a great composer working a seam he did not choose, producing one work, the B minor Sonata, that stands with the century's best and two that argue with their own frames. A critic who reports only the sonatas understates him; a defender who reports only the ballades overstates the ease of the case. The criterion of structural ambition, applied evenly, places him high but not unchallenged, and it is the criterion on which reasonable judges, given the same scores, will continue to differ by a place or two in either direction. Profiles, again, rather than verdicts: the next section assembles his.

Two more exhibits belong in this section's evidence because they test the criterion outside the solo repertoire's centre. The Fantasia in F minor, which Samson reads as one of the most ambitious single spans of the century, binds a wandering, chorale-like introduction, a march of gathering weight and a lyrical second world into a trajectory whose returns are earned across thirteen minutes without recourse to sonata law. And the Cello Sonata of the last years, written for his friend Auguste Franchomme, shows the chamber discipline fully operational when he chose to use it: four movements of argued chamber writing in which the cello is a protagonist and not a guest. Neither exhibit rescues the catalogue from the charge of narrowness, and neither is offered for that purpose. They mark the boundary of the split verdict precisely: where he chose the large span or the shared medium, the structural powers were present and formidable; the openness of the question is why he chose them so seldom, and on that question the evidence of the life, the market and the health all speak, and none of them speaks of incapacity.

## Where he wins and where he does not

The profile can now be drawn, and it should be drawn without mercy in both directions. He wins on harmonic originality, where the analytical record puts him among the century's pioneers and where the small forms turn out to be the laboratory of the advance. He wins on the transformation of his instrument, a consequence so complete that it has become invisible: the modern piano, its technique, its pedagogy and its sound-ideal are substantially his creation, transmitted hand to hand ever since. He wins, more equivocally, on influence within his practice, a continuous century and a half of absorption by the pianists and composers who followed. The works on which these wins rest are examined one by one in [the survey of Chopin's greatest compositions](/2011/04/21/frederic-chopin-greatest-compositions/), and this article's profile is offered as the argumentative frame for that evidence rather than as a substitute for it.

He does not win where the symphonic criteria said he would not, and the article's long defence of criteria must not be allowed to blur the fact. He wrote no large public work for forces beyond the piano that belongs to the repertoire's centre; the concertos are beloved and their orchestral dress remains, by general consent, the thinnest part of them. His counterpoint, though real and Bach-fed, never faced the test of the fugue or the Mass. Whole territories of the art, the stage, the chorus, the orchestra as an engine of public argument, are blank in his catalogue, and a ranking that prizes the completeness of a composer's conquest of his art's means will place him below Bach, below Beethoven, below Mozart, on evidence and not on prejudice. The article's claim was never that those rankings are rigged in every particular. It is that they are partial in a way their users rarely declare, and that beside them stands another profile, on criteria equally musical, in which the same composer stands near the top. Both profiles are true. The debate is about which one gets called the verdict.

The symmetry of the popular errors belongs in the profile too, because he loses on a third front that neither camp notices. To the popular image he is overrated in one specific way: the poet of pure feeling, the improviser of beautiful sadness, is credited with a spontaneity the scores refute on every page, and the music's intellectual substance, the counterpoint, the harmonic engineering, the formal invention, disappears beneath the compliment. To the scholarly ranking he is underrated in the mirror-image way: the analyst of structures is discounted for failing examinations in genres he rebuilt rather than sat. The two errors are one error, the substitution of the portrait for the work, wearing a romantic face in the concert hall and a classical face in the seminar room. A profile that corrects both at once is the only kind worth assembling, and it is the business of the remaining sections to state what fairness would require of any judge who attempts it.

One more line belongs in the profile, on the criterion of survival in use, because it is the only criterion the future administers impartially. Bach required a revival; the revival of 1829 and the century of scholarship after it rebuilt a reputation the eighteenth century had let lapse. Chopin's music has never lapsed. There was no rediscovery because there was no burial: the works passed from his pupils to their pupils in an unbroken chain of teaching and performance, through decades when the official histories ranked him in the second class, sustained not by institutions but by the daily decisions of players who found the music necessary. Survival without institutional life support is a kind of evidence the other criteria cannot supply, the evidence of use under no obligation. On that criterion he stands with the very few, and any profile that omits it has left out the testimony of the only jury that sits every day.

## What a fair comparison would require

A fair comparison between composers of different kinds would begin by publishing its criteria and its weights, the way an honest judge publishes reasons. It would compare each composer first against the problems he set himself and only then against the problems others set, and it would treat an unattempted genre as missing evidence rather than as a failed examination, while allowing the attempt itself to count where it was made. It would discount, by a stated factor, everything known to be a deposit of transmission: the portraits, the gendered vocabulary, the performance traditions, the national machineries, the editorial choices that decide which text is being judged. And it would present its result as a profile across criteria, with the disagreements between criteria left visible, instead of compressing the profile into a single rank and calling the compression objectivity. The general survey of [the greatest composers of all time](/2025/04/07/greatest-composers-of-all-time/) attempts the ranking under declared criteria of its own, and the exercise is worth more than the answer it reaches, because the criteria are where the argument actually lives.

Run under those rules, the comparison between Chopin and the symphonic masters changes its character. It stops being a trial with a defendant and becomes what it should always have been: a study of how different kinds of excellence are distributed, and of how rarely they coincide in one career. The question whether he is greater than Beethoven dissolves, and in its place stand answerable questions. Who advanced the harmony further from where he found it? Who solved the formal problems he set himself more completely? Whose music has been in continuous, unbroken use by the practitioners of its art? On some of those questions Beethoven wins by distances that no advocacy can close. On others Chopin wins the same way. A comparison that cannot say both sentences has not yet begun to be fair, and a culture that insists on the single rank will keep manufacturing the old debate forever, because the single rank is the one thing the evidence will never supply.

A worked example shows the rules operating rather than merely stated. Set Chopin beside Schumann, the contemporary the criteria fit most nearly: both pianist-composers of the same generation, both innovators in the short piano piece, one of whom also wrote four symphonies, concertos, an opera and a mountain of chamber music. Under the undeclared single rank, Schumann's breadth settles the matter before the music is heard. Under published criteria the profile separates: Schumann wins outright on range and on the public genres; the two divide the piano cycles between them, Carnaval against the preludes and mazurkas; on harmonic originality the analytical literature awards the deeper transformation to Chopin; on influence through the instrument, the channels traced in section twenty-nine run more densely from the Pole. Neither profile is a scandal, and neither compresses into the other's rank without destroying information. That destruction, performed silently, is what the usual verdict on Chopin has always been: a profile flattened into a place, with the flattening unconfessed.

## What would have to be true for the claim to fail

An argument that cannot state its own defeat conditions is a loyalty, not an argument, so let this article state the conditions under which its defence of Chopin would collapse. The claim made here is that the standard verdict rests on imported criteria, on deposits of transmission and on an unstable text, and that on criteria applied evenly the profile is far higher than the verdict suggests. That claim fails if the deposits can be shown to be negligible: if the gendered vocabulary can be shown to have left the ranking untouched, if the performance tradition can be shown to transmit the scores faithfully, if the editions can be shown to agree wherever it matters. Kallberg's documentary record stands in the way of the first, the pupils' testimony in the way of the second, and the variant sources in the way of the third, but the conditions are stated so that the reader can see what evidence would count, and weigh whether it exists.

The claim fails on its other flank if the music itself, examined without any of the deposits, turns out to be as limited as the verdict always said. That would require the analyses to be wrong: it would have to be shown that the mazurkas' harmony is conventional beneath its surface, that the ballades' forms are loose where Samson finds them exact, that the late works' synthesis is an illusion of sympathetic scholarship. Scholarship has had a century and a half to show these things and has moved steadily the other way, but the possibility is real and the article's honesty depends on naming it. Short of such showings, the defence stands where it was built: not on the assertion that the charges are false, for they are true, but on the demonstration that truth of that kind, measured by those instruments, was never the whole of the case. A verdict on Chopin that survives these defeat conditions is worth having. One that has never faced them is just the settlement of 1810, still collecting its rent.

A third defeat condition guards the argument's other flank, the flank of the criteria themselves. This article has treated the symphonic standard as one tradition's settlement rather than as a law of musical nature, and that treatment would fail if the standard could be shown to be tradition-neutral: if cultures without contact with German criticism had independently converged on the symphony's values, scale, development, public architecture, as the universal measures of musical worth. The comparative evidence runs the other way. Traditions across the world have prized concentration, cyclic return, timbral refinement and mastery within a single instrument's resources, values under which a Chopin would need no defence at all. The standard's locality is a finding, not a slogan, and it is falsifiable in exactly the way stated: produce the independent convergence and the argument of sections two and three falls. Until then the criteria stand convicted of their origin, which does not make them useless, only makes them one instrument among several, to be read alongside the others and never mistaken for the music itself.

## Who gets to set the measurements

Return, at the end, to the question with which the article began, now in its general form. Who decided that a symphony was the test? The historical answer is known: a generation of German critics, Hoffmann at their head, arguing the young Beethoven into the centre of the art; the institutions that canonised their argument in curricula and concert halls; the historians, Dahlhaus the most distinguished, who built the century's story on the symphonic spine. There was nothing corrupt in any of it. The symphony was a glory, the argument for it was sincere, and the tradition it founded produced the histories by which most listeners still navigate. But a measurement set by one tradition, in one place, for its own central figure, becomes an injustice exactly when it forgets its origin and poses as the nature of the art. The measurements are set by whoever writes the histories, staffs the institutions, edits the reference works and frames the questions, and they are reset, slowly and against resistance, whenever someone examines them as measurements rather than reciting them as laws.

The custodians of the measurements are also changing, and the change belongs in the closing account. The reference work that once settled reputations in a paragraph has been joined, and partly displaced, by the critical edition that shows its evidence, by the competition jury that must defend its taste in public, by the recording catalogue that lets any listener audit a century of performances against the score, and by scholarship of the kind this article has drawn on, which treats the verdicts of the past as evidence to be examined rather than as law to be applied. None of these custodians is neutral, and the article's argument has never required neutrality; it has required declaration. Dahlhaus declared his architecture openly, which is why his history can be argued with and learned from at once. The anonymous verdict, the programme note's poet of the piano, the list that hides its weights, declares nothing and so can be corrected by nothing. The demand this debate finally makes is not that the measurements be abolished but that they be signed.

Chopin's case is the standing example because it is so clean: four charges, all true, and a verdict that does not survive the inspection of its instruments. He made no bid for the symphony's throne and would have failed, probably, had he made one; the same hands wrote harmony that the throne's occupants learned from, in pieces sized for a room. What survives of any composer, when the portraits have faded and the machineries have rusted, is the part that was never dependent on them, and the long view of [what makes music last for centuries](/2026/10/21/what-makes-music-last-for-centuries/) suggests that survival answers to use, to teaching, to the daily need of players for music that rewards a lifetime. By that measure the miniaturist has been measured daily for a hundred and eighty years, at pianos in every country, by the severest judges there are: the people who have to play the notes. The debate about his standing will continue, and it should, provided it continues honestly, with its criteria on the table. The measurements were never his. The music was.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are the main criticisms of Chopin?

Four criticisms recur across a century and a half. He wrote no symphony and no opera, and almost nothing that does not require a piano. His forms are small, most of his works lasting a few minutes. His music is called sentimental, feeling indulged beyond what structure earns. And the portrait of the frail invalid poet, manufactured by the first biography of 1852 and repeated ever since, taught listeners to hear confession where the scores direct song. The first two criticisms are factually true, and no honest defence disputes them. The argument of the debate is about the criteria underneath them: the standard by which size, range and large public forms became the measures of a composer was built inside one national tradition, and the question is whether that tradition gets to judge everyone by its own instruments.

### Q: Did Chopin write any symphonies?

No. His catalogue of some two hundred and thirty works contains no symphony, no opera, no string quartet and no Mass. The largest forces he ever used are in the two piano concertos of 1829 and 1830, written when he was nineteen and twenty, and in a small group of chamber works that includes a cello sonata and a piano trio. Everything else is for piano alone. Critics who press the miniaturist charge treat the absence as the central fact of his career, and in one sense they are right: the absence is total and undisputed. Defenders answer that an unattempted genre is missing evidence rather than a failed examination, and that the ambition the symphony usually certifies appears in his work in other units, across the fifty mazurkas that develop as a set and in the ballades that invented a large form outside the sonata's laws.

### Q: Why is writing in small forms held against a composer?

Because the nineteenth century built a hierarchy of genres and placed the large forms at its summit. The symphony, the opera and the sonata were coded public, architectural and masculine; the nocturne, the mazurka and the album leaf were coded private, decorative and feminine, and the coding did the ranking before any individual work was heard. Jeffrey Kallberg's research showed how consistently that vocabulary sorted music by the rooms it was played in and the people who played it there. Large forms also test real capacities, the management of tension across long spans and of forces beyond one player's hands, and the strongest version of the charge says a composer who never sits that examination leaves his powers unproved. The dispute is whether a hierarchy with that social history can serve as a neutral measure of the music it ranks.

### Q: Do the mazurkas contain Chopin's most advanced harmony?

The analytical literature gives a strong yes. Across more than fifty mazurkas spanning his whole career, Chopin ran his harmonic laboratory: the Mazurka in A flat major, Opus 17 Number 4, opens with chords that withhold their key so successfully that analysts describe the opening in competing ways; the late mazurkas carry modal colour drawn from Polish folk practice, including the raised fourth degree; the Mazurka in C sharp minor, Opus 50 Number 3, sets strict canon inside a dance; the final F minor mazurka circles its tonic in chromatic restlessness. Jim Samson's analyses treat the mazurkas as the frontier of his harmonic language. The point matters because the miniaturist charge assumes experimentation lives in large forms. In this catalogue the frontier is in pieces three minutes long, which is why the charge keeps failing at the level of the notes.

### Q: Is depth in one instrument equal to breadth across many?

They are different properties, and the question has no answer until the measures are separated. Breadth tests timbral imagination, public rhetoric and the management of forces too large for one pair of hands; the history of Bach, Haydn and Beethoven shows obligations across many media forcing composers outward into growth. Depth tests the progressive discovery of resources inside one set of constraints, and by that measure the piano after Chopin is a different instrument: he rebuilt the relation between the hands, turned the pedal into an instrument of harmonic resonance, imported the singing line of Italian opera and the counterpoint of Bach into the salon's machine. Which property a ranking prizes is a choice, and an honest ranking declares it. A verdict that reports only breadth has weighed the criterion and called the weighing a judgment of the composer.

### Q: Where does the charge of sentimentality come from?

It has three traceable sources, none of them a property of the scores. The first is the room: the Paris salon was coded feminine and domestic, and the nocturne, his characteristic lyric genre, inherited the coding of the interior and the amateur's music desk. The second is the portrait: the frail, exiled, lovesick invalid of the 1852 biography and of George Sand's memoirs taught listeners to hear the slow movements as confessions. The third is the market: his pieces were the glory of the domestic piano, played in thousands of homes at modest levels of technique, and professional criticism guarded the border between the concert hall and the drawing room by suspecting whatever the drawing room loved. The charge hardened early, travelled with the portrait, and was amplified by a performance tradition that swooned where the scores direct steadiness. It describes the transmission of the music far better than the music.

### Q: Do Chopin's scores ask for the sentimental style?

The evidence of the page says no. His markings are exact and economical: dynamics, phrasing and pedalling specified with unusual care, climaxes rationed, long stretches of the most supposedly lachrymose music proceeding under a slur and a dynamic level and nothing more. His metronome marks, where the scores carry them, point to speeds brisker than the funereal tread the later tradition adopted for the famous slow pieces, and a nocturne at its marked speed cannot swoon. The testimony of his pupils agrees with the page: Karol Mikuli recorded a master who required the strictest steadiness and treated rhythmic licence as a fault, and the rubato the pupils describe is a melody floating over an accompaniment that keeps time like a conductor. The sentimental style is an addition made by performers and ratified by listeners who learned the music through those performances.

### Q: How did performance tradition change Chopin's reputation?

It interposed a style between the scores and the public, and the style was then mistaken for the music. The virtuoso tradition that formed after his death prized the visible signs of feeling: the melody note delayed until the harmony ached, the tempo surging and collapsing at every phrase joint, climaxes underlined repeatedly. Vladimir de Pachmann, the most famous Chopin player of the generation before the First World War, turned mannerism into stagecraft, and early recordings by pianists formed in that tradition preserve the manner in sound for any listener to audit. Popular culture completed the work, fitting the A major Polonaise with love-song words in 1945 and turning the Fantaisie-Impromptu into a song about chasing rainbows in 1918, until a Chopin made of refined sorrow existed independently of the scores. Reputations formed on that figure are evidence about the transmitters, not the transmitted.

### Q: How did critics describe Chopin in gendered terms?

In a vocabulary that modern readers recognise at once once it is pointed out. Across the London, Parisian and German press, his music and his playing were called effeminate, morbid, sickly, languishing and exquisite in the register of things too fine for the public world, while the symphonic line was described in the paired opposites: masculine, vigorous, architectural, healthy. The London Musical World under James William Davison returned to the attack for years, treating the music as an elegant disease in the art. Jeffrey Kallberg's Chopin at the Boundaries assembled this record in 1996 and named its mechanism: to call a nocturne feminine in that period was to assign it a rank, since the feminine named the private, the decorative and the minor. The adjective filed the judgment, and the culture outside music supplied the same ranking for everything else, which is why it passed as criticism.

### Q: Did that criticism affect Chopin's place in the canon?

Yes, and the route runs through institutions rather than reviews. For a century his place in the repertoire was real but partitioned: the concertos and a few large pieces anchored the public claim while the mazurkas, nocturnes and preludes circulated as the intimate Chopin of encores, second halves and the teaching studio. Examination syllabuses graded the lyric pieces as middle-grade accomplishments for the daughters of the middle class, teaching every candidate where this composer belonged. Reference works handed down the formula of the poet, the invalid and the miniaturist of genius, praising the gift and fixing its ceiling in one breath, and reference prose settles questions without announcing that an argument is being made. The ranking long outlived the vocabulary that created it. Its reversal came only when pianists of the recording age and scholars such as Samson, Kallberg, Goldberg and Rink changed what counted as evidence about the music.

### Q: Was Chopin a Polish or a French composer?

The record supports both cases and compels neither, and this article does not resolve the dispute, because it involves living national claims sustained by institutions and by citizens for whom the answer is a matter of identity. The Polish case rests on birth at Żelazowa Wola, a Polish mother, formation in Warsaw under Józef Elsner, the Polish dances at the foundation of his style, the documented anguish of his exile after the 1830 uprising, and the heart that lies in a Warsaw church. The French case rests on a French father, an adult life lived entirely in Paris from 1831, French citizenship from 1835, French publishers, pupils and audiences, and the body that lies at Père Lachaise. His own practice was double without strain: Polish in his letters home, his dances and his griefs; French in his language of business, his manners and his art's finish.

### Q: Who benefits from the nationality dispute?

Nearly everyone who holds a piece of him. For Poland, Chopin is the supreme proof that a nation erased from the map for over a century remained a civilisation: the Warsaw competition founded in 1927 made his name an institution the whole pianistic world must visit, and the bicentenary of his birth in 2010 was proclaimed and funded as a national year. For France the claim is quieter and institutional: the grave, the manuscripts in the national library, the Conservatoire lineage descending from his pupils, the steady presentation of Chopin as a glory of French culture. Publishers, record companies, festival towns and tourist boards on both sides hold budgets that depend on the attachment, and the monastery at Valldemossa markets a third share from one winter's stay. A dual identity raises no funds and headlines no anniversary, which is a large part of why the singular claims persist.

### Q: What does it mean that no authoritative Chopin text exists?

It means that for a significant number of works there is no single document that can be pointed to as the work itself. Because Chopin published simultaneously in Paris, Leipzig and London to protect his rights in an age before international copyright, he prepared separate copies for separate publishers at different sittings, and they differ: ornaments realised differently, chords voiced another way, dynamics and pedalling present in one source and absent in another. His pupils' annotated scores preserve further readings, made in lessons after publication. The result is a population of authorised variants around a stable core. The core is genuinely stable; the themes, harmonies and forms are not in dispute, and the instability is no licence for relativism. But at the music's expressive joints the text must be chosen, by editors and by every performer, and a verdict on the music is partly a verdict on those choices.

### Q: Should performers choose between competing editions themselves?

In this repertoire they have no real alternative, and the honest course is to choose knowingly. The older collected edition prepared under Paderewski's name chose among variants and sometimes conflated them without saying so, which spared the performer decisions by hiding them. The Polish National Edition prepared under Jan Ekier took the opposite discipline, reconstructing each work from the full source picture and printing the evidence with the text, so the choices are visible and can be audited. A performer working from that evidence decides passage by passage: the French first edition against the German, the printed ornament against the reading a pupil wrote down in a lesson. Those decisions are audible, and serious players keep them the way scholars keep citations, ready to defend a text source by source. Choosing blindly from a conflated page is also a choice; it is merely one the performer cannot explain.

### Q: Is it fair to compare Chopin with Beethoven?

It is fair under declared rules and unfair under the rules usually applied in silence. A fair comparison publishes its criteria and its weights, compares each composer first against the problems he set himself and only then against the problems others set, and treats an unattempted genre as missing evidence rather than as a failed examination. Run that way, the comparison stops being a trial and becomes a study of how different kinds of excellence are distributed. Beethoven wins by distances no advocacy can close on the large public forms, on development across long spans, on the conquest of every medium his age offered. Chopin wins on comparable distances in the distance travelled from the harmony he found to the harmony he left, and on the transformation of a single instrument's resources. A comparison that cannot state both sentences has not yet begun to be fair, whatever its conclusion.

### Q: How do I judge a composer outside the symphonic tradition?

Start by refusing the borrowed yardstick until it has been inspected. Ask what problems the composer set himself, in the genres he chose, and judge the solutions at the level of the notes: the harmony's distance from what he inherited, the forms' internal necessity, the resources discovered inside his medium. Then ask what the tradition that ranks him was built to measure, and discount whatever in your first impression turns out to be a deposit of transmission: the portrait, the nickname, the performance manner, the national machinery, the edition that silently chose your text. Compare him, finally, against the whole field on criteria you state in advance, publishing your weights, and accept the profile that emerges instead of demanding a single rank. A composer outside the symphonic tradition is not a failed symphonist awaiting a verdict. He is a different distribution of excellence, and the judging has to be able to see distributions.

### Q: How do I tell score markings from performance convention?

Compare three witnesses and distrust any one of them alone. The first is the page itself in a source-critical edition: markings printed from the best evidence, with variants shown, in the National Edition's manner, rather than a conflated text that hides its choices. The second is the documented teaching tradition: what the composer's pupils recorded about tempo, touch and rubato, which for Chopin means Mikuli's testimony of rhythmic discipline and the annotated scores Rink has studied. The third is the history of performance itself: early recordings and the manners of famous interpreters, heard as evidence about their era rather than about the composer. Where the three agree, you are close to the score's demand. Where a beloved effect appears only in the third witness, in the tradition and nowhere in the markings or the teaching, it is a deposit, and it should be enjoyed, if at all, under its own name.

### Q: Which works make the strongest case for Chopin?

The case is strongest where the criteria of this article converge. The four ballades invented a large form outside the sonata and sustained it four times without repeating a solution, the Fourth in particular binding lyric material into an argument analysts are still unpacking. The Barcarolle and the Polonaise-Fantaisie fuse the character piece with the large design in the final years. The Fantasia in F minor builds a thirteen-minute span of earned returns without sonata law. The mazurkas, played as a set, carry the harmonic frontier of the century inside dance forms. The twenty-seven études re-founded piano technique by finding the music inside mechanical problems. The B minor Sonata stands with the century's best in the inherited form he supposedly could not handle. A listener who works through those works has met the composer the charges were invented to avoid meeting.

### Q: Where should a skeptical listener start with Chopin?

Start where the charges are weakest rather than where the portrait is strongest. Avoid the nocturnes for the first hearing, not because they are lesser but because a century of sentimental performance hangs over them; come to them later, in brisk and disciplined performances, and hear how little they swoon. Begin with the four ballades, which give the skeptic large structures to test, and with a book of mazurkas played complete, where the harmonic daring and the rhythmic wit are undeniable within minutes. Add the Barcarolle for the late synthesis and the B minor Sonata for the inherited form under maximum pressure. Then read one charge against him and check it against the notes while they are fresh. Skepticism is the right instrument for this repertoire, provided it is aimed at the evidence and not only at the reputation, which has been aimed at for a hundred and fifty years already.

### Q: What would change the verdict on Chopin?

Evidence of specific kinds, and the debate is healthier for naming it. The standing would fall if the analyses were shown to be wrong: if the mazurkas' harmony proved conventional beneath its surface, if the ballades' forms proved loose where Samson finds them exact, if the late works' synthesis dissolved under scrutiny. It would fall if the deposits proved negligible: if the gendered vocabulary could be shown to have left the rankings untouched, if the performance tradition proved faithful to the markings, if the editions agreed wherever it matters. It would rise further if the criteria themselves were reformed in public, with rankings published as profiles across declared measures rather than as single places. None of these is a matter of taste, and that is the point. A verdict that can name what would change it is an argument; one that cannot is an inheritance, and most verdicts on this composer, for most of his history, have been inheritances.
