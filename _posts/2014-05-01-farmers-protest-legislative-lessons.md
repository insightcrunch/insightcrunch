The farmers' protest that held the borders of Delhi for a little over a year is usually argued about as a verdict on agricultural policy, and that argument has never been settled and probably cannot be. This article treats the episode as something narrower and more answerable: a failure of legislative process, in which three statutes travelled from ordinance to enactment to repeal without passing through most of the checkpoints the parliamentary system maintains for exactly such laws. Between June 2020 and December 2021, the three farm laws were promulgated as ordinances while Parliament was not sitting, converted into Acts in a monsoon session marked by a refused division and suspended members, stayed by the Supreme Court without any finding on their validity, and finally repealed by a bill that neither House debated. At every stage, an institution reached for the instrument that ended discussion fastest. The question this article answers is the one that matters for every future law of consequence: which specific stage of the process, had it been used, would most likely have prevented the confrontation?

The disagreement itself can be stated in terms neither side would reject. The Union government held that Indian agriculture was trapped in a regulated marketing system that depressed farm incomes, that the three laws merely added choices alongside that system rather than replacing it, and that a determined minority of farmers, concentrated in the states that benefit most from public procurement, had been permitted to veto a reform Parliament had enacted. The farm unions held that the laws were drafted without consulting those they governed, that they dismantled the only marketing and price structure Punjab and Haryana farmers could rely on, that they offered no statutory assurance of any price in return, and that the manner of their passage, by ordinance first and by voice vote later, showed a legislature unwilling to hear the people its law would govern. Both statements contain claims of fact, and both contain judgments of value. The sections below separate the two, give each side's case in the strongest form its own holders have given it, and then turn to the machinery: the committee that never met, the division that was never taken, the stay that decided nothing, and the repeal that debated nothing.

A word on why a protest occupies the centre of a legislative history at all. Statutes are usually followed through the institutions that make and apply them, the ministry, the House, the court, the enforcing officer, because those institutions generate the record. The farm laws inverted the pattern. For fourteen of the seventeen months of their existence they generated almost no institutional record of the ordinary kind: no committee report, no recorded division, no judgment, no amendment, no implementation worth the name under the stay. The record they generated was made outside the institutions, in the talks that failed, the camps that endured, the court's extraordinary interim order and the electoral calculation that ended them, and a history that confined itself to the formal sources would describe three Acts that were passed, stayed and repealed without ever explaining any of the three events. The protest is therefore not background to this legislation. It is, on the evidence, the mechanism by which the legislation was contested, suspended in fact before it was suspended in law, and finally withdrawn, and it is examined here with the same documentary care, and the same refusal to take any participant's account of it on trust, that the series applies to a statute book.

## What the three laws actually said

Most public argument about the farm laws misdescribed their text, in both directions, and any account of the protest has to begin with the text stripped of characterisation. The three statutes are treated in full in [the account of the three farm laws of 2020](/2014/04/01/farm-laws-2020-explained/), and what follows is the minimum a reader needs to follow the process argument. The first statute, the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act, created a category called the trade area, meaning any place other than the premises of a market yard regulated under a state agricultural marketing law, and allowed trade in farmers' produce inside that area without the licence and market fee that the state system imposed. Its sixth section barred any market fee, cess or levy on such trade, and a trader needed little more than a permanent account number to begin buying. The second statute, the Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act, gave a statutory frame to farming agreements between a farmer and a sponsor, with a price mechanism the Act called a guaranteed price, disputes routed to the sub-divisional authority rather than to a civil court, and an express bar on recovery against the farmer's land. The third, the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, rewrote the stock-limit regime for cereals, pulses, oilseeds, onions and potatoes, so that limits could be imposed only on defined triggers of extraordinary price rise.

Three features of the text matter for everything that followed, because they were the features a committee stage would have examined first. The trade and commerce Act did not abolish the regulated mandis, and it did not abolish the minimum support price, which has never rested on a statutory foundation in the first place; it simply built a parallel, untaxed channel beside the mandis and left the price system exactly as statutory as it had been before, which is to say not statutory at all. The fee bar in the trade area had a fiscal consequence the debate rarely named: market fees collected in the regulated yards finance the market committees and, in states such as Punjab, a rural development fund drawn on the same transactions, so a migration of trade out of the yards was also a migration of revenue away from state institutions. The dispute provisions of the two principal Acts channelled grievances to executive officers and barred the civil courts, a design choice that recurs across Indian regulatory statutes and that lawyers' bodies criticised in this instance with unusual sharpness. None of these features is a slogan. Each is a clause, with a foreseeable incidence on identifiable people, which is precisely the class of question a select committee exists to interrogate before a bill becomes law rather than after.

The farming agreement Act regulated the encounter at the architecture's other end. A sponsor, a company, processor, exporter or aggregator, could contract with a farmer or a farmer producer organisation for the produce of a coming crop, under a written agreement whose frame the Act prescribed: the price to be paid was to be stated or made determinable by a formula, quality and grading standards were to be specified against which delivery would be tested, and the agreement was to run for the crop cycles the Act laid down. Two protections in the text answered older abuses of contract farming that the movement knew well: the agreement could not transfer the farmer's title in the land, and the farmer's holding could not be proceeded against for the recovery of the sponsor's dues. The Act also carried the divide's seed in its machinery. Disputes under an agreement were routed away from the civil courts, first to conciliation and then to the sub-divisional authority, with an appeal upward within the revenue administration, a ladder of forums in which the individual farmer would face the sponsor's legal department bearing the costs of travel, time and proof, and from which the jurisdiction of the courts stood excluded. The third law was of a different kind: the amendment to the Essential Commodities Act confined the power to impose stock limits on cereals, pulses, oilseeds, edible oils, onion and potatoes to extraordinary circumstances such as war, famine, extraordinary price rise and natural calamity of a grave nature, and tied the trigger to steep price-rise thresholds, so that the state's emergency weapon against hoarding would sleep unless prices moved violently. Economists had demanded that liberalisation for decades, on the ground that the standing threat of a stock limit order deters the private storage and processing investment that smooths prices between harvests. The unions read the same clause from the other end, as the removal of the one instrument that had ever stood against engineered scarcity, and as the complement, with the trade area, of a system in which a few large buyers would face many small sellers at every node of the chain.

The constitutional setting mattered as much as the clauses. Agriculture sits in the State List of the Seventh Schedule, and Parliament's route into the subject ran through the Concurrent List entry covering trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, foodstuffs and other goods, supplemented by the Union's power over inter-state trade. The unions and several state governments argued that a law restructuring how farm produce is marketed across the whole country was agriculture legislation in substance whatever entry was cited for it, and that the Centre had used a trade power to occupy a state field. The government answered that trade and commerce in farm produce is squarely a concurrent subject and that Parliament had legislated on it for decades, most visibly through the Essential Commodities Act itself. That question, of competence, was pleaded before the Supreme Court and, for reasons the judicial section of this article explains, was never answered by it. It remains the largest purely legal loose end of the episode: three Acts were enacted, stayed, and repealed, and no court ever pronounced on whether Parliament had the power to make them.

## The record the ordinances arrived with

The confrontation of 2020 and 2021 was the third act of a longer failure, and its shape is unintelligible without the first two. Agricultural marketing in India was built, state by state from the middle of the twentieth century, on the regulated yard: a notified market area in which specified produce could be sold only through licensed channels, by open auction, subject to a market fee that financed the market committee and the rural infrastructure around it. The system's virtues were real, a visible price, a regulated weighbridge, a buyer of last resort where the state procured, and so were its documented vices, a licensed cartel of traders and commission agents in many yards, fees that taxed the farmer's price rather than the trader's margin, and a legal bar on the farm-gate and inter-state transactions through which a more competitive market might have formed. Reform of the system had been official policy, in the anodyne sense that committees kept recommending it, for a generation before 2020. What a generation of committees could not do was make a state legislature enact it, and the reason is the analytical key to the whole episode: the fee. A state that liberalised its marketing law on the model the Centre circulated in 2003, and again in revised form in 2017, was voting to reduce a revenue stream that its own market boards and rural development funds depended on, for the benefit of farmers whose gains would be diffuse and delayed. A minority of states amended their laws in part; Bihar repealed its marketing Act altogether in 2006, an experiment each side of the later debate read to its own advantage; and the structure the committees had diagnosed survived substantially intact into the pandemic year. The Centre's eventual decision to legislate over the states' heads was, whatever else it was, a response to a genuine two-decade stalemate of cooperative federalism, and the states' fury at the method was, whatever else it was, a defence of a revenue model and of a constitutional allocation alike.

The immediate trigger was the pandemic. In May 2020, as part of the economic package the government assembled for the lockdown period, the Finance Minister announced agricultural marketing reform among the measures intended to free supply chains that the lockdown had exposed as brittle, and on 5 June 2020 the three ordinances followed. No draft had been published for comment. No consultation paper had been issued. The ordinance-making power, Article 123 of the Constitution, permits the President to legislate when Parliament is not in session and circumstances require immediate action, subject to the ordinance lapsing unless approved within six weeks of Parliament's reassembly, and its use for a permanent restructuring of the country's farm marketing, rather than for a measure whose urgency expired with the emergency, was itself one of the choices the unions put in issue from the first week. The government's answer, that the supply disruptions of the lockdown were the urgency and that the monsoon session would convert the ordinances into Acts within the constitutional window, was formally complete. What it did not answer was the standing objection, developed in the Supreme Court's own jurisprudence on re-promulgation and repeated by the opposition in September, that the ordinance route chosen as a matter of routine converts Parliament from the author of legislation into its ratifier, presented with a working system already in force and a government committed to its defence. The farm bills were debated in that shadow: members were not being asked whether India should have these laws, in the abstract, but whether they would vote to dismantle, within weeks of its creation, a legal regime the executive had already switched on. Sequence is a form of power in a legislature, and the sequence here had been arranged, as completely as the Constitution permits, before the legislature met.

## A chronology of the confrontation

The protest did not begin at Delhi's borders, and its prehistory explains its discipline. The three ordinances were promulgated on 5 June 2020, and farm unions in Punjab were demonstrating against them within the same month, first in district-level actions and then, as the monsoon session approached, through a state-wide rail blockade that began in late September 2020 and a general shutdown of the state on 25 September 2020. The political system registered the pressure early: Harsimrat Kaur Badal resigned from the Union Cabinet on 17 September 2020 as the bills were being taken up, and her party, the Shiromani Akali Dal, the government's oldest ally, left the ruling alliance altogether on 26 September 2020. The bills passed the Lok Sabha on 15 and 17 September 2020 and the Rajya Sabha on 20 September 2020 in the circumstances the machinery section examines in detail, and the President's assent followed on 24 September 2020 for the two principal Acts and on 26 September 2020 for the Essential Commodities amendment, with publication in the Gazette on 27 September 2020. Punjab's answer was legislative as well as street-level: on 20 October 2020 its Assembly passed amendment bills designed to blunt the central Acts within the state, a move whose legal limits the states section of this article returns to.

The Punjab phase of the agitation had already demonstrated the movement's method before Delhi saw it. The rail blockade that began on 24 September 2020 stopped the movement of grain, fertiliser and coal through the state at the height of the paddy season; the unions calibrated it, relaxing the tracks for goods trains in late October 2020 after negotiation with the state government while keeping the passenger blockade and the toll plaza occupations in force, so that pressure on the Centre was maintained at a cost the state's own farmers and traders could bear. The calibration mattered for what followed. A movement that could open and close a state's railways by decision was a movement with a functioning chain of deliberation, and the discipline of the border camps, which outsiders later treated as a surprise, was the same discipline rehearsed on the tracks.

The coordinating body of the movement, the Samyukta Kisan Morcha, was formed on 7 November 2020, a joint platform that brought together more than forty farmer organisations at its formation, with hundreds of further organisations associating with its calls as the agitation grew. Its structure mattered to its stamina: the Morcha worked by consensus among constituent unions with sharply different ideologies, announced its programmes collectively, and dealt with the government as a single delegation, which made it slow to satisfy and impossible to split by selective concession. On 26 November 2020, under the call the movement named Delhi Chalo, columns of farmers from Punjab moved through Haryana toward the capital, meeting barricades, water cannon and tear gas at the border crossings, and, on being prevented from entering the city, settled where they had been stopped. The sites that formed in those days, at Singhu on the highway toward Haryana's interior, at Tikri on the western road, and at Ghazipur on the eastern crossing into Uttar Pradesh, with smaller encampments at Shahjahanpur and Palwal, became the geography of the protest for the following year.

### Why did the protest camps form on Delhi's borders rather than inside the capital?

The camps formed at the borders because the march was stopped there. The unions had sought permission to assemble inside Delhi and were refused or offered grounds they considered inadequate, while Haryana and Delhi police sealed the crossings with barricades, trenches and parked vehicles. The columns settled where they stood.

Rather than disperse, the columns made the blockade points themselves the protest sites, and the highways at Singhu, Tikri and Ghazipur became self-sustaining encampments a short distance from the capital they were pressing.

Formal negotiation ran alongside the encampment from the start, and its rhythm is a large part of the story. Eleven rounds of talks were held between the government's ministerial panel, led by the Agriculture Minister, Narendra Singh Tomar, and the Morcha's delegation between 14 October 2020 and 22 January 2021, with a further meeting at the level of the Home Minister on 8 December 2020, the day of a country-wide general strike the unions had called. The talks produced agreement once, on 30 December 2020, and on the two least central items in the unions' charter: the government accepted that stubble burning would be decriminalised for farmers and that the existing arrangement on electricity charges would not be disturbed. On the charter's centre, repeal of the three laws and a statutory guarantee of the support price, the positions did not move. The government's written proposals offered amendments to answer stated objections, including a written assurance that procurement at the support price would continue, and culminated, at the tenth round on 20 January 2021, in an offer to suspend the implementation of the three laws for up to eighteen months while a joint committee examined the issues. The Morcha considered the offer overnight and rejected it on 21 January 2021, and the eleventh round, on 22 January 2021, ended with ministers stating that the government had offered its best and with no further date fixed. Why the talks failed is a question the FAQ section of this article takes up directly, because the answer is contested at its root: the government read the repeal demand as maximalism that no negotiation could satisfy, and the unions read every lesser offer as a device to exhaust the encampment without conceding the point of principle.

The low point of the negotiation, and the clearest view of what separated the sides, came on 9 December 2020. The government sent the unions a written set of proposals: amendments it was prepared to make to answer stated objections, including on the registration of traders and the fee question in the trade area, together with a written assurance that procurement at the support price would continue undisturbed. The Morcha's general body considered the document and rejected it the same day, on the stated ground that amendments and assurances left the laws themselves, and the method that had produced them, intact. A written offer is the moment at which a negotiation's real structure becomes visible, and the structure visible here never changed afterwards: the government was negotiating the content of three statutes it intended to keep, and the unions were negotiating the existence of three statutes they intended to end. Everything the two sides said to one another across eleven rounds was commentary on that single incompatibility, and the January suspension offer, the largest concession the government ever put on the table, failed for the same reason the December document had, that it proposed to pause the laws rather than to remove them. The winter deepened around the deadlock. The camps endured the coldest weeks with their populations thinned by rotation and replenished by it, the unions observed a day of homage in December 2020 for those who had died in the agitation's first weeks, and the movement's discipline, no violence at the sites, no damage to public property, kitchens open to any who came, including the police posted opposite, became itself an argument the unions made to the watching country, that a movement capable of this order was entitled to a hearing the legislature had refused it.

The Supreme Court entered the episode in the second week of January 2021, hearing challenges to the laws alongside petitions about the protests themselves, and on 12 January 2021 it stayed the implementation of all three Acts until further orders and constituted a committee to hear stakeholders and report, the judicial episode examined on its own terms in a later section. A fortnight later came the day that cost the movement most. The unions had negotiated permission for a tractor parade on agreed routes on Republic Day, 26 January 2021. A substantial section of the parade broke from the agreed routes, reached the ITO crossing in central Delhi after clashes with police, and a group entered the Red Fort, where a religious flag was raised on a flagpole of the complex. One protester, Navreet Singh, died during the day; the Delhi Police stated that his tractor had overturned, and his family maintained that he had been shot, and the two accounts were never reconciled in any public finding. The Morcha condemned the violence, suspended some of its own planned programmes, and disowned the organisations and individuals it held responsible for the departure from the agreed route. The government and its supporters treated the day as proof of the movement's character; the unions treated it as a deviation the movement had neither planned nor endorsed and pointed to the discipline of the months before and after. What is not disputed is the effect: public sympathy, which the encampments had cultivated carefully, narrowed sharply in the days that followed, and state administrations moved to clear the sites.

The movement's survival past that point turned on a single evening. On 28 January 2021 the Ghaziabad administration served notice on the Ghazipur encampment to vacate, and Rakesh Tikait, the Bharatiya Kisan Union leader whose organisation anchored that site, made an emotional public appeal, in tears before the cameras, refusing to leave and calling on farmers to come. Through that night and the following days, farmers returned to Ghazipur in large numbers, a mahapanchayat at Muzaffarnagar on 29 January 2021 relaunched mobilisation across western Uttar Pradesh, and the eviction effort collapsed. From February 2021 the protest settled into its long middle period: rotating contingents keeping the camps staffed while villages farmed, a parallel farmers' parliament conducted at Jantar Mantar during the monsoon session in July 2021, and periodic country-wide actions, including a shutdown call in September 2021, that kept the charter before the public without a second march on the capital. The camps were, by every account including hostile ones, elaborately organised, with community kitchens, medical camps, libraries, and rosters that treated the encampment as a village transplanted rather than a crowd assembled.

The movement also built the apparatus of a counter-public. From the Singhu site, volunteers produced a newsletter, the Trolley Times, named for the tractor trolleys that served as the camps' housing, carrying the movement's account of itself in Punjabi, Hindi and English to readers the national media's framing did not reach. Mahapanchayats, the traditional mass assemblies of the region's farming communities, carried the mobilisation far beyond the camps through 2021, most visibly at Muzaffarnagar on 5 September 2021, where press reports numbered the attendance in the lakhs and where the assembled farmers took the demand for repeal into the electoral politics of Uttar Pradesh. A country-wide shutdown called for 27 September 2021, the anniversary of the laws' assent, closed markets and highways across many states without a second march on the capital. The pattern across the year is worth recording because it contradicts the description the movement's critics sometimes gave of it, of a single static siege: the encampment was the anchor of the protest, but its energy was continuously projected outward, into assemblies, publications, state politics and the calendar of anniversaries, in a manner that required the government to answer the charter afresh every few weeks while conceding nothing on its substance.

The state's answer in February 2021 ran through the criminal process, and it forms part of the legal record of the protest. After the Republic Day violence, the Delhi police registered cases against protest leaders and against journalists who had reported the day's events, issued lookout notices, and fortified the three protest sites with concrete blocks, concertina wire, trenches and, in photographs that became emblematic of the confrontation, iron nails embedded in the road surface at the barricades; the farmers answered the nails, in a gesture the press recorded at Ghazipur, by planting flowering saplings in the soil beside them. In the middle of February 2021 the police arrested a young climate activist, Disha Ravi, in connection with an online toolkit, a shared document of campaign suggestions publicised by the Swedish activist Greta Thunberg, and charged her with sedition and criminal conspiracy; a Delhi court granted her bail ten days later, the order recording the paucity of the evidence and the standing of citizens as conscience keepers of government in a democracy. The Morcha treated the arrests as an attempt to criminalise the agitation's support networks; the police maintained that the proceedings concerned specific offences and a document they characterised as part of a coordinated campaign against the state. The episode matters to a legislative history for one reason: with the statutes stayed in court and the talks suspended, the state's continuing engagement with the movement ran, for that season, through the machinery of criminal law, and the bail order in the toolkit case remains the period's clearest judicial statement on the line between a protest's international amplification and its criminalisation.

The gravest single incident of the year came on 3 October 2021 at Tikunia in Lakhimpur Kheri district of Uttar Pradesh, where farmers had gathered to protest a visit by the state's Deputy Chief Minister. Vehicles in a convoy associated with Ashish Mishra, the son of the Union Minister of State for Home Affairs, Ajay Mishra Teni, drove into the protesters. Four farmers and a local journalist died after being struck or in the crush that followed, and three men travelling with the convoy were killed in the violence that erupted afterwards. The state police investigation, conducted by a special investigation team whose work the Supreme Court later had monitored by a retired judge, concluded in December 2021 that the driving into the crowd was a deliberate and pre-planned act rather than negligence, and a charge sheet filed on 3 January 2022 named Ashish Mishra as the principal accused. He and his father deny the charges, and the case proceeded through the courts at a pace the unions repeatedly characterised as the second injustice of the episode. Lakhimpur Kheri hardened every position it touched: it converted the unions' demand for the minister's dismissal into a test of the government's good faith, and it made the eventual settlement harder, because the Morcha's final charter carried Lakhimpur compensation and prosecution demands alongside the legislative ones.

The end, when it came, came from the executive and not from the legislature or the courts. On 19 November 2021, the festival of Gurpurab, the Prime Minister addressed the nation and announced that the three laws would be repealed, saying that the government had failed to convince a section of farmers of the laws' merits despite sincere effort, and appealing to the encampments to return home. The Union Cabinet approved the repeal bill on 24 November 2021, and on 29 November 2021 the bill was introduced and passed in both Houses on a single day, by voice vote, with the opposition's demand for a discussion refused, in a symmetry with the original passage that the process argument of this article does not let pass. The President's assent followed on 30 November 2021, and the short statute that did the work, [the Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021](/2014/04/15/farm-laws-repeal-act-2021-explained/), closed the formal life of the three Acts. The Morcha did not treat the announcement as the end. It sought written commitments on the remainder of its charter, and through the first week of December 2021 the two sides exchanged drafts until, on 9 December 2021, the Agriculture Secretary, Sanjay Agarwal, signed a letter to the Morcha recording commitments on a committee for the support price, withdrawal of cases, and compensation for the families of the dead. The Morcha announced the suspension of the protest the same day, and from 11 December 2021 the encampments were vacated, the protesters leaving in what the movement called victory marches, a little over a year after the columns had been stopped at the same crossings. The Morcha's own count of the encampment is 380 days.

## The economics underneath the argument

Both cases rested on a political economy that neither stated in full, because each side's full statement embarrassed its own slogans. The system's centre was procurement. The Food Corporation of India and state agencies purchase wheat and paddy at the support price in quantities that feed the public distribution system and the food security legislation's entitlements across the country, and the geography of that purchase is radically concentrated: Punjab, a state with a small fraction of the country's farm households, has in year after year supplied the largest single share of the wheat entering the central pool, and Punjab and Haryana together a dominant share of two crops on which distant states' rations depend. That concentration is what the government's narrowness argument was pointing at, and it is also what the unions' national-function argument was pointing at; the same fact carried both. Around the purchase stood the fiscal architecture of the procurement states. A mandi transaction in Punjab carried, on the state's own stated position as recorded in the analyses of the period, a combined statutory take of about eight and a half per cent, market fee, a rural development fund levy, and the commission agent's charge, divided among the market board, the fund and the agent. Aggregated over the procurement of two crops a year, the fee stream financed the rural roads, market yards and link infrastructure of the state, and the market board and the rural development fund were among the Punjab state's significant revenue instruments. The trade area's exemption of the parallel channel from that entire stack was therefore not, from Chandigarh's vantage point, a detail of market design. It was a forecast of the defunding of the system that physically gathered the nation's grain, issued by a legislature in which Punjab's representatives were a small minority, under a constitutional theory the state disputed.

The farmer's side of the ledger had its own unspoken entries. Procurement at the support price is, for the Punjab and Haryana grower, not one marketing option among several but the harvest's destination, planned into the crop choice, the credit cycle and the household's year; the paddy-wheat rotation itself, ecologically costly in groundwater and stubble, is the rotation the purchase system rewards. A reform that promised better prices elsewhere asked that grower to discount a certain buyer, arriving at a known date at a known price within walking distance of the farm, against the possibility of better prices from buyers who might not arrive, in a market whose information, grading, weighing and payment systems would all be new, and whose disputes would be heard by an executive officer rather than a court. Economists who supported the reform answered, correctly, that the certain buyer was also a ceiling, that prices in the open market for many crops in many seasons run above the support price the mandi rarely beats, and that the system's certainty was purchased by locking the region into a water-hungry monoculture. Economists who opposed it answered, also correctly, that optionality is worth most to those who can wait, store and travel, and least to the grower with two hectares, a loan falling due and a perishable harvest. The standoff between a floor that functions as a ceiling and a market that functions as a gamble is the permanent tension of Indian agricultural price policy, older than the farm laws and untouched by their repeal. What the legislative process owed both sides, in 2020, was a forum in which that tension would be examined with the figures on the table, and what the process in fact provided was two monologues, delivered a hundred kilometres apart, in a legislature and at a barricade.

## The case the government made

The government's case deserves to be stated as its holders stated it, because the process critique that follows is only interesting if the substantive reform had real force behind it. The first strand was pedigree. Ministers argued that agricultural marketing reform had been recommended, in substantially this direction, for two decades before 2020: an expert committee on agricultural marketing chaired by Shankarlal Guru reported in 2001 in favour of loosening the regulated-market monopoly, the Centre circulated a model marketing Act to the states in 2003 and a revised model again in 2017, and the high-level committee on restructuring the Food Corporation of India chaired by Shanta Kumar reported in 2015 in favour of renegotiating the procurement-heavy system. Two state governments of different parties had amended their marketing laws in the same direction without central compulsion, and the principal opposition party had gone into the 2019 general election promising to free farm trade from the marketing Acts altogether. On this strand, the ordinances of June 2020 were not an ambush but the execution, overdue in the government's telling, of a consensus that had been assembled report by report and never acted on because states would not move first.

The second strand was the design claim. The laws, ministers insisted in Parliament and in the Agriculture Minister's open letter to farmers of December 2020, added a channel and removed none: the mandis would continue, procurement at the support price would continue, and the Prime Minister told the Rajya Sabha in February 2021 that the support price had existed, existed, and would continue to exist. Contract farming under the second Act was framed as protection rather than exposure, since the agreement had to state its price terms at the outset, the farmer's land was placed beyond recovery, and the sponsor bore the commercial risk the farmer could not. The Essential Commodities amendment was framed as the removal of a standing deterrent to investment in storage and processing, with stock limits retained for genuine emergencies under the price triggers. Economists of standing supported the direction in print: Ramesh Chand of the NITI Aayog argued that the reforms addressed the structural stagnation of farm incomes, and Ashok Gulati, the agricultural economist later placed on the Supreme Court's committee, had advocated marketing liberalisation for years while urging that price assurance be handled with care. The government also pointed to the consultation it said had occurred: the model Acts had been with the states for years, the agriculture ministry had held discussions with state governments and farmer representatives in the period before the ordinances, and the ordinance route itself, it argued, was a response to a pandemic-season urgency about food supply chains that did not admit a full session's delay.

The pedigree argument had a partisan edge that ministers sharpened as the opposition's support for the protest grew. The Congress party's manifesto for the 2019 general election had itself promised to repeal the agricultural produce marketing legislation and to make trade in agricultural produce free of restrictions, and the economic survey tradition the government cited had been assembled under administrations of both coalitions. The opposition answered that a manifesto promise to reform a state system by consent was not a mandate to override it by central statute, and that its objection had never been to reform but to this reform's method and content: the fee exemption, the court bar and the missing price floor were choices the 2020 text made that no committee report and no manifesto had ever proposed in that combination. That answer deserves to be stated because it locates the true disagreement with precision. By the end of 2020 the argument was no longer between reform and the status quo, if it had ever been; it was between two reforms, one that freed trade while keeping the regulated yard taxing and the floor priced, and one that freed trade by disabling both, conducted as an argument about betrayal because the legislative process had provided no room in which it could be conducted as an argument about clauses.

Running through the design claim was a constitutional aesthetic the government's law officers stated most clearly in the fight over the state amendment bills: the idea of one national market. The Constitution itself, in Article 301, declares that trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free, and the Union's advisers argued publicly that a patchwork of state marketing monopolies, each taxing the grain that crossed its yards, was the negation of that declaration in the sector where it mattered most. On this view the trade and commerce Act did not invent a policy; it executed a constitutional commitment two generations old, and the farmers of Punjab, whose produce fed the country, were the intended beneficiaries of a market finally as large as the nation their grain already served. The unions never accepted the premise that the mandi fee was a tax on movement rather than the price of the infrastructure that made movement possible, but the one-market argument explains a feature of the government's conduct that pure electoral calculation does not: its genuine incomprehension, visible across the talks, that a law offering farmers more buyers could be received as an attack. Each side had a theory of what the mandi was. The statute was written from one of them, without the hearing that might have forced the other into the text.

The third strand was the governability argument, and it was the one ministers pressed hardest as the encampment lengthened. The protest, on the government's account, was geographically narrow at its core: its centre of gravity was Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh, the belt where state procurement of wheat and paddy at the support price is most complete and where the commission-agent economy of the regulated yards is most developed, and it therefore represented, in the phrase ministers used repeatedly, the interests of the best-protected farmers in the country rather than the condition of the Indian farmer generally. Parliamentary Affairs Minister Pralhad Joshi put the institutional version of the point during the monsoon session itself, stating that the government commanded comfortable numbers in the Rajya Sabha, 110 against 72 on his count, and that a division would have changed nothing but the noise. The developed form of the argument is a genuine one about representative government: a legislature elected by the whole country had enacted a reform within its competence, and a government that withdraws legislation whenever an affected interest can sustain a long encampment at the capital has, in effect, transferred a veto from the legislature to whichever group can mobilise longest. On this account, the year of refusal to repeal was not obstinacy but the defence of a constitutional premise, and the eventual repeal was justified by its makers not as concession but as a judgment that national unity and the approaching electoral calendar in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh made the cost of persistence higher than the cost of retreat.

## The case the farmers made

The unions' case also had three strands, and the first was about how the laws arrived. The ordinances were promulgated in June 2020, in the first pandemic summer, without the unions having been consulted on drafts they had no opportunity to see; the bills that replaced them were taken through Parliament in days, without reference to any committee, over a demand for a recorded vote that was refused. For the Morcha, this sequence was not a procedural footnote but the substance of the grievance, because a farming agreement Act and a trade Act that between them reordered the marketing of every crop in the country were, in its submission, the paradigm case of legislation that the committee system and the division exist to slow down. The demand for a division in the Rajya Sabha, pressed on the floor by members including the Trinamool Congress's Derek O'Brien, was for the unions the symbol of the whole method: the ordinary mechanism by which a minority places its dissent on record and a majority demonstrates its strength was declined at the moment it was most needed, and the eight members suspended the next day confirmed, in the movement's telling, that the House had been managed rather than persuaded.

The second strand was economic, and it was specific rather than rhetorical. The trade area, the unions argued, was not a neutral second channel: because trade inside the regulated yards bore market fee and cess and trade outside bore neither, the new channel was subsidised by its exemption, and volume would migrate until the yards withered, taking with them the price discovery of the open auction and the state institutions the fees financed. The support price had no statutory existence, which the government presented as continuity and the unions presented as the exposure itself: procurement at that price depended on the yards, the agencies and the political economy of the two states that supplied the bulk of the public distribution system's grain, and a reform that weakened the yards while offering no legal floor left the farmer with an assurance that ministers could repeat but no court could enforce. The farming agreement Act's guaranteed price was, in their reading, a price agreed with a sponsor of vastly greater bargaining power, with disputes routed to a sub-divisional officer rather than a court open to them by right, and the Essential Commodities amendment removed, at the same moment, the stock-limit discipline that had been the state's one instrument against hoarding by the largest buyers. Kaushik Basu, a former chief economic adviser to the Union government, gave the sceptical position an establishment voice, arguing in print that the laws were flawed in design and that the deadlock centred on the refusal of a statutory price guarantee. The unions also answered the narrowness charge directly: Punjab and Haryana grow the wheat and rice that the public distribution system distributes across India, so an interest that looks regional in a headcount is national in its function, and the states with the most complete procurement were not defending a privilege but demonstrating what a working purchase system looks like to farmers who have never had one.

Two features of the agrarian structure underneath the argument deserve statement, because the government's case treated the mandi system as a tax on the farmer and the unions treated it as a welfare state in miniature, and both descriptions fasten on something real. The commission agent, the arhtiya, whom the reform's advocates presented as the middleman the trade area would liberate the farmer from, is in the procurement belt also the farmer's banker: the agent advances seed, fertiliser and consumption credit across the season against the standing crop, without the documentation the formal banking system requires of a smallholder, and the relationship, exploitative in its interest burden and indispensable in its availability, is the credit infrastructure of the village. A reform that routed trade away from the yards threatened, in the unions' account, to strand that credit system before any replacement existed, and to leave the small farmer facing the sponsor of a farming agreement as a debtor faces a creditor, alone. Behind that account stands the arithmetic of Indian landholding: the Agricultural Census records a structure dominated by small and marginal holdings, the large majority of operational holdings running to under two hectares, farmed by households for whom a single failed price discovery is not a bad quarter but the loss of the year's subsistence. Risk is not distributed evenly across a market, and the unions' deepest argument was about its distribution: the laws transferred the risks of price, of counterparty failure and of legal process from institutions that could pool them to households that could not, and called the transfer freedom. The government answered, with equal structural warrant, that the existing system already concentrated those risks on the farmer, in the form of the yard's cartelised bidding and the agent's interest, and that a choice of buyers could not make a seller worse off than a monopoly had. That exchange, between two true descriptions of the same village, is the substantive argument the country never got to hear tested in a committee room.

The third strand was federal. Agriculture is a State List subject, and the unions, supported by the governments of Punjab and other opposition-ruled states, argued that Parliament had entered the field through the trade and commerce entry in a manner that left the state competence nominal. Punjab's Assembly acted on the argument on 20 October 2020, passing amendment bills that sought to restore the support price as a condition of valid sale within the state and to exempt small holdings from attachment, and Rajasthan moved comparable amendments in the same season. The legal fate of those bills is instructive and is examined with the states question in the FAQ section: amendments of that kind, repugnant to a central law on a concurrent subject, require the President's assent to operate within the state, and that assent was not given, so the state route the critics proposed as the democratic alternative produced paper rather than law while the central Acts stood. The unions' conclusion from the whole sequence was stated by their leadership in identical terms across the year: laws made without hearing the governed, in a field the Constitution assigns to the states, carrying no enforceable price, would be answered not with amendments but with repeal, because amendment would concede the legitimacy of the method that produced them. That maximalism was a choice, and its costs are part of the record; it was also, on the unions' own account of their mandate, the only position their constituent organisations had authorised the delegation to carry.

## The states' parallel answer, and its constitutional limit

One strand of the controversy was conducted entirely in legislatures, and its fate is the federal lesson of the episode. Punjab's response to the central Acts was to legislate against them. On 20 October 2020, in a special session, the Punjab Assembly passed a resolution rejecting the three Acts and adopted amendment bills aimed at the central statutes' operation within the state: the bills sought to restore the protections the state system had carried, by making the support price a condition of a valid purchase of wheat and paddy, by preserving a fee on trade the central Act had exempted, and, in a companion amendment to the criminal procedure code, by shielding agricultural land up to two and a half acres from attachment. The Assembly's near-unanimity was itself a datum: every member present except the two of the ruling party at the Centre supported the bills, including members of the party whose own government in an earlier decade had piloted marketing reform in the state. Rajasthan's Assembly passed comparable amendment bills in the same season. The Chief Minister of Punjab led a delegation of legislators to the Governor to press for assent, in a piece of constitutional theatre that stated the states' case in its purest form: a subject the Constitution assigns to the states had been legislated on by the Centre, and the state legislature was using the only instrument the Constitution gives it in reply.

The instrument is weaker than it looks, and the sequel demonstrates the design. Where a state law and a central law on a Concurrent List subject conflict, the central law prevails, unless the state law has been reserved for the President's consideration and receives the President's assent, in which case it prevails within that state under Article 254 of the Constitution. The farm legislation sat across the entries: agriculture in the State List, trade and commerce in foodstuffs in the Concurrent List, inter-state trade in the Union List, so that the state amendments, to the extent they contradicted the central Acts, required the assent route to operate at all. The Punjab bills were forwarded through the Governor for that assent. The Governor sought clarifications from the state government, the bills travelled to the President, and the assent was never given; the bills remained in the constitutional antechamber where reserved state legislation waits, neither law nor rejection, for the remainder of the central Acts' lives, and the repeal at the Centre eventually made their object moot. Rajasthan's bills met the same stillness. The episode thus supplies a controlled experiment in Indian federalism: a state with an overwhelming legislative consensus, acting on a subject it regarded as its own, against central laws it regarded as unconstitutional in their reach, could not give its own answer legal effect within any time that mattered, because the Constitution's mechanism for resolving the conflict runs through the very executive whose legislation was in dispute. Critics of the central laws drew from this the conclusion that the federal objection and the democratic objection were the same objection, that the states had been denied both their competence and their remedy. Defenders of the central legislation answered that Article 254 is the Constitution's own settlement, that trade in foodgrains is a national market by design, and that a patchwork of state vetoes over a concurrent subject is precisely what the assent mechanism exists to discipline. Both are accounts of the same text. What the record adds is the timing: between the Punjab Assembly's vote in October 2020 and the repeal in November 2021, the federal machinery produced no decision of any kind, and a constitutional question with a designated resolver was resolved, in the event, by a crowd at the border and a prime ministerial broadcast rather than by the resolver.

## The machinery that was never used

The Indian Parliament possesses a well-stocked set of instruments for slowing legislation down, and the farm bills met none of them. A bill of consequence can be referred, after introduction, to the departmentally related standing committee that shadows its ministry, or to a select committee of the House constituted for that bill alone, or to a joint committee of both Houses. Referral is not a favour the opposition begs for; it is the ordinary course for complex legislation, and session reviews by PRS Legislative Research record a long decline in its use, with the share of bills sent to committees falling across successive Lok Sabhas to a small minority in the Seventeenth. What a committee does with a bill is unglamorous and irreplaceable: it takes evidence from the ministry, from affected interests, and from independent experts; it examines the text clause by clause; it produces a report, often with dissent notes, which the House then has before it when the bill is debated. The farmers' objections, the revenue consequences of the fee bar, the enforceability of the guaranteed price, the civil court bar, and the competence question were each, in form, precisely the kind of point that evidence-taking exists to surface while surface can still change text. The deep examination of [how Parliament's committees scrutinise bills](/2022/04/01/parliamentary-committees-bill-scrutiny/) belongs to the article that owns that subject; what belongs here is the fact of the omission, and that motions to refer the farm bills to a select committee were moved in the Rajya Sabha and defeated by voice vote on the same day the bills themselves passed.

What referral would have set in motion deserves a paragraph of its own, because the committee system is invisible to most citizens and was invisible, by choice, in this legislation's life. A select committee of the Rajya Sabha is drawn from that House for the single bill before it, in proportion to party strength, under a chair the House appoints; a joint committee draws from both Houses; the standing committee on agriculture is a permanent body that shadows the ministry across all its business. Each works the same way at the point that matters. The committee settles a programme of evidence: the ministry appears to explain and defend its text, the affected interests appear to state their objections, independent experts appear to test both, and the proceedings, unlike the floor debate, are conducted by question and answer on particular clauses rather than by set speech on the bill in general. The committee then goes through the text clause by clause, may propose amendments which the government of the day, commanding the committee's arithmetic as it commands the House's, is free to accept or resist, and reports to the House with the evidence and any dissent notes attached. Two properties of this machinery explain its value in a case like the farm bills. The first is that it separates the question whether a clause works from the question whether the bill should exist, so that a government can concede the fee problem or the forum problem without conceding the reform, and an opposition can win amendments without winning the argument about the bill itself. The second is that it manufactures the one asset the farm laws never had, a public record, made while change was still possible, in which the objections and the government's answers to them stand side by side. A movement that forms after such a record exists must argue against a document; a movement that forms in its absence argues against a silence, and silence is the easier opponent. The presiding officers' place in this machinery is the other piece of the design the episode exposed. The decision to refer a bill, the decision to allow a division, the decision how long a debate runs, all pass through the Chair's discretion, exercised under rules the House has made for itself and policed by conventions the Chair is expected to honour. The office is therefore both powerful and exposed: powerful, because its discretions decide whether the minority's instruments function; exposed, because a Chair seen to exercise every discretion in the majority's favour converts each procedural ruling into evidence for the minority's account of the institution itself. September 2020 made the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha a protagonist of the farm laws story, a position the design of his office exists to prevent, and the forty-seven members who sought his removal were registering, in the only form available to them, the proposition that the referee had joined the match.

### What is a division in the Rajya Sabha, and how does a member claim one?

A division is the recorded vote that follows when the Chair's declaration of the result is challenged. Rule 252 of the Rajya Sabha's Rules of Procedure governs the putting of the question by voice. Where the declaration is challenged, the rules provide for votes to be counted and recorded by the automatic vote recorder or in the lobbies.

The events of 20 September 2020 turned on the space between that rule and its application. The Deputy Chairman, Harivansh, presided. As the two principal bills were taken up, opposition members demanded a division on the motions before the House, including the motions to refer the bills to a select committee. The demand was not granted, and the bills were declared passed by voice vote amid sustained disorder in the chamber. The accounts of why diverge along a line that has never been bridged. The position stated by the Chairman, M. Venkaiah Naidu, and by the government in the days that followed, was that the Deputy Chairman had been willing to allow a division if members returned to their seats and order was restored, that a recorded vote cannot be conducted in a House in disorder, and that the demand was pressed from the well rather than from seats. The opposition's account was that the demand was made and refused, that disorder followed the refusal rather than preceding it, and that members then went into the well because the ordinary channel had been closed to them. Eight opposition members were suspended on 21 September 2020 for their conduct during the sitting: Derek O'Brien and Dola Sen of the Trinamool Congress, Rajeev Satav, Ripun Bora and Syed Nasir Hussain of the Congress, Sanjay Singh of the Aam Aadmi Party, and K. K. Ragesh and Elamaram Kareem of the Communist Party of India (Marxist). A notice by forty-seven opposition members seeking the Deputy Chairman's removal was rejected at the threshold, the Chairman holding that the fourteen days' notice the Constitution requires had not been given, and opposition parties carried their objection out of the House altogether, petitioning the President not to assent to bills passed in that manner.

Two things about the division deserve emphasis because they are persistently misunderstood outside Parliament. The first is that a division almost never changes an outcome. The government of the day had the numbers in the Rajya Sabha, as its own spokesman stated in numbers, and a recorded vote on 20 September 2020 would have passed both bills. What a division changes is the record: it fixes each member's position in a form constituents and history can read, and it demonstrates that the majority exists rather than asserting it on the Chair's hearing of a shout. The demand for a division is therefore best understood as a demand for the ordinary constitutional courtesy of being counted in defeat, and its refusal converted a legislative defeat the opposition expected into a grievance about the legitimacy of the process itself, which is a far more durable political asset. The second is that the discretion involved is real but convention-bound. The Chair must judge whether a demand is genuine or dilatory and whether the House is in a condition to vote, and no rule can remove that judgment. The convention that has grown around the judgment, across presidencies of both Houses, is that a division demanded on a bill by a substantial body of members is conceded, because the cost of conceding it is minutes and the cost of refusing it is the argument the country then had for fourteen months. The select committee question, which the FAQ section addresses in its own right, and the division question are at bottom the same question asked at two different stages: whether the majority was willing to spend the small change of parliamentary time to purchase legitimacy for a reform it believed in. On the evidence of September 2020, it was not, and the price of that saving is the subject of the artifact that closes this article's analysis.

## A court that paused a statute

On 12 January 2021, in Rakesh Vaishnav v Union of India, a bench of the Supreme Court headed by the Chief Justice of India, S. A. Bobde, stayed the implementation of the three farm laws until further orders and constituted a committee of four to consult stakeholders and report to the Court. The order is the most legally unusual event of the episode, and it deserves the careful treatment the brief of this series assigns it, because the Court suspended the operation of three validly enacted statutes without holding, or purporting to hold, any part of them unconstitutional. The bench had, in the hearings of the preceding day, criticised the government's handling of the agitation in strong terms, and the order recorded the Court's apprehension that the protest, then in its seventh week at the capital's borders in winter, carried a real risk to life and public order. The stay was paired with directions that protected the protesters' material position while it operated: procurement at the minimum support price was to continue as before, and no farmer was to be dispossessed of land by reason of anything done under the stayed laws.

### What did the Supreme Court actually order on 12 January 2021?

The Court stayed the implementation of all three farm laws until further orders, without striking down any provision or deciding the challenges to their validity. It directed that procurement at the minimum support price continue and that no farmer be dispossessed of land under the new laws, and it constituted a four-member committee to hear stakeholders and report.

The route by which the question reached the Court adds an irony the legal commentary of the time dwelt on. The lead case from which the order takes its name, Rakesh Vaishnav v Union of India, was a petition by a supporter of the farm laws, who had come to court against the protests themselves and the blockades they maintained. Challenges to the laws' validity, raising the competence and federalism objections, were also before the Court, as were petitions about the conditions at the protest sites. The bench heard the whole cluster together across the second week of January 2021, and its displeasure in the hearings was directed less at the laws than at the handling of the confrontation: the Chief Justice told the government's law officers, in open court on 11 January 2021, that the Court was disappointed with the manner in which the government had dealt with the agitation, and asked whether the laws could not be held back while a settlement was attempted. The government declined to hold them back itself. The order of the following day did with the Court's hand what the Court had invited the executive to do with its own, and did it in a form, an interim stay coupled to a consultative committee, that had no close precedent in the Court's treatment of primary legislation. Students of the Court's interim practice will search its reports in vain for a parallel in which a bench, without recording any prima facie view on validity, suspended a statute in order to improve the atmosphere of a negotiation between the statute's makers and its opponents. The absence of precedent is not by itself a condemnation; courts meet unprecedented situations with the powers they have. But it fixes the correct description of the order, which is that the Court invented a remedy for a political emergency and clothed it in the forms of interim relief, and it is on that description, rather than on any dispute about what the order said, that the argument over its propriety has always turned.

The case for the order, made by the bench itself and defended by those who welcomed it, runs as follows. A court confronted with a mass agitation, a stalled negotiation, and a credible apprehension of violence is not required to sit inside the categories of ordinary interim relief while people freeze at barricades. The stay was interim, not final; it decided no one's rights; it preserved the status quo on procurement and land while removing, at a stroke, the unions' stated fear that the laws would begin operating against them mid-negotiation. Its stated purpose, in the order's own words, was that a pause would assuage the hurt feelings of the farmers and encourage them to come to the negotiating table, and the committee was the instrument by which the Court hoped to inform itself and the parties. On this reading, the Court used a recognised interim power for the oldest judicial purpose there is, the prevention of irreparable harm, and the harm it had in view was not to a party's legal rights but to the public peace on which every other right depends. Defenders also note what the order conspicuously did not do: it did not grant the unions' demand for repeal, which no court could have granted, and it did not spare the government the embarrassment of having its legislation frozen by the judiciary, which is not the act of a court captured by the executive.

The case against the order was made at the time by lawyers, by editorial writers, and by the unions themselves, and it has not weakened with repetition. The settled approach of Indian constitutional courts is that enacted legislation carries a presumption of constitutionality, and that an interim stay of a statute is an exceptional order granted where a prima facie case of constitutional infirmity is shown and the balance of convenience demands it. The 12 January order identified no prima facie infirmity in the three Acts; the competence challenge, the federalism challenge, and the challenges to particular provisions were left entirely unexamined, and the stay rested instead on the state of the streets. The Hindu's editorial response named the difficulty precisely, describing the order as an attempt to impose a compromise on the parties, in which the stay functioned as an instrument of the Court's own arrangement rather than as a conclusion from any legal defect in the laws. Mediating a political dispute between a government and a mass movement is, on this critique, not a judicial function at all, and a court that undertakes it purchases a few weeks of calm at the price of two institutional injuries: it relieves Parliament and the executive of the obligation to resolve a legislative conflict politically, and it leaves the citizen and the market in a limbo in which three Acts are simultaneously law and not law, their validity undecided and their operation suspended, for as long as the Court's patience or the parties' stamina lasts. That limbo is in fact what happened. The laws were never implemented, never adjudicated, and never formally alive for a single day of ordinary operation between the stay and the repeal.

Committees of the Court are not in themselves an aberration, and the distinction is worth drawing because it locates the farm laws committee's novelty exactly. Indian courts appoint committees and commissioners within litigation they are deciding: to find facts in environmental and municipal matters, to monitor compliance with their own orders, to administer a process the executive has failed to administer. In each such case the committee serves an adjudication that exists independently of it, and its report returns to a court that will decide. The committee of 12 January 2021 was constituted in the opposite posture. There was no adjudication it served, since the bench had formed no view on the challenges and would form none; its task was to consult the parties to a political dispute and report to a court that proposed, on the strength of the consultation, to facilitate a settlement between them. When the largest party to the dispute refused the consultation, the committee's remaining work, hearing the willing and reporting to a sealed file, demonstrated the limit of the device: a court can compel attendance at its own proceedings, but it cannot compel the belief, on which mediation depends, that the mediator has not already decided. That belief is the currency the committee spent on its first day, when its members' published views became the story, and no subsequent diligence could restore it.

The committee deepened the difficulty rather than relieving it. Its four members, the former Member of Parliament and farm leader Bhupinder Singh Mann, the Shetkari Sanghatana president Anil Ghanwat, and the agricultural economists Ashok Gulati and Pramod Kumar Joshi, had each expressed public support for the direction of the reforms before their appointment, a fact the Morcha seized on within hours, announcing that it would not appear before a committee whose members had already declared the conclusions the consultation was meant to test. Counsel for the unions told the Court the same in the hearings. Mann recused himself on 14 January 2021, stating that he would not compromise the interests of Punjab and its farmers by serving, and the remaining three members heard the government, state governments, and those farmer organisations willing to engage, and submitted their report to the Court on 19 March 2021. The Court never published the report, never resumed the hearing to a conclusion, and never relied on the document in any order. Anil Ghanwat, having written repeatedly to the Court seeking its release, published the report himself on 21 March 2022, after the repeal had made it a historical document; its contents, recording that most organisations which engaged with the committee favoured the laws with amendments, changed nothing by then and were received by the unions as confirmation of the objection they had made at the start. The committee episode thus supplies a small, complete parable inside the larger one: an institution improvised to create the consultation the legislative process had skipped, and then disabled by the same defect, the appearance that the consultation's outcome had been arranged before it began. For readers pursuing a matter of their own through material of this kind, the general account given in this article is legal information and analysis, and no substitute for advice on an individual case, where the forum and limitation rules are unforgiving.

## The facts that remain disputed

A contested episode accumulates contested numbers, and the honest treatment of the sharpest of them, the deaths, is to report a discrepancy rather than to select a figure. The Morcha maintained a running list of those it counted as having died in the course of the agitation, at the camps, on the roads to and from them, and in connected incidents, and on 4 December 2021 it sent the government a list of 702 names. Leaders of the movement put the toll higher in public statements as the year closed, at around 750, and the figure the movement later settled on in its commemorations is 736. Against this stands the position the Union government placed on the parliamentary record. Answering questions in the Lok Sabha in the winter session of 2021, the Agriculture Minister, Narendra Singh Tomar, stated that his ministry had no record of any farmer having died during the agitation, and that the question of compensation therefore did not arise. The two positions are not two estimates of one number; they are answers to different questions. The Morcha's list counts deaths the movement attributed to the agitation, including deaths from illness, cold, cardiac events and road accidents among people who had travelled to or lived at the protest sites, and its critics, in Parliament and outside it, objected that such a list proves participation rather than causation. The ministry's answer asserts an absence from its own records, which were never designed to count such deaths and were not supplemented by any official enumeration. No agreed count exists, no commission was charged with producing one, and a reader offered any single figure for the deaths of the farmers' protest, from any direction, is being offered an artefact of the dispute rather than a finding. The list's assembly is itself part of the record: volunteers and journalists maintained the documentation on which the Morcha's count rested, in circumstances where keeping names, dates and family details accurate and safe was a practical problem, of the kind that makes reporters covering contested movements keep their source material in protected workspaces, including [a private workspace built to protect journalists and their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/).

Characterisation was the third front of the factual war, and the brief of any honest account is to decline both sides' vocabularies. In the protest's first months, figures in and around the ruling party described the movement as misled at best and, at the sharp end, as infiltrated by separatist and extremist elements, a charge given its widest currency when the Prime Minister of Canada's expressions of concern for the protesters drew a formal diplomatic protest from India at the end of November 2020 and a domestic argument about foreign hands. The Morcha rejected the characterisations as a device to avoid the charter's substance, pointed to the movement's open leadership, its published demands and its discipline at the sites, and treated every escalation of the vocabulary as evidence for its founding claim, that the state preferred to describe the protesters rather than to hear them. The movement's own vocabulary ran in the opposite direction, naming the laws with an adjective of colour and the government's corporate supporters by name in a boycott campaign against named business houses. Neither vocabulary is a finding. What the record supports is narrower: a movement whose organisations, demands and methods were public and consistent throughout; a government that never produced evidence in any forum connecting the movement's leadership to the designs its supporters alleged; and a public argument in which the contest over what to call the protesters frequently displaced the contest over what the statutes said, to the cost of both.

Even a single death exhibits the whole structure. Navreet Singh, a young farmer from Uttar Pradesh, died during the tractor parade of 26 January 2021 when his tractor overturned near the ITO crossing in central Delhi, in the day's most contested minutes. His family and the movement maintained that he had been shot; the post-mortem examination recorded fatal head injuries consistent with the overturning of the vehicle, and the police maintained that no bullet injury was found. The family rejected the official account and sought, through the courts, a fresh investigation, and the two versions stand unreconciled on the record. The case is recorded here because it compresses the episode's evidentiary condition into one file: a death at the boundary between a disciplined movement and its most violent day, a medical record and a family's conviction in direct conflict, and no forum in which both were ever tested, because the legal proceedings that might have tested them were not the proceedings either side was fighting. An account that resolves the conflict in either direction would be claiming knowledge the record does not contain; an account that omits it would be concealing the record's character.

Scale is disputed in the same structural way. The numbers present at the border camps at any one time fluctuated with the agricultural calendar, since the movement's own method was rotation, villages relieving one another so that sowing and harvest continued; estimates by journalists and researchers of the population of the sites ranged across the year from the tens of thousands to, at the January peak, several hundred thousand, and the organisers' figures ran higher than the police estimates throughout. Nothing in the argument of this article depends on choosing between them: a mobilisation at the lowest credible estimate was large enough to hold three national highways for a year, and at the highest it does not acquire any additional legal significance.

### Did the farm laws abolish the minimum support price?

No. The minimum support price is an executive policy, announced crop by crop on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, and it has never been created by statute. The three farm laws neither abolished it nor guaranteed it; they were silent on it, and procurement continued throughout.

The unions' argument was different and narrower: that procurement in practice depended on the regulated yards and state agencies the trade law weakened, so a price with no legal floor would wither with the machinery that delivered it.

Whether the support price was in genuine danger is the analytical form of the same dispute, and it is worth stating precisely because it is the point on which the two cases most completely talk past one another. The government was right about the text and about the administrative fact: the laws did not touch the price announcement, procurement at the announced price reached record levels in the very years of the controversy, and no instruction withdrawing procurement was ever produced. The unions were right about the mechanism they feared: a support price is not self-executing, it is delivered through physical purchase at particular places by particular agencies financed within the regulated system, and a reform that exempted the parallel channel from the fees that financed that system, while making no statutory provision for the price, asked the farmers of the procurement belt to exchange a working institution for a ministerial assurance. Which of those two correct statements should have governed the policy judgment is a question about how much weight to give institutional dependence against textual silence, and it is a value-laden judgment about risk, not a fact that further evidence about the laws' wording could settle. The competence question is different in kind: whether Parliament had the power it exercised is a question of law with a right answer, and it is the one question in the entire episode that an authoritative institution was seised of and never answered, since the Supreme Court's stay froze the Acts without a hearing on validity and the repeal then removed the subject matter from beneath the pending challenges.

## Five points at which this could have gone differently

The analysis so far converges on a single claim, which is the namable claim of this article: the committee stage is the cheapest thing a government can buy. Every objection that matured into a fourteen-month confrontation, the fee exemption's effect on state revenue, the absence of any price floor, the bar on the civil courts, the burden of the dispute mechanism on the weaker party, and the competence objection itself, was in form exactly the kind of point a select committee exists to surface, in a room where surfacing it costs the government an amendment rather than a movement. A reference would have consumed weeks. The reform, pursued without it, was lost entire, together with a year of the capital's highways, a session of Parliament, the credibility of the Chair's office in the upper House, and a measure of the Supreme Court's institutional capital that is harder to rebuild than a statute. The table below sets out the five decision points at which a different choice was available, what was done at each, what the alternative was, what the alternative would have cost, and what it might have prevented. It is this article's findable artifact, and it is written to transfer: none of its five rows is specific to agriculture, and a legislator or staffer reading any future bill of consequence can run the same five questions over it.

| Decision point | What was done | The alternative available | What the alternative would have cost | What it might have prevented |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The route into law, June 2020 | Three ordinances promulgated while Parliament was not sitting, later replaced by bills | Awaiting the monsoon session and introducing ordinary bills, with a pre-legislative consultation on published drafts | Some weeks of delay, and the loss of the pandemic moment as a justification for speed | The founding grievance that the laws were made without the governed being heard |
| The committee stage, September 2020 | No reference to any standing, select or joint committee; motions for reference defeated by voice vote | Reference to a select committee of the Rajya Sabha or the standing committee on agriculture, with evidence from unions, states and experts | Weeks of examination, some amendments conceded, the fee and dispute clauses in particular exposed to revision | The transformation of clause-level objections into a movement for total repeal |
| The vote, 20 September 2020 | Passage by voice vote in the Rajya Sabha over a demand for a division; eight members suspended the following day | Conceding the division the opposition demanded, and carrying the bills on a recorded count | Minutes of House time, and a permanent record of each member's position | The legitimacy grievance that outlasted the legislative defeat and armed the protest's narrative |
| The judicial response, January 2021 | A stay of all three Acts without any finding on validity, and a committee of the Court's own to consult stakeholders | A prompt hearing on the constitutional challenges, with interim protection confined to procurement and dispossession | The Court's time on an urgent roster, and the risk of a definitive answer either side disliked | Ten months of statutory limbo and a precedent for suspending legislation as a negotiating instrument |
| The exit, November 2021 | A repeal bill introduced and passed in both Houses on one day without discussion | A debated repeal, in which the government stated its reasons and the House examined what the replacement policy would be | A day or two of parliamentary time, and questions the government preferred not to answer | The symmetry of grievance, in which the repeal repeated the method of the enactment it undid |

The rows are not equal, and ranking them is the analytical heart of the comparison. The ordinance decision and the repeal decision are the most defensible of the five: governments legislate by ordinance in genuine urgency more often than constitutional theory approves, and a repeal the executive has already announced is a formality whose debate would have been theatre. The division refusal is the least defensible in proportion to its cost, because it purchased nothing at all; the bills would have passed anyway, and the only thing the refusal produced was the protest's founding document. The stay is the hardest to judge, because it was addressed to a real danger and because the alternative, a prompt constitutional adjudication in the middle of an encampment, carried risks of its own that the bench could see from the courtroom. But the committee omission is the decision on which the others depend. A bill that has survived a committee has answers, on the record, to the objections its opponents will raise; a government that has heard the unions' evidence in a committee room can still defeat them in the House, but it cannot be accused of refusing to hear them, and its ministers negotiate the following winter from a position documented rather than asserted. The unions' maximalism might have survived a committee stage intact, and the confrontation might have happened anyway over the statutory price, which no committee could have conjured into the text against the government's will. What the committee would certainly have changed is the distribution of blame for the confrontation, and in a dispute that ran for fourteen months on competing accounts of who refused to listen, that distribution was not a detail.

The fifth row carries one further dispute that belongs to the record, about the repeal's timing. The announcement of 19 November 2021 was made on Gurpurab, the birth anniversary of Guru Nanak, a day of the first weight in Punjab, and the Prime Minister's address framed the decision as the government's own conclusion, reached because it had failed, despite sincere effort, to convince the section of farmers who remained unpersuaded. Opposition parties attributed the timing instead to the assembly elections then approaching in Punjab and Uttar Pradesh, and treated the repeal as an electoral instrument whose sincerity the calendar exposed. The government rejected the attribution, pointing out that it had held the laws through a year of protest that included state elections it had contested and lost ground in, and that a decision priced in votes would have been taken at the point of maximum electoral return rather than three months before polling. The truth of the motive is not recoverable from the record and this article does not pretend to recover it. What the record does settle is the sequence's institutional meaning, independent of motive: a reform defended for a year as constitutionally enacted and economically necessary was withdrawn by executive announcement and ratified by a legislature that was permitted no more debate on the withdrawal than it had conducted on the enactment, so that the two decisions that bookend the farm laws were both taken, in form, by the executive alone, with Parliament summoned to each as witness rather than as author.

### Can Parliament repeal an Act without debating the repeal bill?

Yes. Nothing in the Constitution or in the rules of either House requires a minimum debate on a bill, and a repeal bill is an ordinary bill. The Farm Laws Repeal Bill was introduced and passed in both Houses on 29 November 2021, by voice vote, in a single day, with the opposition's demand for a discussion not accepted.

The absence of debate was lawful; the criticism of it is about convention and candour, not about validity.

## Two movements, two endings

The other great street movement of the period that changed legislation, the anti-corruption mobilisation behind Anna Hazare in 2011, is the comparison that isolates the variable this article has been tracking, and the full account of [the Lokpal bill and the Anna Hazare movement](/2011/12/01/lokpal-bill-anna-hazare-movement/) is the record against which the contrast should be read. The Hazare movement demanded a statute, the Jan Lokpal of its own drafting, and the parliamentary system, after failing to accommodate the demand at first, eventually processed it through the system's own instruments: the government's bill was examined by the departmentally related standing committee in the second half of 2011, passed the Lok Sabha in December of that year, and was then referred by the Rajya Sabha, on 21 May 2012, to a select committee of that House, which reported on 23 November 2012 with sixteen recommendations, fourteen of which the government accepted. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act that emerged in December 2013 satisfied neither the movement, which considered it diluted, nor the government, which had resisted its premises, and it was criticised from both directions for a decade afterwards. But it was never answered by a year of encampment, because the movement's central procedural complaint, that nobody in the system would examine its case, had been made false by the system's own machinery. The farm unions' case received the opposite treatment at every corresponding step: no standing committee, no select committee, no recorded vote, no debated repeal. Two movements of comparable scale and comparable suspicion of the political class thus produced opposite relationships between street and statute, and the difference between them is not the virtue of the participants or the merit of the causes. It is the presence or absence, in the legislative pathway, of the one institution designed to convert a crowd's objection into a text's amendment. That is the sense in which the series thesis holds here with unusual completeness: the record around these statutes, the consultations that did and did not happen, the votes that were and were not counted, mattered more to their fate than anything in their text.

A second comparison, without a movement at all, sharpens the same point from the government's side. The goods and services tax, a constitutional amendment at least as intrusive into the states' fiscal domain as the farm laws were into their marketing domain, travelled the full institutional road: a select committee of the Rajya Sabha examined the amendment bill in 2015, reported with recommendations and dissent notes, and the legislation that emerged was a negotiated text whose state buy-in had been constructed clause by clause, with a compensation settlement for the states' revenue fears written into the design. The reform survived, and survives, not because it was modest but because the system had processed its objections before its opponents needed a barricade to file them. Set the three sequences side by side and the pattern is the whole argument in miniature: the tax reform that went to committee endures; the anti-corruption statute that went to committee endures, dented and criticised but unrepealed; the farm reform that went to no committee, took no recorded vote and conceded no amendment was repealed entire, without a debate, eleven months after its passage, by the same majority that had enacted it. Content does not explain that distribution of outcomes. Procedure does.

## What survived the repeal

The repeal answered the first demand of the charter completely and the rest of it not at all, and the settlement that ended the encampment was executive rather than legislative, recorded in the Agriculture Secretary's letter of 9 December 2021 rather than in any statute. The surviving demand, the one that outlasted the protest and returned to the borders in a later mobilisation, is the statutory guarantee of the support price, and its legal architecture is treated in [the account of the support price, procurement and the legal framework](/2014/07/01/msp-procurement-legal-framework-india/) that owns the subject in this cluster. What belongs here is its procedural afterlife. The December letter committed the government to constitute a committee on the support price and related questions; the committee was notified in July 2022 under the chairmanship of Sanjay Agarwal, the former agriculture secretary who had signed the letter, with a remit that included making the support price more effective and transparent and promoting crop diversification, but which did not commit the government to a legal guarantee. The Samyukta Kisan Morcha declined to nominate members to it, holding that a committee whose terms of reference did not include the guarantee itself could not be the settlement the letter had promised, and the committee's work proceeded without the participation of the body whose demand had created it. The pattern is the farm episode in miniature, repeated in peacetime: a committee used as an instrument of deferral because its constitutive choices, who chairs it, what it may consider, and whose absence it can survive, were made in a manner that predetermined the use its intended beneficiaries would make of it.

The committee's afterlife followed the script its constitution had written. The Morcha's refusal to nominate left the body to consult governments, experts and organisations other than the one constituency whose demand had created it, and the demand itself was the one item its remit had been drawn to exclude from commitment: the terms of reference asked how the support price could be made more effective and transparent, a question about administration, where the charter's question was whether the price would be guaranteed by law, a question about obligation. No legislation on the price followed, and the committee produced no public settlement of the question it had been given, so that the instrument chosen to close the account became, in the unions' continuing argument, further evidence that consultation offered without the power to concede the point at issue is a technique of management rather than of agreement. It is the same objection the Morcha had made to the Supreme Court's committee in January 2021, and the repetition, under a different government instrument and in a quieter season, is what converted the farmers' committee scepticism from a negotiating posture into a settled doctrine of the movement: that a committee's value is fixed at its constitution, by who appoints it, what it may consider, and whether those it consults believe its report can change anything.

The demand returned physically in February 2024, under changed leadership. The Samyukta Kisan Morcha (Non-Political), a formation that had separated from the Morcha, and the Kisan Mazdoor Morcha called a second march on Delhi, and from 13 February 2024 their columns were held at the Shambhu and Khanauri crossings on the Punjab and Haryana border, behind fortifications considerably heavier than those of 2020. Four rounds of talks with a ministerial panel followed, and in the round that ended past midnight on 18 February 2024 the government offered a five-year arrangement under which central agencies would purchase five crops, three pulses, maize and cotton, at the support price; the unions rejected the offer as a partial measure that left the general guarantee unaddressed, and the march resumed against the barricades. On 21 February 2024 a young protester, Shubhkaran Singh, died at Khanauri of a head injury sustained in the clashes there; the farmers alleged that he had been shot by Haryana police, the Haryana police denied firing on the protesters, and the Punjab police registered a murder case on his father's complaint, so that the central fact of the second mobilisation's gravest day stands, like the deaths question of the first, in the form of an unresolved dispute between named positions. The encampment at the two crossings then persisted for over a year, its leader Jagjit Singh Dallewal undertaking a prolonged hunger strike from late November 2024, until, on 19 March 2025, Punjab police detained the leaders as they returned from a further round of talks in Chandigarh and cleared both sites, with press reports putting the number detained above four hundred and fifty. The second mobilisation thus ended by police action rather than by settlement, and its charter, headed by the statutory price guarantee, remains where the repeal left it: the standing unfinished business between the Indian state and the farmers of the procurement belt, and the standing exhibit in any argument about what the process failures of 2020 cost.

The two smaller items conceded in the talks of December 2020 completed their own quieter journeys, and their paths record the same institutional habit in miniature. The stubble-burning penalty that the air quality ordinance of the protest's first autumn had carried was dropped from the Commission for Air Quality Management legislation when the permanent statute came before Parliament, so that a concession extracted at the negotiating table survived the repeal that disposed of the larger charter. The electricity amendment the unions had feared, with its movement towards direct transfer of the farm power subsidy to the consumer, was introduced as a bill in 2022 and referred to the standing committee on energy, and had not been enacted; the one element of the reform package that travelled the committee road is also the one that travelled slowest. Neither outcome was inscribed in the repeal's settlement, and both were processed, in the end, by instruments of scrutiny and consultation of the very kind the farm laws themselves had bypassed. A third survival ran in the opposite direction. The legal theory on which the central legislation had rested, Parliament's power to legislate for agricultural marketing through the trade and commerce entries against the State List's claim for agriculture, was never adjudicated, because the stay kept the challenges frozen and the repeal removed their subject. The theory therefore remains available, untested and unrebuked, for a future Parliament with a future reform, and the states' counter-theory with it. The constitutional question the farm laws posed was the largest they posed, and it is the only one the episode contributed nothing towards answering.

## What is settled, what is contested, and what is a disagreement about values

The closing duty of a controversy article is taxonomy, and this episode sorts cleanly once the sorting is done honestly. Settled, because the record establishes them beyond serious dispute: the laws were made by ordinance first and enacted without committee scrutiny and, in the upper House, without a recorded vote; the protest was a mass mobilisation sustained at the capital's borders from late November 2020 to the middle of December 2021 under a coalition formed for the purpose; the Supreme Court stayed the laws without deciding their validity and never decided it; the repeal was enacted without debate and restored the prior legal position while leaving the price guarantee and the other charter demands unmet; and the demand for a statutory price survived the repeal and remobilised. Contested on the facts, and likely to remain so: the number of the dead, where a maintained list and an official absence of record face one another without a common counter; the scale of the camps at their peak; the responsibility for the departure from the agreed route on 26 January 2021 and for the death of that day; and the sequence of offers and refusals by which the talks failed, where each side's account makes the other's intransigence the cause. Contested on values, and not resolvable by any evidence either side could produce: whether a reform Parliament enacts within its competence should yield to a sustained mobilisation by the interest it burdens, or whether the mobilisation was the only hearing the burdened interest was ever going to get; whether price assurance for food producers is a cost the public owes or a distortion the public pays for; and whether the regional concentration of the protest made it a veto by the protected or a demonstration by the experienced. On these, this article has presented the strongest form of each position that its holders have given it, and takes no side.

Whether the lesson was learned is a question the subsequent record answers only in part, and the part it answers is worth stating without triumph. The practice of the Parliament that followed the repeal did not transform; bills of consequence continued to pass with limited committee scrutiny in many sessions, and the ordinance and the voice vote remained in regular service. But the counter-examples are also on the record, and they are not trivial. The three criminal law bills of 2023, a recodification touching every citizen rather than one sector, were referred to the parliamentary standing committee on home affairs, whose examination produced recommendations that altered the texts before enactment, a sequence the farm bills never had. The Waqf amendment bill of 2024, contested on lines recognisably similar to the farm bills, a centralising reform in a field of state and community sensitivity, was sent to a joint parliamentary committee that sat for months and took evidence on a large scale before the bill returned to the Houses. No rule changed to compel either reference; each was a choice, made by a government that had counted the cost of the alternative in 2020 and 2021. That is how conventions in a legislature actually move, not by amendment of the rule book but by the accumulation of priced examples, and it is the strongest evidence available that the farm laws episode taught the institution the thing this article has argued it teaches.

On the one question that is neither factual in that contested sense nor a bare value choice, the question of legislative method, the article does take the verdict its evidence supports. The process failures were not the protest's misfortune; they were the government's choices, made at five identifiable points, at prices that were knowable at the time, and the cheapest of the forgone instruments, the committee reference, was the one whose absence every later failure compounded. A legislature that wishes its reforms to survive contact with the interests they reorder does not need to surrender to the street. It needs to be able to show the street, and the courts, and its own members, a record in which the objections were heard while hearing them could still change the text. In the farm laws episode no such record exists, because at every point where the system offered to make one, the system was declined. That, and not the fate of any clause, is the lesson the farmers' protest leaves to Indian lawmaking.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How long did the farmers' protest last?

The encampment at Delhi's borders lasted from 26 November 2020, when the Delhi Chalo columns were stopped at the crossings, until 11 December 2021, when the sites were vacated after the government's written commitments of 9 December 2021. That is a little over twelve months, and the Samyukta Kisan Morcha counts the protest at 380 days. The wider agitation was longer than the encampment: unions in Punjab were protesting from June 2020, within days of the ordinances, and had blockaded the state's railways in the autumn of 2020, months before the march on Delhi began. Measured from the first demonstrations to the vacation of the camps, the movement ran for roughly eighteen months.

### Q: Which organisations led the farmers' protest?

The protest was led by the Samyukta Kisan Morcha, a joint platform formed on 7 November 2020 that brought together more than forty farmer organisations at its formation, with hundreds of further organisations associating with its programmes as the agitation grew. Its constituents spanned the ideological range of the farm movement, including factions of the Bharatiya Kisan Union, among them the organisations led by Rakesh Tikait in western Uttar Pradesh and the Ekta Ugrahan faction in Punjab, Punjab's cluster of thirty-two unions, the All India Kisan Sabha, and the Krantikari Kisan Union associated with Darshan Pal, who served as one of the Morcha's public faces. The Morcha acted by consensus, spoke through a rotating set of leaders rather than a single president, and negotiated with the government as one delegation. An earlier body, the All India Kisan Sangharsh Coordination Committee, had coordinated farm protests from 2017 and helped prepare the ground from which the Morcha was formed.

### Q: What were the main objections to the farm laws?

The unions' objections ran to method and to substance. On method, the laws had been promulgated as ordinances without consultation with the unions, passed without reference to any parliamentary committee, and carried in the Rajya Sabha by voice vote over a demand for a division. On substance, the trade area created by the first Act was exempt from the market fee borne by trade inside the regulated yards, which the unions argued would drain the yards of volume and destroy their price discovery. The support price had no statutory guarantee, and the unions held that procurement depended on the yard system the trade law weakened. The farming agreement Act routed disputes to a sub-divisional officer and barred civil courts, and the Essential Commodities amendment removed stock limits the unions regarded as the one discipline on large buyers. Behind all of it stood the federal objection that agriculture is a state subject.

### Q: What did supporters of the farm laws argue?

Supporters, led by the Union government, argued that marketing reform had been recommended for two decades, from the Shankarlal Guru expert committee of 2001 through the model marketing Acts circulated to the states in 2003 and 2017 and the Shanta Kumar committee of 2015, and that the laws merely gave farmers a choice of buyers while leaving the mandis and procurement at the support price untouched. They argued that contract farming stated its price terms in advance and placed the farmer's land beyond recovery, that freeing stock limits would draw investment into storage and processing, and that economists including Ramesh Chand of the NITI Aayog and Ashok Gulati supported the direction of reform. Institutionally, they argued that Parliament had acted within its competence and with the numbers to pass the bills, and that a reform enacted by the national legislature should not fall to a veto exercised by the interest it burdened.

### Q: What does the farm laws episode show about Indian lawmaking?

The episode shows that the safeguards of the legislative process are load-bearing rather than decorative. Three statutes of national consequence were made by ordinance, enacted without committee examination and without a recorded vote in the upper House, stayed by the Supreme Court without any decision on their validity, and repealed without debate, so that at no point in their entire life did any institution create a record of the objections to them being tested while testing could still change the outcome. It shows that the committee reference, the cheapest instrument in the process, is the one whose absence compounds: each later failure, the refused division, the improvised judicial committee, the silent repeal, re-enacted the original omission. And it shows that a determined, disciplined mobilisation outside Parliament can defeat a law that Parliament enacted, where the law's makers have declined to build the record that might have legitimised it.

### Q: Should the farm bills have gone to a select committee?

On the evidence of what followed, yes, and the case is practical rather than ceremonial. A select committee of the Rajya Sabha, or the standing committee on agriculture, would have taken evidence from the unions, the state governments that stood to lose market fee revenue, the commission agents, and independent economists, and would have examined the fee bar, the dispute mechanism and the civil court bar clause by clause. Every one of those points was later pressed on the street for a year. Referral would have cost weeks; the government's majority in committee could have reported the bills out substantially intact, and the resulting record would have deprived the protest of its strongest claim, that the governed were never heard. The counter-argument, that committees delay reform and become theatres for the opposition, is a real one, but a government confident of its case and its numbers risks little by submitting to evidence it can answer.

### Q: Was staying the farm laws an appropriate judicial remedy?

The question divides lawyers because the order of 12 January 2021 used a recognised power for an unrecognised purpose. The case for it is that the Court faced a mass encampment in winter, a stalled negotiation, and a credible apprehension of violence, and that an interim stay, which decided no one's rights and protected procurement and possession meanwhile, created the space in which a settlement might be reached. The case against is that a stay of legislation is ordinarily granted only on a prima facie finding of constitutional infirmity, and the order identified none: it suspended three valid statutes because of the state of the streets and appointed a committee to mediate a political dispute, which is not a judicial function. The Hindu's editorial page described the order as an attempt to impose a compromise. What is not in dispute is the consequence: the laws were left in limbo, valid and inoperative, until repeal relieved the Court of deciding them.

### Q: What happened after the farmers' protest ended?

The encampments were vacated from 11 December 2021 and the protesters returned home in processions the movement called victory marches. The Samyukta Kisan Morcha did not dissolve; it retained its charter's unmet demands, headed by a statutory guarantee of the support price, and continued periodic country-wide actions. The government constituted the promised committee on the support price in July 2022 under the chairmanship of the former agriculture secretary Sanjay Agarwal, and the Morcha declined to nominate members to it because its terms of reference did not include the legal guarantee itself. Criminal cases arising from the protest were withdrawn through the decentralised process the December 2021 letter had set in motion, at varying speeds across jurisdictions. In February 2024 a successor mobilisation under different leadership returned to the Punjab and Haryana border with the price guarantee at the head of its demands, and the question the protest had posed to the legislative process remained open.

### Q: Why did the farmers' protest begin in Punjab and Haryana?

The protest began where the system the laws disturbed was most complete. Punjab and Haryana operate the densest network of regulated yards and the most extensive public procurement of wheat and paddy at the support price in the country, and Punjab supplies the largest share of the grain the public distribution system distributes. Farmers there sold through the mandis, depended on the commission agents who finance the crop cycle, and had the most to lose if trade migrated to an unfee'd parallel channel and the yards withered. The two states also possessed the strongest farm unions in India, an organisational inheritance of decades, capable of sustaining a blockade and then an encampment. The government read the geography as evidence that the protest defended the best-protected interest in Indian agriculture; the unions answered that the grain of those states feeds the whole country, so the interest was national in function even where it was regional in location.

### Q: What did the government offer the farm unions during the talks?

Across eleven rounds of talks between 14 October 2020 and 22 January 2021, the government's offers moved in stages. It first offered amendments to meet stated objections, including on the civil court bar and the registration of traders, and a written assurance that procurement at the support price would continue. On 30 December 2020 it conceded two subsidiary demands, agreeing that stubble burning would be decriminalised for farmers and that the existing electricity arrangement would not be disturbed. Its largest offer came at the tenth round, on 20 January 2021: to suspend the implementation of the three laws for up to eighteen months while a joint committee of government and union representatives examined the issues. The Morcha considered the proposal overnight and rejected it on 21 January 2021, and the final round the next day closed with the ministers stating that nothing further could be offered and no new date fixed.

### Q: Why did the talks between the government and the farm unions fail?

The talks failed because each side's minimum was the other's impossibility. The Morcha's mandate from its constituent unions was repeal of the three laws plus a statutory guarantee of the support price, and its leaders maintained that amendments to laws made without consultation would legitimise the method that produced them. The government was willing to amend, to assure, and eventually to suspend, but not to repeal, which it regarded as surrendering Parliament's enactment to street pressure and abandoning a reform it believed in. Trust compounded the structure: the unions read the eighteen-month suspension offer as a device to exhaust the encampment past the point of endurance, and the government read the repeal demand as proof that no concession would be banked. The December 2020 agreement on two subsidiary items showed the machinery of negotiation worked; what it could not bridge was the single question of whether the laws would exist at all.

### Q: Why did the farm unions refuse to appear before the Supreme Court's committee?

The Morcha announced within hours of the order of 12 January 2021 that it would not appear before the committee, and its reasons were stated consistently from the first day. All four members appointed, Bhupinder Singh Mann, Anil Ghanwat, Ashok Gulati and Pramod Kumar Joshi, had publicly supported the direction of the farm laws before their appointment, and the unions held that a consultation whose members had declared its conclusion in advance was not a consultation. The Morcha also objected in principle: it had come to Delhi to negotiate with the government that made the laws, not to plead before a body the Court had created, and its counsel told the Court the unions did not seek the committee's mediation. Mann recused himself on 14 January 2021, and the remaining members heard the government and those organisations willing to appear, reporting to the Court on 19 March 2021. The Court never published the report; Anil Ghanwat released it himself on 21 March 2022.

### Q: Did the Supreme Court declare the farm laws unconstitutional?

No. The Supreme Court never decided the constitutional challenges to the farm laws at all. In Rakesh Vaishnav v Union of India it stayed the implementation of the three Acts on 12 January 2021 until further orders, directed that procurement at the support price continue and that no farmer be dispossessed of land under the laws, and appointed a committee to consult stakeholders. The challenges, which raised Parliament's competence to legislate on a subject the petitioners placed in the State List, were never heard to a conclusion on their merits. After Parliament repealed the Acts in November 2021 there was nothing left for the challenges to operate on, and they were overtaken by the repeal. Accounts that describe the Court as having struck the laws down, or as having upheld them, are both wrong: the one authoritative legal question of the episode was posed to the one institution that could answer it, and it remains unanswered.

### Q: How many farmers died during the farmers' protest?

No agreed figure exists, and any single number should be treated as a position in the dispute rather than a finding. The Samyukta Kisan Morcha maintained a running list of those it counted as having died in the course of the agitation, at the camps, on journeys to and from them, and in connected incidents, and on 4 December 2021 it supplied the government with a list of 702 names; movement leaders put the toll at around 750 in public statements, and the figure the Morcha later used in commemoration is 736. The Union government's position, stated by the Agriculture Minister in the Lok Sabha in the winter session of 2021, was that the ministry had no record of any farmer having died during the agitation, so the question of compensation did not arise. The list counts deaths the movement attributed to the agitation, including illness, cold and road accidents; no official enumeration was ever made.

### Q: What happened at Lakhimpur Kheri in October 2021?

On 3 October 2021, at Tikunia in Lakhimpur Kheri district of Uttar Pradesh, farmers had gathered to protest a visit by the state's Deputy Chief Minister when vehicles in a convoy associated with Ashish Mishra, son of the Union Minister of State for Home Affairs, Ajay Mishra Teni, drove into the crowd. Four farmers and a local journalist died after being struck or in the crush, and three men travelling with the convoy were killed in the violence that followed. A special investigation team of the state police concluded in December 2021 that the driving into the crowd was a deliberate and pre-planned act rather than negligence, and a charge sheet filed on 3 January 2022 named Ashish Mishra as the principal accused. He and his father deny the charges. The Morcha made the minister's dismissal and the prosecution of the case part of its settlement charter, and the pace of the proceedings remained a grievance long after the encampments ended.

### Q: What were the main sites of the protest camps on Delhi's borders?

The three principal sites were Singhu, on the national highway running north from Delhi into Haryana, Tikri, on the western road toward Bahadurgarh, and Ghazipur, on the eastern crossing into Uttar Pradesh at the Ghaziabad border. They formed in the last days of November 2020 where the Delhi Chalo columns were stopped, and each grew into a self-organising encampment of tents and tractor trolleys stretching along the blocked highway, with community kitchens, medical camps, libraries and stages. Singhu was the largest and served as the movement's symbolic centre, Tikri was anchored largely by unions from Haryana and Punjab, and Ghazipur, organised around the Bharatiya Kisan Union's strength in western Uttar Pradesh, became the movement's eastern anchor and the site of its closest brush with eviction in January 2021. Smaller encampments stood at Shahjahanpur on the Delhi to Jaipur highway and at Palwal. All the sites were vacated from 11 December 2021.

### Q: How were the protest camps supplied and organised for over a year?

The camps ran on the logistics of the villages behind them. The langar, the community kitchen tradition maintained by gurdwaras and by the unions themselves, supplied cooked food at a scale of tens of thousands of meals, with grain, vegetables and fuel arriving in a continuous traffic of donated supplies from Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh. Staffing worked by rotation: constituent unions rostered villages and families to relieve one another at the sites so that sowing, harvesting and household work continued at home, which is why the population of the camps rose and fell with the agricultural calendar. The encampments built their own infrastructure as the year turned, tents giving way to insulated shelters against the winter and coolers against the summer, with medical camps staffed by volunteer doctors, laundry and repair services, libraries, and schools for children at the sites. The unions state the camps were financed by donations from supporters in India and in the diaspora; allegations of organised external funding were made by the movement's opponents and denied by the Morcha, and no official finding settled the question.

### Q: What role did state governments play in opposing the farm laws?

Opposition-ruled states opposed the laws politically and legislatively. Punjab led: its Assembly resolved against the central Acts and, on 20 October 2020, passed amendment bills designed to make sale below the support price invalid within the state and to protect small holdings from attachment, and Rajasthan moved comparable amendments in the same season. The legislative route ran into the Constitution's design. Agriculture is a state subject, but the central laws rested substantially on concurrent and Union entries, and a state amendment repugnant to a central law on a concurrent subject operates only if it receives the President's assent under Article 254. The Punjab bills were forwarded for assent and the assent was not given, so they never took effect. States also mattered administratively: the protest sites stood in Haryana and Uttar Pradesh as much as in Delhi, the policing of the borders was state work, and the Punjab government of the day extended the movement open political support while the Haryana government manned the barricades against it.

### Q: What happened at the Shambhu border in February 2024?

In February 2024 the demand for a statutory support price produced a second march on Delhi, called by the Samyukta Kisan Morcha (Non-Political) and the Kisan Mazdoor Morcha, bodies distinct from the Morcha of 2020. From 13 February 2024 the columns were held at the Shambhu and Khanauri crossings on the Punjab and Haryana border, behind barricades heavier than those of 2020. Four rounds of talks followed, and in the round ending past midnight on 18 February 2024 the government offered five years of purchase at the support price for five crops, three pulses, maize and cotton, through central agencies. The unions rejected the offer as partial and resumed the march. On 21 February 2024 Shubhkaran Singh, a protester of twenty-one, died at Khanauri of a head injury sustained in clashes there; the farmers alleged police firing, the Haryana police denied firing on protesters, and the Punjab police registered a murder case on his father's complaint. The camps then remained in place for over a year, until Punjab police cleared both sites on 19 March 2025 after detaining the leaders returning from a further round of talks.

### Q: How long does a select committee usually take to examine a bill?

A select committee works to the time the House gives it, which is commonly a few months, extendable on the committee's request. The comparison closest to hand is the Lokpal bill: the Rajya Sabha referred it to a select committee on 21 May 2012, and the committee reported on 23 November 2012, about six months later, with sixteen recommendations of which the government accepted fourteen. Committees on less charged bills frequently report within a single session or two, and a House determined to move quickly can set a short deadline and hold the committee to it. Set against the farm laws' own timeline, ordinances in June 2020 to repeal in November 2021 with a year of encampment between, even the slowest realistic committee reference would have consumed a small fraction of the time the confrontation consumed, which is the arithmetic at the centre of the case for referral.
