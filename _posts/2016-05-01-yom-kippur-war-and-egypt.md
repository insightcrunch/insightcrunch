At about 2:05 on the afternoon of 6 October 1973, while Israel stood almost silent on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the Jewish calendar, the east bank of the Suez Canal erupted. More than two thousand Egyptian guns opened a barrage of a weight no Arab army had ever delivered, and shells fell along the whole length of the Bar-Lev Line, the chain of Israeli strongpoints dug into the sand on the far shore. The first Egyptian infantrymen were already in their rubber boats, paddling across the canal under the falling shells toward fortifications Israel had held unchallenged for six years. What followed across the next three weeks would cost both nations thousands of lives, and it would end with armies still facing each other on both banks of the canal, neither capital able to claim the total victory its newspapers promised. Yet the war mattered less for that inconclusive battlefield result than for what it repaired in Egypt after the disaster of 1967. The crossing itself was the message: Egypt could plan in secret, Egypt could strike with discipline, and Egypt would no longer accept the terms of its own humiliation. The true victory of October 1973 was psychological and diplomatic, won not in the tally of captured ground but in the recovery of Egyptian and Arab pride and the forced reopening of negotiations that had been frozen for years.

The surprise was nearly total. Israeli intelligence had watched Egyptian exercises on the canal for months and judged them drills, and most of Israel's standing army was on leave for the fast. Within hours, Egyptian engineers were turning high pressure water cannon on the great sand ramparts the Israelis had raised along the east bank, melting walls of packed sand into the canal while infantry secured footholds on the far side. The operation, code named Badr for the Prophet Muhammad's first military victory, committed five Egyptian infantry divisions to the initial assault. In synagogues and homes across Israel, reservists abandoned the fast and reported to their units as the sirens sounded, many reaching the front only to find Egyptian infantry already dug in on the east bank. From the Egyptian point of view, this was the reversal of everything 1967 had taught the Arab world to expect. From the Israeli point of view, it was a shock that would shake the government, the military establishment, and national self confidence for a generation. To understand why Anwar Sadat ordered his armies across that water, and why he believed a war he could not fully win was worth fighting at all, one has to go back to the defeat that made the crossing necessary.

## Six Years of Humiliation: What 1967 Took from Egypt

The defeat of June 1967 was the single most traumatic event in modern Egyptian history to that point. On the morning of 5 June, Israeli aircraft struck Egyptian airfields and destroyed the bulk of the Egyptian Air Force on the ground within hours. The armored divisions in Sinai, stripped of air cover, were outmaneuvered and broken in days. When the fighting stopped, Egypt had lost the entire Sinai Peninsula up to the Suez Canal, along with the Gaza Strip it had administered since 1949. Jordan had lost the West Bank and East Jerusalem. Syria had lost the Golan Heights. The Suez Canal, Egypt's great waterway and a major source of national revenue, was closed by sunken ships and debris and would stay closed for eight years. Egyptian accounts at the time minimized the scale of the disaster, but Israeli historians estimate Egyptian military deaths in the range of ten thousand to fifteen thousand, with hundreds of tanks and aircraft destroyed or captured.

The political wound cut deeper than the territorial loss. Gamal Abdel Nasser, the charismatic president who had made Egypt the voice of Arab nationalism, offered his resignation on 9 June in a broadcast that stunned the nation. Massive crowds poured into the streets of Cairo and other cities demanding that he stay, and he withdrew the resignation the following day, but the spell of invincibility was broken. Egypt had entered 1967 promising to liberate Palestine and had emerged with Israeli soldiers standing on the east bank of its own canal, able to look across the water at Egyptian positions. The revolutionary legitimacy of [the Free Officers who had seized power in 1952](/2015/12/01/1952-revolution-free-officers/) had rested on the promise that they would restore Egyptian dignity after decades of British domination and monarchy. That legitimacy now depended on undoing the catastrophe. Nothing less than the recovery of Sinai, by negotiation or by force, could close the account opened by [Gamal Abdel Nasser's devastating defeat in 1967](/2016/01/01/gamal-abdel-nasser-of-egypt/), and Nasser himself declared that Egypt's policy would be the removal of the effects of aggression.

The Arab response hardened the deadlock. At the Khartoum summit of August and September 1967, Arab leaders adopted the famous three refusals: no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, and no negotiations with Israel. The United Nations Security Council passed Resolution 242 in November 1967, calling for Israeli withdrawal from occupied territories in exchange for peace, a formula Egypt accepted and Israel interpreted in far more limited terms. The gap between the two readings never closed. Meanwhile the economic damage compounded the humiliation. Egypt lost the toll revenue of the closed canal, the oil fields of Sinai at Abu Rudeis, and the tourist income of Sharm el-Sheikh, while defense spending consumed a crushing share of the national budget. Sinai was not merely occupied territory. It was more than twice the area of the rest of Egypt combined, and its loss meant that a foreign army stood a short drive from Cairo. For Egyptians of every political stripe, the map itself was an insult that had to be corrected.

## The War of Attrition, 1967 to 1970

Nasser refused to accept a ceasefire line as a permanent border, and in March 1969 he declared the existing truce dead, opening what became known as the War of Attrition. Along the canal, Egyptian artillery pounded Israeli positions in the Bar-Lev Line while Israeli guns answered across the water. Commandos raided in both directions. In the air, the two air forces fought grinding battles of attrition over the canal zone. Egypt could not win this contest on its own, and Nasser turned ever more deeply to the Soviet Union, which supplied advanced surface to air missile batteries, aircraft, and thousands of advisers. By 1970 Soviet pilots were flying combat missions over Egypt in Soviet markings, a fact both Moscow and Cairo obscured. Israel answered the missile threat with deep penetration bombing raids into the Egyptian interior in early 1970, striking targets near Cairo in an effort to pressure Nasser into halting the artillery war. The raids inflicted real damage but also deepened Soviet involvement, as Moscow dispatched more pilots and newer missile systems to defend Egyptian airspace.

Neither side achieved its aims. Egypt's gunners inflicted steady casualties on the Israeli canal garrisons but could not dislodge them. Israel's air superiority could punish Egypt but could not force a political settlement. Israeli military casualties in the War of Attrition are recorded in Israeli sources at roughly seven hundred dead. Egyptian losses were substantially heavier, with Western estimates placing them at several thousand dead, though precise Egyptian figures were never published. The fighting ended not in decision but in exhaustion and superpower pressure. The American secretary of state, William Rogers, proposed a ceasefire framework in June 1970, and both sides accepted a halt that took effect on 7 August 1970. The guns fell silent along the canal, but nothing about the underlying confrontation had changed. Israel still held Sinai. Egypt still demanded its return. The ceasefire was a pause, and everyone on both banks of the canal understood it as one.

Three weeks after brokering a ceasefire between Jordan and the Palestinian fedayeen during the Black September crisis, Nasser died of a heart attack on 28 September 1970. He was fifty two. His funeral in Cairo drew millions of mourners in one of the largest public gatherings of the century, a measure of what he had meant to Egyptians and to Arabs beyond Egypt. It was also a measure of the burden he left behind. He had rebuilt the shattered Egyptian army with Soviet help, restored a measure of national cohesion, and kept the claim to Sinai alive through three years of artillery war. He had not recovered a single kilometer of lost territory, and he had left his successor a frozen front, a restless army, and a nation that equated patience with surrender.

## Sadat Inherits a Stalemate

Anwar Sadat, Nasser's vice president, succeeded to the presidency in October 1970. Few observers at home or abroad expected him to last. He was widely regarded as a transitional figure, a loyal subordinate without Nasser's charisma or the army's deep allegiance, and the powerful figures around him, including the pro Soviet interior minister Ali Sabri, assumed they could manage or replace him. Sadat confounded them. In May 1971, in what he called the Corrective Revolution, he arrested Sabri and his allies and purged the centers of power that threatened his authority. The move established Sadat as Egypt's undisputed ruler, but it did not solve the problem that would define his presidency. Israel still occupied Sinai, the canal was still closed, and the ceasefire line was still treated in Cairo as a national disgrace.

Sadat understood that time was working against him. In 1971 he declared it the year of decision, promising the Egyptian people that the year would bring either a settlement or action to recover the occupied land. The year passed with neither. No Israeli government showed any willingness to withdraw from Sinai, which Israel valued as a strategic buffer and where it had begun building settlements and developing the oil fields. No American administration showed any inclination to press Israel. The phrase that captured the period, used by Egyptians and foreign observers alike, was no peace, no war: a stalemate in which diplomacy produced nothing and the army waited for orders that never came. For Sadat, the failure of the year of decision was politically dangerous. A president who promised decision and delivered delay risked looking weaker than the predecessor whose shadow still covered him, and Egyptian students and intellectuals were already demonstrating in the streets, demanding either war or an honest admission that none was coming.

The stalemate also had a logic that favored Israel, and Israeli leaders said so openly. From Jerusalem's perspective, the ceasefire lines of 1967 were defensible, the Bar-Lev Line made an Egyptian crossing appear prohibitively costly, and Israeli air superiority was assumed to guarantee that any Egyptian attack would be detected and crushed. Time, in this view, consolidated Israel's position: every year of quiet made the occupation more normal and the Egyptian threat more theoretical. Sadat drew the opposite conclusion from the same facts. If quiet served Israel and diplomacy served no one, then only a deliberate shock could change the equation. He began to argue, first to his generals and then in public, that Egypt had to act even at terrible cost. He publicly declared that he was prepared to sacrifice enormous numbers of Egyptian soldiers to break the deadlock, a statement his critics called reckless and his supporters called the first honest accounting of what recovery would require. Behind the rhetoric lay a colder calculation. Egypt did not need to defeat Israel outright. It needed to shatter the assumption that the status quo could continue indefinitely.

## When Diplomacy Stalled: Rogers, Jarring, and the Soviet Expulsion

Sadat did not turn to war before testing every diplomatic path available to him, and the record of those failures is essential to understanding his decision. The United Nations mediator Gunnar Jarring conducted shuttle diplomacy between the parties through 1970 and 1971. In February 1971 Jarring presented an aide memoire in which Egypt expressed readiness to enter a peace agreement with Israel on the condition of full Israeli withdrawal from Sinai to the pre June 1967 lines. Israel declined to commit to complete withdrawal, insisting on secure and recognized boundaries to be determined through direct negotiations, and the initiative collapsed. From Cairo's perspective, the episode proved that Israel would not return Sinai for promises. From Jerusalem's perspective, Egypt was demanding the substance of peace terms while refusing the direct negotiations that could produce them. Both readings contained truth, and the gap between them proved unbridgeable.

The American channel offered no more. The Rogers proposals of 1969 and 1970 had produced the ceasefire but no movement toward withdrawal. Henry Kissinger, who took charge of Middle East policy in the Nixon administration, is reported by participants in later accounts to have regarded the stalemate as serving American interests, since it cost Washington little while Egypt remained tied to Moscow. Whether or not the remark is rendered exactly, American policy in these years applied no serious pressure on Israel to withdraw from Sinai, and Sadat concluded that Washington would not act until the situation on the ground forced it to. Diplomacy, in his reading, was not merely slow. It was structurally incapable of delivering Sinai so long as Israel faced no cost for holding it.

It was against this background that Sadat made his most dramatic peacetime move. In July 1972 he ordered the expulsion of the Soviet military advisers from Egypt, some fifteen thousand men by most Western and Egyptian estimates, though figures cited at the time ranged from ten thousand to twenty thousand. The advisers were given days to leave, and they left. The motives were multiple and debated. Egyptian accounts stress that Sadat wanted to free Egypt's military decision making from Soviet control, since Moscow had consistently restrained Egyptian offensive planning while refusing to supply the offensive weapons Egypt requested. Other historians emphasize the signal to Washington: by casting out Moscow's men, Sadat was telling the Americans that Egypt could be pried from the Soviet orbit if the United States engaged seriously on Sinai. Still others point to domestic politics, where the visible Soviet presence had become a symbol of dependence. Whatever the precise mixture, the gamble failed in its immediate aim. Moscow continued to sell Egypt arms, which it needed for revenue and influence, but Washington still did not press Israel. Sadat had removed the advisers and gained nothing from the Americans. The lesson he drew was stark. Neither Moscow nor Washington would move on Egypt's behalf. If Sinai was to be recovered, Egypt would have to create the crisis that made recovery unavoidable.

## Why Egypt Chose the Yom Kippur War

The decision for war was therefore not a spasm of rage but a strategy, and its logic deserves to be stated plainly because it explains everything about how Egypt fought. Sadat and his planners, above all the war minister Ahmad Ismail Ali and the chief of staff Saad el-Shazly, designed a limited war with political rather than purely military objectives. Egypt would cross the canal, breach the Bar-Lev Line, and establish bridgeheads some ten to fifteen kilometers deep on the east bank, under the protection of a dense umbrella of Soviet supplied surface to air missiles that would neutralize Israel's air advantage. Egyptian forces would then hold those bridgeheads against the inevitable Israeli counterattack. The aim was not to drive Israel into the sea, which Egyptian planners knew was beyond their strength, but to inflict a shock: to destroy the myth of Israeli invincibility, to demonstrate that the status quo carried real costs, and to force the United States and the Soviet Union to intervene diplomatically to stop a war neither superpower wanted. A limited military success would become a lever for negotiations. In Sadat's phrase, reported by his aides, Egypt had to shake the situation before anyone would take it seriously.

This strategy required a partner, and Sadat found one in Syria's Hafez al-Assad. Through 1972 and 1973 the two leaders coordinated plans for a two front war: Egypt would strike across the canal while Syria attacked on the Golan Heights, forcing Israel to divide its reserves between two distant fronts. The coordination was real but imperfect, as the two countries' war aims differed. Syria sought the full recovery of the Golan and, in the ambitions of some of its planners, far more. Egypt sought a limited crossing and a diplomatic opening. Each ally understood the other's minimum needs, and each accepted the risks of the joint enterprise. The secrecy surrounding the planning was extraordinary. Even most Egyptian field commanders learned the true date only days beforehand, and the elaborate deception plan, repeated exercises, demobilization announcements, and carefully leaked misinformation, convinced Israeli intelligence that no attack was imminent.

The choice of 6 October itself was deliberate on multiple levels. It was the tenth day of Ramadan, the Muslim holy month, a date on which Arab soldiers could be portrayed as fighting with faith while Israeli planners might assume reduced readiness. It was also Yom Kippur, the Jewish Day of Atonement, when Israel effectively shut down: the radio played solemn music, the roads emptied, and much of the standing army was on leave. The timing maximized surprise while also serving the practical needs of the crossing, since Egyptian planners wanted specific moonlight and tidal conditions on the canal. Whether the religious symbolism or the military calculation weighed more heavily is debated by historians of the war, but the effect is not in dispute. Israel's intelligence establishment, captive to what its own postwar inquiry called the conception, the entrenched belief that Egypt would not attack without first achieving air superiority and that Syria would not attack without Egypt, dismissed the warning signs. Egyptian accounts claim the deception succeeded beyond their hopes. Israeli historians estimate that even a few days of clear warning would have changed the opening battles dramatically.

None of this means the decision was easy or universally supported inside Egypt. Senior officers warned Sadat that the missile umbrella could not cover an advance deep into Sinai and that Israeli armor would exact a terrible price once Egyptian forces moved beyond it. The economy was strained, the army's Soviet equipment was formidable on paper but untested in the kind of war Sadat envisioned, and failure would destroy not only Sadat's presidency but possibly the regime itself. Sadat overruled the doubters. He had concluded, after the failures of Jarring, Rogers, and the Soviet expulsion, that the alternative to war was not peace but the slow normalization of defeat. A nation that accepted the canal as a permanent border, he argued, would accept anything. The war he ordered was therefore an instrument of diplomacy by other means: a calculated, limited, and terribly costly bid to force the world to pay attention to a grievance that six years of quiet had buried. He would later pursue a negotiated settlement with Israel directly, but that belonged to a later chapter. In 1973 the negotiation had to be earned with blood.

By the autumn of 1973, then, every element of Sadat's design was in place. The army had been rebuilt and equipped for the specific task of crossing water under fire. Syria was committed to a coordinated offensive. The superpowers were unprepared, Israel was unwarned, and the date had been chosen to compound every advantage of surprise. The Egyptian president had staked his legitimacy, his regime, and the lives of tens of thousands of his soldiers on a single proposition: that a war Egypt could not fully win could still accomplish what six years of humiliation, three years of attrition, and endless diplomacy had not. The order went out in the first days of October. What happened at 2:05 on the afternoon of the sixth would test that proposition against the reality of Israeli resistance, and the story of the crossing itself is where the war begins.

## The Yom Kippur Deception

Long before the first Egyptian infantryman slipped into a rubber boat, the battle for surprise had been fought and won inside the minds of Israel's intelligence chiefs. Egypt's deception plan, built through 1972 and 1973, rested on a single insight: the Israelis had convinced themselves that Egypt could not attack until it matched Israel in the air, and that Sadat's expulsion of Soviet military advisers in July 1972 had pushed that day far into the future. Egyptian planners, led by Minister of War Ahmed Ismail Ali and Chief of Staff Saad el-Shazly, set out to confirm that belief at every turn while preparing the largest opposed river crossing attempted in modern war.

Sadat's own words were part of the camouflage. In 1971 he had proclaimed the "year of decision," promising action that never came, and through 1972 and 1973 he issued threats of war at press conferences and in interviews with Western journalists, each deadline passing quietly. Israeli analysts, reading the transcripts, concluded that they were dealing with a bluffer, a leader who talked to frighten rather than to prepare. The assessment hardened into contempt: Aman briefings described Sadat as a politician performing for Arab audiences, not a commander preparing an offensive. The judgment was not baseless, since Egypt's army in 1971 and 1972 genuinely lacked the weapons and training for the crossing, and Sadat knew it. Every genuine Egyptian military move in 1973 was therefore filtered through a political judgment that Sadat did not mean what he said.

The method was repetition. Through late 1972 and 1973 the Egyptians staged a succession of large exercises along the canal, each one moving bridging trains, artillery, and whole divisions to the water in daylight, each one ending with a stand-down and a return to barracks. Israeli observers logged the buildups, Israeli intelligence filed them as routine, and Israeli reservists learned to treat mobilization alerts as expensive false alarms. The exercises served a genuine purpose as well: Egyptian engineers rehearsed the crossing so often that the assembly of a pontoon bridge became muscle memory, and staff officers refined their timetables against real conditions on the canal bank.

In May 1973 the deception sharpened into a weapon aimed at Israel's treasury and nerves. Egypt and Syria staged a coordinated mobilization that forced Israel into a costly partial call-up of its reserves. For days the Israeli cabinet weighed the possibility of war, the mobilization absorbed a heavy bill, and then the Arab armies stood down and nothing happened. Israeli analysts drew exactly the lesson Cairo intended: the next warning would probably be another bluff. From that spring onward, every genuine indicator of attack had to compete with the memory of May, and the memory usually won.

The final layer was applied in September. Egypt announced the demobilization of 20,000 troops and granted home leave to thousands of officers, some of whom departed openly for the umrah pilgrimage to Mecca. Syrian units on the Golan front were told that their own buildup was merely an exercise. Egyptian bridging equipment kept rolling toward the canal in full view of Israeli observation posts, because it had done so a dozen times before without consequence. Even the date was camouflage. The 6th of October fell during Ramadan, when Israeli analysts assumed that fasting soldiers would be in no condition to fight, and on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, when Israel itself would be at a standstill.

Secrecy was enforced with a severity that matched the deception. Shazly compartmentalized the plan so tightly that division commanders learned the true objective only days before the attack, and brigade and battalion officers were told even later. The rank and file were informed that they were deploying for another edition of the Tahrir exercises, and they believed it, because they had deployed for exercises so many times before. Bridging trains moved to the canal under the cover story of engineer training, artillery registered its targets during practice shoots, and staff officers carried the real orders in sealed envelopes. When the order to attack finally came down on the morning of 6 October, many Egyptian soldiers learned for the first time that the exercise was war.

## Why 6 October, and Why Two in the Afternoon

The choice of Yom Kippur was deliberate, and its logic was cold. On the holiest day of the Jewish calendar, Israel's roads stood empty, its radio networks fell nearly silent, and its reservists, the backbone of its army, were in synagogues or at home with their families, unreachable by the normal channels of recall. A mobilization ordered on that day would move more slowly than on any other day of the year. The Egyptians and Syrians also counted on the fasting month: Israeli intelligence read Ramadan as a guarantee of Arab passivity, reasoning that soldiers weakened by the daylight fast could not sustain an offensive. Egyptian commanders knew their men would fight while fasting, and Israeli overconfidence did the rest of the work for them.

The date itself was fixed at a secret meeting in Damascus in late August 1973, when Sadat and Syria's Hafez al-Assad agreed that both fronts would strike together on 6 October. Egyptian staff officers had studied the calendars for months: October offered workable tides in the canal's northern sector, moonlight suited to night consolidation work, and weather clear enough for the air force. Ramadan provided religious cover, since Israeli analysts could not imagine an army attacking while its soldiers fasted, and Yom Kippur provided the operational paralysis. The joint decision also solved a strategic problem neither country could solve alone: Israel would have to fight in two directions at once, and its reserves could not be in both places.

The hour of attack, 2:00 PM, was a compromise hammered out between Cairo and Damascus after weeks of staff argument. Egyptian planners had wanted an evening assault, so that the setting sun would blind Israeli gunners looking west across the water; Syrian planners had wanted a dawn attack on the Golan, where the morning light favored the defense differently. Fourteen hundred hours split the difference and served Egypt's purpose well: at that hour in early October the sun stood in the southwest, behind the troops moving east, throwing glare into the eyes of the defenders on the far bank. The afternoon start also left several hours of daylight for the infantry waves and the engineers to work before nightfall, and staff officers had consulted the tide tables for the canal's northern reaches before fixing the date.

## Operation Badr

The operational plan bore the name Badr, after the battle of 624 in which the early Muslim community won its first great victory, and the name was chosen to tell every soldier what the crossing meant. Conceived by Shazly and approved by Sadat, the plan called for five infantry divisions to cross the canal simultaneously along its length: the 2nd, 16th, and 18th Divisions of the Second Army in the north, and the 7th and 19th Divisions of the Third Army in the south. Each division would seize a bridgehead 10 to 15 kilometers deep on the east bank, dig in, and wait. The advance was deliberately limited, because Egyptian forces were to remain under the umbrella of their surface-to-air missiles, the SA-2, SA-3, and mobile SA-6 batteries backed by ZSU-23-4 guns, which were meant to neutralize the Israeli Air Force over the battlefield.

The plan's second element was the infantry itself, reimagined as a tank-killing force. Israeli doctrine expected its armored divisions to race to the canal and crush any bridgehead within hours, so Egyptian assault infantry crossed carrying large numbers of Sagger wire-guided antitank missiles and RPG-7 rocket launchers, weapons that allowed foot soldiers to destroy tanks at ranges beyond the reach of tank guns. The Egyptian staff expected the Israeli armored counterattack, welcomed it, and intended to bleed it white against prepared infantry before any deeper advance. A second phase, a drive toward the Sinai passes, existed on paper, but Badr's first and decisive act was the crossing and the consolidation of the bridgeheads.

Badr's restraint was its most controversial feature, then and in the arguments that followed. The plan deliberately halted the infantry 10 to 15 kilometers east of the canal, well short of the Sinai passes, because beyond that range the troops would outrun their missile cover and meet the Israeli Air Force on its own terms. Egypt's armored divisions were held west of the canal as a reserve, ready to cross only after the bridgeheads were secure. Officers within the Egyptian command argued that the pause surrendered the initiative, and the argument would split the high command in the days ahead. Shazly's answer was arithmetic: the plan traded ground for Israeli tanks, and it would work only if the infantry stood its ground and let the armor come to it.

## The Bar Lev Line

Waiting on the east bank was the most formidable defensive system in the Middle East, the Bar Lev Line, named for Chief of Staff Haim Bar-Lev, who had ordered its construction after the artillery duels of 1968. Built at a reported cost of about $300 million, it ran the length of the canal, [the canal whose modern history had shaped the region](/2015/08/01/suez-canal-british-power-egypt/), for roughly 160 kilometers: a continuous sand rampart 20 to 25 meters high, sloped steeply on its eastern face to stop tanks and bulldozers, backed by 22 fortified strongpoints, the maozim, each a concrete and steel position with underground quarters, mortars, and machine guns. In front of the rampart lay deep minefields and belts of barbed wire, and Israeli engineers had reportedly laid pipes beneath the canal's surface that could pump flammable liquid onto the water and set it ablaze beneath the assault boats.

For all its concrete and sand, the line was a tripwire rather than a wall, and Israeli doctrine said so openly. Its purpose was to give warning and to hold for the hours needed for Israel's armored reserves to reach the front, not to stop an army by itself. On the morning of 6 October it was held thinly: Israeli accounts put the garrison at only a few hundred men scattered among the strongpoints, backed, according to those same accounts, by the roughly 290 tanks of the Sinai armored division deployed in depth behind the canal. The Egyptians understood the line's true strength, and it was not the forts. It was the sand, and they had spent two years devising a way to wash it away.

The most dramatic of the line's secrets was the underwater fuel system, and it never fired a shot. Israeli engineers had laid pipes and outlets along the canal bed that were supposed to release a flammable mixture onto the surface and ignite it, turning the water into a wall of flame beneath the assault boats. Egyptian accounts claim that frogmen slipped across on the night of 5 October and sealed the outlets with concrete, while Israeli accounts dispute how complete or functional the system ever was. What is not disputed is the outcome: when the first boats crossed on the afternoon of 6 October, the canal did not burn. Whether through sabotage or through a system that existed more in blueprints than in working order, the water stayed open.

### How did water cannons breach the Bar Lev sand walls?

Egyptian engineers mounted imported high-pressure dredging pumps on barges along the canal and aimed the jets at the base of each rampart. The water liquefied the sand and washed it into the canal, collapsing walls built to stop bulldozers and tanks. Gaps that machinery would have needed a full day to carve were opened within two to five hours.

The pumps had been the plan's best-kept engineering secret. Egyptian officers had purchased the dredging equipment abroad, ostensibly for canal maintenance, and tested it against full-scale mockups of the rampart built in the desert far from Israeli eyes. On the afternoon of 6 October, engineer teams landed with the first infantry waves, dragged hoses to the base of the rampart at dozens of preassigned points, and opened the valves. Israeli strongpoint commanders, watching from their bunkers, reported walls of sand melting into the canal as if the desert itself were dissolving. By the time the first tanks needed to cross, the engineers had cut scores of gaps through which armor could climb the far bank.

## The First Waves: Artillery, Boats, and Aircraft

At 2:00 PM on 6 October, with the sun glaring into Israeli observation posts, the Egyptian Air Force struck first. Some 200 aircraft, according to Egyptian accounts, swept low over Sinai to hit airfields, radar stations, Hawk missile batteries, and command posts in a single coordinated wave, then turned for home before Israeli fighters could react in strength. Minutes later, at 2:05, the artillery opened: Egyptian accounts put the barrage at about 2,000 guns and mortars firing a 53-minute preparation that Egyptian gunners had rehearsed for months, targeting strongpoints, observation towers, and minefield lanes with preregistered fire. The bombardment was among the heaviest concentrations of artillery fire in any war since 1945, and it was designed less to destroy the fortifications than to stun their defenders and keep their heads down while the infantry crossed.

The infantry went over in small boats. Egyptian accounts describe a first wave of roughly 8,000 men carried across in more than a thousand rubber and wooden assault boats, paddling the 180 to 200 meters of open water under the cover of the barrage. Commandos and specially trained assault teams landed first, storming the crest of the rampart and working along it to isolate the strongpoints, while engineers followed with the hoses and pumps. Behind them came the second and third waves with heavier weapons: Sagger teams, mortars, and ammunition, ferried across before a single bridge existed. Israeli defenders who raised their heads found the far bank already crowded with Egyptian infantry digging in.

The opening air strike, for all its coordination, achieved less than its planners hoped. Egyptian pilots, flying at the edge of their range and training, damaged runways and radar sites across Sinai but destroyed few Israeli aircraft, most of which were airborne or sheltered by the time the bombs fell; Israeli accounts stress that the air force survived the first day largely intact. On the ground the strongpoints fell unevenly. Most of the maozim, isolated and pounded by artillery, surrendered or were overrun within the first two days, but the northernmost fort, Budapest, near Port Said, held out against repeated assaults and remained in Israeli hands. The uneven fate of the forts mattered less than the fact behind it: the line as a system had ceased to function by nightfall.

## Bridges, Ferries, and the Buildup

With the rampart breached, the engineers turned to the canal itself. Soviet-designed PMP pontoon bridges, heavy ferries, and prefabricated spans began going together in the late afternoon, assembled under fire by crews who had rehearsed the work hundreds of times. Egyptian histories report that the first bridges were carrying vehicles across within hours of the infantry assault, an engineering tempo that stunned observers on both sides. The Israeli Air Force, which doctrine had assigned the task of smashing the bridges, could not reach them: every approach ran through the missile umbrella, and the first Israeli aircraft that tried were shot down by SA-6 batteries whose performance the pilots had never faced before.

The buildup ran through the night without pause. Tens of thousands of Egyptian soldiers crossed on 6 October, and Egyptian accounts claim that more than 100,000 men were on the east bank within the first 24 hours, a figure Western estimates generally reduce to the tens of thousands while agreeing that the scale exceeded anything Israeli planners had imagined possible in a single day. Tanks, artillery, and air defense units rolled over the pontoons in a continuous stream, and by dawn the bridgeheads held not just infantry but the heavy weapons needed to defeat armor. The crossing, the operation every staff college had called impossible under modern fire, had been accomplished in an afternoon and consolidated in a night.

## Surprise in Tel Aviv

In Israel, the morning of 6 October began in disbelief. Aman's assessment, the famous concept, held that Egypt would not attack without air superiority and long-range strike weapons, and therefore that the Egyptian buildup was another exercise. Warnings accumulated and were dismissed one by one: King Hussein had flown secretly to meet Prime Minister Golda Meir on 25 September to warn that war was coming; Soviet advisers' families were evacuated from Egypt and Syria on 4 and 5 October; and in the early hours of 6 October the Mossad's prized source in Cairo reported that the attack would come that day, though he gave the hour as 6:00 PM, four hours late. Chief of Staff David Elazar wanted full mobilization and a preemptive air strike; Defense Minister Moshe Dayan feared that a preemptive strike would cost Israel American support, and Meir sided with caution.

Mobilization was finally ordered late on the morning of 6 October, only hours before the first shells fell, and the Yom Kippur standstill did its work: reservists were hard to reach, roads were empty of the civilian traffic that usually carried them, and units assembled piecemeal through the afternoon. The intelligence failure was not a lack of information but a failure of interpretation, and both sides have drawn their lessons from it. Israeli histories describe a command blinded by its own doctrine and by the memory of May's false alarm; Egyptian histories describe a deception campaign that worked exactly as designed, from the exercises to the demobilization announcement to the choice of the holy day itself.

For Israel's Southern Command, the afternoon brought a shock for which no plan existed. General Shmuel Gonen's headquarters watched the barrage fall, the boats cross, and the rampart dissolve, and fed its armor into the battle in the only way it could: piecemeal, platoon by platoon, as units arrived from their Yom Kippur dispersal. Israeli tank crews, trained to decide battles by aggressive maneuver, drove into killing zones where Egyptian infantry, concealed in their trenches, guided Sagger missiles onto turret after turret. By evening Southern Command's picture of the front was fragmentary, its communications jammed and overloaded, and the canal it had been told no army could cross was behind the Egyptian army.

## Nightfall on the East Bank

By nightfall on 6 October, the canal's east bank belonged to Egypt. Five bridgeheads stretched a few kilometers deep into Sinai, and tens of thousands of infantrymen were digging trenches, siting Sagger teams, and registering their artillery on the approaches the Israeli tanks would have to use. The strongpoints of the Bar Lev Line still held out in isolated pockets, but they had been bypassed and cut off, their garrisons fighting in the dark while the Egyptian buildup flowed around them. Israeli armor that rushed toward the canal in small groups that afternoon, exactly as Egyptian planners had hoped, ran into walls of antitank missiles and was destroyed piecemeal before it could mass.

Through 7 and 8 October the Egyptians consolidated rather than exploited. Engineers widened the breaches, laid more bridges, and brought forward the air defense batteries that extended the missile umbrella over the bridgeheads. Israeli counterattacks on 8 October, launched in haste and without coordination, broke against the prepared infantry and cost Israel dearly in tanks. When the first phase of Operation Badr closed, Egypt held five lodgments east of the canal, its army across the water in strength, its soldiers dug in on ground that had been Israeli for six years. The surprise was complete, the crossing was secure, and the war's opening act belonged to Cairo. The gamble planned across two years of exercises, deception, and engineering had paid its first dividend.

## Holding the Bridgeheads

By the morning of 7 October 1973, the Egyptian Second and Third Armies held five bridgeheads on the eastern bank of the Suez Canal, roughly ten kilometers deep into Sinai. Israeli doctrine since 1967 held that any Arab penetration would be crushed by swift armored counterattacks supported from the air, and the first Israeli responses on 7 and 8 October followed that doctrine faithfully. What followed revealed how thoroughly the Egyptians had prepared for exactly that response.

The Israeli counterattacks of 7 October came from the surviving elements of the regular armored division stationed in Sinai, commanded by Major General Avraham Adan, known as Bren, with brigades led by officers who had fought in 1967 and expected a repeat of that experience. Israeli tank columns advanced toward the canal to throw the Egyptians back into the water before the bridgeheads could be consolidated, and they advanced into prepared killing zones. Egyptian infantry in company-sized strongpoints waited behind berms and in shallow trenches, then engaged at ranges where the Sagger wire-guided anti-tank missile, the Soviet AT-3, was most lethal: two-man teams, one guiding and one carrying the reload, could destroy a tank from more than two kilometers away across flat ground that favored the defender. Where tanks closed the distance, Egyptian soldiers with RPG-7 rocket-propelled grenades engaged from flank positions, often from ditches invisible to Israeli gunners until the rocket was already in flight.

The result on 7 October was a shock for the Israeli armored arm. Tanks advanced without adequate infantry support, a habit inherited from 1967 when Arab infantry had rarely stood their ground, and were methodically picked apart by weapons their crews had not faced in this density before. Egyptian accounts and Israeli after-action reports alike describe armor-led charges dissolving under crossfire from multiple missile teams. Israeli commanders, operating under the shock of surprise with incomplete information, fed their brigades into the fight piecemeal, and the Egyptians, who had rehearsed the defense of the bridgeheads for years, destroyed them piecemeal in turn.

On 8 October the fighting intensified. General David Elazar, the Israeli chief of staff, ordered a major counterattack intended to cut across the Egyptian bridgeheads and reach the canal crossings, but coordination between the Israeli brigades faltered, intelligence about Egyptian positions proved inaccurate, and the attacking columns again ran into layered anti-tank defenses. The 8 October counterattack collapsed with heavy Israeli tank losses, forcing the Israeli general staff to reconsider the conduct of the campaign in Sinai. Estimates of Israeli armor destroyed in the first days vary across sources, with accounts placing cumulative losses in the hundreds of tanks by the end of the second week.

### How did Egyptian infantry stop Israeli tanks in the first days?
Egyptian infantry stopped the Israeli armor with wire-guided Sagger missiles and RPG-7 teams fighting from prepared positions inside the bridgeheads. The SAM umbrella overhead kept Israeli aircraft away, so the tank columns advanced without air cover into long-range crossfire. The armor-led counterattacks of 7 and 8 October broke against these defenses and were destroyed in detail.

The deeper significance of those two days lay in what they proved about the Egyptian soldier. In 1967 the army had collapsed under air attack and confused command; in 1973 the infantry stood its ground against the armor that had humiliated it six years earlier. The Sagger required the operator to keep a moving tank in his sight through the entire flight of the missile, demanding steadiness under fire, and months of live firing on desert ranges had made the procedure routine. The defense of the bridgeheads was the execution of a plan, and the plan held.

## The Missile Umbrella

The infantry's success depended on a condition overhead that the Egyptians had built at enormous expense: a layered air defense system that made the skies over the canal zone lethal for Israeli aircraft. In 1967 the Israeli Air Force had destroyed the Egyptian air force on the ground within hours and then operated at will over the battlefield, and every Egyptian plan since then had started from the determination never to repeat that experience. The answer was a missile umbrella, a dense network of surface-to-air missile batteries echeloned behind the canal, designed to cover the bridgeheads to a depth of roughly ten to fifteen kilometers.

The umbrella had three main missile layers. The SA-2 threatened aircraft at high altitude, the SA-3 covered the low to medium bands, and the SA-6, the newest and most dangerous, was a mobile system that could move with the advancing troops and employed radar frequencies the Israelis had not fully analyzed before the war. Below the missiles sat the ZSU-23-4, a self-propelled gun mounting four radar-guided 23mm cannon, which filled the lowest altitudes where aircraft tried to slip beneath the missile envelopes. Egyptian accounts credit this system with denying the Israeli Air Force the freedom of action it had enjoyed in 1967, and Israeli accounts acknowledge early aircraft losses that forced a sharp change in tactics.

The effect was immediate. On 7 and 8 October, when Israeli armor desperately needed close air support against the Egyptian bridgeheads, the aircraft that came to help flew into the missile engagement zone. Israeli pilots, trained for the permissive skies of 1967, discovered that flying low brought them into ZSU-23-4 fire and that climbing brought them into SA-6 envelopes. The air force took losses in the opening days that Israeli sources describe as the heaviest the service had suffered in such a short span, and the response was to pull back from the canal zone and mount suppression campaigns against the missile batteries while the ground battle developed without decisive air intervention.

The umbrella also shaped Egyptian decisions. The ten-kilometer depth of the bridgeheads was not an accident of timid command; it was the reach of the missile cover. Egyptian planners, led by War Minister General Ahmed Ismail Ali and Chief of Staff General Saad el-Shazly, understood that advancing beyond the umbrella meant exposing the field army to the full weight of Israeli air power, the arm that had decided 1967. The decision to hold at that line was a conscious trade, security under the missiles against the temptation of a deeper advance, and for the first week of the war it looked like wisdom.

## The Golan Front

While Egypt crossed the canal, Syria attacked the Golan Heights on the northern front, and the two-front war was the strategic premise of the entire Egyptian plan. The Syrians struck with massed armor and artillery on the afternoon of 6 October, achieving surprise comparable to Egypt's against the thin Israeli defenses on the plateau. For the first two days Syrian tank columns advanced deep into the Golan, and the Israeli position was described as desperate, with reserves rushed north in a mobilization that strained the entire defense establishment.

The Syrian advance could not be sustained. By 8 October the Israeli counterattacks on the Golan, fed by the first waves of mobilized reserves, began pushing the Syrians back, and by 10 and 11 October the Israelis had recovered the lost ground and were advancing into Syria itself, toward the road to Damascus. The northern front stabilized in Israel's favor within a week, and that carried a direct consequence for Egypt: the entire Egyptian war plan had assumed that Israel, fighting two major fronts simultaneously, would be unable to concentrate its full strength against either. Once the Golan was secured, Israel could shift the weight of its mobilized power southward to Sinai, and Syrian appeals for Egypt to press its own offensive grew more urgent as Syrian forces fell back.

## Mobilization and the Shift of Momentum

Israel's recovery began, as its recoveries had in 1948 and 1967, with mobilization. The surprise of 6 October had caught the standing army at peacetime strength, a thin screen of regulars backed by a mobilization system that could field the bulk of the nation's combat power within days. By the end of the first week that system was delivering: reservists poured into depots, brigades assembled, and the order of battle in Sinai grew steadily heavier. Israeli accounts describe a transformation over the second week of October from desperate defense to a force capable of offensive action, and the Egyptian high command understood that the window in which it held the advantage was narrowing.

The shift was not only a matter of numbers. Israeli command culture, bruised by the failures of 7 and 8 October, began to adapt. Tank commanders learned, at brutal cost, that Sagger teams had to be suppressed by artillery and attacked by combined arms rather than by armor alone, and the air force developed tactics for operating at the edges of the missile engagement zones. The adaptation was uneven, but the direction was clear: the force stunned on 6 October was learning, while the Egyptians, dug into their bridgeheads, were largely static.

Egyptian perspectives on this period emphasize the defensive stand and the political logic behind it. The war aim, as defined by President Anwar Sadat and General Ismail, was limited: to shatter the status quo and create the conditions for a negotiated recovery of Sinai. From that standpoint the bridgeheads were already a victory. The Israeli perspective saw something different: a pause that squandered the initiative and gave the mobilizing Israeli army time to assemble the blow that would eventually fall.

## The Halt at Ten Kilometers

The halt was not a failure of nerve discovered after the fact; it was the plan. The Egyptian operational concept envisioned the crossing, the seizure of bridgeheads, and then a pause to consolidate and bring up heavy equipment, with further advances only under conditions that protected the army from Israeli air power. General Shazly, who had designed the crossing in detail, argued that the field armies should not advance beyond the SAM cover until the missile batteries themselves could be moved forward to extend the umbrella, a process that required time and engineering work.

There was also a political dimension the military planners could not ignore. Sadat needed the war to produce a shock and then a settlement, and an army destroyed in the open desert would have produced neither. The memory of 1967, when Egyptian armor had been annihilated in Sinai by air attack, hung over every Egyptian staff discussion of a deeper advance, and Israeli intelligence concluded by the second week of October that the Egyptians intended to remain in their bridgeheads, an assessment that shaped Israeli planning for the counterstroke already taking form.

Yet the halt carried its own dangers, and both Egyptian and Israeli analysts recognized them. A static defense surrendered the initiative to an enemy whose entire military tradition was built on seizing it. The ten-kilometer line, for all its missile cover, was still a narrow strip, and the Israeli army, once fully mobilized, could maneuver around its flanks and strike at the canal crossings from the rear. Egyptian officers who favored a continued advance argued that the momentum should have been exploited to seize the Sinai passes, the Gidi and Mitla defiles some thirty kilometers east of the canal. That argument lost inside the Egyptian high command in the first week, but it would be revived under pressure, and the revival would produce the 14 October offensive.

## The Armored Push of 14 October

The pressure came from Damascus. As Israeli forces on the Golan pushed into Syria and threatened the approaches to the capital, Syrian leaders appealed to Cairo to relieve the northern front by attacking in Sinai. Sadat, committed to the two-front strategy and regarding the alliance with Syria as both a military necessity and a political obligation, faced a demand that cut against his own operational plan. Egyptian and Syrian accounts differ on the tone and urgency of these exchanges, but the outcome is not in dispute: on the night of 13 to 14 October, Sadat ordered the Egyptian armies to launch a major armored offensive eastward toward the Sinai passes.

The attack on 14 October was the largest tank battle of the war on the southern front. Egyptian armored and mechanized divisions, more than a thousand tanks by most accounts, advanced out of the bridgeheads in several columns aimed at the passes, beyond the missile umbrella into open desert. There they met an Israeli defense that had spent a week preparing for exactly this move: Israeli armor, heavily reinforced and fighting on ground of its own choosing, met the Egyptian columns head-on, while Israeli aircraft, freed from the densest missile zones, attacked the advancing formations from above.

The result was a defeat for the Egyptian offensive and a loss of armor on a scale that shocked Cairo. Egyptian tanks, advancing in formations that Israeli accounts describe as rigid and poorly coordinated, were engaged at long range by Israeli tank gunnery that exploited superior fire control and crew training. Figures for Egyptian tank losses on 14 October are contested, with accounts placing them in the range of two hundred to more than two hundred fifty tanks destroyed or disabled in a single day, against Israeli losses described as far lighter, in the dozens. The precise numbers remain disputed, but the direction of the exchange is not: the offensive failed to reach the passes, the columns fell back to the bridgeheads, and the Egyptian armored reserve was severely weakened.

The consequences ran deeper than the day's losses. The battle confirmed to the Israeli command that the Egyptian army could be beaten in maneuver warfare outside its missile cover, and it confirmed to the Egyptian command that the offensive had been a mistake. Shazly, who had opposed the attack, regarded it as political pressure overriding military logic; Sadat's defenders argued that the alliance with Syria left no choice. The offensive consumed the mobile reserve just as Israel was finalizing its own plans to cross the canal, and that timing would prove fateful.

## Planning the Counterstroke

Even before 14 October, Israeli planners had been working on the operation that would reverse the war in Sinai. The concept, developed by the southern front command under Major General Shmuel Gonen and refined under Elazar and Defense Minister Moshe Dayan, called for crossing the Suez Canal to the west bank, establishing a bridgehead on the Egyptian side, and then destroying the SAM batteries and supply lines sustaining the Egyptian armies east of the canal. The logic was direct: the missile umbrella that protected the bridgeheads could not easily be turned to face a threat from behind, and an Israeli force west of the canal would sever the crossings on which the Second and Third Armies depended.

The plan required a crossing point, and Israeli reconnaissance settled on Deversoir, at the northern end of the Great Bitter Lake, where the canal narrowed and where a gap had developed between the Egyptian Second Army to the north and the Third Army to the south. The gap reflected the seam between two army sectors, and Israeli intelligence had identified it as the weakest sector of the canal defense. The operation would demand a breakthrough to the canal, the rapid emplacement of bridges under fire, and then the passage of armored divisions to the west bank before the Egyptians could seal the breach.

The command arrangements were contentious on the Israeli side. Gonen, the front commander, and Major General Ariel Sharon, commanding a division on the southern front, clashed over the conduct of operations, with Sharon advocating an immediate crossing and Gonen favoring a more methodical approach. Elazar and Dayan mediated, and the eventual plan combined both instincts: a deliberate breakthrough battle followed by aggressive exploitation west of the canal. The 14 October Egyptian offensive, by throwing Egypt's armor against prepared Israeli defenses and weakening it, simplified the Israeli problem, and the crossing operation, codenamed Operation Stouthearted Men, was set in motion. Egyptian intelligence detected the buildup, but Cairo did not grasp the scale of what was coming.

## The Chinese Farm

The battle that opened the road to the canal took its name from an unlikely landmark. Near the Deversoir crossing point stood an Egyptian agricultural experimental station equipped with machinery imported from abroad; Israeli soldiers, seeing the unfamiliar equipment, called it the Chinese Farm, and the name attached itself to three days of fighting that ranked among the bloodiest of the war. The farm sat astride the routes the Israelis needed to reach the canal, and the Egyptians had fortified it with infantry, anti-tank weapons, and artillery.

The fighting began on the night of 15 October, when Sharon's division, tasked with breaking through to the canal, attacked the Egyptian positions around the farm and the adjacent road junction. What the Israelis expected to be a rapid penetration became a grinding battle. Egyptian infantry, fighting from trenches and bunkers with the same Sagger and RPG weapons that had stopped the armor on 7 and 8 October, held their positions through the night and into the following day, while Israeli tanks advancing in the darkness stumbled into close-range ambushes at ranges of tens of meters, where infantry anti-tank teams dominated.

On 16 October the Israelis committed more forces, including elements of Adan's division, and the battle expanded into armored clashes around the farm and the nearby crossroads. The Egyptians fed reinforcements into the sector, including armored brigades, and the fighting seesawed through the day with heavy losses on both sides. Casualty and tank-loss figures for the battle are disputed, with both sides claiming to have inflicted the heavier damage, but the intensity of the engagement is one of the few points on which Egyptian and Israeli narratives agree.

The strategic significance of the Chinese Farm lay not in who held the ground at any given hour but in what the battle enabled. While Egyptian forces were fixed around the farm, Israeli engineers and bridging units moved toward the canal at Deversoir, and the farm battle absorbed the Egyptian reserves that might otherwise have been thrown against the crossing. By the time the fighting subsided on 17 October, the Israelis had secured the corridor to the canal, and the crossing, the true objective of the operation, was underway.

## Crossing at Deversoir

On the night of 15 to 16 October, while the battle raged at the Chinese Farm, Sharon's forces reached the canal at Deversoir and began the operation that would transform the war. The first Israelis across were small parties, paratroopers and engineers who crossed in rubber boats and secured a foothold on the western bank: a few hundred meters of shoreline held by lightly armed troops against an Egyptian rear area that had not expected to become a front. Through the night and the following day the Israelis worked to widen it and to bring across the means to sustain an armored force on the far bank.

The critical engineering challenge was the bridging. The Israelis had prepared a massive roller bridge, a prefabricated structure designed to be towed to the canal and launched across it, along with pontoon bridges and ferries. Moving these under fire through the contested corridor was an operation of extraordinary difficulty: Egyptian artillery shelled the crossing area, Egyptian aircraft attacked the bridgehead, and engineers worked under continuous fire, with bridging equipment damaged and repaired and damaged again. The roller bridge was finally emplaced after a tortuous journey to the canal, and by the night of 17 to 18 October tanks were crossing to the west bank in growing numbers.

The Egyptian response was hampered by a command crisis. Reports of Israeli forces west of the canal reached Cairo in confused and contradictory form, and the high command struggled to assess whether the penetration was a raid or a major operation. Shazly, recognizing the danger, proposed withdrawing forces from the east bank to confront the crossing, which would have meant abandoning the bridgeheads won at such cost. Sadat and Ismail rejected the proposal, insisting that the bridgeheads be held and that the crossing be dealt with by local reserves. The dispute between the chief of staff and the political leadership slowed the Egyptian response at the moment when speed mattered most, and Egyptian counterattacks against the Israeli bridgehead were launched piecemeal and repelled by Israeli armor now operating with growing strength west of the canal.

Sharon, once across, pushed his forces westward and northward. His division fanned out on the west bank, striking at Egyptian SAM batteries, supply depots, and command posts, and the missile umbrella began to collapse from behind as its batteries were overrun or forced to displace. The logic of the Israeli plan was vindicated in the most direct way: the weapons that had kept the Israeli Air Force at bay were being destroyed by tanks, and with the umbrella torn open, Israeli aircraft returned to the battlefield over Sinai in strength.

By 18 October the Israeli bridgehead west of the canal was secure and expanding. Adan's division crossed in force, and the Israelis began reducing the Egyptian air defenses and isolating the Egyptian armies east of the canal. The Second Army to the north and the Third Army to the south found their supply lines under attack, their rear areas overrun, and their freedom of maneuver gone. The war in Sinai had been inverted: the Israelis held a bridgehead on the Egyptian side of the waterway, the SAM umbrella was disintegrating, and the Egyptian field armies faced the prospect of encirclement. The crossing at Deversoir, conceived as a counterstroke, had become the lever that pried the Egyptian position apart.

## The Widening Israeli Corridor on the West Bank

By the morning of 17 October 1973, the Israeli crossing at Deversoir on the west bank of the Suez Canal remained a narrow and exposed lodgment. Major General Ariel Sharon's division held the bridgehead site itself, with engineers working to complete the roller bridge and pontoon crossings that would carry armor across in strength. Egyptian artillery and air attacks continued to pound the crossing zone, and for much of the day the outcome of the Israeli gamble still hung in the balance. What changed the equation over the next forty eight hours was the speed with which Israeli commanders poured follow on forces across the canal and widened the bridgehead before Egyptian defenses on the west bank could solidify. By the night of 18 October, elements of Major General Avraham Adan's division were crossing, and the Israeli presence west of the canal was growing from a raid into a sustained offensive. The crossing of the canal had been the gamble; widening the corridor was the exploitation, and Israeli doctrine demanded that it happen fast.

The fighting on the east bank decided whether that exploitation could proceed. In the area Israeli troops called the Chinese Farm, near the Great Bitter Lake, Egyptian infantry and armor contested every meter of ground through 17 and 18 October, inflicting severe Israeli losses. Yet by 18 October resistance had eased enough for engineers to work their bridges with fewer interruptions, and the flow of tanks, artillery, and supplies onto the west bank accelerated. Sharon pushed north toward Ismailia, while Adan turned south toward the Egyptian Second and Third Army rear areas. The Egyptian high command, caught between the crossing and its established bridgeheads east of the canal, struggled to improvise a response. Chief of Staff General Saad el-Shazli argued for pulling armor back from the east bank to crush the crossing before it matured, but President Anwar Sadat and the political leadership rejected any withdrawal that might look like retreat. The disagreement paralyzed Egyptian countermeasures at the moment when a concentrated response might still have narrowed the Israeli corridor. The window in which the corridor could have been sealed closed by the end of 18 October, and from that point the Israeli force on the west bank grew daily in size and reach.

By 20 October the corridor was no longer a bridgehead but a broad wedge of Israeli controlled ground extending tens of kilometers west and south of Deversoir. Israeli armor moved freely across the desert between the canal and the Suez to Cairo road, raiding air defense sites, supply depots, and command posts. The SAM batteries that had shielded Egyptian forces were overrun or destroyed on the west bank, and with the missile umbrella torn open the Israeli air force operated over the canal zone with growing effectiveness. Egyptian command and control west of the canal deteriorated as headquarters were displaced and communications cut. On the east bank, the Second Army in the north and the Third Army in the south still held their bridgeheads, but their rear areas were now contested ground, and Israeli forces were moving around their flanks rather than through their fronts. The Israeli general staff now saw a chance to convert a tactical crossing into a strategic reversal: rather than merely relieving the canal front, the west bank force could threaten an entire Egyptian field army.

## The Encirclement of the Egyptian Third Army

South of the Great Bitter Lake, the Egyptian Third Army held the southern sector of the canal bridgeheads under the command of Major General Abd al-Munim Wasel. Its forces, estimated in most accounts at roughly forty five to fifty thousand men, occupied positions east of the canal around Suez city and the southern crossing points, with supply lines running back through the Sinai passes and across the canal by bridge and ferry. As Israeli forces on the west bank pushed south during 20 to 22 October, they began to sever these lines one by one. Israeli columns reached the roads running east of the canal that fed the Third Army's bridgehead, then cut the lateral routes, and finally pressed toward the Suez to Cairo highway, the last paved artery linking the army to the Egyptian heartland. Each road cut tightened the noose. By 23 October the Third Army was fighting in two directions at once, holding its canal positions against Israeli pressure from the east while its rear areas on the west bank fell under Israeli control.

### How was the Egyptian Third Army trapped?
The Israeli corridor widened west of the canal in the southern sector, severing the roads and supply lines feeding the Third Army south of the Great Bitter Lake. Encamped near Suez city with its rear cut, the army held position under siege conditions until negotiated convoys restored limited resupply.

The encirclement was achieved by maneuver rather than by a single battle. Israeli armor did not storm the Third Army's prepared positions; it sat astride the roads behind them. Once the corridor on the west bank stretched south past the latitude of Suez city, the army's overland supply ceased. Egyptian attempts to resupply by small boat across the Gulf of Suez and by air drop could not sustain tens of thousands of men, and Israeli air superiority made even these expedients costly. The Third Army's commander kept his forces in place, maintaining the canal bridgeheads and the defenses around Suez city, but the army was now a besieged garrison rather than a maneuvering field force. From the Egyptian perspective, the situation was a siege imposed on troops that had performed well in the crossing and the first week of the war; from the Israeli perspective, it was the legitimate fruit of the west bank offensive, a hostage force whose fate could be traded at the negotiating table. Both readings were accurate, and the tension between them would shape the diplomacy of the war's final days.

The battle for Suez city itself, on 24 and 25 October, showed that the encirclement was not the same as a collapse. When Israeli forces attempted to enter the city, Egyptian infantry, local defense units, and armed civilians met them with determined urban resistance. Israeli columns that pushed into the streets were ambushed, lost armor to close range anti tank fire, and withdrew after taking casualties. The Egyptians held Suez city, and the Israelis settled for completing the ring around it rather than storming it again. The city became the symbol of the Third Army's predicament and its defiance: cut off, under bombardment, short of supplies, but unbroken. For Cairo, the army's survival became a matter of national honor as well as military necessity; for Jerusalem, the army's hunger became leverage, a bargaining chip that Israel was in no hurry to surrender without concessions.

## American and Soviet Resupply

While the armies maneuvered, the superpowers were feeding the war. The United States began its airlift to Israel, Operation Nickel Grass, on 13 October, after the first week of fighting had shown that Israeli consumption of munitions, aircraft, and armor far exceeded prewar planning. Over the following weeks, American C-141 and C-5 transports flew more than five hundred sorties, delivering over twenty two thousand tons of tanks, shells, missiles, and other war material to Israeli airfields. The airlift was a political decision as much as a logistical one. Nixon and Kissinger concluded that Israel must not be allowed to lose, nor to win so completely that the Soviet position in the Middle East collapsed, leaving Washington to manage the wreckage alone. The resupply gave Israel the material means to sustain the west bank offensive through the third week of October, and Egyptian and Soviet observers understood exactly what it signified: American power was now directly engaged in the war's outcome.

Moscow ran its own airlift to Egypt and Syria, beginning around 10 October and continuing through the month, with hundreds of Soviet transport sorties delivering tanks, aircraft, missiles, and ammunition, supplemented by sealift to Syrian and Egyptian ports. Soviet leaders had not sought the war and were privately alarmed that Sadat had launched it without full consultation, but once fighting began they could not let their clients be defeated by American supplied forces without responding in kind. The two airlifts turned the October war into a proxy contest of industrial capacity, stiffening each client's determination to keep fighting. American officials noted with concern that Soviet pilots and advisers were deeply embedded in Egyptian and Syrian operations, while Soviet officials noted with equal concern that the American airlift was tilting the battlefield against their allies. The result was a war in which neither superpower controlled the fighting but both had raised the stakes of its outcome.

The economic dimension of superpower involvement arrived on 17 October, when the Arab members of OPEC announced an oil embargo against the United States and other supporters of Israel, coupled with production cuts. The embargo responded directly to the American airlift and Washington's open alignment with Israel, signaling that the war's consequences would reach far beyond the battlefield. For the Nixon administration, the embargo added a domestic and economic cost to every day the war continued, sharpening the incentive to bring the fighting to a close on terms that preserved American credibility with both Israel and the Arab states. For Moscow, the embargo was welcome evidence that the Arab world could wield economic weapons alongside Soviet backed military efforts. The embargo did not decide the war's military outcome, but it ensured that both superpowers felt the war as a crisis rather than a distant proxy engagement.

## The Race to the Suez to Cairo Road and Kilometer 101

With the Third Army's encirclement nearly complete, Israeli forces pressed west toward the Suez to Cairo highway, the road that tied the canal zone to the Egyptian capital. Control of this road would deepen the encirclement, threaten Cairo's approaches, and give Israel maximum leverage for the negotiations that all sides could see approaching. Israeli armor reached the highway and pushed along it to a point marked Kilometer 101, roughly one hundred kilometers from Cairo, establishing positions that placed the Egyptian capital's outer defenses within the shadow of the Israeli advance. The symbolism was as potent as the military reality. An Israeli force sitting astride the road to Cairo, one hundred kilometers from the capital of the largest Arab state, was a statement that the war had reversed: Egypt, which had opened the war with a triumphant crossing, now faced an enemy deep inside its own territory.

The Egyptian response was hampered by the same command paralysis that had let the corridor widen. Counterattack orders were issued and countermanded; units moved without clear objectives; the political leadership feared any major redeployment would signal panic. Meanwhile, the ceasefire diplomacy was already underway, and both sides understood that the final lines on the map would reflect the positions held when the guns stopped. This created a perverse incentive that Kissinger later described with cold clarity: each side had reason to keep fighting, and especially to keep advancing, until the ceasefire took effect, because every kilometer gained in the final hours became a bargaining asset. The Israeli drive to Kilometer 101 was the clearest expression of this logic, and the Egyptian fury at what Cairo called ceasefire violations during 23 to 25 October was its mirror image. Whether particular advances violated Resolution 338 became one of the war's most bitterly contested questions, with each side accusing the other of exploiting its ambiguities.

## The 24 to 25 October Crisis

The diplomatic crisis that brought the superpowers to the edge of direct confrontation began with the ceasefire itself. UN Security Council Resolution 338, adopted on 22 October, called for a ceasefire within twelve hours, the implementation of Resolution 242, and negotiations toward a just and durable peace. Kissinger had negotiated its terms in Moscow with Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev on 20 and 21 October, then flown to Tel Aviv to press them on Prime Minister Golda Meir. Israel accepted the resolution but delayed implementation, while its forces continued the operations that completed the Third Army's encirclement and the advance to Kilometer 101. Egypt, which had accepted the ceasefire, accused Israel of cynical and systematic violation; Israel countered that Egypt had violated the ceasefire first and that its own operations were defensive responses. Most later accounts agree that both sides exploited the ceasefire's loose language and the difficulty of halting large formations on twelve hours' notice, but that Israel's continued advances after 22 October were the larger and more consequential breach.

On 24 October, Sadat, alarmed by the tightening siege of the Third Army and convinced that Israel was using the ceasefire as cover to finish the encirclement, appealed to Washington and Moscow for a joint superpower force to supervise the truce. The appeal put Brezhnev in a difficult position. He had pledged Soviet support to Egypt, and Soviet prestige was now tied to the survival of the Third Army, yet a joint force with the Americans risked entangling Moscow in arrangements it could not control. Brezhnev's answer, delivered in a letter to Nixon on the night of 24 October, was blunt: if the United States would not join in enforcing the ceasefire, the Soviet Union would consider acting unilaterally, and Nixon should be aware that Moscow would face the consequences of such a step. The letter was widely read in Washington as a threat to introduce Soviet troops, possibly airborne forces already on alert, into the war zone.

Nixon's response, shaped largely by Kissinger while Watergate diminished the president's capacity, was to raise American forces worldwide to DEFCON 3 in the early hours of 25 October. The alert, the first of its kind since the Cuban missile crisis, put American forces globally on a heightened readiness footing: the 82nd Airborne Division was alerted, naval forces in the Mediterranean were repositioned, and strategic bomber crews were placed on standby. The move was designed as a signal rather than a prelude to war, a demonstration that Washington would not accept unilateral Soviet military intervention in the Middle East. It worked as a signal. Moscow, which had no desire for a direct clash with the United States over Egypt, backed away from the threat of unilateral action. Later accounts from both capitals suggest each side believed the other had been closer to military action than was the case, a classic pattern of crisis misperception. The DEFCON 3 alert remains the most dangerous moment of superpower confrontation in the October war, and one of the most dangerous of the entire Cold War, because it showed how a regional war could pull both superpowers toward steps neither had planned.

## UN Resolutions and the Emergency Force

The diplomatic machinery for ending the war moved through the UN Security Council. Resolution 338 of 22 October, as noted, demanded the ceasefire and set the framework of negotiations under appropriate auspices with the aim of implementing Resolution 242. When the ceasefire failed to hold and the superpower crisis erupted, the Council acted again. Resolution 340, adopted on 25 October, demanded an immediate and complete ceasefire, the return of the parties to the positions held when the 22 October ceasefire was to take effect, and established the United Nations Emergency Force, UNEF II, to supervise it. The resolution deliberately excluded the permanent members of the Security Council from contributing troops to the force, a provision insisted upon by both superpowers to avoid placing American and Soviet soldiers in direct contact on the ground. Command of UNEF II went to Finnish Lieutenant General Ensio Siilasvuo, and the first contingents began arriving within days.

### How was the ceasefire arranged in late October 1973?
UN Resolutions 338 and 340 demanded a ceasefire and authorized a UN emergency force, while American and Soviet pressure, sharpened by Washington's DEFCON 3 alert, compelled both sides to stop. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger shuttled between capitals to lock in the details and start direct military talks at Kilometer 101.

The resolutions worked because they combined legal authority with raw power. Resolution 338 supplied the ceasefire demand and the negotiating framework; Resolution 340 supplied the enforcement mechanism in the form of UNEF II and the explicit demand to return to the 22 October lines. But paper alone would not have stopped the armies. What stopped them was the convergence of superpower pressure: Washington's warning to Jerusalem that continued defiance would cost Israel American support, Moscow's warning to Cairo that Soviet backing had limits, and the shared superpower interest in avoiding a direct clash that the DEFCON 3 crisis had made terrifyingly concrete. Sadat accepted the ceasefire because the alternative was the destruction of the Third Army and possibly a wider disaster; Meir accepted it because Israel had achieved its military objectives on the west bank and because American pressure, including the implicit threat to the resupply relationship, was unmistakable. The ceasefire that took hold on 25 October was therefore less a negotiated agreement between the belligerents than a settlement imposed by circumstances the superpowers had created, and its durability would depend on whether those circumstances could become something the parties themselves owned.

## Kissinger's Shuttle and the Kilometer 101 Talks

With the ceasefire nominally in place, Kissinger began the intensive diplomacy that would define the war's aftermath. His method, which the press soon called shuttle diplomacy, meant flying between capitals with proposals and counterproposals, using American leverage over each party to extract concessions neither would have granted the other directly. In the last days of October, his immediate objective was narrow: to stabilize the ceasefire, to get UNEF II deployed, and to start direct talks between Egyptian and Israeli officers on implementing the military aspects of the truce. The venue chosen for those talks was Kilometer 101 on the Suez to Cairo road, the marker of the Israeli army's deepest advance, a location that concentrated the war's final symbolism into a single tent. Beginning on 28 October, Egyptian and Israeli officers met there face to face, the first direct military negotiations between the two states.

The Kilometer 101 talks, led on the Egyptian side by Major General Mohamed Abdel Ghani el-Gamasy and on the Israeli side by Major General Aharon Yariv, produced on 11 November a six point agreement that addressed the immediate military issues: the observance of the ceasefire, the supply of the Third Army and of Suez city, the exchange of prisoners, the deployment of UN checkpoints, and the eventual disengagement of forces. The agreement was a military document, not a political settlement, and it deliberately left the larger questions of territory and peace for later. Its significance lay in the fact of direct negotiation itself. Two armies that had been trying to destroy each other three weeks earlier were now bargaining over convoy routes and checkpoint procedures, with UN officers and American intermediaries smoothing the edges. The talks did not resolve the underlying conflict, and both sides kept maneuvering for advantage within the agreement's terms, but they established the pattern for the war's aftermath: American mediation, direct military to military contact, and step by step agreements on concrete issues.

## The Relief of the Third Army and the Guns Falling Silent

The most urgent item at Kilometer 101 was the fate of the encircled Third Army. By late October the army's situation was deteriorating: food, water, and medical supplies were running short, and Egyptian accounts described soldiers on reduced rations while Israeli accounts emphasized that the shortages, though real, were being managed and in some cases exaggerated for diplomatic effect. What is not disputed is that the army could not have sustained itself indefinitely without resupply, and that Israel understood the humanitarian and political value of controlling that resupply. Under the 11 November agreement and arrangements worked out through UNEF II and the International Committee of the Red Cross, convoys of food, water, and medicine reached the Third Army through UN supervised checkpoints. Supplies were limited and sometimes delayed by disputes over routes and inspections, but they relieved the immediate crisis. The Third Army stayed in its positions, its weapons intact, its honor preserved in Egyptian eyes; Israel retained the strategic fact of the encirclement as leverage for the disengagement negotiations to come.

Prisoners were exchanged in the weeks that followed, the wounded were evacuated, and UNEF II took up positions between the armies, creating a buffer that made renewed full scale fighting far more difficult. Sporadic incidents continued, and the ceasefire lines remained tense, but the great battles of October were over. The guns fell silent along the canal in the last days of the month, first in fact and then, as the Kilometer 101 agreement took hold, in law. For Egypt, the war's end brought a strange mixture of triumph and anxiety: the crossing of 6 October remained a genuine achievement that had restored Egyptian military pride, but the encirclement of the Third Army was a wound that would shape Cairo's diplomacy for years. For Israel, the relief at having reversed the war's opening disasters was tempered by the shock of the war's cost and by the knowledge that military victory had not produced security. For the superpowers, the October crisis warned how quickly a regional war could escalate beyond anyone's control, and both Washington and Moscow emerged determined to manage the aftermath rather than risk a repetition. The silence that settled over the canal zone in late October 1973 was not peace, but it was the precondition without which no peace could ever have been attempted.

## The Result, Argued Honestly

Any honest account of the October War must begin by holding two stories that refuse to reconcile, because both contain real evidence. The Egyptian story, the one told in Cairo and repeated every 6 October, holds that Egypt won. The Israeli story, told in Jerusalem with maps showing an army on the west bank of the canal and Syrian territory seized on the Golan Heights, holds that Israel won. The task here is not to split the difference for politeness. The task is to weigh each claim on its evidence and explain why each side can defend its verdict without inventing one.

The claim of total Egyptian victory runs as follows. On 6 October 1973, after six years of humiliation following the Six Day War of 1967, the Egyptian armed forces crossed the Suez Canal, breached the Bar Lev Line, and planted their flag on the eastern bank, ground that had been in Israeli hands since 1967. No Egyptian army had ever managed such an operation against Israel. For the first week of the war, Egyptian divisions held their bridgeheads against every Israeli counterattack, and the Syrian front fought Israel to a standstill on the Golan. When the guns stopped on 25 October, Egyptian forces still held ground east of the canal, territory they had taken by force of arms and never surrendered. The war broke the diplomatic stalemate that Israel had used to freeze the status quo, and within two years it produced disengagement agreements that began the process of Israeli withdrawal from Sinai. In this reading, the Third Army's encirclement was a local tactical setback, dramatic but strategically empty, because the war was never meant to liberate all of Sinai by force. It was meant to break the stalemate, prove the army could fight, and create the political conditions for diplomacy. On all three counts, Egypt succeeded. This is the strongest version of the Egyptian case, and it cannot be dismissed, because its central facts are correct. Egypt did cross. Egypt did hold. The stalemate did break.

The claim of total defeat runs as follows, and it too deserves its strongest form. After the initial Egyptian success, Israel recovered. Within days, Israeli intelligence corrected its catastrophic failure, the reserves mobilized, and Ariel Sharon's division began forcing a crossing to the west bank of the canal on the night of 15 to 16 October. From that bridgehead Israel widened its corridor, drove toward Ismailia and Suez, and by the time the ceasefire took effect on 25 October had completely encircled Egypt's Third Army, an estimated 20,000 to 30,000 men trapped east of the canal with their supply lines cut. On the Syrian front, Israel not only repelled the offensive but pushed back beyond the 1967 lines, ending the war with its guns closer to Damascus than Syria's guns to Tel Aviv. Egyptian forces held their bridgeheads, but Israeli forces stood on the west bank, closer to Cairo than the Egyptians were to any major Israeli city. In this reading, Egypt's limited objectives were a rationalization invented after the fact, because the real test of an offensive is whether it achieves what it set out to achieve, and an army that ends the war with its supply lines severed and one of its field armies surrounded has been defeated. This is the strongest version of the Israeli case, and it also cannot be dismissed, because its central facts are also correct. Israel did recover. The Third Army was encircled. The battlefield on 25 October favored Israel.

Adjudication requires separating the military map from the political purpose, because this was a war fought with political instruments, and judging it by the military map alone misreads what it was for. Militarily, the second half of the war belonged to Israel. The recovery after the first week was one of the most remarkable turnarounds in modern military history. Israeli commanders adapted to the missile umbrella, crossed the canal, and achieved a position of real tactical dominance by the ceasefire. The encirclement of the Third Army was not theater. It was a genuine operational achievement, and any account that waves it away insults the intelligence of the reader. Egyptian casualty figures reinforce how heavy the fighting was. Counts are disputed and source dependent, but Israeli dead are commonly cited around 2,500 to 2,800, while Egyptian dead estimates vary widely by source, roughly 5,000 to 15,000, with wounded far higher on both sides. No source should be quoted with more precision than the evidence allows, and anyone offering an exact total is selling certainty the archives do not provide.

But the military map is not the whole story, because the war was designed as a political operation with limited military means. Sadat's aim was never to drive Israel into the sea. His aim was to shatter the post 1967 stalemate, which had allowed Israel to sit behind its defensive lines while diplomacy went nowhere, and to demonstrate that Egypt could not be ignored. On those terms the war worked. Egypt shattered the myth of Israeli invincibility that had paralyzed Arab politics since 1967. It proved to Egyptians, and to the world, that the Egyptian army could plan, fight, and hold ground against the Israeli military. It seized and held territory east of the canal through the end of the fighting, which meant that the postwar negotiations began from a position where Egypt had something it had not had since 1967: land taken back by force, however limited the strip. And it broke the diplomatic deadlock so completely that within sixteen months the two sides signed a disengagement agreement that began returning Sinai to Egyptian control. A war that fails militarily cannot produce the aggressor's political objectives. This war produced them. That is the core of the adjudication: Israel won the military recovery, Egypt won the political transformation the war was fought to achieve, and the two facts do not cancel each other.

The fair verdict, then, is a mixed result with a clear center of gravity. On the battlefield of the second half, Israel prevailed. Across the arc of the war's purpose, Egypt prevailed. Historians who insist the war was an unqualified triumph for either side are not reading the evidence; they are reading their sympathies. The honest account grants Israel its recovery and its encirclement of the Third Army as genuine military achievements, and grants Egypt its limited war aims as genuinely achieved. The war did not liberate Sinai by force. It made the liberation of Sinai by negotiation possible, and that distinction matters, because it is the difference between the war Egypt planned and the war its critics claim it failed to fight.

The superpower dimension of the war belongs in this adjudication, because neither side fought alone and neither side controlled the ending. When Israel's ammunition and aircraft losses mounted in the first week, the United States launched a massive airlift, Operation Nickel Grass, that kept the Israeli war effort supplied. When Egyptian and Syrian losses mounted, the Soviet Union ran its own airlift to its Arab clients. The war ended not when one side achieved its battlefield objectives but when Washington and Moscow, alarmed at the risk of direct confrontation, imposed a stop. On 24 October the Soviet leadership threatened unilateral intervention to save the encircled Third Army, the United States answered by raising its nuclear alert level, and Henry Kissinger flew to Moscow to hammer out the ceasefire terms. It means the encirclement of the Third Army never became the annihilation some Israeli commanders wanted, because the superpowers froze the battle in place. It also means Egypt's political gains were partly underwritten by Soviet protection and American diplomacy, not by battlefield position alone. A war decided by two superpowers stepping in is a war whose result belongs to politics as much as to arms, and that is precisely the reading that favors Egypt's interpretation: the war was always a political instrument, and the politics worked.

There is one more piece of evidence the honest account must include, because it shaped everything that followed. The war was accompanied by the Arab oil embargo of October 1973, which quadrupled oil prices and plunged the Western economies into crisis. The embargo was not Egypt's doing alone, but it was made possible by the war Egypt and Syria started, and it gave the Arab cause a form of economic leverage it had never possessed. The embargo's pain in Washington and Europe is part of why the United States pressed Israel toward disengagement after the war. Military historians sometimes treat the embargo as a separate story. Strategically it was the same story: the October War attacked the stalemate on every front at once, military, diplomatic, and economic, and the combined pressure is what broke it.

## Pride Restored: Why 6 October Became Armed Forces Day

Egyptians celebrate 6 October as Armed Forces Day because it is the date the humiliation ended. The Six Day War of 1967 had done more than cost Egypt the Sinai, Gaza, and its air force. It had broken something in the national self image. For six years Egyptians lived with the knowledge that their army had collapsed in days, that their territory was occupied, and that every diplomatic effort to recover it had run into the wall of Israeli indifference and great power maneuvering. The crossing of the canal on 6 October 1973 reversed that narrative in a single afternoon. Soldiers who had grown up in the shadow of 1967 waded through water, scaled the sand ramparts with hoses, and stood on the eastern bank under fire. The psychological effect in Egypt was immediate and electric. People who had learned to speak of the army with embarrassment spoke of it with pride, and that pride has not faded, because it attached itself to the one fact no later setback could erase: Egyptians had crossed, and they had stayed.

The war functioned as a psychological victory even for Egyptians who understood the military complexities. The encirclement of the Third Army, which Israeli accounts emphasize, was real, but it happened on ground that Egypt had taken in the first days of the war, and it did not undo the fact that the crossing had succeeded. National memory tends to anchor on the turning point, not the aftermath, and the turning point was 6 October. That is why the date became Armed Forces Day, and why it remains one of the most emotionally charged dates on the Egyptian calendar. It marks the moment Egypt stopped being the country that had lost in 1967 and became the country that had fought back.

The observance of the day shows how deeply the war entered national life. For years the anniversary was marked by a military parade reviewed by the president, displaying the equipment and the soldiers of the war. The October War Panorama, a vast museum and memorial complex in Cairo, lets visitors walk through a reconstruction of the crossing. School curricula teach the war as the redemption of 1967. State media replays the footage of the water cannons breaching the sand ramparts every October. These rituals do political work, and a critical reader should recognize that, but they also reflect a genuine popular attachment. The pride of October 1973 is one of the few things in modern Egyptian history that survives the country's political divisions, claimed by Nasserists and Sadatists, by the state and by ordinary citizens who had family at the front.

The diplomatic victory followed the psychological one, and this is where the war's true importance lies. Before October 1973, the Middle East was frozen. Israel held its occupied territories and saw no reason to negotiate seriously. The Arab states had no leverage. The superpowers managed the conflict without resolving it. After October 1973, nothing was frozen. The war had demonstrated that the status quo carried a price, and that demonstration is what moved diplomacy. The first disengagement agreement, signed in January 1974, pulled Israeli forces back from the canal and established a United Nations buffer zone, the first Israeli withdrawal from territory taken in 1967. The second disengagement agreement, signed in September 1975, pulled Israel back further in Sinai and returned the Abu Rudeis oil fields to Egypt. Each agreement was a step that the prewar stalemate had made unthinkable. Each was made possible by the leverage the war created.

The mechanics of that diplomacy deserve attention, because they show how a limited war converts into negotiated gains. The ceasefire itself was imposed by United Nations Security Council Resolutions 338, 340, and 341, passed between 22 and 27 October 1973, which called for a halt to the fighting and the start of negotiations. The United Nations Emergency Force, UNEF II, deployed into the buffer zone between the armies, separating forces that had been trying to destroy each other days earlier. Then came Henry Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy, weeks of flights between Cairo, Jerusalem, and Damascus, extracting concessions step by step. Sadat proved a willing partner in this process, and his willingness was itself a product of the war: having achieved his limited objectives, he had no interest in another round of fighting, and every interest in converting military leverage into diplomatic currency. The disengagement agreements were the first time Israel had ever withdrawn from Arab territory captured in 1967 as part of a negotiated arrangement. That precedent, once established, could not be unmade.

Sadat understood this leverage better than anyone, and he spent the years after the war converting it into a settlement. His diplomacy was controversial in the Arab world, and it would eventually cost Egypt its standing in the Arab League, but it flowed directly from the logic of the October War: fight a limited war to break the stalemate, then negotiate from the strength the war created. The road that began at the canal in October 1973 ran through the disengagement agreements of 1974 and 1975 and onward to [the peace Sadat would later negotiate at Camp David](/2016/04/01/anwar-sadat-and-camp-david/). That later diplomacy belongs to another article in this series, but its roots are here, in the decision to use war as the instrument that made peace negotiable.

This is also why the war occupies such a central place in the story of [modern Egypt since 1952](/2015/11/01/modern-egypt-since-1952/). The revolution of 1952 had promised dignity and independence. The defeat of 1967 had betrayed that promise. The war of 1973 restored it, not completely and not permanently, but enough that Egypt could negotiate its future from a position of self respect rather than humiliation. For readers of this series, the October War is the hinge between the Nasser era and the Sadat era, between defeat and recovery, between the Egypt that lost Sinai and the Egypt that would get it back.

## Studying the October War

The dates that anchor this war are few, and they repay memorization. 6 October 1973 is the crossing and the date Egypt celebrates. 14 October marks the failed Egyptian armored offensive that exposed the army to the Israeli counterstroke. 15 to 16 October is the start of the Israeli crossing to the west bank, the hinge on which the second half of the war turned. 22 October and 24 October are the two United Nations ceasefire resolutions, the second of which finally held, and 25 October is the day the guns fell silent with the Third Army encircled. January 1974 and September 1975 are the two disengagement agreements that converted the war's leverage into territory. A reader who can place these dates in order and explain what changed at each one understands the war's structure.

The table above is the article's working instrument. Use it as a test of comprehension rather than a summary to skim. Cover the middle column and reconstruct what happened in each phase from the phase name alone. Then cover the right column and recall the evidence for each phase. This habit separates a reader who has memorized a narrative from one who can defend it. When a claim about the war sounds too simple, return to the table and ask which phase it describes and what evidence supports it. Most oversimplifications of the October War collapse under that question, because they borrow the drama of one phase and apply it to the whole. The claim that Egypt won an unqualified victory borrows the crossing phase and ignores the counterattack phase. The claim that Israel won an unqualified victory borrows the counterattack phase and ignores the political outcome phase. The claim that the war achieved nothing borrows nothing at all; it simply refuses to read the disengagement agreements. Each of these readings fails the same test, which is why the test is worth practicing.

The table also trains the reader to think in terms of aims and means, the central analytical skill this article has tried to build. Ask of each phase not only what happened but what it was for. The crossing was the means to shatter the stalemate. The counterattack was Israel's means to restore its deterrent and improve its bargaining position. The political outcome was the conversion of both armies' sacrifices into a new diplomatic reality. A reader who can keep aims and means distinct will not be confused by the paradox that defines this war: the side that lost the second half of the fighting gained the larger share of the war's purpose.

The table also serves as the bridge to the wider series. The crossing phase connects backward to the Six Day War of 1967, which created the Bar Lev Line and the humiliation the crossing was designed to erase. The counterattack phase connects to the military history of Israel and to the question of how a smaller state recovers from strategic surprise, a theme that runs through several articles in this series. The political outcome phase connects forward to the disengagement agreements and to the diplomacy that would eventually return Sinai to Egypt, and it connects sideways to the economic history of the period, since the war and the oil embargo that accompanied it reshaped the global economy of the 1970s. A student of the series should be able to trace these connections in both directions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Yom Kippur War?

The Yom Kippur War was the October 1973 war in which Egypt and Syria launched a coordinated surprise attack on Israel. Egypt struck across the Suez Canal into the Israeli-held Sinai Peninsula while Syria attacked the Golan Heights. The fighting lasted from 6 to 25 October and drew in the United States and the Soviet Union, which backed opposite sides and nearly confronted each other directly. Egypt's initial crossing shattered Israel's sense of security and breached the Bar Lev Line, though Israel later counterattacked across the canal and encircled Egypt's Third Army. The war ended with a United Nations ceasefire and no clear battlefield winner. Egypt remembers it as the October War and celebrates it as the victory that restored national pride after the 1967 defeat and opened the road to the peace negotiations that followed.

### Q: What year was the Yom Kippur War?

The Yom Kippur War was fought in 1973. The attack began on 6 October 1973, the Jewish holy day of Yom Kippur, which fell during the Muslim holy month of Ramadan that year, so Egyptians also call it the Ramadan War. The choice of year reflected six years of stalemate after Egypt's defeat in the 1967 Six-Day War. President Anwar Sadat had spent 1971 and 1972 seeking a diplomatic settlement and expelling Soviet military advisers to court American mediation, but negotiations went nowhere. By 1973 he concluded that only a shock on the battlefield would force Israel and the superpowers to negotiate seriously. The war lasted nineteen days, ending with a ceasefire on 25 October 1973, and its aftermath shaped Middle Eastern diplomacy for the rest of the decade.

### Q: Why did Egypt attack Israel in 1973?

Egypt attacked because six years of diplomacy after the 1967 defeat had produced nothing. Israel still occupied the Sinai Peninsula, the Suez Canal sat idle as a fortified frontier, and neither Washington nor Moscow would pressure Israel while the situation stayed quiet. Sadat concluded that Egypt had to shatter this "no peace, no war" stalemate by force. His aims were limited rather than maximal: he did not expect to destroy Israel, but to cross the canal, inflict a shock, and create a crisis big enough to drag the superpowers into serious negotiations. The attack was therefore a political instrument as much as a military one, designed to make the status quo untenable and to restore Egyptian and Arab pride after the humiliation of 1967.

### Q: Did Egypt win the Yom Kippur War?

The honest answer is mixed, and Egyptians and Israelis remember it differently. On the battlefield, Israel recovered from the surprise, crossed back over the canal, and encircled Egypt's Third Army, so by purely military measures the war ended badly for Egypt. But Egypt achieved its political aims: the crossing proved the army could fight, shattered Israel's sense of invulnerability, ended the post-1967 stalemate, and forced the negotiations that eventually returned Sinai. Egypt therefore celebrates the war as a victory, and in the sense that mattered most to Sadat, the psychological and diplomatic sense, the claim has real substance. The war's true result was political rather than military, which is why both victory and defeat narratives contain part of the truth.

### Q: How did the Egyptian army cross the Bar Lev Line?

Egyptian engineers crossed first in small boats under an artillery barrage, then breached the great sand ramparts with high-pressure water cannons mounted on barges, washing away in hours what would have taken days to bulldoze. Through the gaps they laid pontoon bridges and operated ferries, pouring armor and supplies across. Within the first day tens of thousands of troops were on the east bank, and Egyptian accounts claim over 100,000 crossed in the first 24 hours. The Bar Lev forts, designed to hold with small garrisons until reserves arrived, were isolated and overrun. By nightfall Egypt held bridgeheads along the canal and had achieved the complete tactical surprise the plan required.

### Q: Why do Egyptians celebrate the October War?

Egyptians celebrate the October War because it restored the pride that the 1967 defeat had destroyed. For six years Egypt had lived with occupation, a closed canal, and the sense that its army could not fight. The canal crossing reversed all of that in a single day and proved Egyptian soldiers could defeat Israeli forces in battle. The 6th of October became Armed Forces Day, a national holiday marked by military parades and commemorations. The celebration is not really about the war's messy final weeks, when Israel counterattacked; it is about the moment Egypt seized the initiative, ended the stalemate, and set the country on the road to recovering Sinai through negotiations.

### Q: How did the Yom Kippur War lead to peace?

The war led to peace by making the pre-1973 stalemate impossible to sustain. Before the fighting, neither Israel nor the superpowers felt any urgency to negotiate; after it, all sides recognized that another war could drag Washington and Moscow into direct confrontation. The shock of the Egyptian crossing gave Sadat the standing to negotiate, and the ceasefire diplomacy, including Kissinger's shuttle missions and the Sinai disengagement agreements of 1974 and 1975, created a working relationship between Egypt and Israel. That momentum carried through to Sadat's historic visit to Jerusalem and the Camp David negotiations, which this series covers in the article on Sadat. The war did not cause the peace directly, but it opened the only road that led there.

### Q: What was the outcome of the Yom Kippur War?

The war ended on 25 October 1973 with a United Nations ceasefire and no decisive battlefield winner. Israel had crossed to the west bank of the canal and encircled Egypt's Third Army, but Egypt held bridgeheads in Sinai and had achieved its political objectives. The immediate outcomes included the deployment of a UN emergency force, the Kilometer 101 talks, disengagement agreements that pulled both armies back, and the eventual return of the canal zone to Egyptian control. The deeper outcome was diplomatic: the war broke the post-1967 deadlock, restored Egyptian confidence, shook Israeli politics, triggered the Arab oil embargo, and set in motion the negotiations that would eventually bring peace between Egypt and Israel.

### Q: Why did Egypt choose Yom Kippur for the attack?

Egypt chose Yom Kippur for maximum surprise. On the Jewish Day of Atonement, Israel shut down: radio and television went silent, transport stopped, and much of the army was on leave, which slowed mobilization. The date also fell during Ramadan, when Muslim soldiers were fasting, a fact Egyptian planners used to reinforce the deception that no attack was coming. October offered favorable tides and moonlight for the canal crossing, and the 2 PM start time put the afternoon sun at the attackers' backs. The timing was thus a calculated weapon, turning Israel's calendar and Egypt's own religious observance into instruments of deception.

### Q: What was Operation Badr?

Operation Badr was the Egyptian operational plan for the canal crossing, named for the Prophet Muhammad's first great victory at the Battle of Badr. Drawn up under War Minister Ahmed Ismail Ali and Chief of Staff Saad El Shazly, it called for a massive artillery barrage, infantry crossings in small boats, the breaching of the Bar Lev sand ramparts with water cannons, and the rapid laying of pontoon bridges. Crucially, the plan was deliberately limited: Egyptian forces were to seize bridgeheads about 10 kilometers deep and stop under the cover of their SAM missile umbrella rather than drive for the Sinai passes. This restraint reflected Sadat's political aims; the operation was designed to shock, not to conquer.

### Q: How did Egypt deceive Israel before the 1973 attack?

Egypt ran one of the most successful deception campaigns in modern warfare. For months the army staged large exercises along the canal, then repeatedly stood down, training Israeli intelligence to expect another false alarm; a May 1973 mobilization had already cost Israel dearly when nothing happened. Egypt announced demobilizations, granted leave, and spread stories that soldiers were going on pilgrimage. Equipment was moved openly by day and hidden by night. Syrian cooperation was masked as routine activity. By October, Israeli intelligence had grown so accustomed to Egyptian "exercises" that the real buildup blended into the noise, and the attack achieved almost complete strategic surprise.

### Q: Why did the Egyptian advance halt after crossing the canal?

The advance halted because the plan required it. Operation Badr called for bridgeheads roughly 10 kilometers deep, the maximum distance Egypt's SAM missile umbrella could protect from the west bank. Pushing beyond that cover toward the Sinai passes would have exposed Egyptian armor to the Israeli air force, which had been neutralized only inside the missile shield. There was also a political logic: Sadat wanted a limited shock that would force negotiations, not a deep drive that might invite a catastrophic defeat. When Egypt did lunge for the passes on 14 October, partly to relieve Syria, the attack failed with heavy losses, vindicating the original caution.

### Q: What happened at the Battle of the Chinese Farm?

The Battle of the Chinese Farm, fought from 15 to 17 October 1973 near an agricultural research station named for its foreign-made equipment, was among the war's bloodiest tank battles. Israeli divisions under Ariel Sharon and others fought through Egyptian defenses to reach the canal at Deversoir, losing many tanks in brutal close-range fighting. The battle's purpose was to secure the crossing point for Israel's counteroffensive to the west bank. Egyptian forces fought hard and inflicted severe losses, but the Israelis broke through, and the crossing that followed changed the war's course by carrying the fighting onto Egyptian-held ground west of the canal.

### Q: How did Israeli forces cross back over the Suez Canal?

Israeli forces crossed at Deversoir, in the sector between the Egyptian Second and Third Armies, on the night of 15 to 16 October. After the bloody fighting at the Chinese Farm cleared the approach, engineers laid a prefabricated bridge and operated ferries while paratroopers secured the west bank. Ariel Sharon's division led the crossing, followed by more armor. The Israelis then widened the corridor on both banks, fanning out westward to cut Egyptian supply lines. The crossing was a bold gamble that succeeded because the seam between the two Egyptian armies was thinly held, and it reversed the war's momentum by putting Israeli troops on Egypt's side of the canal.

### Q: Why did the United States and the Soviet Union intervene in the war?

Both superpowers intervened because their clients were fighting and a regional defeat could shift the Cold War balance. The United States launched Operation Nickel Grass, a massive airlift resupplying Israel with weapons and equipment from 13 October, after Israel's losses mounted. The Soviet Union ran its own airlift to Egypt and Syria. As Israeli forces encircled Egypt's Third Army, Moscow threatened unilateral action to save its ally, and Washington responded by raising its worldwide military alert to DEFCON 3 on 25 October. The crisis showed how close the war came to a superpower confrontation and forced both sides to impose the ceasefire.

### Q: What was the Arab oil embargo during the Yom Kippur War?

The Arab oil embargo was the decision by Arab petroleum-exporting countries, led by Saudi Arabia, to cut oil production and ban exports to the United States and other supporters of Israel during the war. Announced in October 1973, it was meant as an economic weapon backing the Egyptian and Syrian war effort. The embargo lasted until March 1974 and, combined with production cuts, quadrupled oil prices and triggered fuel shortages, inflation, and recession across the Western world. Though it did not change the battlefield, it demonstrated Arab economic leverage, reshaped global energy politics, and added to the international pressure for a settlement.

### Q: How many soldiers died in the Yom Kippur War?

Casualty figures are disputed and vary by source, so all numbers should be treated as ranges. Israeli dead are commonly cited at around 2,500 to 2,800, with roughly 7,000 to 9,000 wounded. Egyptian dead are harder to fix: estimates range from about 5,000 to 15,000 depending on the source, with tens of thousands wounded. Syrian dead are usually placed near 3,000. The wide Egyptian range reflects both wartime secrecy and the politicization of memory on all sides. What is certain is that the war was the bloodiest of the Arab-Israeli wars for Israel and cost Egypt heavily, particularly in the failed 14 October offensive and the siege of the Third Army.

### Q: Why is the war called the October War in Egypt?

Egypt calls it the October War because the name centers the Egyptian experience: the attack began on 6 October, and the date anchors the national commemoration, Armed Forces Day. "Yom Kippur War" is the Israeli and Western name, referring to the Jewish holy day on which the attack fell. Egyptians also sometimes call it the Ramadan War, since 6 October 1973 corresponded to 10 Ramadan. The naming difference reflects perspective: each name marks what mattered most to the namer. For Egypt, October means the crossing, the restored pride, and the victory narrative; the religious calendar of the other side is secondary.
