Financialisation is the word a generation of economists, sociologists, and political commentators reached for when they tried to name a change they could all see and none of them could quite measure: money appeared to be making money at a distance from the making of things. Profits gathered in lending, trading, and the management of claims. Households carried debts that earlier generations would have found unthinkable. Firms whose names were attached to factories, ships, and mines earned growing portions of their income from advances, holdings, and fees. Yet the word conceals a difficulty that this article places at its centre. The term carries several definitions that do not coincide, and Marxist analysis, which possesses the most developed theory of where profit comes from, faces a specific problem in accommodating the phenomenon at all. If profit originates in production, in the surplus value created there, then a swelling financial sector cannot be manufacturing new profit out of its own activity. It can only be receiving profit made elsewhere, extracting income from some source outside production, or appearing to grow because of the way profits are recorded. Every Marxist account of financialisation is, whether its author says so or not, an answer to that single constraint. Naming the constraint, and the three answers the tradition has given to it, converts a sprawling literature into an intelligible argument.

![Ledger books, loan contracts and share certificates arranged on a desk, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The plan of what follows is dictated by the problem rather than by chronology. Definitions come first, because a reader who cannot separate the narrow statistical sense of the term from its broader institutional senses will misread every later claim. Marx's own categories come second: interest-bearing capital, the division of gross profit between interest and the profit of enterprise, and the peculiar entity he called fictitious capital. Only with those categories in hand can the theoretical problem be stated in its exact form, and the statement matters, because the three answers differ precisely in which horn of the problem they accept. One answer treats financial expansion as redistribution driven by stagnation in production. A second locates a distinct source of gain in the direct extraction of income from workers outside the workplace. A third argues that much of the measured shift is an artefact of accounting and corporate structure. A historical variant, compatible with the first answer, reads the whole episode as a class strategy. The evidence is then weighed indicator by indicator, the strongest objection is given its full force, and the article closes by stating what would settle the dispute, including the finding that would defeat its own organising verdict.

## One word, several phenomena: what financialisation names

Begin with the definitions, because the literature's confusions begin there too. The canonical formulation in the Marxist and heterodox literature is the one offered by Gerald Epstein in the introduction to his edited volume of 2005: financialisation means the increasing role of financial motives, financial markets, financial actors, and financial institutions in the operation of domestic and international economies. The definition is deliberately capacious. It gathers motives, markets, actors, and institutions into a single tendency, and its breadth explains both its popularity and its weakness. A definition that covers the motives of managers, the size of markets, the behaviour of households, and the income of banks will register the phenomenon almost anywhere one looks, and a concept that registers everywhere discriminates nowhere. Epstein's formulation works as an umbrella under which researchers shelter while they measure different things, and much of the apparent disagreement in the field is the sound of those different measurements being compared as though they were one.

A narrower definition, associated with Greta Krippner's work of 2005, restricts the term to a pattern of accumulation in which profits accrue primarily through financial channels rather than through trade and commodity production. Krippner's version has the great virtue of being operational: it points at profit shares in national accounts and asks where the income of firms and sectors is booked. Its corresponding weakness is that it inherits every ambiguity of the accounts themselves, an inheritance that the third answer in this article exploits at length. Between Epstein's breadth and Krippner's precision sit intermediate definitions emphasising the dominance of shareholder value as a governing norm inside firms, the growth of financial assets relative to tangible assets, or the penetration of financial calculation into everyday life. Natascha van der Zwan's survey of 2014 usefully sorted the literature into three strands, one concerned with regimes of accumulation, one with the corporation under shareholder value, and one with the financialisation of daily existence, and the sorting itself demonstrates the point: the strands study different objects and cite different evidence while sharing a title.

For Marxist purposes the definition that matters is the one stated in this article's framework: financialisation denotes the increasing weight of financial motives, markets, institutions, and income in economic activity, including the growth of financial profit as a share of total profit and the extension of financial relations into household income. Two clauses in that formulation do theoretical work. The first clause, concerning the share of profit, connects the phenomenon to the distribution of surplus value, which is where Marxist theory has something distinctive to say. The second clause, concerning household income, connects it to the wage, and therefore to the second of the three answers, in which workers appear not only as producers of surplus value but as payers of interest and fees out of revenue. A definition lacking the first clause drifts toward sociology of markets; a definition lacking the second cannot see expropriation at all. Holding both clauses together is what makes the Marxist version of the concept more than a synonym for the growth of banks.

### What does the narrow definition of financialisation measure?

It measures where profit is booked rather than where goods are made. Krippner's formulation tracks the share of income accruing through financial channels in the accounts of firms and sectors, comparing that share against income from trade and commodity production, so the definition is a measuring instrument pointed at the distribution of profit between activities.

Definitions also differ in what they silently assume about causation, and the assumptions matter more than the wording. A definition centred on motives invites an account in which managers and investors changed their minds, adopted new norms, and thereby transformed the economy; explanation then runs from culture to structure. A definition centred on profit shares invites the opposite direction of travel, in which pressures inside accumulation pushed capital toward financial channels whatever anyone believed, and the new norms arrived as rationalisations of a movement already under way. Marxist writers overwhelmingly prefer the second direction, not from dogma but because their framework locates the motor of change in the production and reinvestment of surplus value. When surplus value cannot find profitable employment in expanding production, on the stagnationist telling, it seeks returns in claims upon future income, and the culture of finance follows the money rather than leading it. The definitional choice, made quietly on a first page, has therefore already selected half the explanation before the evidence is reached.

There is a further ambiguity worth isolating because it generates needless controversy. Financialisation is sometimes used as the name of an epoch, a stage of capitalism succeeding an earlier industrial or managerial stage, and sometimes as the name of a tendency operating within an unchanged structure. The epochal usage promises a periodisation and invites arguments about when the stage began and whether it has ended. The tendency usage makes the weaker and more defensible claim that certain mechanisms strengthened over a span of decades. The Marxist accounts examined here mostly require only the tendency claim, since the surplus constraint binds at every moment rather than at stage boundaries. A reader who converts each author's tendency claim into an epoch claim will find contradictions the authors never asserted, particularly around reversibility: a tendency can weaken without an epoch ending, and several writers in this tradition expect exactly such weakening whenever the underlying pressures in production change.

### Why do definitions of financialisation disagree with one another?

They disagree because they select different objects. One definition counts motives and norms inside firms, another counts profit booked through financial channels, a third counts the debts and assets of households. Each object is real, each series can move independently of the others, and arguments arise when a finding about one object is quoted as a finding about another.

The practical consequence for readers is a discipline of translation. Whenever a writer announces that financialisation rose, stalled, or reversed, the first question is which object the sentence is about. A rise in household indebtedness can coexist with a fall in the financial sector's share of profit, because households can borrow heavily from institutions whose margins are compressed. A rise in shareholder payouts can coexist with stagnant lending, because distribution to owners is not the same flow as credit creation. The literature's most cited series do not always move together, and the periods in which they diverge are analytically precious, since divergence separates mechanisms that coincidence had bundled. The three answers in this article predict different patterns of divergence, which is one reason the trichotomy earns its keep: it tells the reader which divergences would embarrass which account, long before any verdict is attempted.

It also follows that the popular image of the phenomenon, a single tide lifting every financial magnitude at once, should be distrusted. What the decades of expansion actually show, on the careful readings, is a family of related movements with a common pressure behind them and different proximate mechanics in each market and each balance sheet. Marxist theory's contribution is to name the common pressure. The pressure is the difficulty of employing surplus value profitably in production at the scale at which it is produced, together with the countervailing discovery that income can be drawn from wages already paid and from claims on income not yet produced. Whether that naming succeeds is the question the rest of the article examines, answer by answer, with the accounting objection kept in view throughout, because an explanation of a statistical shadow would be an embarrassment the tradition can ill afford and its best writers have been commendably willing to risk.

Epstein's breadth, criticised above as a weakness for measurement, has a compensating virtue for history: it registers the phenomenon's simultaneity. The decades of expansion really did transform motives, markets, actors, and institutions together, and a definition narrow enough to measure will always leave a residue of the transformation uncounted, which partisans of the narrow measure mistake for a refutation of the broad one. The mature practice of the literature is bilingual: Krippner for the magnitudes, Epstein for the map of the territory the magnitudes inhabit, and an explicit statement, in any serious work, of which language a given claim is speaking. Students should imitate the bilingualism deliberately. An essay that opens with the broad definition, narrows to the measurable claim for its evidence, and returns to the broad definition for its conclusion, with the transitions announced, displays a command of the concept that neither language alone can display, and markers reward the display because it is the visible form of the distinction between a phenomenon and its indicators.

## Marx's categories: interest-bearing capital and the division of profit

The third volume of Capital contains the theoretical machinery this whole debate runs on, and it repays slow reading, because Marx's treatment of interest is more subversive than the textbook summary suggests. Start with the elementary transaction. A money capitalist advances a sum to a functioning capitalist, who employs it in production or trade and returns it with an increment. The increment, interest, is not generated by the loan. It is a portion of the profit the functioning capitalist obtains, handed over as the price of using another's money. Gross profit therefore splits into two streams with two owners: interest, flowing to the lender, and profit of enterprise, retained by the borrower. The split is a division within surplus value, a partition of a magnitude produced elsewhere, and everything in the Marxist theory of finance is a commentary on that partition. The categories themselves are treated at length in the discussion of [marx on money and the value form](/2010/08/15/marx-on-money-and-the-value-form/), to which this article defers for the foundations and from which it borrows only what the financialisation problem requires.

Two features of the arrangement deserve emphasis because later arguments turn on them. The first is that the division is quantitative, not qualitative. Nothing in the production process marks one slice of profit as naturally belonging to the lender and another to the borrower; the proportions are settled by competition between money capital and functioning capital, by custom, and by the relative scarcity of loanable funds. Marx accordingly denies that any natural rate of interest exists. The average rate appears stable over long stretches, which tempts observers to treat it as a law of nature, yet its stability is the stability of an institutional settlement, not of a physical constant. The second feature is that the transaction doubles the capital in appearance. The same sum exists once as money in the lender's hands, present as a claim, and again as functioning capital in the borrower's enterprise, present as machinery, materials, and wages advanced. This doubling is innocent in the loan itself and becomes fateful in its descendants, because a capitalism rich in claims learns to trade the claims as though each were an additional capital, and the aggregate of paper wealth can then grow far beyond the capital actually at work.

### How does interest-bearing capital differ from money used in trade?

Money used in trade is spent and returns through the sale of commodities, disappearing into each purchase and reappearing at each sale. Interest-bearing capital is instead alienated for a period under a contract of return with an increment, so its owner parts with its use while retaining a claim, and the sum functions as capital for two parties.

The figure of the money capitalist also clarifies a persistent misunderstanding about who exploits whom. In Marx's schema the lender does not exploit the borrower in the technical sense. Both are capitalists dividing between them a surplus extracted from labour in production, and their quarrel over the division is a quarrel within the propertied classes, closer in structure to the division between industrial profit and commercial profit or between profit and rent than to the confrontation at the point of production. This is why the tradition resists describing interest as a second exploitation layered on the first, at least where the borrower is a functioning capitalist. The case changes entirely when the borrower is a worker borrowing against wages, and that change of case is the hinge on which the expropriation answer turns. Readers should register the hinge now: the same contractual form, a loan at interest, is a division of surplus value in one setting and a deduction from revenue in another, and a theory that cannot mark the difference will misclassify the entire growth of household credit.

### Who are the money capitalist and the functioning capitalist?

The money capitalist owns a sum and lends it, receiving interest as a share of profit without directing any production. The functioning capitalist borrows the sum, organises production or trade with it, and retains the profit of enterprise after interest is paid. Marx treats the pair as two personifications of one capital, dividing one surplus between them.

Interest-bearing capital reaches its developed form when lending is systematised, pooled, and professionalised, when deposits gather scattered sums into a common reservoir and the reservoir's managers allocate credit across the economy. Marx watched an early version of this system and grasped its double character. On one side, the pooling of money capital is a genuine socialisation of a function: credit economises on idle balances, accelerates turnover, and permits undertakings beyond any single fortune. On the other side, the system perfects the appearance that money breeds money without mediation, the form Marx regarded as the most fetishised in the entire economy, capital seemingly generating its increment by its own nature as a tree bears fruit. The appearance matters for the financialisation debate because a mature credit system trains everyone inside it, managers and ministers and mortgage holders alike, to read interest as a property of money rather than as a transferred share of profit. The theoretical problem of this article is invisible from inside that appearance. It becomes visible only when the partition is remembered, which is the first service the old categories perform for the new literature.

### Why is interest a portion of profit rather than a separate revenue?

Because the increment the lender receives is paid from the profit the borrower's capital obtains, and no other source exists within the circuit. The borrower's enterprise yields a gross profit grounded in surplus value; the contract assigns part of that yield to the lender. Interest is therefore a derived revenue, a claim on profit already produced, not an independent spring.

One more category completes the toolkit, and it concerns the profits of the financial firms themselves rather than of individual lenders. Marx treats the earnings of banks and kindred enterprises under the heading of commercial capital: their operations, though necessary to circulation, do not themselves produce surplus value, and their profit is a deduction from the total surplus, obtained because the functioning capitalists surrender part of their profit in exchange for services that cheapen circulation overall. The modern financial corporation, with its trading desks and advisory arms and asset management fees, fits awkwardly but recognisably into this frame. Its income is real income, its employees are really employed, its offices really occupied; the framework's claim is not that the activity is illusory but that its revenue is a transfer, a share of a surplus produced in the sectors where labour is set to work on materials and machines. That claim, generalised, is the surplus constraint in embryo. The next section takes up the category that makes the constraint hardest to hold in the imagination, the category of claims that circulate as capital while being, in strict accounting, titles to revenue that has not yet been produced by anyone.

A subtlety in the division of gross profit rewards attention, because it governs the politics of the rate of interest wherever that politics appears. Since the division between the interest share and the profit of enterprise is quantitative, a rise in the share taken by interest does not require any change in production at all; it requires only a shift in how scarce loanable funds are relative to demand, or in the bargaining position of lenders. Whole epochs of complaint by industrialists against the terms of credit, and of complaint by lenders against the profligacy of borrowers, are the sound of that partition being renegotiated, and the framework's counsel is to hear such complaints as distributional evidence rather than as analysis. The rate of interest, on this view, is the most political price in the economy precisely because it is the least anchored: no cost of production disciplines it, no labour embodied in it can be computed, and its level at any moment expresses the balance between the owners of money and the organisers of production, mediated by the state whose own borrowing anchors the scale. Financialisation altered that balance structurally by enlarging the class of pure claimants and by giving the functioning firm's own managers a personal stake in the claimants' side of the partition, and the alteration, more than any single flow, is what the phrase the dominance of finance means when the literature uses it with precision.

## Fictitious capital: claims that circulate as wealth

Fictitious capital is the category readers most often meet first and understand last, because its name invites a moral reading that Marx did not intend. In the third volume the term denotes claims on future surplus value that circulate as though they were capital in their own right. A government bond is the cleanest instance. The state has spent the borrowed sum, perhaps long ago, on purposes that produced nothing saleable; the capital, considered as value set to work, no longer exists. What persists is a title entitling its holder to a stream of payments from taxation, and that title is bought and sold at a price formed by capitalising the expected stream at the going rate of interest. The title is real as property and fictitious as capital: it commands revenue, it can be accumulated, pledged, inherited, and lost, yet it corresponds to no functioning capital anywhere in the economy. Shares present the subtler case. A share in an enterprise corresponds to real capital at work in the firm's premises, but the share's price is again a capitalisation of expected earnings, and the two magnitudes, the value of the installed capital and the market valuation of the claims upon it, move by different logics and can diverge without limit in either direction for long periods.

The mechanics of capitalisation deserve a paragraph of their own, since they explain how financial wealth can multiply while productive capacity stands still. Any regular revenue, divided by the going rate of interest, yields a notional capital sum. A payment stream of a given size is therefore worth twice as much on paper when the rate halves, though nothing in the world of production has changed at all. Every participant in the market experiences the revaluation as enrichment, portfolios swell, collateral expands, and borrowing against the swollen values feels prudent. From the standpoint of the total economy, however, the same future surplus has simply been priced higher. The claims have appreciated; the surplus they claim has not been produced twice. Here, in a single formula, is the analytical core of the Marxist suspicion of financial expansion: the system of capitalised claims possesses an internal principle of growth that is entirely disconnected from the growth of the underlying revenue, and its growth therefore cannot be read, without further argument, as growth of profit in the sense the theory cares about.

### Does fictitious capital refer to fraud or forgery?

It does not. The term names a legal and routine form, not a deception. A bond or a share is fictitious capital because its price is a capitalisation of expected future revenue rather than the value of capital actually functioning, and the form arises from ordinary credit and joint stock arrangements without any dishonesty on anyone's part.

Rudolf Hilferding built the first systematic Marxist account of modern finance on these foundations in Finance Capital, published in 1910, and his book remains the ancestor of every later treatment. Hilferding analysed the joint stock company, the promoter's profit earned by floating enterprises whose capitalised earnings exceeded their installed capital, and the growing interpenetration of banking and industry. Two of his insights survive every subsequent revision. The first is that the share form separates ownership from function so completely that the owner becomes a pure recipient of revenue, a money capitalist in generalised form, whose relation to production is exhausted by the dividend. The second is that fictitious capital is not a excrescence on the system but one of its normal products: wherever earnings can be capitalised, a paper double of capital comes into existence and acquires interests of its own. Later writers who treat the paper economy as a recent corruption of an earlier honest industrial order are, on this point, less Marxist than Hilferding, for whom the doubling was inscribed in the credit system from its maturity onward.

### How can a claim be traded as though it were capital itself?

Because the claim yields a regular revenue, and any regular revenue can be capitalised at the prevailing rate of interest to yield a price. Once priced, the claim is bought and sold, pledged as security, and counted in balance sheets, so it performs the social functions of capital for its owner while remaining, for the economy, a title to revenue.

The divergence between paper wealth and functioning capital has a temporal structure that matters for crisis theory, though crisis theory belongs to a companion article, the [marxist crisis theory guide](/2013/10/01/marxist-crisis-theory-guide/), and is only touched here. Claims are priced on expectations of future surplus, and expectations can be revised downward in an afternoon, while the capital installed in production can be written down only through the slower processes of bankruptcy, obsolescence, and physical decay. Financial wealth is therefore capable of a speed of contraction that productive wealth is not, and the history of credit systems is punctuated by episodes in which the paper structure collapsed toward the revenue actually available to service it. Marx's own remarks on these episodes stress that the collapse destroys claims, not the underlying capacity to produce, which is why the aftermath of a financial collapse can feature idle factories alongside ruined portfolios: the factories were never the fictitious part. For the financialisation literature the point generalises into a warning about measurement. If a large and growing share of recorded wealth and income consists of capitalised claims, then aggregates built from market prices will register expansions and contractions that have no counterpart in the production of surplus value, and any account of the period must decide, indicator by indicator, how much of the movement is in the claims and how much in the claimed.

There is a distributional edge to the category as well. Holders of fictitious capital receive revenue without any necessary connection to the fortunes of the particular production from which the revenue ultimately derives, and the class of pure revenue recipients, rentiers in the older vocabulary, acquires a political weight disproportionate to its numbers whenever paper wealth concentrates. The rentier's interest in the stability of claims, in low inflation of the currency the claims are written in, and in the priority of creditors in every settlement, becomes a standing pressure on economic policy in the generic sense, a pressure this article notes as a mechanism without naming any policy, in keeping with the discipline of its brief. The connection between fictitious capital and rentier income also explains why the boundary between interest and rent, examined in the [rent versus profit debate](/2021/02/15/rent-versus-profit-debate/), blurs in financialised conditions: a capitalised claim on ground rent, on a royalty, or on a future wage all present the same face to the market, the face of a revenue with a price, whatever the revenue's source in production or in transfer.

Hilferding's analysis contains one further element that the modern literature has recovered with profit: his account of promoter's profit, the gain realised when an enterprise is floated as a company. The promoter gathers a going concern whose earnings can be projected, capitalises those earnings at the going rate to obtain the share capital's nominal value, and sells the shares at that value, pocketing the difference between the capitalised worth of the earnings and the capital actually installed in the enterprise. The gain is fictitious capital in the act of manufacture, a revenue drawn from the difference between a flow and its capitalisation, and Hilferding saw that the joint stock form made the manufacture systematic: every successful flotation minted paper wealth in excess of the capital at work, and the excess accrued to whoever controlled the conversion. The modern descendants of the promoter, the sponsors who assemble enterprises for listing and the intermediaries who price the conversion, occupy the same position in the circuit, and their incomes, among the most spectacular of the financialised period, submit to the same tracing: they are paid from the difference between what future earnings are expected to be and what the assets cost, a difference that exists only as a claim on surplus not yet produced. No passage in the classical literature better deserves the student's labour, since it shows the category of fictitious capital not as a description of paper but as an analysis of a business, with premises, personnel, and a margin.

## The surplus constraint: the problem every account must answer

Now the problem can be stated exactly, and its exactness is the article's chief offering. Within Marx's framework, profit in the aggregate is surplus value in its transformed and distributed form. Interest is a portion of profit assigned to lenders. The earnings of financial firms are drawn from the total surplus in exchange for the work of circulation. Dividends are profit distributed to owners. Fees charged to enterprises are paid from their revenues and ultimately from the same fund. It follows that the financial sector, considered as a whole, cannot add to the total of profit by its own operations. Its income is a share, and a share can grow in only three ways. The share can grow at the expense of the other shares, which is redistribution. The total can grow because surplus value is being produced on a larger scale or extracted at a higher rate in production, in which case finance is a beneficiary of a change located elsewhere. Or income can be drawn from a fund that is not profit at all, from the revenue of workers or of the state, in which case something genuinely new has entered the analysis and requires its own category. A fourth possibility stands apart from the three: the measured share can grow because the accounting conventions that assign income to sectors have shifted, in which case the phenomenon is partly an artefact and the theory is being asked to explain a mirage.

### Can financialisation create profit?

not within Marx's framework. Interest and financial income are shares of surplus value produced in commodity production, so a rise in financial profit must be a redistribution from productive capital, an extraction from a source outside production, or an artefact of how profits are measured and attributed.

That disjunction, redistribution or extraction or artefact, is the surplus constraint, the namable claim around which this article is built. Its power is organisational before it is anything else. A literature that presents itself as a crowd of positions, stagnationists and expropriation theorists and sceptics of the statistics talking past one another in different journals, resolves under the constraint into a set of answers to one question, each answer defined by which branch of the disjunction it takes and each therefore commensurable with the others. Disagreements that looked temperamental become disagreements about identifiable magnitudes: how much of the measured rise survives the accounting corrections, how much income genuinely flows from wages to lenders, how much is a reshuffling among capitals. The constraint also disciplines defenders of the framework, because it forbids the lazy formulation in which finance is said to have become more profitable full stop, as though profitability were a property a sector could secrete. Anyone writing inside the tradition must complete the sentence: more profitable out of what fund, at whose expense, measured how. Writers who cannot complete it have not yet joined the argument.

### Why must every Marxist account answer the same constraint?

Because the framework permits no other source of profit. If all financial income is a share of surplus value, an extraction from revenue, or a statistical artefact, then any description of financial growth is implicitly a claim about which of those three it is, and making the claim explicit is what turns description into theory.

The constraint has a corollary that is frequently missed and that cuts against a popular rhetoric. If financial income is a share, then the growth of the financial share is not, by itself, evidence that the economy's centre of gravity has moved from production to finance in the sense of value creation. Production remains, on the theory, the sole site where the fund originates, however small its measured share of employment or however large the towers of the financial districts become. The image of an economy that has stopped making things and lives by trading claims is, within the framework, a description of appearances that the theory exists to correct. What can change, and change profoundly, is the distribution of the fund, the bargaining power attached to controlling its flows, the exposure of households to its demands, and the stability of a structure in which claims multiply faster than the revenue that must service them. Marxist writers who keep the corollary in view write about power and fragility. Those who lose it drift toward a moralised contrast between productive virtue and financial vice that owes more to older radical traditions than to Marx, for whom the functioning capitalist's profit was no more earned, in the moral sense, than the lender's interest.

A second corollary concerns the rate of profit. Financial expansion can raise the return on equity for individual capitals without raising the aggregate rate of profit on capital advanced in production, because leverage divides a given profit among a smaller base of owners' funds. Reported returns in a financialised economy therefore flatter the underlying accumulation, and a literature that tracks returns on equity will perceive dynamism where the aggregate accounts show stagnation. This divergence is not a detail. It is one of the mechanisms by which the first answer, the stagnationist answer, explains the subjective experience of the period: the decades of financial expansion felt prosperous to the owners of assets precisely because claims were appreciating and returns on equity were buoyant, while the production of surplus value relative to the capital stock grew slowly or not at all. The constraint thus generates a prediction about perception, that asset holders and wage earners would inhabit different experienced economies, and the prediction is among the more quietly confirmed features of the period the literature describes.

It is worth pausing on what the constraint is not. It is not an empirical finding that could be overturned by a striking statistic, and it is not a definition smuggled in to win an argument. It is a consequence of the labour theory of value applied consistently to revenues, and it stands or falls with that theory's account of where new value originates. Critics of the framework are free to reject the premise; several schools do, treating financial services as productive of value in the same sense as transport or storage, and within those schools the problem of this article simply dissolves. The constraint's interest lies inside the tradition and at its border. Inside, it organises. At the border, it identifies the exact point at which a critic must press: show that a financial activity creates value rather than transferring it, and the trichotomy collapses. The verdict defeater stated later in this article is the empirical cousin of that theoretical challenge, framed in terms the tradition's own researchers can test.

One more distinction completes the statement of the problem, the distinction between the level of financial profit and its share. The constraint governs shares and sources, not levels. Total financial income can rise absolutely for decades while its share of total profit stagnates, if production expands alongside. Conversely the share can rise while absolute financial income falls, in a contraction that hits productive profit harder. Much journalistic writing confuses the two, treating any large number attached to finance as evidence for the thesis. The literature at its best is careful here, and Krippner's share based definition was designed precisely to enforce the care. When the evidence section of this article speaks of rises, it speaks of shares and ratios sustained over decades, never of headline magnitudes, and it names the economies and the measures involved, because the constraint makes the composition of the total, rather than the size of any part, the theoretically meaningful object.

The constraint's border with rival frameworks can be drawn at one more place, the treatment of money itself. In the classical presentation, money is the form in which value appears, and credit money, the promises to pay that banks create in lending, is a superstructure on the value relations beneath. Some later writers in the tradition have argued that the superstructure has acquired an autonomy the founders did not foresee, that a system in which credit is created at the stroke of a pen and extinguished in repayment generates its own dynamics of expansion and collapse, irreducible to the production of surplus in any given period. The argument is serious and the article records it as the framework's live frontier rather than as a heresy. Even here, however, the constraint reasserts itself at the level of settlement: credit created from nothing must still be serviced from something, and the revenues from which the system's obligations are ultimately met are the profits of enterprises and the incomes of households, so that the autonomy of credit governs the timing and the violence of the reckoning without altering the identity of the fund that reckons. A theory of the superstructure's dynamics and a theory of the fund's origin are, once again, complements misfiled as rivals, and the reader who can hold the filing straight has understood the present state of Marxist monetary theory as well as this survey can teach it.

## The first answer: redistribution under stagnation

The oldest and most developed answer in the tradition holds that financial expansion is what surplus value does when production will not have it. The lineage runs through the theory of monopoly capital constructed by Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, whose joint work of 1966 argued that mature capitalism generates a rising surplus it cannot absorb. Absorption was their organising problem: the system's normal outlets, consumption by capitalists, investment in new capacity, and wasteful expenditure of various kinds, chronically lagged behind the surplus seeking placement, and the shortfall expressed itself as stagnation, idle capacity, and idle funds. The later writers of the same school, John Bellamy Foster and Fred Magdoff prominent among them, extended the argument to the financial sphere and gave the extension a name that states the whole theory in four words: the stagnation and financialisation thesis, elaborated in their work of 2009 and in the journal literature around it. On this account the turn to finance is not a departure from the stagnation problem but one of its solutions, temporary and self undermining: surplus that cannot be profitably reinvested in expanding production is placed in financial assets, and the returns on those assets are claims on a surplus whose production the placement did nothing to enlarge.

The mechanism deserves spelling out, because redistribution can sound like a bookkeeping triviality when it is in fact a claim about the dynamics of the whole system. Consider the position of a large corporation whose markets are growing slowly and whose existing plant already meets demand. Its profits arrive regardless, since market power protects its margins, but reinvesting those profits in additional capacity would worsen the overcapacity that threatens its prices. The corporation therefore returns funds to shareholders, accumulates financial assets, acquires other firms, or lends. Each use converts profit that might have pressed on production into a financial placement. Multiply the corporation across an economy of concentrated industries and the aggregate result is a swelling demand for financial assets, rising prices for those assets, expanding balance sheets among intermediaries, and a growing share of total profit booked in financial form. Nothing in the sequence requires anyone to have chosen financialisation as a project. The outcome is the sum of defensive decisions by firms confronting the same arithmetic of surplus and outlets, which is why this answer pairs naturally with an account of the period's subjective texture: an economy can be simultaneously stagnant in its factories and euphoric in its markets, and the euphoria is not a refutation of the stagnation but one of its products.

### What happens to surplus that cannot be reinvested productively?

On the absorption account it seeks placement in claims. Firms distribute it to owners, hold it as financial assets, buy other companies, or lend it, and intermediaries multiply instruments to receive it. The placement earns returns, but those returns are shares of surplus produced elsewhere, so financial growth records a blockage in production rather than an escape from one.

The redistribution answer draws support from the shape of the period it describes, always remembering that this article states directions and durations rather than magnitudes. The decades of financial expansion were not decades of rapid growth in the advanced economies; by the standards of the postwar generation they were slow, and the slowness lengthened as the expansion proceeded. Investment in plant and equipment, relative to profits, trended downward across the same span, while distributions to shareholders and purchases by firms of their own equity trended upward. The conjunction is exactly what the absorption mechanism predicts: profit and placement parting company, the fund growing relative to the outlets in production and pooling instead in claims. Writers in this tradition, among them Engelbert Stockhammer in his analyses of the period from 2004 onward, add a feedback loop that tightens the trap. As shareholder oriented governance spreads, managers are pressed to prioritise distributions and asset prices over long horizon investment, so financialisation, once begun as a symptom, becomes a cause of the very stagnation that produced it, a cumulative causation the school regards as the period's signature dynamic.

### Is financial profit on the absorption account newly created wealth?

No. It is profit produced in commodity production and redirected. The lender, the fund, and the trading firm receive shares of a surplus whose size their activities did not determine. Asset price gains add a further layer of appearance, since capitalisation can raise the paper value of claims while the underlying flow of surplus follows its own slower path.

The strength of the redistribution answer is its unity: a single mechanism accounts for slow growth, rising financial shares, and the peculiar coexistence of corporate plenty with public austerity of investment. Its difficulties are equally definite, and the tradition's internal critics have stated them well. The first difficulty is chronological looseness. Stagnation, in some form, can be found in many decades, including decades when finance remained modest, so the mechanism needs a supplementary account of why the surplus pooled in claims during one era and in other outlets, armaments or advertising or suburbanisation, during others. The school's historians answer with the specific exhaustion of the postwar outlets, and the answer is plausible, but it imports a contingency the pure theory does not supply. The second difficulty is that the account treats financial profit as entirely parasitic on a surplus whose production it leaves unexamined in the financial period itself. If the rate of exploitation rose during the same decades, through the lengthening or intensification of work or the global relocation of production toward lower wage zones, then part of finance's growing share rode on a genuinely growing fund, and the redistribution was from labour to capital in the first instance rather than from productive capital to finance in the second. The school's best writers concede the point and incorporate rising exploitation as a companion tendency; the concession matters because it shows the three answers are not sealed compartments, a theme the comparison section develops.

A third difficulty is the one the accounting answer will press hardest: the absorption story was built partly on the same profit share statistics whose construction the sceptics question, and if those statistics overstate the shift, the stagnationist is explaining a magnitude that partly does not exist. The school's defence is to rest its case on the conjunction of several indicators rather than on any single series, on investment weakness and distribution strength and debt growth moving together over decades, a pattern no single accounting convention manufactures. That defence is methodological, and it is the right shape for the dispute: the argument is about whether a syndrome is real, and syndromes are established by the company their symptoms keep.

Giovanni Arrighi's historical work, above all The Long Twentieth Century of 1994, gives the redistribution answer a longer arc and a different emotional register. Arrighi discerned a recurring pattern across centuries of capitalist development: periods of material expansion, in which capital is committed to trade and production, give way when competition compresses returns to periods of financial expansion, in which capital retreats into liquidity and claims. Each systemic cycle, in his telling, ends in a financial autumn that is simultaneously a sign of maturity, a harvest for the centres that organised the preceding expansion, and a prelude to reorganisation under new leadership. Financialisation, on this telling, is neither novel nor a policy error; it is the recurrent late phase of an accumulation regime, and its profits are the rents of position drawn from a world economy whose productive frontier has moved elsewhere. The Arrighian variant matters for the trichotomy because it shows the redistribution answer operating at the scale of centuries rather than business cycles, and because it predicts the phenomenon's recurrence under any institutional settlement, a prediction the policy oriented versions of the literature conspicuously avoid.

The absorption account's treatment of waste deserves extraction, because it connects the financialisation thesis to the tradition's oldest anxieties about the system's rationality. Baran and Sweezy catalogued the sales effort, the vast apparatus of advertising and product differentiation, among the absorbers of the surplus, since it employed labour and capital to redistribute demand among sellers rather than to enlarge the product. Finance, in the later extension, is the sales effort's successor at a higher level: an apparatus that redistributes claims upon the product rather than the product's buyers, maintained from the fund at a cost that grows with the fund's own difficulty of placement. The parallel is more than rhetorical. Both apparatuses are, in the framework's technical sense, unproductive employments that the system finds indispensable, since without the sales effort the surplus cannot be realised and without the financial apparatus it cannot be placed, and both therefore grow, not despite the stagnation they testify to, but as the form the stagnation takes when it is successfully managed. A capitalism that must spend an increasing share of its surplus on the machinery of realising and placing that surplus is exhibiting, in the tradition's judgement, the specific irrationality of a mature system: technically capable of abundance, organisationally committed to the manufacture of scarcity and of claims, and stable precisely insofar as the commitment holds. The judgement is a verdict about the whole, delivered by a theory of the parts, and it is the deepest stratum of the first answer's appeal to its adherents.

## The historical variant: financial expansion as class strategy

A fourth position runs alongside the first answer and is compatible with it, differing in what it adds rather than in what it denies. Where the absorption account explains financial expansion by the arithmetic of surplus and outlets, the class strategy account explains it by agency: a capitalist class, facing a squeeze on its income and authority during the profitability difficulties of the 1970s, recomposed itself around finance and thereby restored both. The most systematic statement is the work of Gérard Duménil and Dominique Lévy, whose analyses from the 1990s onward and whose volume The Crisis of Neoliberalism of 2011 read the entire period as a social order constructed by and for the highest income strata, with finance as its commanding institution and the recovery of upper class income as its measurable achievement. On their telling the alliance between financial institutions and the managerial strata of large firms is the period's ruling bloc, and the famous growth of financial profit is the fiscal record of that bloc's victory.

The analytical gain of the variant is that it explains features the absorption mechanism leaves opaque. Stagnation arithmetic does not by itself explain why the costs of adjustment fell so asymmetrically on wages, why the wage share declined across decades in economy after economy, or why the institutions of collective bargaining weakened in the same period in which shareholder institutions strengthened. A class strategy account connects those facts: they are what a successful offensive looks like in the statistics. The variant also restores politics to a literature that can sound hydraulic, as though surplus flowed into finance the way water finds a crack. Duménil and Lévy insist that channels are dug, that legal frameworks, governance norms, and the internal hierarchies of firms were rebuilt by identifiable actors pursuing income and power, and that the rebuilding could have gone otherwise under a different balance of forces. For readers of this series the insistence will be familiar from the treatment of [marxism and modern capitalism](/2020/11/01/marxism-and-modern-capitalism/), the hub article for the cluster, where the period's order is likewise presented as made rather than merely undergone.

### How does the class strategy account differ from the absorption account?

It differs in the role it assigns to agency. The absorption account derives financial expansion from the difficulty of placing surplus in production, a pressure that operates whoever governs. The class strategy account accepts that pressure and adds a subject: an upper stratum that reorganised firms, markets, and rules around finance in order to restore its income and authority.

The variant has its own exposure to criticism, and honesty requires naming it. A strategy implies a strategist, and the documentary record of the period shows improvisation, conflict among capitals, and unintended consequences at least as clearly as it shows design. Financial firms and industrial firms fought over the division of profit throughout the expansion; managers enriched themselves in ways owners repeatedly tried and failed to discipline; the architecture that emerged was nobody's blueprint in full. Defenders of the variant reply that class strategy in the Marxist tradition has never meant a committee with minutes. It means the patterned outcome of many actors with aligned interests exploiting a conjuncture, intelligible as strategy in retrospect because the alignment was real. The reply is respectable, and it clarifies what the variant claims: not conspiracy but convergence, the systematic selection, among the responses a crisis made available, of those that raised the income share and authority of the top strata. So understood, the variant is less a rival to the absorption answer than its political completion, supplying the who for the mechanism's how, and the two are best read as one answer with two storeys.

There is a measurement consequence buried in the class strategy account that deserves extraction, because it illustrates how theoretical choice steers empirical work. If the period's essence is the restoration of upper class income, then the decisive series are distributional in a personal sense: the share of the highest incomes, the composition of those incomes as between salaries, dividends, interest, and capital gains, and the interlocking of the managerial and financial strata who receive them. Duménil and Lévy's empirical practice follows exactly that logic, tracking income strata where the absorption school tracks sectoral profit shares. The two literatures can therefore both be right about their own series while appearing to dispute the period's character, one pointing to the fund's placement problem, the other to the victors' recovered share. The surplus constraint adjudicates nothing between them, since both describe redistributions, and the article's verdict section will treat them as a combined first answer whose defeater must be aimed at redistribution as such. What the variant adds to the combined answer is a prediction the pure mechanism lacks: that the order built around finance would defend itself politically with the tenacity of an income stream, and that any reversal of financialisation would therefore be a distributional conflict and not a technical adjustment. The prediction has worn well enough to count as one of the literature's genuine successes, and it is stated here, in keeping with the article's discipline, as a mechanism about interests rather than as commentary on any government's conduct.

The class strategy account gains credibility from a comparison it invites and its rivals avoid: the comparison of the period's settlement with the settlement it replaced, read as a distribution of security as well as of income. The order that the recomposition displaced had socialised a remarkable range of risks and rewards: employment was comparatively secure, provision for old age was substantially collective, credit was rationed and comparatively cheap for housing, and the highest incomes were compressed by the fiscal and institutional arrangements of the postwar decades. The financialised order reprivatised the risks, decompressed the incomes, and converted security itself into a portfolio of claims whose value each household must monitor. Duménil and Lévy's insistence that the second order was constructed, by actors who knew their interests, is at its most persuasive in this comparative register, because the two orders differ too systematically, across too many independent institutions, for the difference to be the residue of accidents. Systems of provision do not reprivatise themselves in a dozen domains at once by drift; they are carried, and the carriers leave the evidence of their carrying in the distributional statistics the account began from. The article's compound verdict absorbs the point by treating the class strategy as the first answer's political storey, and the absorption is principled rather than diplomatic: the mechanism explains why surplus sought placement, the strategy explains who arranged the placement's terms and pocketed its rents, and a complete history of the period requires both storeys in that order.

## The second answer: financial expropriation

The second answer is the boldest of the three, because it alone claims that financialisation found money in a place the classical framework did not look for it: in the wages of workers, taken not at the point of production but in the sphere of circulation, through the ordinary contracts of borrowing, housing, insurance, and retirement provision. Its author is Costas Lapavitsas, whose papers from 2009 onward and whose book Profiting Without Producing of 2013 gave the position its canonical form and its name, financial expropriation. The argument begins from an observation about the period's institutional transformation. As employers withdrew from the direct provision of housing finance, pensions, and insurance, and as public provision contracted or stagnated, workers were driven to meet the same needs as customers of financial institutions, borrowing for homes and education, paying into managed funds for old age, and purchasing cover against illness and accident. Finance thereby acquired a direct relation to labour, no longer mediated by the functioning capitalist. Banks and funds confronted the worker as an individual revenue holder, and the interest, fees, and margins extracted in that confrontation constitute, for Lapavitsas, a distinct form of profit, drawn from revenue rather than from surplus value produced in the lender's own enterprise.

The theoretical stakes are precise and should not be blurred. In the classical schema, the worker's wage is revenue, spent on means of subsistence, and capital's claims on the worker end at the factory gate, the surplus having been extracted inside production. Interest paid by a worker out of wages is, in the older vocabulary, a secondary exploitation, a deduction that lowers the real wage without entering the production of surplus value itself. Lapavitsas's move is to argue that the scale and systematisation of the deduction in the financialised period justify promoting it from an incidental category to a structural one. When access to housing, education, and retirement security is routinely mediated by interest bearing contracts, the deduction is no longer a misfortune befalling individual improvident workers; it is a regular channel through which a portion of the social wage is transferred to financial capital, organised by institutions that cultivate the channel as a line of business. The expropriation is financial because its instrument is the loan and the account rather than the working day, and it is expropriation rather than exploitation because what is taken was already the worker's revenue, not value newly produced under the taker's direction.

### What is financial expropriation in the strict sense?

It is the systematic extraction of income from workers by financial institutions outside production, through interest on their borrowing, fees on their accounts and funds, and margins on the insurance and pension provision they must buy as individuals. Lapavitsas distinguishes it from exploitation because the source is the worker's revenue, the wage already paid, not surplus created in an enterprise.

### Does expropriation replace exploitation or stand beside it?

It stands beside it. The wage relation in production continues to generate surplus value on the classical account, and nothing in the expropriation thesis revises that mechanism. The claim is additive: a second channel of transfer has grown alongside the first, reaching the worker as borrower and purchaser of provision, and the two channels together describe the worker's relation to capital.

The evidence marshalled for the second answer differs in kind from the evidence for the first, and the difference is diagnostic. Where the absorption school cites profit shares and investment ratios, the expropriation literature cites the balance sheets of households: the rise of household debt relative to income across the advanced economies over the decades of expansion, the spread of mortgage indebtedness down the income distribution, the growth of borrowing for education and for consumption smoothing, and the conversion of retirement provision from defined promises by employers into individual accounts bearing market risk and management charges. These series have a robustness the profit share series lack, a point the accounting section will develop, because a household's debt is a contractual fact recorded in loan books, not a classification decision in a national accounts office. A reader persuaded by the accounting objection to distrust the headline story of financial profit can still see, in the debt series, a transformation of the worker's economic life that demands theoretical registration. That is the second answer's stronghold, and its critics have generally attacked its theoretical labelling rather than its facts.

### Where does household borrowing enter the expropriation account?

At its centre. The growth of lending to households, for housing above all and increasingly for education and consumption, is the channel through which finance reaches the wage directly. Each contract converts part of future wages into an interest stream, and the aggregate makes banking income partly a levy on labour income rather than a share of industrial profit.

The criticisms of the expropriation answer are instructive and come from inside the tradition as much as from outside it. The first is conceptual: by naming a transfer of revenue a form of profit, the account risks dissolving the framework's central distinction between the production of surplus value, and its distribution. If every deduction from anyone's income counts as profit creation for its recipient, then the grocer and the landlord also create profit out of wages, and the category loses the precision that made the surplus constraint possible. Lapavitsas's defence is that the financial case is distinguished by its form: the loan creates a fictitious capital, the worker's future wage is capitalised and traded, and the extraction is conducted by the credit system as a system, with the worker's dependence engineered by the withdrawal of collective provision. The defence identifies real differentiae, though whether they justify the promotion to a distinct source of profit, rather than a distinct mechanism of transfer, remains the live question. The second criticism is quantitative in spirit: even granting the channel's existence, its magnitude relative to the surplus produced in production may be modest, in which case expropriation is a significant fact about workers' lives but a minor fact about the aggregate profit that the trichotomy exists to explain. The third criticism observes that interest paid by workers is partly a return of their own deposits and pension savings through the circuit, since the funds lent are gathered substantially from workers' deferred wages, so the net transfer is smaller than the gross flows suggest, and the class geometry is more tangled than a simple levy implies: workers as savers receive a portion of what workers as borrowers pay, minus the considerable margin the intermediaries retain.

None of these criticisms restores the comfort of ignoring the phenomenon. The withdrawal of collective and employer provision, and the consequent exposure of households to financial markets as individuals, is among the best documented social transformations of the period, and a Marxism that registered it only as a footnote to the profit share would be failing its own standard of concreteness. The measured verdict this article will reach treats expropriation as a real channel of transfer, theoretically misdescribed if it is presented as a new source of value, and quantitatively open: its weight in the aggregate is an empirical question the literature has posed better than it has answered. That verdict already illustrates the surplus constraint doing its work, since the disagreement between Lapavitsas and his critics is not about whether households were drawn into finance, which nobody serious denies, but about which branch of the disjunction the drawing in belongs to: a new source, or a redistribution whose payer is the wage rather than industrial profit. The trichotomy survives the dispute because the dispute is conducted in its terms.

One refinement of the expropriation thesis, present in its author's later statements and worth the reader's attention, concerns the role of the state as the channel's silent partner. The withdrawal of collective provision that drives households into financial contracts is rarely a simple disappearance; it is more often a conversion, in which a public or employer obligation is replaced by a subsidised private one, the subsidy flowing through tax advantages for individual accounts and borrowing. The conversion matters analytically because it routes public revenue into the channel: the state pays part of the cost of the household's financialisation, and the intermediaries' margins are thereby underwritten from taxation as well as from wages. The expropriation account, refined in this way, describes a three party circuit in which the worker's future income is committed to finance with the state's assistance, and the refinement strengthens the account's institutional realism at the price of complicating its slogan. It also supplies a further testable implication: the channel should be deepest where the conversion of provision was most complete and most subsidised, a cross national prediction that the comparative literature has begun to examine with results broadly favourable to the account. A thesis that generates predictions about institutional variation, and survives their examination, is earning its promotion from mechanism to research programme, whatever the final verdict on its central label.

## The third answer: an artefact of attribution

The third answer is the uncomfortable one, because it is aimed at the evidence the other two answers share. It holds that the apparent shift toward finance is substantially an accounting phenomenon: a large share of the financial income recorded for non-financial corporations arises from internal treasury operations and from the attribution of profits across jurisdictions and subsidiaries, so the measured rise in financial profit overstates, perhaps greatly, the structural change it is taken to prove. The argument was developed most forcefully by James Crotty in his analyses of the non-financial corporate sector from 2005 onward, and kindred cautions appear in the work of Özgür Orhangazi, whose studies of non-financial firms' financial activities in 2008 combined the measurement warning with a real mechanism, arguing that financial placements and payouts crowded out physical investment even after the accounting exaggerations were discounted. The sceptical answer therefore comes in a strong form, the shift is largely a mirage, and a moderate form, the shift is real but smaller than the headlines, and the moderate form commands the wider assent.

Understand the mechanism of the mirage, because it is concrete rather than statistical in the abstract. A large corporation is not a single productive unit with a bank account; it is a group, holding subsidiaries that manufacture, subsidiaries that sell, and subsidiaries whose entire business is finance: captive lending arms that fund customers' purchases, treasury companies that borrow and lend for the whole group, holding entities in jurisdictions chosen for their treatment of income. The profits of the group's financial subsidiaries are financial income in the accounts. Yet from the standpoint of the consolidated enterprise, much of that income is the group's own profit wearing a financial costume: the lending arm's margin is part of the price of selling the group's products, the treasury company's earnings are the group's borrowing costs rearranged, and income booked where it is taxed lightly is production profit that has travelled. When such groups grow more elaborate, as they did across the decades in question, the accounts register a rising tide of financial income inside the non-financial sector even if the underlying activities of the groups change hardly at all. The sector boundary, which the statistics must draw somewhere, is drawn through the middle of unified capitals, and the drawing manufactures the trend.

### How can reported financial income rise without any structural change?

When a corporate group reorganises where its income is booked. If a manufacturer creates a lending subsidiary to finance its customers, the margin on those loans appears as financial income, though the group's business is unchanged. If profits are attributed to holding entities in lightly taxed locations, the recorded geography and sector of profit shift while production stays put.

### What do treasury operations inside large firms do to the statistics?

They convert the internal finance of a group into measured financial activity. Funds borrowed by a treasury subsidiary and lent onward within the group, interest flowing between affiliates, and earnings retained in financial subsidiaries all enter the accounts as financial income and assets, inflating the apparent weight of finance relative to a consolidated view of the same enterprises.

The artefact answer draws strength from a second measurement difficulty, the treatment of the financial sector's own output. National accounts impute a value to banking services that are paid for through interest margins rather than explicit charges, and the imputation conventions have changed over time and differ between statistical systems. A portion of the financial sector's measured growth in some economies and some decades is a creature of those conventions: revise the imputation and the sector shrinks on paper without a single desk closing. Researchers who work closely with the accounts, Krippner among them in her methodological asides, have flagged the sensitivity of the headline shares to such choices. The sceptical conclusion is not that nothing happened; the towers, the trading volumes, and the household debts are not imputations. The conclusion is that the single most quoted magnitude in the debate, the financial share of profit, is among the least trustworthy, and that a theory built to explain its precise trajectory risks the fate of astronomy before the telescope corrections: an elaborate machinery fitted to an artefact of the instrument.

The artefact answer must itself be kept honest, and its honest limits are three. First, an artefact of sector attribution is not an artefact of existence: the income was earned by someone, and relocating it from the financial column to the industrial column changes the story about distribution among capitals without touching the aggregate of profit or its origin in surplus value. The surplus constraint is indifferent to the correction, since both columns are shares of the same fund; what the correction damages is the narrative of a great sectoral migration, not the framework. Second, several series central to the broader phenomenon do not pass through the contested conventions at all. Household debt is recorded in credit statistics, employment in finance is counted in persons, the market capitalisation of listed firms is observed in prices, and the share of income accruing as interest and dividends to households is visible in distributional accounts. A scepticism that dissolves the profit share leaves these standing, and they describe a transformation that still requires explanation. Third, the direction of the accounting bias is not eternally fixed. Conventions that flattered financial income in one era can penalise it in another, particularly when loss recognition rules tighten, so the artefact argument must be re-derived period by period rather than wielded as a universal solvent.

What the third answer permanently changes is the standard of proof. After Crotty and Orhangazi, no Marxist writer can responsibly cite a profit share without stating the treatment of non-financial corporations' financial income, the imputation conventions in play, and the sensitivity of the conclusion to both. The literature's centre of gravity shifted accordingly, toward indicators with cleaner provenance and toward consolidated views of corporate groups. That shift is a sign of health. A research programme that can be embarrassed by its own measurement specialists into better evidence is behaving as a science should, and the episode deserves recording in any assessment of the tradition's current state: the accounting objection was raised by researchers sympathetic to the broader project, published in its journals, and absorbed into its practice. The reader will meet the objection again in this article at full strength, as the brief for the article requires, before the verdict attempts to say how much of the phenomenon survives it.

The artefact account has an implication for the other two that its authors state less often than its logic warrants, and fairness requires the article to state it for them. If the measured financialisation of profit is substantially a migration of bookkeeping, then the stagnation school's chronology is compromised at its foundation, since the school's evidence for the timing of the great placement, the decades in which surplus allegedly flooded into claims, rests on the same series the consolidation deflates. The school's reply, that its case rests on the syndrome rather than the series, is sound as far as it goes, but it concedes the sceptics' methodological point in the act of answering it: the profit share can no longer be quoted as the thesis's headline, and the thesis must be rebuilt, as its careful exponents have rebuilt it, on investment weakness, distribution strength, and the household series. Intellectual honesty of that order, the rebuilding of a position on narrower evidence at a critic's demand, is rarer in the social sciences than the financialisation literature's civility makes it appear, and the episode should be taught as the field's exemplary controversy for that reason alone: a research programme confronted by an internal measurement critique, absorbing the critique, and emerging with a smaller claim and a stronger warrant. The artefact row of the table is therefore not the trichotomy's sceptical garnish. It is the discipline under which the other two rows now operate, and any future statement of either that ignores it can be dated, by that ignorance, to the literature's youth.

## The three answers table

The three answers table is the article's findable artifact, the compressed form in which the argument can be carried away, taught, and tested. Read it row by row. Each row is a complete theory in miniature: a claim about where financial profit comes from, a consequence for the total of surplus value, an evidence signature by which the account could be recognised in the data, and the difficulty its critics press. The fourth row records the historical variant, which shares the first row's answer about sources and adds its claim about agency, and is included because a table of the literature that omitted Arrighi and the class strategy school would misdescribe the field's actual population.

| Answer | Where the profit comes from | Implication for total surplus value | Evidence that would support it | Principal difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Redistribution under stagnation | Surplus produced in commodity production, redirected from productive capital to financial placements | Total unchanged by finance itself; growth of the financial share mirrors blockage in production | Weak investment beside strong distributions, slow growth across the decades of expansion | Stagnation appears in decades without financial expansion, so the mechanism requires a supplementary history |
| Financial expropriation | Workers' revenue, extracted through lending, fees, and the individual purchase of provision | A channel of transfer outside production adds to finance's take without adding to surplus value | Household debt rising relative to income, borrowing spread across the income distribution | The transfer may be modest beside the surplus produced in production, and its labelling as profit is contested |
| Accounting artefact | Nowhere new; the income was always industrial profit, recorded in financial form by group structures | Total unchanged and the measured shift itself shrinks under consolidated accounting | Financial income of non-financial groups concentrated in treasury and lending subsidiaries | Several series, household debt and financial employment among them, do not depend on the contested conventions |
| Class strategy variant | Surplus redistributed as in the first answer, under the direction of a recomposed upper stratum | Total unchanged by finance itself; the distribution among persons shifts sharply toward the top | Recovery of the highest income shares and the interlocking of financial and managerial elites | Strategy implies design, while the record shows improvisation and conflict among capitals as well |

A table disciplines its readers in ways prose cannot. It forbids the blending of rows. Much commentary on financialisation proceeds by taking the pathos of the second row, the evidence of the first, and the scepticism of the third, and stirring them into a general lament about the power of finance; the table refuses the stir, because each row's evidence signature is different and a finding that confirms one row may disconfirm another. If household debt stagnates while financial profit shares climb, the expropriation row weakens and the redistribution row strengthens. If consolidated accounting erases most of the profit shift while debt climbs, the artefact row and the expropriation row divide the field between them. The table also exposes the empty cell problem in the literature: positions that cannot fill the fourth column, that cannot name their own principal difficulty, are usually slogans rather than accounts, and students should learn to ask for the fourth column of any theory they are offered, in this field and beyond it.

### By what principle does the three answers table arrange its rows?

By source. Each row must declare where its financial profit originates, in surplus redirected from production, in wages intercepted by contract, or in the recording practices of corporate groups, and the variant row adds the agency behind the first. Declared sources yield distinct predictions, so the arrangement converts a crowd of positions into a set of testable claims.

Comparisons among the rows should be made at the level of mechanisms, not of political flavour. The redistribution and expropriation rows agree that something real grew; they disagree about the payer, productive capital in one case and the wage in the other, and the disagreement is in principle measurable by tracing the income statements of financial firms back to their counterparties, asking how much of the sector's margin is earned from enterprises and how much from households. The artefact row disagrees with both about the size of the thing to be explained and is testable by consolidation exercises of exactly the kind its authors performed. The variant disagrees with the first row only about the weight of intention, a disagreement historical rather than statistical in character, settled if at all by archives and memoirs rather than by accounts. A literature whose disputes sort so cleanly into an accounting question, a tracing question, and a historical question is a literature in good order, whatever its conclusions, and the sorting is the surplus constraint's doing: by forcing every position to declare a source, the constraint assigns each dispute to the tribunal competent to settle it.

One further use of the table deserves mention, since teachers report it as the artifact's classroom virtue. Set the table before a seminar with the fourth column covered, and ask each participant to supply the difficulty they would press against the row they most favour. The exercise reverses the usual politics of the seminar room, in which difficulties are ammunition reserved for opponents, and it reproduces the actual ethic of the literature at its best, in which Crotty's scepticism was a service performed by a friend of the project and Lapavitsas's boldest claim was sharpened by critics who shared his premises. A framework that can organise its own refutations is not thereby proven; it is thereby shown to be a framework rather than a mood, which is the prior question on which everything else in this article depends.

The table has one deliberate omission that a careful reader should notice and approve. It contains no row for the claim that financialisation is a conspiracy of bankers, a fraud perpetrated on an otherwise sound economy, or a fall from a golden age of honest industry, because none of those claims is held by any author the literature counts as a contributor, and a table of the field's answers must not dignify the journalism's inventions with rows of their own. The omission is itself a finding about the field: for all its internal quarrels, the Marxist literature on finance is unanimous that the phenomenon is structural, produced by the normal operation of the categories the founders supplied, and explicable without recourse to the wickedness of any generation of financiers. That unanimity distinguishes the literature from the popular discourse that borrows its vocabulary, and it is the reason the article has been able to organise the field by a theorem rather than by a temper. Readers who meet, in the wild, an account of financialisation that requires villains should apply the table as a test: ask which row the account occupies, and watch it fail to occupy any, since villainy is not a source of profit and the table admits nothing that is not.

## Evidence by direction and duration

The evidence for financialisation is a syndrome, not a statistic, and this section describes it under the discipline the brief imposes: directions and durations, the economies and measures named, magnitudes withheld. Four families of indicators carry the case. The first is the share of profit booked by the financial sector in the national accounts of several advanced economies, which rose from the early 1980s across roughly three decades, with interruptions at moments of financial distress, before levelling in the years after the great contraction of credit at the end of that span. The second is the financial income of non-financial corporations, the series Krippner's definition privileges, which rose as a share of those corporations' receipts across the same decades in the economy she studied most closely and in others where the exercise has been repeated. The third is the indebtedness of households relative to their incomes, which climbed across nearly all advanced economies over the same period, driven first by housing debt and later by borrowing for education and consumption. The fourth is the wage share of national income, treated in full in the companion piece on [labour share evidence](/2021/03/01/labour-share-evidence/), which declined across the period in most advanced economies, a decline the class strategy account claims as its signature and the other accounts must accommodate.

Comparison across these families requires the care the comparison basis demands. Financialisation indicators differ substantially by economy and by definition, because the organisation of housing finance, pension provision, and corporate structure differs, and those organisational differences are themselves part of the phenomenon. An economy in which retirement provision runs through individual accounts will show financial penetration of household life that an economy of public pensions conceals, though the second economy's workers may be no less dependent on financial returns through their funds' holdings. An economy of bank based corporate finance will show a different sectoral shape from an economy of market based finance, while the underlying pressures, surplus seeking placement and households seeking provision, operate in both. The literature's comparative work, Stockhammer's cross economy studies and van Treeck's analyses prominent among them, therefore proceeds by relating each economy's indicators to its own institutions rather than by ranking raw levels, and the article follows that practice: the claim is that within most advanced economies, across the decades under discussion, the four families moved in the directions stated, a claim about a family resemblance among national trajectories rather than about a single world magnitude.

Duration matters as much as direction, because the surplus constraint turns the evidence into a question about sources, and sources reveal themselves over time. A redistribution driven by stagnation should deepen as the underlying blockage persists and should relax if profitable outlets in production reopen. An expropriation channel should grow with the stock of household contracts and should be comparatively insensitive to the business cycle, since the debts are long and the provision is compulsory in effect. An artefact should track the organisational fashions of corporate groups, rising as groups financialise their internal structures and falling when tax or regulatory settlements change the incentives to book income in financial form. The observed syndrome, decades long, comparatively steady through cycles, and visible in household series that no accounting fashion governs, fits a compound of the first two mechanisms better than either alone, with the third inflating the headline magnitudes by an amount the specialists continue to contest. That compound judgement is the article's eventual verdict in embryo, and it is stated here as the evidence section's finding so that the objection section can try to break it.

A word about what the evidence cannot do, since overclaiming is the besetting sin of this literature's popularisers. The syndrome establishes that financial claims, financial incomes, and financial relations with households grew in weight across a long period. It does not by itself establish the direction of causation between financial growth and the stagnation of production, and both directions have serious defenders: the absorption school reads finance as stagnation's symptom that became its accomplice, while a rival reading, represented in different idioms by Thomas Palley's work from 2007 onward, treats the financialised growth model as a regime that generated its own demand for a time, through asset appreciation and debt financed consumption, before exhausting itself. Nor does the syndrome establish that the growth of finance lowered the growth of production by the full amount of its own expansion, a zero sum inference the constraint does not license: the constraint governs the origin of profit, not the allocation of investment, and it is entirely consistent with the framework that credit expansion simultaneously redistributed profit and, for a period, sustained demand that stagnation would otherwise have lost. Keeping those separations clean, between origin and allocation, between share and level, between symptom and cause, is most of what distinguishes the literature's strong contributions from its journalism, and the reader who carries the separations away has the article's method even where they reject its conclusions.

The evidence also has a geography of absences that honest exposition should record. The syndrome is documented overwhelmingly for the advanced economies, and the concept's export to economies at other income levels has proceeded unevenly, with some researchers finding the mechanisms transformed by dependence on foreign capital flows and others questioning whether the term names anything there beyond the ordinary operations of underdeveloped credit systems. Within the advanced group, the timing differs by economy in ways the literature maps onto institutional inheritances: housing finance systems, pension settlements, and the legal treatment of shareholder rights all left fingerprints on the national trajectories. Those differences are not noise around a universal signal; they are the means by which mechanisms can be identified, since a mechanism that predicts identical outcomes under different institutions is unfalsifiable, while one that predicts the observed pattern of divergence, strong household channels where provision was privatised, strong corporate channels where governance was shareholder oriented, earns its generality by surviving the comparisons.

A further evidentiary refinement has entered the literature through the study of income from wealth at the level of the household, and it bears on the class strategy row with unusual directness. Distributional accounts that separate the population by its sources of income show the period's signature in cross section: at the summit, income arrives predominantly as property income and as the salaries of the claims industry and the executive suite; in the broad middle, as wages supplemented, and increasingly encumbered, by the household's financial contracts; at the base, as wages and transfers against which the costs of basic financial access, the fees for accounts, payments, and small borrowing, levy a proportionately heavier toll than any other group bears. The cross section confirms what the time series suggested, that the financialised order distributes its costs and its revenues along the gradient of wealth with a consistency no accidental process produces, and it supplies the expropriation account with its most granular evidence, since the toll on the poorest households' transactions is a deduction from revenue in the purest form the category admits, unmediated by any employer's profit or any asset's appreciation. Poverty, in a financialised order, is itself financialised: the less a household has, the more of what it has the circuit takes for the mere conduct of its economic life.

## The accounting objection at full strength

The brief for this article requires the strongest objection to be stated at full strength before it is answered, and the objection is the accounting one, pressed here as its authors would press it. Consider what the headline claim asserts: that the centre of profit making shifted from production toward finance across the decades of expansion. Now consider the instrument. The claim rests on accounts that assign the income of complex corporate groups to sectors according to the legal form of the receiving entity. A group that builds vehicles and lends the purchase price to its customers through a captive subsidiary books the lending margin as financial income. A group that centralises its borrowing in a treasury company and on lends to its own divisions manufactures internal interest flows that the statistics record as financial activity. A group that parks its intellectual property in a holding entity in a lightly taxed location, and charges its operating companies for the use of their own designs, converts production profit into royalty and interest income attributed elsewhere. Multiply these arrangements by the general elaboration of group structures across the period, driven by tax competition among jurisdictions and by the professionalisation of corporate finance departments, and the accounts will report a financialisation of profit even in a world where the same goods are made in the same plants by the same workers throughout. The objector concludes that the literature has theorised a bookkeeping migration, that the measured growth of financial profit substantially records changes in corporate structure and tax planning rather than changes in where profit originates, and that the grand narratives of stagnation and expropriation are explanations of a statistical shadow, impressive in proportion to their distance from the accounts' construction.

### Which indicators survive the accounting objection intact?

The contractual and headcount series survive. Household debt is recorded in loan contracts rather than imputed, financial employment is counted in persons, and household debts cannot be reclassified by a group's internal structure. The profit share series, by contrast, must be discounted by the consolidation exercises, which is why careful versions of the thesis rest on the syndrome.

The answer begins by conceding the objection's centre. The headline profit share evidence is substantially affected by the practices described; the consolidation exercises performed by the sceptics themselves show a smaller structural shift than the raw accounts announce, and any honest statement of the redistribution thesis must carry the discount. Conceding that, the answer then asks what remains standing after the discount is applied in full, and the residue is not nothing. The household series remain: debts contracted by persons, at levels relative to income that earlier decades did not approach, serviced out of wages under contracts whose terms the expropriation literature has examined in detail. The employment series remain: a larger share of the workforce works in financial and closely allied activities than did before the expansion, and those workers' wages are paid from revenues that the framework must source somewhere. The distributional series remain: income in the form of interest, dividends, and capital gains occupies a larger place in the receipts of the highest strata. And the corporate behaviour that the objection treats as pure bookkeeping is not behaviourally inert: a group that has built a lending arm acquires interests in credit conditions, a treasury organised for tax attribution acquires interests in the jurisdictions that host it, and the organisational facts harden into political ones whatever their accounting origin. The artefact, in short, is partly self fulfilling; structures adopted for recording become structures that act.

The deeper answer to the objection is methodological, and it returns to the surplus constraint for the last time in this connection. The constraint never depended on the profit share. It is a theorem about sources, derivable from the categories without any statistics at all, and the trichotomy it generates classifies explanations rather than certifying magnitudes. The accounting objection, at its strongest, revises the size of the phenomenon the first two answers must explain; it does not touch the classification, and its own positive claim, that the income was industrial profit in financial dress, is itself a redistribution claim wearing sceptical dress, since it asserts that the fund's origin never moved. The objector and the stagnationist therefore agree on the framework's central point, that finance originates nothing, and disagree about the geography of the shares. That is a genuine and important disagreement, and it is exactly the kind a mature literature should be having: about measurement, within a shared theory of what the measurements mean. The verdict this article reaches can now be previewed in one sentence. Financialisation names a real compound transformation, of distribution among capitals, of households' relation to credit, and of corporate organisation, whose headline profit magnitudes are inflated by accounting practice, and whose explanation requires the first two answers in combination, discounted by the third.

There is a temptation, having answered an objection at length, to declare it disposed of, and the temptation should be resisted in print as firmly as in thought. The accounting objection retains a live remnant that future work must honour: for any particular economy and any particular decade, the burden of proof lies on whoever cites a profit share to show the consolidation sensitivity of the number, and where that showing has not been made, the correct scholarly posture is suspension rather than citation. The literature's most cited figures predate the objection and circulate still in textbooks and lectures without their health warning, a lag between research front and received summary that this article exists partly to correct. Readers who take one practical rule from the whole discussion might take that one: never repeat a financial share without its provenance, and treat any account of the period that does so as unrevised, whatever its other merits.

The objection's afterlife in the wider culture deserves a note, because it illustrates how a scholarly correction degrades in transmission. In the hands of commentators hostile to the whole literature, the accounting objection is routinely inflated into the claim that financialisation itself was invented by academics, a mirage of the seminar room dispelled by a glance at the accounts. The inflation reverses the objection's own content. Crotty's exercises were performed to measure a phenomenon their author believed in, by methods he published so that believers would measure it better, and the distance between that intention and the journalistic use is the distance between a research programme and its caricature. The article has insisted on stating the objection at full strength precisely so that its actual strength, considerable and insufficient, can be seen in one view: considerable, because the headline magnitudes do not survive it intact; insufficient, because the syndrome's contractual core does. Readers who hold both halves of that judgement simultaneously will be proof against the two corruptions of the debate, the credulity that quotes the headline and the cynicism that quotes the objection, each of which is a way of not reading the literature the article has surveyed.

## The corporation under shareholder value

One strand of the literature, the second in van der Zwan's sorting, locates financialisation inside the large corporation itself, in a change of governing norm. The doctrine that a firm exists to maximise the value of its shares, and that managers should be paid in instruments whose worth rises and falls with that value, reorganised the interior life of enterprises across the period. William Lazonick's work, including his widely cited analysis of 2014 on profits without prosperity, documented the accompanying shift in the disposition of corporate funds: resources that an earlier managerial norm retained for investment and for the security of employees were distributed instead to shareholders, through dividends and through purchases by companies of their own shares, on a scale that made distribution, in many large firms, the dominant use of profit. The Marxist reading of the shift connects it to the categories already established. A manager remunerated in equity becomes, in part, a money capitalist lodged inside the functioning firm, his income a capitalised claim on the firm's future earnings; the boundary Marx drew between the two personifications of capital, already formal in the loan contract, dissolves inside the executive suite, and the firm's own decisions begin to be taken from the standpoint of the claim rather than of the production the claim is on.

The consequences traced by this strand are concrete. Investment horizons shorten, because the claim's price capitalises near earnings more heavily than distant ones. Employment relations harden, because labour costs are the most compressible charge against the earnings the price reflects. Corporate treasuries swell into profit centres, feeding the accounting phenomenon examined earlier from a behavioural root: the treasury subsidiary is not only a tax device but the organisational expression of a firm that now regards its financial operations as a business in themselves. And the firm's relation to its own future changes character, as the capacity to meet obligations from retained earnings gives way to a dependence on continuous access to markets for funds, a dependence that transmits every tremor in those markets directly into decisions about plant, product, and payroll. The strand's contribution to the trichotomy is to supply the redistribution answer with its microeconomic machinery. The absorption school described surplus pooling in finance at the level of the whole economy; the shareholder value literature shows the pooling being performed, firm by firm, by identifiable governance mechanisms, and a mechanism that can be watched at work in boardrooms is harder to dismiss as a statistical shadow than one inferred from aggregates alone.

Critics of the shareholder value strand raise two cautions that a balanced account should register. The first is that distribution to shareholders is not, within the framework, a leakage from accumulation in any simple sense, since the distributed funds return to the capital market and may fund other enterprises; the objection from the standpoint of the economy as a whole is not that the money vanishes but that the circuit lengthens, the claims multiply, and the portion of the social surplus consumed in the maintenance of the circuit itself, in fees and margins and the incomes of its personnel, grows with the lengthening. The second caution is chronological: the practices the strand documents were themselves responses to pressures, including the profitability difficulties the class strategy account describes and the rise of institutional holders of equity whose demand for distributions organised the managers' incentives. Governance, on this reading, is a transmission belt rather than a prime mover. Both cautions are compatible with the strand's evidence, and both illustrate the article's recurring lesson that the literature's positions are layers of one explanation more often than they are rivals, with rivalry concentrated at the specific points the three answers table marks: the source of the fund, the size of the shift, and the weight of design against pressure.

One more development inside the firm completes the shareholder value picture: the rise of the treasury and the internal bank as a career and a power base. In the managerial firm of the quiet decades, finance was a service department that raised funds for decisions taken elsewhere. In the financialised firm the treasury became a profit centre with its own targets, its own dealing operations, and its own claim on the group's strategy, and the chief financial officer rose from bookkeeper of the enterprise to co author of its direction. The categories register the change exactly. A department that exists to cheapen circulation is a cost to be minimised; a department that returns a profit is a claimant to be fed, and its profit, traced through the constraint, is the group's own surplus booked at the point of its most financial presentation, together with whatever the treasury's external dealings extract from counterparties. The internal bank is thus the accounting objection's mechanism and the shareholder value mechanism fused in a single office: it manufactures financial income by structure while genuinely altering the firm's behaviour, since a group whose treasury must earn will hold its liquidity in tradable claims, time its payments for advantage, and organise its subsidiaries for the attribution of income, in ways a group with a service treasury would not. Behaviour follows the booking, which is why the article's answer to the artefact objection insisted that arrangements made for the ledger end up governing conduct.

## Households: the financialisation of daily life

The third strand in van der Zwan's sorting follows finance into the household, and its literature, associated with writers such as Randy Martin, whose Financialization of Daily Life appeared in 2002, and with a generation of sociologists after him, studies the remaking of ordinary economic conduct. Saving for old age becomes the management of an investment portfolio exposed to market movements. Housing becomes simultaneously shelter and the household's principal leveraged asset, its price a matter of daily attention. Education becomes an investment in the self, financed by borrowing against future earnings. Insurance, credit scoring, and the continuous assessment of personal riskworthiness organise access to the necessities of life. The strand's Marxist significance is that it describes, at the level of lived experience, the subjective side of the expropriation channel and of the wage's changing position. The worker of the classical texts met capital in the labour market and at the point of production; the worker of the financialised period meets it additionally at the bank counter, in the pension statement, and in the monthly schedule of payments that claims a fixed portion of the wage before any consumption begins.

Two analytical points emerge from the strand that the aggregate literature needs. The first concerns risk. The older settlements of the advanced economies placed several large risks, of unemployment, illness, and old age, substantially on collective shoulders, through employers and public provision. The financialised settlement reprivatises those risks onto household balance sheets, where they are borne with the aid of financial products whose costs and failure modes fall on the buyer. The transfer of risk is a distributional fact independent of the profit share controversy, and it changes the meaning of the wage even where the wage's size is unchanged: a wage that must self insure is a smaller effective wage than one paid under collective cover, a deduction that appears in no employer's accounts and that the expropriation account is right to insist on registering, whether or not its theoretical label prevails. The second point concerns discipline. A household carrying large fixed obligations experiences the labour market differently from an unencumbered one; the need to service payments on schedule raises the cost of job loss, of industrial action, and of any interruption of earnings, and thereby alters the balance of forces in the wage bargain without any employer needing to intend the alteration. Several writers in the tradition have drawn the conclusion that indebtedness functions as a quiet technology of labour discipline in the financialised period, complementing the older disciplines of the reserve army and the factory regime, and the conclusion connects this cluster's concerns to the treatment of the [reserve army of labour](/2010/12/15/reserve-army-of-labour-explained/) elsewhere in the series.

The household strand also corrects a temptation in the other two answers, the temptation to treat the period's subjects as passive material on which structures work. The ethnographic and survey literatures show households strategising within their constraints: timing borrowing against expected earnings, pooling risks across extended families, treating housing equity as a private welfare state to be drawn on in emergencies, and developing vernacular financial literacies of considerable sophistication. None of that agency dissolves the structural facts; a chess player is not less constrained by the board for playing well. But it changes the political prognosis the literature draws. A population that experiences its security as invested in asset values acquires interests in the defence of those values, and the constituencies for policies that protect wages and those that protect asset prices can pull apart even within the same households, a division inside the working class's material interests that any serious strategy must reckon with. The class strategy variant of the first answer predicted the defence of the financial order by its beneficiaries at the top; the household literature extends the prediction downward, in attenuated form, and thereby explains part of the order's durability better than either the absorption mechanism or the expropriation channel can do alone.

The household literature has a quantitative spine that deserves the same prominence as its ethnography, and it is the changing composition of household obligations across the life course. In the settlement of the quiet decades, a household's encounters with finance were episodic: a mortgage taken in youth and discharged by middle age, a hire purchase contract for a durable good, a pension accumulated invisibly by an employer's scheme. In the financialised settlement the encounters are continuous and cumulative. Borrowing for housing extends later into life and is repeatedly refinanced; borrowing for education loads the beginning of working life with obligations that earlier cohorts met from public funds; the pension is a portfolio whose management is a standing household task; insurance against the interruptions of earnings is purchased product by product; and the credit record, that continuous assessment of the household's reliability as a payer, becomes a condition of access to housing, employment screening, and ordinary services. The cumulative character matters for the expropriation debate's unresolved quantitative question. Each contract's deduction is small enough to be borne and regular enough to be budgeted, and the aggregate deduction, the share of the social wage committed to financial payments before consumption, is correspondingly easy to underestimate from any single contract's terms. The literature's household researchers have done the field a service by insisting on the aggregate: the relevant magnitude is not the margin on any loan but the portion of a lifetime's wages that the contract structure, taken whole, assigns to the financial sector, and on that magnitude the expropriation channel's claim to structural significance ultimately rests.

## Interest, rent, and the boundaries of the claim

A reader who has followed the argument this far may wonder why the tradition insists so strenuously on the derivative character of financial income, when landlords' rent, which is equally derivative, attracts less theoretical anxiety. The comparison is instructive and is pursued in depth in the companion treatment of the rent versus profit debate; here its outline suffices. Rent, in Marx's analysis, is a portion of surplus value surrendered to the owner of a monopolised condition of production, land above all. Interest is a portion surrendered to the owner of money. Both are deductions, both presuppose production elsewhere, and neither troubled the framework's founders as a source of value. The anxiety attaching to finance is of a different origin: scale and self reference. Rent is bounded by the scarcity of the asset; financial claims are bounded by nothing internal, since each generation of claims can serve as collateral for the next, and the capitalisation formula converts every tremor in the rate of interest into a revaluation of the entire paper stock. A rentier economy grows at the pace of its rents. A financial economy can appear to grow at the pace of its own expectations, which is why the tradition's writers return to it with a fascination they do not bring to ground rent, and why the category of fictitious capital, rather than the theory of rent, carries the analytical load.

The boundary work matters practically because the period blurred the categories in the assets themselves. Housing is at once a produced commodity, a site of ground rent, a leveraged financial asset, and the collateral of the household debt stock; a single dwelling can carry all four identities through one family's accounts. Intellectual property generates income that presents as profit to its owner, behaves as rent in its dependence on legal monopoly, and is capitalised and traded as a financial claim. The literature's finer contributions refuse to assign such assets a single essence and instead trace the flows: which portion of the income is a portion of surplus produced in an enterprise, which is a scarcity payment, which is a deduction from wages, and which is the paper gain of capitalisation awaiting its test against future revenue. That tracing discipline is the surplus constraint operationalised, and it generalises beyond finance to every revenue whose form conceals its source, which in a mature capitalism is most of them.

### Why does each financial revenue have to be traced back to a source?

Because every revenue is either value produced in an enterprise or a transfer of value produced in one. Interest is assigned from profit, fees are charged against revenues, and capital gains are revaluations of claims on future profit. Tracing connects an income's form, which names its payer, to its source, which names the labour whose surplus sustains it.

There is a historical irony in the boundary disputes that the literature savours and that a long view makes vivid. The founders wrote when the joint stock company and the modern credit system were young enough to be denounced as swindles by respectable opinion, and their achievement was to treat the new forms not as frauds upon an honest capitalism but as capitalism's own logic developed: the socialisation of production advancing inside private appropriation, the claim detaching itself from the enterprise, the owner dissolving into the rentier. Financialisation, on the long reading Arrighi sponsors, is the same logic at a later stage of the same development, and the writers who announce it as a fall from an industrial Eden have mistaken a phase of the system's maturation for a deviation from its nature. The tradition's cooler heads have always known that the factory and the exchange are one organism's organs. What changes across the phases is the distribution of the surplus among the organs, the exposure of households to the claims the system issues, and the speed at which the paper superstructure can be revalued against the revenue beneath it. Those are large changes, quite large enough to justify a literature, and they do not need the assistance of a golden age that never existed.

The frontier separating interest from rent has one more province in which the period's developments outran the founders' examples: the income drawn from owning the infrastructures of circulation themselves. The marketplaces, networks, and platforms through which claims and payments move are privately owned conditions of everyone else's activity, and the charges for their use present the analyst with the rent problem in modern dress, a payment for access to a monopolised facility, capitalised into the owner's valuation and defended as a return on investment. The companion treatment of the rent versus profit debate pursues the classification in its own terms; here the point is the constraint's indifference to the label. Whether the infrastructure owner's income is booked as profit, rent, or fee, the tracing question is identical: the income is paid from the revenues of the users, which resolve into enterprise profits and household wages, and its growth is therefore a redistribution whose size measures the facility's monopoly rather than any addition to the fund. The period multiplied such facilities and such incomes, in finance above all, where the exchanges, clearing systems, data services, and index providers through which the claims economy operates charge their toll on every transaction the economy performs. A complete anatomy of financial income would reserve a line for the tolls, and the line would be found, on tracing, to be among the most stable and least cyclical of all, since it is levied on the activity of circulation itself and not on the fortunes of any party to it.

## Misreadings: five errors the literature invites

Popular discussion of financialisation recycles a handful of errors with great fidelity, and the article's brief requires them corrected in one place. The first error is to treat financialisation as a single agreed phenomenon, a thing whose rise and fall can be announced like a tide. The definitions section showed a family of objects sharing a name; every confident headline about the phenomenon should be met with the question of which object is meant, and most headlines cannot answer it. The second error is to say that finance creates value, usually on the ground that financial firms employ labour and sell services for which customers pay. Payment establishes revenue, not creation; the framework's question is whether the activity adds to the social product of value or transfers a share of it, and the entire literature examined here is the working out of that question, which cannot be settled by pointing at a payslip. The third error is to hear fictitious capital as an accusation of fraud. The category names a form, the capitalised claim, which arises from honest contracts and audited accounts; its fictitiousness is a matter of what the paper corresponds to, not of anyone's intentions, and the correction matters because the fraud reading invites the useless remedy of prosecuting individuals for a structural property.

The fourth error is to present the expropriation account as the orthodox Marxist position, the tradition's settled doctrine on finance. It is one answer among three, advanced by a named author against named critics inside the tradition, and its promotion of a revenue transfer to a source of profit is precisely what the orthodoxy, insofar as the word means anything here, disputes. Presenting it as consensus flatters it and misleads the student, who will meet its critics in the same journals and conclude wrongly that someone has been caught in an error. The fifth error is the mirror of the fourth: to read the accounting objection as a denial that anything changed, a debunking that dissolves the period into a clerical illusion. Its authors claim a discount, not a disappearance; the household debts, the employment, the reprivatised risks, and the governance revolution inside firms all survive their corrections, and the strongest version of their argument improves the thesis it discounts by forcing it onto evidence that can bear weight.

### Is finance parasitic on the rest of the economy?

The metaphor misleads more than it explains. Within the framework, financial operations are functions of circulation that produce no surplus value and are paid from it, which makes the sector derivative rather than parasitic: a parasite harms a host that would flourish without it, while circulation performed dearly is a cost the system bears for services it cannot do without.

Behind the five errors sits a sixth, too diffuse for the list and too important to omit: the assumption that the argument is really about whether finance is good or bad, so that positions can be sorted by their warmth toward bankers. The surplus constraint is not a moral instrument. It yields the same classification for a reader who admires financial ingenuity as for one who deplores it, because classification by source is prior to evaluation. Marx's own treatment models the priority: the credit system is, in his pages, simultaneously an engine of development that socialises capital and accelerates production beyond every private limit, and the purest form of the fetish in which value appears to breed from paper. Holding both judgements together is not indecision; it is what a theory of a contradictory object looks like when it is working. Readers who want a verdict on finance's moral worth will find the materials for several in this literature, arranged by the constraint into their proper relations. Readers who want the prior question answered, where the money comes from, have the article's actual subject, and it is the prior question that the tradition, at its best, has taught its students to ask first of every impressive revenue, in finance and far beyond it.

## Why finance grew: the proximate drivers

The three answers explain the growth of finance considered at the level of the surplus and its distribution, but readers reasonably ask for the proximate drivers, the mechanisms at street level through which the expansion was actually transacted. Four recur across the literature, and each connects cleanly to the constraint. The first is the growth of institutional pools of savings. As provision for old age was channelled into funded schemes, vast sums accumulated under professional management, and the managers' demand for tradable claims became a structural feature of markets: a standing bid for paper, supplied by the deferred wages of millions, which raised the capitalised value of revenue streams and rewarded the manufacture of new ones. The second driver is the internationalisation of balance sheets. Firms and funds came to hold claims across borders as a matter of routine, multiplying the layers of intermediation between any act of production and the revenues drawn from it, and lengthening the chains along which fees and margins are charged. The third is the transformation of banking from a business of holding loans to a business of originating and distributing them, so that the same underlying borrowing supports repeated earnings as it passes along a chain of arrangers, holders, insurers, and servicers. The fourth is computational: the cheapening of calculation made it possible to price, slice, and monitor claims of a granularity that paper era finance could not have administered, and the supply of administrable claims expanded to meet the new capacity.

None of these drivers competes with the three answers; they are the answers' plumbing. Institutional pools are the form in which workers' deferred wages became the era's great stock of loanable capital, feeding both the redistribution of surplus toward claimants and the expropriation channel that charges the same workers for access to provision. Originate and distribute banking is the redistribution mechanism wearing a workflow. Computation is the enabling condition that Hilferding's generation lacked, the reason the capitalisation of revenues could extend to income streams, from student obligations to future royalties, that an earlier system could not have packaged. A useful discipline follows for readers of business journalism: whenever a driver is announced as the explanation of financial growth, ask which answer's plumbing it is. A driver that cannot be connected to a source of funds is a description of motion, not an explanation of income, and the literature's maturity can be measured by how rarely its serious contributions offer plumbing in place of sources.

A fifth driver deserves separate notice because it transformed the raw material on which all the others worked: the conversion of illiquid loans into tradable claims as a routine industrial process. In the older banking model a loan lived and died on the books of its maker, and the stock of fictitious capital grew only as fast as lenders accumulated. Once loans could be gathered, pooled, and sold onward as claims on the pool's payments, the manufacture of fictitious capital acquired an assembly line. The same underlying borrowing could support earnings at origination, at pooling, at sale, at servicing, and at each subsequent transfer, and the volume of claims in existence decoupled from the volume of new lending, since existing loans could be refinanced, repackaged, and traded repeatedly. The Marxist analysis of the assembly line is characteristic in its refusal of both available sentimentalities. Against the celebration of the process as the democratisation of credit, the framework notes that the multiplication of claims multiplies the deductions from the underlying revenue, each handler taking a margin from a flow that originates, at the end of the chain, in an enterprise's profit or a household's wage. Against the denunciation of the process as a fraud upon an honest system, the framework notes that the assembly line is the credit system's own logic perfected, the capitalisation of revenue streams extended by technique to streams previously too small or too scattered to capitalise. What the assembly line changed, in the theory's terms, is the elasticity of fictitious capital: its supply no longer waits upon the slow accumulation of loanable funds but responds elastically to the demand for claims, and an elastic supply of claims against a comparatively inelastic flow of surplus is, in the long run, the arithmetic of the repricing crises the crisis literature studies.

The drivers have a historiographical use beyond their explanatory one: they date the phenomenon's phases without recourse to the contested magnitudes. The era of the institutional pools precedes and prepares the era of the tradable loan; the era of shareholder governance reorganises the corporation while the assembly line for claims is still being built; the computational cheapening arrives last and multiplies everything that preceded it. A history of the period written along the sequence of its mechanisms has the advantage of being checkable against the records of practice, the manuals, the prospectuses, and the professional literatures in which each mechanism announced itself, and several of the field's best narratives are built exactly so. The sequence also explains the literature's own chronology. Epstein's umbrella was needed when the simultaneity of the transformation was the discovery; Krippner's measure, when the discovery required a magnitude; Lapavitsas's channel, when the household's transformation became impossible to treat as a side effect; Crotty's correction, when the magnitudes had hardened into a headline. Each contribution answered the question its moment posed, and the trichotomy, which the article has used as a logical device, is equally a chronology of the field's maturation, a sequence in which a phenomenon was named, measured, mechanised, and audited in turn.

## Pension funds and the paradox of workers' capital

No institution of the period concentrates its contradictions more neatly than the pension fund. The fund gathers the deferred wages of workers and invests them in claims on the surplus those same workers, and their successors, will produce. The worker therefore appears on both sides of the surplus constraint: as producer of the fund from which all revenues flow, and as beneficiary of revenues that are shares of that fund, minus the charges of the intermediaries who manage the passage. Peter Drucker noticed the paradox early, announcing in 1976 a pension fund socialism in which employees, through their funds, had become the effective owners of the means of production in the advanced economies. The Marxist literature's response is a model of the framework's precision. Ownership of claims is not ownership in the sense that confers control or appropriation at production's point; the funds' holdings are fictitious capital in the strict sense, capitalised titles to future surplus, and their beneficiaries' relation to production remains that of sellers of labour power whose retirement income depends on the successful extraction of surplus from the next generation of sellers. The form socialises the claim while leaving the relation untouched, and indeed binds the beneficiaries' security to the rate of exploitation of their successors, an intergenerational knot the tradition's writers regard as among the period's most consequential and least discussed structures.

The pension case also disciplines the expropriation debate from an unexpected angle. The charges levied on pension saving, management fees and the spreads of the institutions through which contributions pass, are a deduction from workers' revenue as surely as interest on their borrowing, and Lapavitsas's framework accommodates them naturally. Yet the same funds are also the lenders whose interest income the classical schema assigns from industrial profit, so a single institution stands in both columns of the trichotomy at once, extracting from wages on one side of its ledger and receiving redistributed surplus on the other. The lesson generalises: the three answers classify flows, not institutions, and any attempt to sort the period's organisations into the guilty and the innocent by answer will fail on the first balance sheet examined. What the classification sorts is the analyst's sentences. A claim that a fund's income grew is incomplete until its growth is decomposed into the part drawn from enterprise profit, the part drawn from contributors' charges, and the part that is revaluation of its claims, and the decomposition, not the institution's reputation, is where the theory lives.

The pension paradox has a final twist that the literature on intergenerational justice has begun to exploit. Because the fund's claims are on the production of the working generation, the real value of a pension is determined, at the moment of payment, by the ratio between the retired population's claims and the output available to honour them, whatever the portfolio's paper value may say. A society cannot save for its collective old age by accumulating claims in the way an individual can, since the claims are only ever titles to a future product that the future's workers must create and surrender. Financialisation obscured that arithmetic by denominating security in asset values, and the obscurity was functional: it allowed each cohort to believe its provision independent of the next cohort's productiveness and willingness to be taxed, until demography or a repricing reimposed the connection. The framework's insistence that claims are claims, on a fund that production must supply, is at this point not a piece of sectarian theory but the plainest statement of the pension problem available in any vocabulary, and the tradition is entitled to note that its categories reached the statement first because they never forgot what a claim is.

## The state inside the credit system

An account of financialisation that confined itself to private actors would omit the load bearing institution, described here under the article's discipline in generic terms only. The modern credit system rests on public foundations at three depths. At the first depth, the unit in which claims are denominated and the legal enforcement of contracts are public provisions, without which the capitalisation of future revenue would have no stable measure and no reliable compulsion behind it. At the second depth, the safety of the payments system and of deposit like claims is underwritten publicly, so that the most money like of private assets carry an implicit public guarantee whose value never appears in the sector's accounts as a cost. At the third depth, in episodes of distress the public balance sheet stands behind the paper structure directly, absorbing claims whose private valuation has collapsed and thereby setting a floor under the capitalisation of the rest. Marxist writers draw from these facts a conclusion about the nature of the financial order rather than about any administration: the order is a public private construction in which the risks of fictitious capital are socialised at the margin while its revenues remain privately appropriated, and the asymmetry is not an abuse of the system yet one of its operating principles, visible whenever the alternative, a general collapse of claims, threatens the production the claims are drawn upon.

The state also appears in the story as a borrower and therefore as a manufacturer of the safest fictitious capital, the public debt, whose interest is paid from taxation and whose stock provides the benchmark against which all other revenues are capitalised. The growth of that stock across the period, and the growth of the markets organised around it, belong to the syndrome as much as any private series, and they complicate the moral geography of the literature in a useful way. The rentier income drawn from public debt is a transfer from taxpayers, among whom workers predominate, to claim holders, among whom pension funds predominate, so the circuit once again runs from wages to claims with the state's machinery as its conduit and workers' own savings among its destinations. A theory that sorted the world into a productive people and a parasitic finance would choke on that circuit. The surplus constraint does not choke, because it never moralised the flows in the first place: it asks of the interest on public debt only the classificatory question, from which fund it is paid, and answers, from revenue raised by taxation, itself drawn from wages and profits in proportions that fiscal analysis can estimate. Classification first, evaluation after, and evaluation transformed by the classification: that sequence is the article's method in its purest form.

The public debt's role as benchmark deserves one more turn of the screw, because it locates the state inside the capitalisation formula itself. Every private revenue is priced by comparison with the return on the safest public claims, so the state's borrowing terms set the denominator by which the entire stock of fictitious capital is valued. A state that borrows cheaply thereby inflates, mechanically, the paper wealth of every holder of private claims in its jurisdiction, and a state whose borrowing costs rise deflates that wealth with the same impersonality. The tradition draws from this a conclusion about the period's political economy that requires no conspiracy and no named policy: the interests of the claim holding classes and the creditworthiness of the state are fused by arithmetic, and any government of any complexion inherits the fusion as a constraint on its freedom, since measures that raise the state's borrowing costs tax the balance sheets of its own citizens' pension funds before they tax anyone else. Analysts of the older school called a simpler version of this the confidence of the markets and treated it as a political fact; the framework restates it as a valuation fact, which is stronger, because confidence can be defied by the resolute while arithmetic cannot. The financialised state's room for manoeuvre is set in the market for its own fictitious capital, and the literature that grasps this has explained, in advance and in general terms, the characteristic disappointments of governments elected to defy it.

One classical distinction, drawn by Marx in his treatment of the credit system and too rarely quoted in this literature, organises the drivers section's material from an unexpected angle: the distinction between credit as a lever of accumulation and credit as a lever of centralisation. Credit accelerates accumulation by mobilising idle funds for enterprises that could not otherwise undertake their projects, and in that employment it is, within the framework, a progressive force that cheapens the expansion of production. Credit accelerates centralisation by placing the funds of the many at the disposal of the few who command the channels, allowing capitals to absorb one another, and fortunes to be assembled, at a pace the slow accumulation of individual profit could never match. The financialised period ran both levers at full extension, and its characteristic institutions are unintelligible unless the two are separated: the same pool of household savings that financed, at one remove, the equipment of enterprises also financed the absorption of those enterprises into groups whose treasuries then booked the profits as financial income. A literature that celebrates the first lever and deplores the second, or the reverse, has mistaken a single mechanism's two effects for two mechanisms, and the founders' distinction, restored, is the correction. The surplus constraint tells the analyst where the income comes from; the two levers tell the analyst what the credit that carries it is doing to the structure of ownership while it travels.

## Claims across borders: the international dimension

Financialisation in the advanced economies was never a purely domestic rearrangement, and the literature's international strand, in which the work of François Chesnais from the 1990s onward is foundational, traces the claims as they cross borders. Chesnais described a world regime of accumulation dominated by finance, in which the liberalisation of capital movements allowed surplus gathered anywhere to seek placement everywhere, and in which the owners of claims acquired a structural power over governments and firms alike, exercised through the simple capacity to withdraw. The Marxist analysis of that power runs through the constraint as usual. A claim held across a border is a title to surplus produced under another jurisdiction's labour relations, and the global stock of such titles grew across the period far faster than the production beneath it, multiplying the layers of intermediation and the occasions for fees. The periphery of the system experienced the arrangement asymmetrically: economies that borrowed in currencies they did not issue carried the risk of the capitalisation process in reverse, the sudden repricing of their obligations when expectations turned, while the centres issued the safe assets the world's savings demanded and earned the intermediation income on the traffic in both directions.

Two consequences follow that the domestic literature understates. The first is that the redistribution answer acquires a geography. Surplus produced in one economy and claimed in another is redistribution at world scale, and the stagnation of the centres and the expansion of their claims are compatible, on Arrighi's long view, with rapid production growth at the frontier to which industry migrated: the fund grew globally even where it stagnated locally, and finance in the old centres lived by intermediating the claims on the new production. The second consequence concerns the expropriation channel, which also crosses borders when households in one economy borrow from institutions owned in another, or when the pension savings of one workforce are placed in the claims of foreign enterprises. The trichotomy travels without modification, because it classifies by source rather than by place, and that portability is among the stronger arguments for the constraint as the literature's organising device: a framework that must be rebuilt for each scale is a description, while one that survives the change of scale is a theory.

The international circuit has a further chamber that the domestic accounts render almost invisible: the market in which states and public agencies of surplus economies place their accumulated reserves. Economies that ran persistent export surpluses across the period accumulated claims on the rest of the world, and those claims were held overwhelmingly in the safest and most liquid form available, the obligations of the principal borrowing states and the deposits of the great financial centres. The arrangement completed a global circuit of remarkable elegance and remarkable asymmetry. Production expanded most rapidly in the surplus economies; the surplus value realised there was converted into claims on the deficit economies; those claims financed the consumption and the asset markets of the centres; and the centres' financial sectors collected the intermediation income from the whole rotation. Marxist writers in the international strand describe the circuit as the world form of the redistribution answer, since the centres' financial expansion was sustained by a fund produced substantially elsewhere, and the description has the additional virtue of explaining a fact that purely domestic stagnation theories handle awkwardly, namely that the centres' financial sectors boomed through decades in which the centres' own production grew slowly while world production grew respectably. The fund was global even where the blockage was local, and finance, alone among the period's industries, was organised from the start at the scale of the fund.

The asymmetry carried the usual signature of fictitious capital at world scale. The claims were denominated in the centres' currencies and governed by the centres' law, so the risk of the arrangement settled on the holders least able to bear it: a repricing of the centres' obligations, or a depreciation of their currencies, would transfer wealth from the accumulating periphery to the issuing centre automatically, without any act of expropriation that a treaty could name. The literature notes the arrangement's stability as well as its injustice, since every party's balance sheet depended on its continuation, the periphery's reserves being worthless if the centre's market for them closed, and the centre's consumption being unaffordable if the periphery's accumulation stopped. Interdependence of that depth is the world economy's version of the household's mortgage: a discipline on all parties, experienced by each as a private necessity and by the analyst as a structure. When the tradition's writers claim that financialisation names a regime rather than a fashion, it is circuits of this completeness, binding production in one hemisphere to claims in another through the balance sheets of states themselves, that they have principally in mind.

The international circuit's stability had a domestic price that the literature on the centres records and the trichotomy classifies without difficulty. An economy whose financial sector intermediates the world's claims, and whose currency denominates them, acquires a structural incentive to keep its asset markets deep, its claims honoured, and its households borrowing, since the whole arrangement rests on the centre's obligations remaining the world's preferred asset. The centre's industry, competing against imports financed by the same circuit, bears the corresponding cost, and the hollowing of the centre's production alongside the swelling of its claims is, on the international reading, one process with two faces rather than a coincidence of decline and boom. Arrighi's long view gives the process its historical rhyme: the financial autumn of each successive centre has coincided with the material spring of its successor, and the claims the autumn accumulates are claims on the spring's production. Whether the rhyme governs the present case is a question the evidence of one generation cannot answer, and the article leaves it, as it has left the other great questions its framework frames but does not close, in the keeping of the readers the personas now address.

## Leverage, equity returns, and the flattering of the rate of profit

Return to the corollary flagged when the constraint was first stated, because it rewards a fuller treatment. The rate of profit that governs accumulation in Marx's theory is the ratio of surplus value to the total capital advanced in production. The returns that financialised economies celebrate are typically ratios of profit to equity, the owners' slice of the funds an enterprise employs, and the two ratios diverge systematically when enterprises operate with borrowed money. Leverage divides a given flow of profit by a narrower base of owners' funds and thereby raises the measured return, at the price of raising the fragility of the structure, since the borrowed funds must be serviced in bad years as in good. A corporate sector can therefore exhibit rising returns on equity across decades in which the underlying rate of profit on the capital actually advanced is flat or falling, and the divergence is not a fraud but an arithmetic property of the denominator. The literature that tracks equity returns will report a vigorous capitalism; the literature that reconstructs the aggregate rate, in the tradition of the debate treated in the companion article on the [falling rate of profit debate](/2013/11/15/falling-rate-of-profit-debate/), reports a hesitant one. Both are describing the same decades through different denominators, and the financialisation literature's stagnation wing is, at this point, simply the falling rate literature viewed from the side of the claims.

The point has a further twist that the best writers in the field exploit. Leverage is itself a financial product, manufactured by the same sector whose growth is under examination, so the flattering of returns is endogenous to the phenomenon: finance expands partly by selling enterprises the means to report higher returns without producing more surplus, and the reported returns then testify, in a circle, to the dynamism of the financial age. Breaking the circle requires the analyst to hold the aggregate denominator steady, valuing capital at what is advanced in production rather than at what the claims upon it fetch, and that methodological decision, taken quietly in a footnote, decides whether the period appears as renaissance or as stagnation before a single fact is gathered. Students meeting the literature for the first time should be taught to find the footnote. In this field the choice of denominator is the choice of verdict in embryo, and the three answers table's second column, the implication for total surplus value, is precisely the instrument for detecting which choice an author has made.

The denominator problem has a pedagogical consequence that teachers of the subject report with feeling. Textbooks in the mainstream of economics present the period's corporate returns, its asset prices, and its financial innovations as the record of a dynamic sector meeting new needs, and a student who has absorbed the presentation meets the Marxist literature as a perverse refusal of the obvious. The refusal dissolves once the denominators are exhibited side by side: return on equity against return on capital advanced, market valuation against replacement cost, financial income as booked against financial income on a consolidated basis. Each pair shows the same magnitudes wearing the aspect of dynamism or of stagnation according to the measure chosen, and the student's question changes, as the article has hoped throughout, from which side is lying to which measure answers which question. For the question the surplus constraint poses, where the fund originates and how its division changed, the consolidated and aggregate measures are the pertinent ones, and on those measures the period's financial expansion appears as the literature describes it: a redistribution of a slowly growing fund toward claims, claimants, and the households' own future wages, conducted at historically unprecedented scale and recorded, with systematic flattery, by the accounts of the claims themselves.

## Productive and unproductive labour in the financial office

Behind the surplus constraint stands a classification that the tradition argues about with undiminished energy: the distinction between productive and unproductive labour. In Marx's usage the terms carry no moral charge and no implication about usefulness. Labour is productive, in the specific sense, when it is exchanged against capital and produces surplus value; it is unproductive, in the same narrow sense, when it is exchanged against revenue and produces none, however socially indispensable it may be. The nurse and the teacher may be unproductive in the technical sense while the maker of luxuries is productive, and the classification scandalises only those who import the moral sense the theory explicitly refuses. Financial employment falls, in the classical treatment, on the unproductive side: the clerk, the trader, and the analyst perform the labour of circulation, changing the ownership of values rather than adding to their mass, and their wages are paid from the surplus their operations help to realise and divide. The growth of financial employment across the period is therefore, within the framework, a growth in the share of the social labour force maintained out of surplus value rather than engaged in producing it, a deduction that the system bears because circulation on the modern scale cannot be had for free.

The classification is contested inside the tradition, and the contest matters for the financialisation debate. Some writers argue that parts of modern finance perform functions continuous with production, that logistics, payment systems, and risk allocation are as materially necessary as transport, which Marx himself counted as productive. Others reply that necessity is not the criterion, since many unproductive functions are necessary, and that the criterion remains the form of exchange: whether the labour is bought by capital to valorise it or bought out of revenue to be consumed as a service. The dispute is genuine and unresolved, and the article records it without pretending to settle what the tradition has not. What can be said is that the surplus constraint does not depend on the dispute's outcome in the way readers sometimes assume. Even if some financial labour were reclassified as productive, the revenues of the sector would still have to be traced flow by flow, and the great mass of interest, the paradigmatic financial income, would remain a share of profit by the logic of the loan contract itself. The classification governs the margins of the argument. The constraint governs its centre, which is why the article has built on the constraint and treated the classification as the honourable quarrel it is.

The classification dispute has a history inside the tradition that illuminates its stakes. In the debates of the 1970s over the size of the unproductive sector, some writers argued that the growth of employment in commerce, finance, and administration explained the period's slowing accumulation directly: a rising share of the workforce maintained from surplus meant a falling share available to expand it, a drag on the rate of profit exerted through the wage bill of circulation itself. Others replied that the same growth cheapened circulation per unit of output, releasing capital that would otherwise have been locked in inventories and receivables, so the net effect on accumulation was ambiguous and empirical. The financialisation literature inherited the quarrel and gave it better data. Financial employment's growth across the expansion decades is not in dispute; what the growth cost, net of the circulation economies it purchased, remains genuinely open, and the openness is instructive. It shows the framework operating as its founders intended, as a set of distinctions that organise measurement rather than as a machine that manufactures conclusions, since the same categories that generate the surplus constraint also generate, at this lower level, a question they cannot answer unaided.

There is a distributional refinement the dispute often misses. The wages of unproductive labour are not a homogeneous deduction. The clerk's wage is a cost of circulation in the plain sense, comparable to the rent of the office. The senior trader's bonus, by contrast, behaves economically as a share in the fund being divided, negotiated from inside the division process by agents positioned to intercept it, and several writers have proposed treating the summit incomes of finance as a distinct category, neither wage nor profit but a rent of position within the machinery of distribution. The proposal matters for measurement, because national accounts record those incomes as compensation of employees, swelling the measured wage share of the financial sector and disguising, inside the labour share itself, a flow that belongs analytically to the surplus. The correction, attempted in the distributional literature associated with Duménil and Lévy, reallocates the highest managerial and financial salaries to the property income side of the ledger, and the reallocation sharpens every trend this article has described: the wage share falls further, the property share rises higher, and the class strategy account gains its cleanest statistical expression. Classification, once again, is not bookkeeping pedantry. It is where the theory decides what the facts are.

## Reading a financial firm's accounts through the constraint

Theory earns its keep when it changes what a reader sees in a document, so this section performs the exercise the article has been promising, tracing a stylised financial firm's income through the constraint without naming any institution. The firm's revenue arrives under familiar headings. Interest received on its lending exceeds the interest it pays on its own funding, and the margin between them is its first great stream. Fees and commissions form the second, charged for arranging, advising, managing, and administering. Gains on its own holdings form the third, as the claims in its portfolio are revalued or sold. Read naively, the three streams testify to a productive enterprise selling valued services in a growing market. Read through the constraint, each stream resolves into a source. The interest margin is a share of the profits of the enterprises it lends to, together with a deduction from the wages of the households it lends to, in proportions the firm's disclosures rarely separate and the analyst must estimate from the composition of its book. The fees are charges against the revenues of clients, enterprises paying from profit and households paying from income, for services of circulation whose cost the system bears. The gains on holdings are revaluations of fictitious capital, realisable as income only insofar as future surplus arrives to honour the claims at their new prices, and reversible in an afternoon when expectations turn.

The exercise generalises into a reading habit that students can carry to any set of accounts in any sector. For each revenue line, ask who pays, and from which fund. For each asset, ask what flow of future surplus or revenue the asset is a claim upon, and what happens to its value if that flow disappoints. For each liability, ask whose deferred income has been gathered and on what terms it can be withdrawn. The questions are the surplus constraint translated into bookkeeping, and they retain their power outside the financial sector entirely: the platform firm's commissions, the landlord's rents, and the rights holder's royalties all submit to the same interrogation, as the related treatments in this series of rent and of platform income show. A reader who has internalised the habit possesses the article's argument in its portable form, since the trichotomy is only the habit's application to one literature: three kinds of answer recur because there are three kinds of source a revenue can have, a share of surplus, a deduction from revenue, or a revaluation awaiting its test.

Apply the reading habit once more, to the pension fund whose paradox occupied an earlier section, and the habit's yield can be checked. The fund's statement shows contributions received, benefits paid, expenses charged, and a portfolio whose value closes the account. Trace the contributions and they resolve into deferred wages, a portion of the compensation of the workforce set aside under compulsion of contract. Trace the benefits and they resolve into the consumption of retired workers, financed in the accounting sense by the portfolio and in the economic sense by the output of the generation at work, whose product the retirees' claims command. Trace the expenses and they resolve into the incomes of the claims industry, a deduction from the circuit whose size is a distributional variable, contested between the industry and its clients with the clients' side of the argument chronically disorganised. Trace the portfolio and it resolves into the article's entire subject matter in miniature: public debt serviced from taxation, corporate claims serviced from profit, property claims serviced from rent and wages, each asset a small treaty about the future division of the social product. A reader who can perform that tracing unaided no longer needs the article, which is the proper ambition of any exposition: to make itself dispensable by transferring the method. The three answers, the table, and the verdict are scaffolding around the tracing habit, and scaffolding is honoured by being removed when the building stands.

The reading exercise can be extended to the statements of a large holder of claims, and the extension exposes a feature of the period that the firm's accounts conceal. An asset manager's revenue is a fee on the stock of claims under its administration, and the stock's value is set by the capitalisation of revenues arising in enterprises and households the manager does not control. The manager's income is therefore twice removed from production: a fraction of a valuation of a claim on a fund. Nothing in that remove makes the income less real to its recipient, and the framework does not ask anyone to pretend otherwise; what the remove explains is the income's peculiar sensitivity and its peculiar politics. A fraction of a valuation falls when valuations fall, with no change in any underlying revenue, so the claims industry experiences the repricing of claims as an immediate crisis of its own income, and defends the valuations, in every forum available to it, with the energy of a producer defending a harvest. The defence is the political form of the capitalisation formula, and the literature that has grasped the formula's centrality can predict the industry's positions on questions far from its apparent interests, since all of them reduce, at the margin, to the protection of the denominator and the stock it values.

## Could financialisation recede? The question of reversibility

A tendency is not a destiny, and the literature's more reflective contributions ask what a recession of financialisation would even mean under each of the three answers. Under the redistribution answer, the financial share would subside if profitable outlets in production reopened on a scale sufficient to absorb the surplus, whether through new industries, new geographies of investment, or a rebuilding of public capital stock financed from the fund itself; the mechanism predicts that finance would shrink relative to production without any campaign against it, as water drains when a lower outlet opens. Under the expropriation answer, recession would require the restoration of collective and employer provision in housing, retirement, and insurance, since the channel exists because households must buy individually what was once supplied socially; the channel would narrow as provision widened, whatever happened to profit shares. Under the artefact answer, recession is partly a matter of settlement between jurisdictions and statistical offices, as the rewards for booking income in financial shape rise and fall with the treatment of group structures. The three predicted recessions are different events, and observing which one occurs, if any does, would discriminate among the answers as sharply as the expansion did, a point worth making in an article governed by a structural rather than a topical brief: the framework is built to be read in either direction of the tendency, and its categories will classify a contraction with the same composure they brought to the growth.

History offers the literature one partial precedent, the long subordination of finance in the decades after the great wars of the twentieth century, when capital movements were restricted, credit was directed, and the financial share of profit in the advanced economies remained modest by later standards. The absorption school reads that era as the working of exceptional outlets, reconstruction and suburbanisation and public investment absorbing surplus as fast as it formed. The class strategy school reads it as a settlement imposed on finance by a balance of forces that the subsequent decades reversed. The artefact school notes, correctly, that the era's corporate structures were simpler and its accounts correspondingly harder to dress. The precedent therefore confirms the framework's portability rather than any single row of the table, since each answer can narrate the quiet decades in its own terms and the narration that persuades is the one whose mechanisms also explain the noisy decades that followed. Reversibility, in short, is not a refutation awaiting the thesis in the future; it is a test the thesis has already passed once, in the negative, during the long mid century interval when the phenomenon this article describes was, by every family's indicators, in abeyance.

The reversibility question has a distributional corollary that the literature states plainly and that any honest survey must pass on. A recession of financialisation, on any of the three mechanisms, is not a costless return to an earlier innocence. If the financial share subsides because claims are repriced downward, the losses fall on the holders of claims, and the holders now include the pension savings of the entire workforce, so the correction of the structure would be experienced by millions of households as the destruction of their security, however fictitious the capital destroyed may be in the theory's technical sense. If the expropriation channel narrows because provision is resocialised, the transition requires the public assumption of obligations that private contracts carry, a fiscal transformation of the first magnitude. If the artefact component unwinds through new settlements on corporate taxation and structure, the reported geography of profit will shift again and a new generation of commentators will announce a transformation that the consolidated accounts will once more deflate. The tradition's maturity shows in its refusal to promise that its preferred direction of travel would be painless. A structure that has organised the security of a population around the appreciation of claims has made that population hostage to the claims' value, and the hostage relation, more than any conspiracy of bankers, is the financial order's deepest defence. Analysts who understand the defence counsel accordingly: the unwinding of financialisation, if it comes, will arrive as a distributional settlement imposed by events or negotiated under pressure, and its costs will be allocated by politics, as the costs of its construction were.

## The labour process inside finance

The financial sector is also a workplace, and a literature preoccupied with flows can forget the clerks, analysts, call centre staff, and compliance officers whose labour performs the circulation the theory classifies. Their experience of the period has its own structure. Employment in finance expanded while its internal hierarchy steepened: a narrow stratum of revenue facing staff captured incomes that belong, in the framework's terms, to the distribution of surplus among its claimants, sharing in the fund through salaries and bonuses calibrated to the claims they originate, while the broad base of processing and service staff experienced the familiar disciplines of the modern office, monitoring, targets, casualisation at the margins, and the substitution of software for routine judgement. The sector that personifies capital's command over labour thus reproduces, inside its own walls, the class structure of the economy at large, and several ethnographic contributions to the literature have made the point with material the aggregate writers lacked: the trading floor's culture of conquest rests on floors of invisible maintenance labour, much of it feminised and much of it precarious, whose relation to the surplus is that of any wage labour, exploited in production of the service or maintained from the fund according to the classification dispute recorded earlier.

For the trichotomy the labour process material is a reminder rather than a revision. The incomes of the sector's summit are among the clearest instances of redistribution the period affords, since they are calibrated directly to the volume of claims originated and traded, and they fall when the music of capitalisation stops with a speed that betrays their character as shares rather than as prices of a produced output. The wages of the sector's base, by contrast, are a cost of circulation, deducted from the fund before its division among the claimants. Both facts sit comfortably inside the constraint, and their combination explains a feature of the period's politics that purely distributional accounts miss: the financial workforce is not a class bloc, and its upper and lower reaches have opposed interests in the defence of the order that employs them, a fissure that any account of the order's durability, or of its possible recession, must take into its calculations.

Automation has since added a further storey to the sector's internal structure, and the literature is beginning to register it. The routine labour of circulation, the processing of payments, the monitoring of accounts, the elementary assessment of borrowers, is peculiarly susceptible to substitution by software, since its objects are already symbols and its rules already formal. The consequence within the sector mirrors the consequence the classical theory described for production: a rising organic composition, in which the mass of claims administered per worker multiplies while the workforce performing the administration stagnates or shrinks, and the incomes of the sector concentrate further toward the owners of the systems and the narrow stratum that designs and supervises them. For the surplus constraint the development is neutral in form and significant in degree. The costs of circulation fall, releasing surplus for division; the division itself grows more unequal within the sector's own payroll; and the fictitious capital stock, administered at ever lower marginal cost, can be manufactured and serviced in volumes that a paper based system could not have sustained. Technique, as so often in this history, does not create the tendency but removes the friction that limited it, and the analyst who attributes the expansion to the software makes the plumbing error the drivers section warned against: the pressure came from the fund and its blockages, the software merely let the pressure through.

The labour process material has one more lesson, for the theory of the firm rather than for the trichotomy. The financial sector's internal hierarchy demonstrates that the division between those who appropriate the fund and those who are maintained from it does not coincide with the boundary of any industry, any firm, or any payroll. It runs through every organisation the period built, separating the revenue facing summit, whose compensation is a negotiated share of the flows it intermediates, from the processing base, whose compensation is a cost to be held down like any other. Marx's personifications, the money capitalist and the functioning capitalist, were drawn as separate figures because the nineteenth century housed them in separate bodies; the financialised firm houses both, and their conflict, inside a single compensation committee. The categories survive the cohabitation because they were always categories of function rather than of person, and the analyst who remembers that will read the modern corporation's internal politics, its struggles over bonuses, buybacks, and the treasury's targets, as the old partition of the surplus conducted by other means, which is to say, as political economy continuing inside the organisational chart.

## Objections from outside the framework

Fairness to the reader requires a hearing for the critics who reject the framework's premise altogether, since the surplus constraint binds only those who accept that value originates in production under the value form. The most developed external objection holds that modern economies are service economies, that financial services, intermediation, risk bearing, and the allocation of capital among uses, are productive activities in the only sense that matters, the sense in which customers pay for outputs they want, and that a theory classifying them as transfers mistakes an accounting identity of its own construction for a law of the world. On this view the growth of finance is no more mysterious than the growth of any industry whose outputs are increasingly demanded, and the Marxist problem dissolves because its premise, that the sector's income must be a share of a fund produced elsewhere, is the very thing in dispute. A second external objection, from economic sociology, grants the transfers and denies their significance: what matters about finance, on this telling, is not the source of its income but its performative power, the way its models and metrics remake the enterprises and households they measure, and a literature obsessed with sources will miss the phenomenon's real action, which is cognitive and institutional.

The article's response is measured, because the objections identify the genuine frontier. The first objection is correct that the constraint is premise dependent; every framework's organising theorem is. The question between frameworks is comparative: which premise set explains more of the syndrome with less residue. The Marxist premise earns its keep, its defenders argue, by explaining the features the service view must treat as coincidences, the correlation of financial expansion with slow production growth, the dependence of financial income on asset revaluations that periodically collapse toward the underlying revenue, and the distributional signature of the period, in which the sector's expansion coincided with a falling wage share rather than with a general rise in the rewards of all service work. The second objection is best read as a complement misfiled as a rival: the performative literature describes how financial categories govern conduct, the constraint literature asks where financial incomes originate, and a complete account of the period needs both, as the household strand's findings on discipline and self management already showed. A reader who finishes the article persuaded that the frontier lies exactly here, between a theory of sources and its external critics, has understood the field's present state as well as any summary can convey it.

The performative objection merits a fuller answer than a paragraph of reconciliation, because its best statement, in the work of Donald MacKenzie and the school of social studies of finance that followed his An Engine, Not a Camera of 2006, demonstrates something the constraint literature should absorb rather than merely tolerate. MacKenzie showed that financial theory does not stand outside its object describing it; the models by which claims are priced are built into the machinery that prices them, so that the theory becomes, in his phrase, an engine of the market's operation. Options are priced by formulas whose widespread use alters the behaviour the formulas describe, and measures of risk govern the portfolios whose composition then validates the measures. For the Marxist account the demonstration cuts in an unexpected direction. If the categories of finance are performative, then the category of fictitious capital has a performative shadow of its own: the entire apparatus of valuation presupposes that capitalised claims are wealth, acts on the presupposition in every balance sheet and covenant, and thereby makes the presupposition socially real, whatever its status in the theory of value. The framework can accept the point without surrendering its own, by distinguishing the reality of the claim as a social relation, which nobody in the tradition denies, from the claim's correspondence to produced value, which is the point at issue. A ticket to a concert is really a ticket, commands a price, and can be inherited; the concert is still the music, and a hall full of tickets is not a louder orchestra. Performativity explains how the ticket market organises the audience's conduct. The constraint asks who is playing, and from what fund the players are paid, and the two enquiries, properly kept apart, enrich rather than refute one another.

The external objections have a history of their own that the article should acknowledge before leaving them. The claim that services create value in the same sense that goods production was, in the founders' century, the claim of the economists Marx wrote against, and the modern version differs from theirs chiefly in the scale of the service economy it can point to. The tradition's answer has correspondingly shifted its emphasis: less on the logical classification of particular activities, more on the aggregate behaviour of the revenues, the way the financial sector's income rises and collapses with the valuation of claims rather than with any measurable output of services, a pattern the service theory must treat as an anomaly and the transfer theory predicts as the norm. Arguments between frameworks are settled, insofar as they are ever settled, by such patterns, accumulated until one framework's anomalies outweigh its explanations. The financialisation literature's confidence, where it is confident, rests on that accumulation; its critics are invited, in the same spirit, to accumulate theirs, and the article's defeater stands as the tradition's written invitation to do exactly that.

## Money managers and the new rentier

The classical rentier was an individual, a holder of bonds and shares living on coupons and dividends, and the figure haunted the economics of the early twentieth century as the emblem of a capitalism outgrowing its entrepreneurial youth. The financialised period did not abolish the rentier; it collectivised and professionalised the function. The characteristic recipient of financial revenue is now an institution managing other people's claims: the pension fund, the asset manager, the insurer, the endowment. Behind each institution stand millions of ultimate beneficiaries whose individual stakes are small and whose control over the claims held in their names is nominal, while before each stands a professional stratum whose incomes, unlike the beneficiaries', are large, immediate, and calibrated to the volume of assets under management rather than to the returns those assets ultimately deliver. The Marxist literature fastens on that calibration as the period's emblematic arrangement. A fee charged as a fraction of assets under management is a claim on the stock of fictitious capital itself, payable in good years and bad, rising automatically whenever capitalisation inflates the stock, and therefore a purer form of rentier income than the dividend, which at least waits upon profit. The manager of claims is paid for the existence of claims, and the system's tendency to multiply claims is, from the manager's desk, indistinguishable from prosperity.

This development refines the class analysis of the earlier sections. Duménil and Lévy's ruling bloc of financial executives and senior managers finds its mass base, if the phrase is permitted, in the broad professional stratum of the claims industry, whose members are employees by contract and rentiers by income structure, drawing salaries that participate in the distribution of surplus and of extracted revenue on a scale that aligns their interests with the order's continuation. The stratum's existence helps explain a sociological puzzle of the period, the absence of any significant constituency within the prosperous classes for the older model of capitalism, in which profit was retained and reinvested under managerial control. That model had its own beneficiaries, the engineers and organisers of production; the financialised model replaced them in the income hierarchy with the organisers of claims, and the replacement was accomplished without a shot because it proceeded through the labour market's quiet arithmetic of which skills the fund would pay for. A generation of the ablest graduates was bid away from the production of things into the administration of titles, and the opportunity cost of that migration, in enterprises not improved and processes not redesigned, is among the real if unmeasurable deductions the period's structure imposed on the production of surplus itself.

The collectivised rentier has cousins that complete the period's bestiary of claim holders. The university endowment and the charitable foundation, once sleepy holders of bonds, were drawn across the period into the active management of diversified portfolios, their spending rules tied to the capitalised value of their claims, so that institutions devoted to scholarship and relief acquired a material interest in the appreciation of the paper structure indistinguishable from any fund's. The sovereign wealth vehicle, through which states invest public surpluses in foreign claims, made governments themselves into rentiers of the first magnitude, their peoples' deferred income riding on the surplus produced by other peoples' labour. Each case repeats the pension fund's lesson in a new register: the claim has been universalised as the form in which every institution holds its future, and a form so universal ceases to be visible as a form at all. It presents itself as prudence, as the simple duty of any steward to make resources grow, and the question the framework insists on, grow from what fund and at whose expense, comes to sound like an impertinence addressed to arithmetic. The literature's writers regard that sounding as the ideology of the period in its most effective form, not a doctrine anyone argues but a common sense everyone breathes, and they measure their own success by whether a reader, having finished, can hear the question inside the arithmetic again.

The new rentier's income has a final peculiarity that completes the article's portrait of the period's class structure. The classical rentier's revenue depended on the fortunes of identifiable enterprises and obligations, and its holder bore, however passively, the risk of those fortunes. The collectivised rentier's professional managers have arranged matters more comfortably: the fee on assets under management is payable in all weathers, the losses of a repricing fall on the beneficiaries whose claims shrink, and the manager's own capital is scarcely engaged in the enterprise at all. The tradition has a category waiting for the arrangement, a rent charged on one's place inside the machinery that divides the fund, and the incomes it names are, by the standards of the framework, the period's most purely appropriative: shares of the fund and of extracted revenue, taken not for organising production, not for advancing capital, and not even for bearing the risk of the claims, but for standing at the point in the circuit where the claims are administered. That such incomes should be among the highest in the economy is the financialised order's most economical self description, and the literature that first described them in these terms has been confirmed, in the decades since, by every published schedule of the claims industry's rewards.

## Neighbouring concepts: what financialisation is not

Precision about a concept includes the map of its neighbours, and four neighbours generate persistent confusion. The first is financial deepening, the term development economics uses for the growth of a financial system relative to the economy it serves, usually as a story of progress in economies where credit is scarce and saving is informal. Deepening and financialisation can describe the same arithmetic, a rising ratio of financial assets to output, with opposite valences and different mechanisms: deepening names the construction of a credit system where production lacked one, while financialisation names the expansion of claims where production is mature and its outlets are blocked. Confusing them leads to the export error noted in the evidence section, the application of a theory of mature capitalism's blockages to economies whose problem is the absence of the machinery the theory presupposes. The second neighbour is speculation, the purchase of assets for resale at a higher price rather than for their revenue. Speculation is an activity, episodic and as old as markets; financialisation is a structure, the durable weight of financial income and relations in the economy's ordinary operation. A system can be financialised in quiet markets, its households indebted and its firms distributing, without any speculative fever, and a speculative episode can convulse a system whose financial weight is modest. The literature's care in separating the structure from the episode is one of its underappreciated virtues, since journalism habitually reaches for the fever to explain the structure and thereby misses the decades of calm accumulation of claims in which the structure actually consists.

The third neighbour is usury, the lending of money at interest denounced by moral traditions far older than capitalism. Marx's treatment of usury is a useful purge for contemporary rhetoric. Usurer's capital, in his analysis, is a pre capitalist form that battened on producers without organising production, and its modern descendant, the lending that extracts from households at punitive margins, resembles it functionally; but the developed credit system is a different creature, integrated with production, dividing profit with functioning capital, and socialising the mobilisation of idle funds. To denounce the whole of modern finance as usury is to abandon the distinctions the theory exists to draw, between interest as a portion of profit and interest as a deduction from revenue, between the credit that cheapens circulation and the contract that merely bleeds a borrower. The expropriation literature is sometimes misread as a revival of the usury denunciation, and its author is careful, in the work itself, to refuse the reading: the charge against household finance is not that interest is wicked but that a system has arisen in which access to the means of life passes routinely through interest bearing contracts, a structural claim about provision, not a moral claim about lenders. The fourth neighbour is monetisation in the anthropologist's sense, the spread of money as the medium of social relations into spheres previously organised by custom or kinship. Monetisation and financialisation overlap in the household, where both describe the penetration of monetary calculation into daily life, but they diverge in their object: monetisation concerns money as a medium, financialisation concerns claims as assets, and a society can be thoroughly monetised, every obligation priced and paid in cash, while remaining unfinancialised in the article's sense, its households unindebted and its profits booked where they are produced.

Drawing these boundaries is not pedantry. Each neighbour imports its own literature, its own evidence, and its own politics, and an argument that slides among them can appear to command four literatures' worth of support while actually commanding none. The surplus constraint polices the boundaries from inside, since it defines the phenomenon by the movement of revenues and claims rather than by the presence of money or markets or even of speculation. Where revenues are shares, transfers, and revaluations on the scale the syndrome describes, financialisation in the Marxist sense is present; where money merely circulates more busily, something else, perhaps interesting and perhaps important, is present instead, and the theory does its readers a service by declining to annex it.

Boundary cases test a classification better than central ones, and the literature's handling of two deserves record. The first is lending to the very poor under programmes that present small loans as instruments of emancipation. The framework's analysis is unsentimental and, to its critics, cold: a loan to a household without surplus income is a claim on that household's future wages, and its interest is a levy on revenue in the strict sense, so the expropriation category applies to the celebrated cases exactly as to the commercial ones, and the emancipation claim must be judged by whether the borrowing finances an activity whose return exceeds the deduction, an empirical question the celebration usually declines to ask. The second case is the financing of public goods through claims issued against future taxation, where the borrowing builds assets, schools, networks, drainage, whose services raise the productivity of the whole economy. Here the interest remains a transfer from taxpayers to claimants, but the underlying expenditure enlarges the fund from which the transfer is paid, and the net effect on the surplus can be positive across a generation. The framework accommodates the case without strain, since its question is never whether a flow exists but how it is sourced and what it builds, and the accommodation illustrates the difference between the theory and the slogan derived from it: the slogan says finance extracts, the theory says trace the flow, identify the fund, and reckon the net across time. Slogans are remembered and theories are used, and the article has aimed throughout to equip the reader with the used thing.

The neighbours have one more member whose confusion with the article's subject is the most consequential of all in public debate: globalisation, the integration of production and trade across borders. The two processes travelled together across the decades, shared institutions and vocabularies, and are routinely cited as one another's evidence, yet their mechanisms are distinct and their fortunes separable. Production can integrate across borders while finance remains nationally caged, as it substantially did in the first postwar decades of expanding trade; finance can integrate while production stagnates at home, as the international section of this article described. The Marxist literature's contribution to the separation is the fund's perspective: globalisation reorganises where surplus value is produced, financialisation reorganises how it is claimed, and the period's character arises from the combination, production migrating toward lower wages while the claims on its product accumulated in the old centres. An analysis that fuses the two processes cannot see the combination, and therefore cannot see the period's central tension, between a world economy whose production had never been more integrated and a distribution of its fruits that grew steadily more concentrated in the hands of the claimants. Keeping the processes distinct is not scholasticism. It is the precondition of noticing the tension at all.

## From Hilferding to the present: the tradition's own development

The literature this article has organised did not spring from the decades it describes; it is the third great wave of Marxist theorising about finance, and placing it in the sequence clarifies what is new in it. The first wave belongs to the founders and their immediate successors. Marx supplied the categories in manuscripts published after his death, and Hilferding's Finance Capital of 1910 converted them into a theory of the then new world of cartels, banks, and stock exchanges, arguing that the concentration of capital was socialising production within the shell of private property and preparing, in the banks' ledgers, an apparatus through which a society could in principle take stock of its whole economy. Lenin's wartime pamphlet on imperialism carried a compressed version of the argument to a mass readership, fixing in the tradition's memory the association of finance with monopoly and with the export of capital, though at the cost of freezing a conjunctural analysis into a stage theory that later writers have had to thaw. The second wave belongs to the long stagnation debates of the postwar decades, when Baran and Sweezy rebuilt the theory of surplus and its absorption for an economy of giant corporations, and when the journal they founded became the workshop in which the stagnation and financialisation thesis was eventually assembled. Finance in that wave was still largely an absence, the place where surplus went to be wasted or stored; the third wave's originality was to make it a presence, a set of mechanisms with their own incomes, workforces, and claims on households, requiring the categories to be worked again at the level Marx himself had worked them.

Three features distinguish the third wave's craftsmanship. The first is its empiricism: Krippner's profit shares, Crotty's consolidation exercises, Orhangazi's firm level studies, and Stockhammer's cross economy estimates represent a standard of engagement with national accounts that the earlier waves, writing before the data existed in usable form, could not have met. The second is its internal pluralism under a shared constraint: the three answers disagree sharply while citing one another respectfully, because the constraint gives them a common object to disagree about. The third is its reflexivity about measurement, the accounting objection being the wave's own product, raised by its practitioners against its founders' evidence. A tradition that can generate its own sceptics is in better health than one that imports them, and the point deserves emphasis in an assessment climate where the Marxist writing on finance is sometimes dismissed as a closed system. Closed systems do not publish Crotty against Foster in sympathetic journals and then rebuild their evidence base in response.

The sequence also explains the literature's silences. A tradition formed by Hilferding's question, what does the organisation of finance reveal about the maturity of capitalism, will always be stronger on structure than on event, stronger on the distribution of the fund than on the timing of its crises, and the reader who needs the crisis mechanics must go where this article has repeatedly pointed, to the companion treatment of crisis theory, rather than expecting the financialisation literature to supply what its founding question does not ask. Knowing a literature's founding question is the deepest form of orientation a survey can give, deeper than any summary of findings, because findings date while questions organise the reading of whatever findings arrive next.

The second wave's workshop deserves a closer look, because the financialisation thesis was assembled there over decades rather than announced in a manifesto. The Monthly Review tradition, descending from Baran and Sweezy's collaboration, maintained through the postwar era a single research programme: to track the economic surplus, its generation under monopoly conditions, and the increasingly desperate channels through which the system attempted to absorb it. Military expenditure, the sales effort, and the waste of consumerism were the programme's classic absorbers, each analysed as a use of surplus that sustained demand without expanding productive capacity. When Harry Magdoff and Sweezy turned, in their later essays, to the growth of debt and the swelling of the financial superstructure, the move was continuous with everything that preceded it: finance was the newest and largest absorber, the channel into which the unabsorbable surplus was pouring, and its expansion was therefore dated, in their analysis, not from any financial innovation but from the exhaustion of the older channels. Foster's later systematisation, and the collaboration with Magdoff on the financial crisis literature of 2009, completed the arc by arguing that the absorber had become the system's central instability, since claims pyramided on a stagnating base must periodically collapse toward it. Readers who approach the stagnation and financialisation thesis through its mature statements sometimes miss this genealogy and with it the thesis's deepest claim, which is not that finance grew but that a specific theory of the surplus, built for other purposes a generation earlier, predicted the shape of its growth in advance. Predictive success of that kind, a framework anticipating the phenomenon it was not designed for, is the strongest currency in which research programmes trade, and the tradition may fairly spend it.

A closing observation on the first wave completes the tradition's self portrait. Hilferding wrote as a participant as well as an analyst, a finance minister manqué of a movement that expected to inherit the apparatus he described, and his book's famous suggestion, that taking possession of the great banks would give a new order command of the economy's heights, shaped a century of argument about the road from analysis to transformation. The modern literature has quietly dropped the suggestion's confidence while retaining its premise, that the credit system is the economy's nervous system and that whoever would change the economy must reckon with the claims structure as it stands, its hostages and its constituencies included. The dropping is itself a historical datum: a tradition that once expected the apparatus to be inherited whole now studies the apparatus as a settlement to be renegotiated against the resistance of everyone whose security it holds. Whether that sobriety is wisdom or resignation is a question the article's evidence cannot settle, and it is recorded here, at the end of the historical survey, as the open question the tradition carries into its fourth wave, whose contours the research agenda in the closing sections has already begun to sketch.

## Methods: what counts as evidence for a source

Because the trichotomy classifies revenues by source, its evidence has a characteristic shape that differs from the shape of evidence in most economic controversy, and making the shape explicit will help readers judge new contributions. A source claim is established by tracing: following a flow of income from the account where it appears as revenue to the account from which it was paid, and identifying the fund at the chain's end. Tracing is laborious and often impossible with published data, which is why the literature leans on proxies, and the proxies have a hierarchy of credibility that the accounting objection taught the field to state openly. At the top stand contractual magnitudes, debts owed by identifiable classes of borrower, which cannot be reclassified by anyone's accounting choices. Below them stand headcounts and physical magnitudes, employment in the sector, the stock of dwellings mortgaged, the number of accounts. Below those stand distributional shares computed from micro records, the income composition of households by source. At the bottom, indispensable and suspect, stand the sectoral profit shares of the national accounts, aggregates of aggregates, sensitive to imputation and boundary conventions, to be cited with their construction attached or not at all. A contribution that announces a finding should be asked where on the hierarchy its evidence sits, and a finding that moves a debate will usually be one that brings a higher tier of evidence to a question previously argued from a lower one, as the household debt series did when they entered a controversy that had been conducted in profit shares.

Tracing also explains the literature's characteristic use of the case study. A single corporate group, followed through its subsidiaries, can demonstrate a mechanism, the conversion of industrial profit into financial income by internal structure, with a completeness that no aggregate can match, because the group's own filings perform the tracing for the researcher. Crotty's exercises have exactly this character, and their persuasiveness comes from the mechanism's visibility rather than from any claim that one group stands for the economy. The methodological moral generalises beyond this field: in controversies about classification, the decisive evidence is usually an anatomy rather than a total, a dissection showing how a magnitude is composed, and readers trained to ask for anatomies will find the literature on financialisation unusually willing to supply them, since its central objection was itself an anatomy of the headline number.

An anatomy, promised in the methods discussion, can be performed here in miniature on the single most consequential aggregate in the debate, the financial sector's share of total profit in a representative advanced economy at the height of the expansion. Begin with the headline share as the accounts publish it. The first incision removes the imputed output of banking services that no customer was ever charged for, an entry manufactured by convention to make the accounts complete, whose size varies with the convention chosen and whose removal lowers the sector's measured product without touching any income anyone received. The second incision consolidates the corporate groups, returning to industry the profits that groups booked in their financial subsidiaries, and the sector shrinks again by the amount of the internal migration Crotty documented. The third incision separates the sector's earnings on its own account, the gains from holding and trading claims, from its earnings for services rendered, since the two have different sources in the framework's terms and different stabilities across the cycle. What remains after the incisions is a sector still larger, relative to the whole, than the sector of the quiet decades, but smaller by a substantial margin than the headline announced, and differently composed: more dependent on the household book, less on the corporate one, than the raw figures suggested. The anatomy is the argument. Every stage of it is a decision about classification that a reader can inspect and contest, and the literature's progress since the objection was raised consists largely in the routine performance of such anatomies where a generation ago the headline was quoted whole.

The anatomy method has a defensive use that researchers entering the field should be shown early. Every aggregate in this literature is somebody's construction, assembled for a purpose, statistical or polemical, and the habit of asking for the anatomy is the habit of asking whose purpose the construction serves and what it was built to make visible or invisible. The financial sector's measured size, for instance, swells or shrinks with the treatment of the imputed services of banking, and the choice among treatments is made by offices with their own histories and mandates, not by the phenomenon. None of this makes the aggregates useless; it makes them evidence of a particular kind, testimony rather than photography, to be cross examined rather than quoted. The literature's methodological maturity, purchased in the accounting controversy, consists in having learned to cross examine its own favourite witnesses first, and the lesson transfers intact to every neighbouring field in which a headline magnitude organises a public argument. A generation of students taught to ask for the anatomy before the argument would change the quality of economic controversy far beyond the boundaries of Marxist scholarship, and the tradition may fairly count the prospect among its contributions.

The anatomy's lesson for the adjacent sciences is worth a final statement. Political economy's controversies are disproportionately controversies about the composition of aggregates that present themselves as simple: a profit share, a wage share, a sector's weight. The financialisation debate is the exemplary modern case because its participants, unusually, performed the dissections in public and revised their claims in response, leaving a documentary record from which the method can be learned entire. A researcher trained on that record acquires a reflex that no textbook imparts: on meeting any aggregate that carries an argument, to ask what imputations it contains, where its boundaries are drawn, whose internal transactions it counts as market events, and how its movement decomposes among those decisions. The reflex does not make its holder a Marxist. It makes its holder dangerous to every literature that lives on unexamined magnitudes, including, when the occasion arises, this one.

## The wage share connection

Every account in the trichotomy must sooner or later face the period's most consequential distributional fact, the long decline of the wage share in most advanced economies, documented in the companion treatment of labour share evidence and presupposed throughout this article. The decline matters to the financialisation debate for a reason that is easy to state and hard to overstate: it identifies the fund's other side. If the share of national income accruing as wages fell while the share accruing as profit and property income rose, then the growth of financial income was financed, at the level of the economy entire, substantially by labour's relative loss, whatever the sectoral route the income travelled. The class strategy account claims this fact as its home ground, reading the wage share's decline as the offensive's balance sheet. The absorption account accommodates it as a companion tendency, rising exploitation enlarging the surplus whose placement is the problem. The expropriation account adds its distinctive twist: the wage was squeezed twice, once in production as its share fell, and again in circulation as a growing portion of the reduced wage was committed to interest, fees, and premiums before any consumption began. The double squeeze formulation is the expropriation literature's most quotable product, and it survives the theoretical dispute about labels, since both squeezes are measurable transfers whatever they are called.

The connection also disciplines a lazy inference that the period's commentary often invites, from the growth of finance to the impoverishment of production's workforce by finance directly. The framework's causal order is more exact. The wage share fell for reasons located in production and in the balance of class forces: the relocation of industry toward lower wage zones, the weakening of collective bargaining, the reserve army's replenishment, and the technological reorganisation of work. Finance's growth then proceeded on the enlarged fund those changes released, and finance's own household channels deepened the squeeze at the margin. To blame the financial sector for the whole decline is to mistake the beneficiary for the author, an error the class strategy account exists to correct by restoring the offensive's full breadth, of which the financial recomposition was one theatre among several. Conversely, to absolve finance because it did not author the decline is to miss its role in consolidating the result, converting a distributional shift into a structure of claims, contracts, and disciplines that would persist even if the original pressures relaxed. The literature at its best holds both truths in one sentence, and the sentence is a fair summary of the article's compound verdict viewed from labour's side of the ledger.

A caveat about the wage share itself belongs in the record, since this article has leaned on the series and the methods section's hierarchy requires the leaning to be acknowledged. The labour share is a constructed magnitude. Its numerator mixes the wages of production workers with the salaries of executives, a mixture the classification discussion showed to be theoretically loaded. Its denominator depends on the treatment of self employment income, of housing services imputed to owner occupiers, and of the depreciation of the capital stock, each a convention on which statistical offices differ. The decline survives the plausible reconstructions, which is why the literature rests on it, but its precise size in any economy is a range rather than a point, and writers who quote the range's dramatic end without the range are repeating, in distributional dress, the sin the accounting objection exposed in the profit shares. The symmetry is worth dwelling on. Every camp in this literature has a favourite series whose construction flatters it, and the field's methodological coming of age consists precisely in the generalisation of Crotty's demand: provenance for every number, sensitivity for every claim, and a standing suspicion of any magnitude that arrives without its anatomy. The surplus constraint, which is indifferent to all these constructions because it classifies flows rather than measuring them, emerges from the methodological quarrels with its authority enhanced, the one piece of the apparatus that no revision of the accounts can touch.

A last word on the wage share's place in the compound verdict will keep the article's accounts straight. The decline of the wage share is the one magnitude in the entire syndrome that belongs, in the first instance, to the history of production and class forces rather than to the history of finance, and the article has been careful to assign it there. Its presence in the evidence is nonetheless indispensable, because it identifies the enlargement of the fund on which every financial mechanism fed: the placements had a surplus to place because exploitation's rate and reach grew, the expropriation channel had wages to levy because the wage remained the household's only income while its share shrank, and the class strategy had a victory to record because the shares moved as an offensive would move them. Finance, in the strictest statement the framework allows, was the period's great beneficiary and consolidator rather than its prime mover, and a literature that keeps the assignment straight will be proof against both the demonology that blames the banks for the wage's fate and the apologetics that absolve the claims structure of the fate's consolidation.

## Housing: the hinge asset

If one asset were chosen to exhibit the whole argument in a single object, the literature would choose housing, because the dwelling sits at the intersection of every mechanism the article has described. As a produced commodity, a house embodies surplus value extracted in construction and in the industries that supply it. As a site, it commands ground rent, the scarcity payment for location that no production creates. As a financial asset, it is the collateral against which the household debt stock was built, its price a capitalisation of expected future payments that can swell for years under the pressure of cheap credit and constrained supply, enriching owners on paper while excluding entrants in fact. As the household's principal store of deferred wages, it is the form in which workers' savings are most widely held, so that the same family appears as debtor on one side of the balance sheet and as claimant on the other, paying interest with one hand and receiving capital gains with the other, in proportions that decide whether the financialised order has treated it kindly. And as the object of lending, the dwelling is the expropriation channel's main conduit, the contract through which the largest share of household income is committed to financial institutions across a working life.

The hinge character of housing explains why the politics of the period so often organised itself around property values rather than around wages, a displacement the class analysis predicts once the household's balance sheet becomes its welfare state. A workforce whose security is invested in an asset price experiences public questions through that price: questions of construction, of credit conditions, of local taxation, all present themselves as questions about the value of the family's one great claim. The displacement is not false consciousness in the crude sense, since the interest is real; it is the rational politics of a balance sheet society, and it divides the working class by tenure and by generation as effectively as any division by trade or region divided its predecessors. Writers in the tradition have begun to treat the housing question as the point where the financialisation literature and the older Marxist literature on rent and the city converge, and the convergence promises some of the field's most productive future work, since the categories are ready made and the evidence, in land registries and loan books, sits high on the hierarchy of credibility the methods section described.

The convergence with the urban literature runs deeper than a shared interest in land. The classical theory of ground rent analysed the payment for a monopolised condition of production; the financialised city adds the capitalisation of that payment into an asset price and the organisation of urban development itself around the production of tradable claims. Where the older developer built to sell buildings, the financialised developer builds to manufacture an income stream that can be packaged, rated, and sold to distant holders of claims, and the building's design, tenancy, and even architecture bend toward the requirements of the package: standardised leases, predictable yields, tenants whose covenants reassure a holder who will never see the street. The city that results is legible to the balance sheet before it is legible to its inhabitants, a built environment optimised as fictitious capital. Marxist urbanists, developing lines that descend from the rent theory through the work of David Harvey, whose Limits to Capital of 1982 remains the tradition's most ambitious attempt to integrate the built environment into the theory of accumulation, have traced the consequences for housing provision with a precision the finance literature alone could not reach: the systematic undersupply of dwellings as use values alongside their oversupply as assets, the conversion of shelter into the principal vehicle of household speculation, and the periodic crises in which the claims on urban revenue collapse toward the rents a working population can actually pay. Harvey's concept of the secondary circuit of capital, the switching of surplus from production into the built environment when the primary circuit congests, is recognisably the absorption mechanism with an address, and its presence in the tradition's toolkit since 1982 is further evidence that the financialisation thesis systematised insights the framework had been accumulating for decades.

Harvey's secondary circuit has a temporal signature that the financialisation literature has absorbed into its account of the period's rhythm. Switching surplus into the built environment and into claims postpones the reckoning of overaccumulation rather than abolishing it, since the switched funds return as demands on future revenue, in rents, in interest, and in the maintenance of the structures built. The postponement can last a generation, which is long enough to constitute an era and to furnish the era with its characteristic experience: prosperity in the asset markets alongside stagnation in the wage, each the other's condition. When the switched claims fall due against a revenue that the switching did not enlarge, the era's arithmetic closes, and the literature divides, in the manner the crisis boundary section recorded, over whether the closure is the absorption problem's return or a crisis with determinants of its own. For the purposes of this article the division is left standing, deliberately, as the point at which its subject hands the reader to the companion treatment, with the constraint as the passport both territories honour.

## The falling price of claims: interest and capitalisation across the period

One mechanism of the expansion has been present throughout the article as a formula and deserves a section as a force: the long decline in the prevailing rate of interest across the decades in question, and its mechanical consequence for the capitalised value of every revenue stream in the economy. The arithmetic was stated early. A revenue divided by a lower rate yields a higher notional capital. When the rate falls across a generation, as it did from the high levels at the start of the period to the low levels at its end, the entire stock of claims is revalued upward in a slow motion repricing that no one decides and everyone experiences as wealth. Portfolios swell, collateral expands, pension funds report improved positions, households feel richer against their debts, and governments find their obligations easier to carry, all from a change in the denominator. The Marxist literature's treatment of this repricing is among its most clarifying contributions, because the framework insists on the question the celebration suppresses: the revenues themselves, the flows of surplus and wages from which all payments must come, have not been multiplied by the repricing, so the enlarged paper wealth represents the same future income claimed at a higher price, a larger mortgage on an unchanged harvest.

The repricing also redistributed among claimants in ways the trichotomy can classify. Holders of long dated claims gained most, since their revenues were capitalised over the longest horizons; borrowers gained as the burden of a given debt stock lightened; new entrants to every asset market lost, paying the revalued prices for claims their elders had acquired cheaply. The generational signature of the financialised period, the divergence between the balance sheets of those who held assets before the repricing and those who arrived after, is substantially this arithmetic wearing demographic dress. And the mechanism carries its own limit inside it, which the tradition's writers note with a restraint the article will imitate: a denominator cannot fall forever, and a structure of wealth built on the expectation of its falling acquires a fragility to its stabilisation, let alone its rise, that no individual balance sheet reveals. The claims structure of the period's end thus embodies, in the purest form the theory affords, the definition of fictitious capital with which the article began: titles to future revenue, priced by a formula, whose aggregate value rests on conditions in the market for claims rather than on any enlargement of the revenue they divide.

The repricing mechanism has a mirror image that completes its logic and that the period's later years began to exhibit. When the prevailing rate ceases to fall and merely stabilises, the capital gains that the falling rate supplied disappear from the system's income, though no revenue has been lost; portfolios stop swelling, collateral stops expanding, and institutions that had organised their affairs around the expectation of appreciation discover that their ordinary income, the actual flow of interest and fees, supports a smaller structure of commitments than the appreciated values had seemed to warrant. The discovery is the gentle form of the fictitious capital reckoning, a stagnation of paper wealth rather than its collapse, and the tradition's writers identify it as the characteristic discomfort of a mature financialised order: an economy that has capitalised its future at a low rate has spent its repricing in advance, and must thereafter grow its claims at the pace of its revenues or not at all. Should the rate rise instead, the arithmetic reverses with interest, and every long dated claim in existence is revalued downward in proportion to its distance, a transfer from holders to issuers executed by the formula alone. The distributional politics of such a reversal would pit the generations and tenures against one another along the lines the housing analysis drew, which is why the tradition expects the defence of low rates and high valuations to be the financial order's most tenacious political commitment, pursued by the claim holding classes with the unity that a shared denominator inspires.

The repricing's distributional signature has one more register, the register of the state's own balance sheet, where the period's logic completed its circuit. Public obligations issued in the era of the falling denominator were serviced at rates that declined as the stock grew, so the state's interest bill, relative to its revenues, proved far lighter than the stock's growth threatened, and the lightness was widely read as evidence that the obligations were costless. The framework reads the same facts through the formula: the bill was light because the claims were priced by a denominator that the state's own creditworthiness, and the public underwriting of the system, held down, and the arrangement's stability is therefore the stability of a confidence that the state's promises and the claims' valuations support one another in a circle. Circles of that kind are not fictions; they are the ordinary constitution of a credit system, and they hold for decades at a time. What the theory adds to the observation is the location of the circle's foundation, which is not in the paper at all but in the taxable product of the working population, the fund from which every promise in the circle must finally be redeemed. An analysis that keeps the foundation in view will neither panic at the stock of claims nor relax about it, which is the temperament the article has tried to model throughout.

## Insurance, risk, and the sale of security

Insurance occupies a quieter place in the literature than banking or asset management, yet its logic exhibits the framework with unusual purity. The insurer gathers premiums from many households and enterprises, pools them against the misfortunes of the few, and invests the gathered fund in claims during the interval between collection and payment. Its income has two sources that the constraint separates cleanly. The underwriting margin, the excess of premiums over claims and costs, is a deduction from the revenues of the insured, who pay for the pooling service from wages and profits alike. The investment income is a share of the surplus produced elsewhere, received because the pool's reserves make the insurer a money capitalist of the first rank. Neither source involves the creation of value by the insurer, and yet the service is real and, under conditions of reprivatised risk, indispensable: the household that cannot pool its risks socially must buy the pooling privately, and the growth of the insurance relation is therefore another face of the transformation the household section described, the migration of security from collective provision to individual contract.

The tradition's interest in insurance is sharpened by the industry's role as a holder of long term claims. Because its obligations stretch decades into the future, the insurer is the natural buyer of the longest dated fictitious capital, and its demand helps set the prices at which future revenues are capitalised across the whole economy. The circuit completes a pattern the article has met repeatedly: wages and profits are gathered as premiums, transformed into a fund, placed in claims on future surplus, and returned in part as pensions and settlements to the same population that supplied them, minus the margins of every intermediary along the way. To describe that circuit is not to condemn it; pooling is a genuine social technique, and its private organisation is a historical fact rather than a swindle. The framework's contribution is to make the circuit's deductions visible as deductions, so that the cost of organising security through claims on surplus can be compared, honestly and in the open, with the cost of organising it in any other way. Comparisons of that kind are the beginning of political economy in the classical sense, and the financialisation literature's revival of them, against a culture that treats the existing circuit as nature, is among its services to readers who will never open a volume of Marx.

A contrast case sharpens the insurance analysis. Mutual and cooperative insurers, owned by their policyholders, perform the same pooling technique without the second source of income being appropriated by outside claimants: the investment return on the pooled fund accrues to the pool's members, and the margin between premiums and claims is returned or held as the members' collective reserve. The technique of insurance is therefore separable from the financialised form of its organisation, a separability the framework predicts and values, since it shows that what the theory criticises is never the social function but the circuit through which the function is financed and the deductions the circuit imposes. The same separability holds across the article's whole terrain. Credit allocation, payments, pooling, and the mobilisation of idle balances are functions any complex economy must perform; the Marxist question is invariably about the form, who owns the machinery, from which fund its personnel are maintained, and whose claims its operations manufacture. Readers who carry that distinction away will be immune to the commonest misrepresentation of the tradition, in which its critique of finance is reported as a wish to abolish banking and return to barter with better pamphlets. The tradition's actual wish, visible from Hilferding onward, is nearly the opposite: to take the socialised machinery of credit, which capitalism built and cannot operate justly, as evidence of the forms a rational organisation of production would require.

The insurance circuit offers a final illustration of the article's method applied to a question of reform, stated generically as the brief requires. Suppose the pooling of life's risks were organised so that the gathered fund's investment income accrued to the pool's members or to the public that guarantees the pool, rather than to outside claimants. Nothing in the technique of insurance would change: the premiums, the reserves, the actuarial tables, and the payments would proceed as before, and the only alteration would be in the destination of the margin, the portion of the circuit's flow that the present organisation assigns to the owners of the machinery. The framework's entire politics of finance is contained in that supposition's modesty. The tradition does not propose to abolish the machinery, whose socialisation it regards as among capitalism's genuine achievements; it proposes to notice who the machinery feeds, and to ask, with the tracing habit as the instrument of the noticing, whether the feeding is a necessity of the technique or a property of the ownership. Questions of that form, asked of the pension fund, the insurer, the exchange, and the bank in turn, are the financialisation literature's bequest to any politics that takes it seriously, and they retain their force for readers who reject every other sentence in this article.

## The crisis boundary: what this article has deliberately not argued

A word of scope discipline is owed before the personas and questions that close the article. Financialisation and crisis are neighbours in the literature and strangers in this exposition, by design. The mechanisms by which an overgrown structure of claims collapses toward the revenue beneath it, the sequence of defaults, forced sales, and public rescues through which the collapse proceeds, and the periodisation of the great crises belong to the companion treatment of marxist crisis theory, and importing them here would have doubled the article while halving its precision. What this article claims about crisis is only what the surplus constraint licenses: that a structure of claims grows more fragile as it grows faster than the fund that services it, that the fragility is a matter of arithmetic before it is a matter of psychology, and that the periodic repricing of claims toward revenue is the form in which the constraint reasserts itself against its own appearance of suspension. The tradition's writers divide on how much of crisis theory that much buys. The stagnation school regards the repricing as the absorption problem returning with violence, the moment when the surplus that could not be placed demands its reckoning. Others insist that crisis has its own determinants in the production sphere, in profitability and its tendencies, and that financial fragility amplifies rather than originates the storm. The article takes no side in that division, noting only that both parties conduct it inside the constraint, disputing the weight of a mechanism whose existence neither denies, which is the condition of a literature arguing about the world rather than about its own vocabulary.

The scope discipline has a positive side that should not go unrecorded. By refusing the crisis narrative, the article has managed to treat the financialised order in its ordinary operation, as a going concern that pays its personnel, services its claims, and reproduces its relations decade after decade, and the treatment corrects a bias in the wider discourse, which attends to finance almost exclusively at its moments of breakdown. A structure understood only in its collapses will be misunderstood in its stability, and the stability is the larger part of the phenomenon: the decades in which the claims were honoured, the contracts performed, and the deductions collected on schedule are the decades the three answers exist to explain. The tradition's own history imposed the bias, since its classic texts were written in the shadow of breakdowns, and the modern literature's achievement includes the recovery of the ordinary: the patient documentation of how a financialised economy works on the days when nothing dramatic occurs, which is most days, and on which the distribution the theory describes is quietly and completely effected.

## A worked comparison: the same decades read three ways

The trichotomy can be taught, and tested, by an exercise the article now performs in the open: take the syndrome's four families of indicators and read them three times, once through each answer, noting what each reading illuminates and what it strains to accommodate. Read through the redistribution answer, the syndrome coheres as a single story. Slow production growth and weak investment explain the surplus's search for placement; the rising financial share records the placement; the distributions and buybacks are the placement's corporate machinery; the household debt is a secondary theatre in which the placed funds found borrowers among the population at large. The reading strains at the wage share, whose decline it must import from a companion theory of class forces, and at the household series' sheer size, which sits oddly with their assignment to a secondary theatre. Read through the expropriation answer, the centre of gravity shifts to the household and the wage. The debt series become the headline, the profit shares a reflection, in the sector's accounts, of the levy on labour income, and the reprivatisation of provision the period's decisive institutional fact. The reading strains at the corporate evidence: the treasuries, the captive lenders, and the distributions to shareholders are not plausibly a levy on wages, and an account centred on the household must treat the corporate transformation as a parallel development whose connection to its own mechanism is asserted rather than shown.

Read through the artefact answer, the syndrome dissolves selectively. The profit shares shrink toward their consolidated values, the corporate financial income deflates into internal bookkeeping, and what survives, the debts households owe, the people the sector employs, the risks families now carry alone, stands as a social transformation without a corresponding migration of profit. The reading strains at the behavioural residue: firms reorganised themselves, at real cost and with real consequences for investment and employment, around the production of the very magnitudes the reading deflates, and an artefact that reorganises its observers' world has a claim to be treated as a structure. The exercise's lesson is the compound verdict's inevitability once the evidence is faced whole. Each reading is the best account of the family of indicators nearest its mechanism and the worst account of the family furthest away, and a literature that has produced all three has, perhaps without intending to, triangulated the phenomenon: the compound is not a compromise among the readings but the shape the object takes when each of its faces has been lit by the lamp that suits it.

The worked comparison has a classroom corollary that teachers may find worth the seminar's time. Require each participant to select the family of indicators they encountered first in their own reading of the period, and to notice which answer that family disposed them toward before the others were heard. The exercise reliably demonstrates the article's central methodological claim in biographical form: positions in this literature are usually adopted at the moment a body of evidence is first taken as the phenomenon itself, and the subsequent argument is the defence of that adoption. The trichotomy's pedagogical function is to return the adopter to the moment of choice with the alternatives visible, which is why the three answers table, and not any verdict, is the article's designated artifact. Verdicts age with the evidence. A table that teaches a reader to ask where the money comes from, and what would change the answer, remains useful when every magnitude in the field has been revised, as the field's own history suggests they all eventually will be.

The exercise has a final turn that examiners of the field's future would do well to rehearse. Suppose the coming decades reverse the syndrome's direction in the household series while leaving the corporate series untouched: debts fall as provision is rebuilt collectively, while treasuries continue to book the group's income in financial shape. The compound verdict predicts its own revision in that event with unusual precision. Expropriation would shrink from a structural channel to a historical episode, its author's mechanism intact but its domain contracted. The artefact row would inherit the corporate field entire, its discount becoming the headline. Redistribution would survive in the diminished form of a claim about distributions and placements within the corporate sector, a real but narrower thesis than the one the expansion decades suggested. A framework that can narrate its own contraction, row by row and in advance, has the property the article has prized throughout under the name of portability, and it is the property that distinguishes a research programme from a period piece. The literature's founders wrote for an economy that no longer exists; the test of their categories is that the economy which replaced it, and the one that will replace that, keep arriving in forms the categories can seat at the table.

## Saving, investment, and the glut of loanable funds

Classical political economy expected saving and investment to be two names for one act, the capitalist's abstinence becoming the worker's employment in a single motion. The financialised period exhibited their divorce on a grand scale, and the divorce is the absorption problem stated in the language of funds. The corporate sector of the advanced economies became, in aggregate, a net lender: its retained profits exceeded its investment in plant and equipment, and the excess accumulated as financial assets or was distributed to owners who placed it in the same markets. A sector that in the textbook borrows to invest had become a sector that lends from its profits, and the interest it earned on the lending entered the accounts, once again, as financial income reported by non-financial corporations, feeding the artefact debate from a real root. The counterpart of the corporate surplus was found in the deficits of households and of the state, the two borrowers of last resort in the period's arithmetic, whose obligations absorbed the fund that production would not. The circuit has a bleak elegance in the framework's retelling. Surplus value, produced in the enterprises of the corporate sector, could not return to those enterprises as investment without worsening their overcapacity; it therefore travelled, through the markets for claims, into the mortgages of the workforce and the bonds of the state, and returned to its owners as interest paid from wages and from taxes. The worker thus appears at both ends of the circuit, producing the surplus in the working day and servicing its claims in the evening, and the formulation, which is the stagnation school's and the expropriation school's shared property at this level of abstraction, states the period's structure in a single sentence that neither school would disown.

The divorce of saving from investment also corrects a popular account of the period's low investment, which blames a shortage of funds or a failure of entrepreneurial nerve. Funds were abundant to the point of embarrassment; the price of loanable capital fell across the decades precisely because the supply of placements outran the demand from production. What was scarce was not money but profitable opportunity at the scale the fund required, scarcity being, in the absorption theory's terms, the normal condition of a mature capitalism whose markets grow more slowly than its capacity to produce. Nerve is a psychological category and the framework has no use for it where an arithmetic one serves: a management that declines to build capacity its markets cannot absorb is not timid but literate, and the literature's insistence on the point is among its kindnesses to its readers, since it replaces a morality play about cowardly executives with an analysis of the situation every executive faced.

That divorce has a counterpart in the theory of money that the period's experience made newly legible. In a credit economy, investment is not in practice financed from a pre existing pool of saving; it is financed by the creation of credit, and the saving appears afterward as the income the investment generates is distributed and held. The financialised twist on that old insight is the direction the credit creation took. Where the textbooks imagined credit flowing to enterprises for the expansion of capacity, the period's credit flowed disproportionately into the purchase of existing assets, into housing, into the refinancing of claims, and into the funding of distributions, employments that raise the price of the claims stock without enlarging the flow of commodities from which the claims must be serviced. The distinction between credit that creates the income by which it will be repaid and credit that merely bids up the titles to income created by others is, in the framework's terms, the distinction between an advance against future surplus value in the course of its production and an addition to fictitious capital, and the period's balance sheets are the record of the second employment outgrowing the first. No single statistic announces the outgrowth, which is why the literature argues from the syndrome; but the direction of credit, traced flow by flow in the manner the methods section prescribed, is the shape in which the next generation of the field's evidence will most likely be gathered.

## Generations: who holds the claims, who owes the debts

The balance sheet society divides by age as sharply as by class, and the literature's generational strand, younger than the rest and growing quickly, maps the division. The repricing of claims across the period transferred wealth systematically toward those who held assets early: the cohort that bought housing before the long appreciation, whose pension claims were capitalised as rates fell, and whose working years coincided with the era of employer provision before its withdrawal. The succeeding cohorts met the financialised order at its prices: housing at the revalued level, education at the borrowed level, pensions as individual accounts begun after the great repricing had already occurred, and wages bearing the double squeeze from the start of working life. The same structure that appears, in the aggregate accounts, as a distribution among sectors appears, in the generational accounts, as a distribution among birth years, and the two distributions are one: the surplus extracted from the younger cohorts' production services, in part, the claims accumulated by the older, through the pension funds and property values whose defence is the older cohorts' rational politics.

The tradition brings to this mapping its characteristic refusal of the moralised version. Generational commentary in the period's journalism oscillates between blaming the old for hoarding and blaming the young for improvidence, and both blames mistake a structure for a character. The older cohort did not design the repricing that enriched it; it saved under the rules it was given and holds, in its claims, the only security the reprivatised settlement left it. The younger cohort's indebtedness is not a consumption choice but the price of admission to housing, education, and insured life under the same settlement. What the framework adds to the journalism is the mechanism connecting the cohorts' positions: they are counterparties. The interest the young pay is, in significant part, the income the old receive, intermediated by the funds and insurers whose margins are the circuit's cost, and a politics that set the generations against one another in ignorance of the circuit would leave the intermediation, the one element neither cohort chose, entirely out of account. The class analysis does not dissolve the generational one; it completes it, by showing the channel through which the transfer runs and the hands that take their portion as it passes.

The generational circuit has a geography as well as a chronology, since the claims and the debts are not evenly spread across the territory of any advanced economy. The appreciation of housing, the period's largest single transfer, accrued to the districts where incomes and amenities were already concentrated, while the indebtedness that financed admission to those districts, and the renting that housed those excluded from them, spread across the whole map. A young household's balance sheet thus depended on its postcode as much as on its cohort, and the literature's regional strand has begun to map the interaction: regions where the claims appreciated fastest are regions where the young are most thoroughly excluded from holding them, so that the transfer from debtors to claimants operates within each generation across space as it operates across time between them. The framework's categories handle the spatial form as readily as the temporal, since a claim is a claim wherever its holder sleeps, and the readiness is one more instance of the portability the article has treated as the mark of a theory. What the regional material adds is political texture: the financialised order's constituencies are places as well as classes, and its defence is organised, in the housing question above all, by districts whose prosperity is a capitalised expectation and whose politics is the protection of the denominator.

## The firm as its own bank: internal capital markets

Inside the largest corporate groups a further institution matured across the period that the literature on the firm has only begun to integrate with the financialisation debate: the internal capital market, through which a group's headquarters allocates funds among its divisions as a bank allocates credit among borrowers. Divisions bid for capital with projected returns; headquarters monitors, disciplines, and occasionally starves the laggards; and the group's treasury, sitting at the centre, operates the clearing system through which the divisions' surpluses and deficits net against one another. The arrangement is Hilferding's finance capital turned inward. The bank's function of socialising the allocation of capital, which the founders observed between enterprises, now operates within them, and the division manager stands to headquarters as the functioning capitalist stood to the money capitalist, surrendering a share of the division's profit as the price of its allocation and retaining the remainder as the measure of its performance. The categories travel without friction because the relation is the same relation, a division of surplus between the owner of the fund and the organiser of its employment, wearing an organisational chart instead of a loan contract.

The internal market matters to the article's argument for two reasons. It is, first, a further source of the accounting phenomenon, since the internal interest and transfer prices through which headquarters allocates are precisely the flows that consolidate away in the group's true accounts while swelling the sectoral statistics when the group's financial subsidiaries report separately. It is, second, a mechanism by which the standpoint of the claim penetrates production more deeply than any external shareholder could reach. A division managed as a borrower, against a hurdle set by the market for claims, experiences the financialisation of the whole economy as an internal discipline, and its decisions about employment, sourcing, and investment are taken under the capitalisation formula's jurisdiction without a single share changing hands. The literature on the firm and the literature on finance, long separate conversations, meet in that observation, and their meeting suggests the direction of the field's next synthesis: a theory of the financialised enterprise in which the surplus constraint is applied at the level of the division and the workshop, tracing how the division of the fund at the summit becomes the organisation of labour at the base.

The generational analysis has a counterpart inside the firm that the internal market section prepared. The division of the corporate fund between the claims of the past and the investment of the future is, in the financialised enterprise, an explicit contest: the pension obligations owed to retired workers, the distributions owed to present shareholders, and the capital expenditure that alone can generate the surplus from which both must ultimately be paid, compete in every budget the group approves. The older managerial settlement privileged the third claimant by custom, retaining earnings as a matter of course; the financialised settlement privileges the first two by governance, since both are represented by organised claimants with contractual or market power, while the future's claim is represented only by the judgement of managers whom the settlement has taught to discount it. The framework reads the contest without sentimentality in any direction. The pensioners' claims are deferred wages, as legitimate in the theory's terms as any wage; the shareholders' claims are the ordinary form of the surplus's appropriation; the future's claim is the condition of both being honoured beyond the present cycle. What the financialised governance changed is the discount rate at which the future's claim is heard, and a change in a discount rate, as the capitalisation sections showed, is a change in the present value of everything, the quietest and most consequential form of redistribution the period practised.

## Competition among capitals and the equalisation of returns

A mechanism from the classical theory, seldom invoked in this literature and quietly essential to it, explains how the fund's division among its claimants is policed: the competition of capitals, which tends to equalise the rate of return across employments by moving funds toward the higher yield and away from the lower. In Marx's presentation the tendency produces the average rate of profit, the benchmark against which each capital's earnings are measured and toward which the migration of capital continuously pushes them. Financialisation transformed the machinery of the tendency without abolishing it. Where the founders imagined capital migrating by the slow construction and abandonment of plant, the developed credit system migrates claims at the speed of a keystroke, and the equalisation now operates through portfolios: the fund manager, comparing the capitalised yields of every revenue stream across the economy, performs continuously and impersonally the allocation that competition among industrial capitals performed episodically. The consequence for the theory of distribution is that the division of surplus among interest, dividends, fees, and retained profit is not a set of separate settlements but one settlement, arbitrated by a single market in claims, in which each form of revenue must justify itself against the alternatives at the going rate. A firm that retains earnings its shareholders could place more profitably elsewhere will find its claims repriced until the retention is punished; a lender whose margin exceeds the competitive share will find borrowers refinancing away. The unity of the settlement is the deep reason the surplus constraint can treat the financial sector's income as a share: the share is not negotiated bilaterally, firm by firm, but struck in a single process whose participants experience it as the weather of yields.

The equalisation has a levelling consequence the literature exploits. Because claims migrate toward yield, the expansion of any particularly profitable financial activity competes its own returns down toward the average, and the sector's aggregate share can therefore grow only as fast as the fund and the channels allow, not as fast as ingenuity invents new instruments of extraction. Periods in which financial returns stood far above the general rate were, on this analysis, periods in which the migration was obstructed, by novelty, by regulation's categories, or by the repricing windfall of a falling denominator, and the obstruction's removal restored the tendency with the usual abruptness. The point disciplines the rhetoric of financial exceptionalism in both directions: finance is not a realm exempt from the economy's laws, earning what it pleases by cleverness, nor is it a conspiracy able to hold its share against the arbitrage of its own participants. It is a department of the division of surplus, competing for its portion under the same compulsion as every other department, and its historically enlarged portion is to be explained, as this article has explained it, by the condition of the fund and the channels, not by the virtues or villainies of its personnel.

The equalisation tendency has a distributional footnote that the period made vivid. When claims migrate freely toward yield, the owners of the scarcest and most monopolised revenues, the owners of land in the great cities, of rights in indispensable works, of positions in the infrastructures of circulation, find their revenues capitalised at the general rate and their wealth swollen by every fall in that rate, without any action of their own. The windfall is the rentier's purest income, a gain from the denominator, and its scale across the period helps explain the era's characteristic fortune, the fortune made by holding rather than by doing. The tradition's categories were built for exactly this discrimination: between the profit that organises production, the interest that shares in it, the rent that taxes it, and the capital gain that merely reprices the claims upon all three. A public discourse that calls every large fortune entrepreneurship has lost the discriminations, and with them the ability to ask which of the period's fortunes correspond to any enlargement of the social product. The literature's restoration of the discriminations, unfashionable as the vocabulary sounds, is among its most practical contributions to readers who will never cite it.

## Trust, confidence, and the sociology of the claim

Every claim is a promise, and the system of claims rests on a social achievement so familiar that the literature must work to make it visible again: the willingness of millions of strangers to hold paper, or its electronic successors, in the confidence that the revenue it promises will arrive. Marxist analysis is sometimes accused of reducing that confidence to a veil over material relations, and the accusation misses the tradition's actual treatment, in which confidence is itself a material relation, produced and maintained by identifiable machinery. The machinery includes the law's enforcement of contracts, the accounting profession's certification of the statements on which claims are priced, the credit assessment industry's continuous surveillance of issuers, and the public guarantee behind the payments system described earlier. Each element is labour, much of it highly skilled, devoted to the production of trustworthiness, and each is paid from the fund in the usual way. The sociology of the claim and the economics of the claim are therefore not rival accounts. The claim trades because it is trusted; it is trusted because an industry of verification, itself a deduction from surplus, certifies it; and the industry's growth across the period, the assessors and auditors and analysts multiplying with the claims they certify, is part of the syndrome's employment story, the unproductive labour of circulation expanding in its most characteristic modern form.

Confidence has a rhythm the theory can state without trespassing on the crisis article's ground. In the expansion of claims, each successful verification lowers the perceived need for the next, margins of caution thin, and the industry of trust finds its own standards competed down by the demand for yield, until the stock of claims embodies less verification per unit than the revenue beneath them warrants. The repricing, when it comes, is experienced as a crisis of confidence, a phrase the framework translates without remainder: the claims were capitalised on revenues that the verification machinery had ceased to examine, and the collapse of confidence is the collapse of a price toward the flow it was always, in the end, a claim upon. To say this is not to explain any particular crisis, whose determinants are concrete and belong to the companion treatment. It is to identify the standing relation between the paper structure and its foundation that makes crises of confidence a permanent possibility of the system rather than an inexplicable visitation, and to credit the tradition with having located that relation in the category of fictitious capital a century before the sociology of finance gave it a vocabulary of trust.

Trust's machinery has its own political economy, which the period exposed whenever the machinery's incentives were examined. The assessors of creditworthiness are paid by the issuers they assess; the auditors of accounts are appointed by the enterprises they audit; the analysts whose recommendations move the prices of claims are employed by houses that trade the claims. Each arrangement embeds a conflict between the production of trust and the sale of comfort, and the literature on the sector's governance documents the predictable result: verification that is rigorous in quiet times, when rigour costs no one a fee, and accommodating in the expansion, when the demand is for certification of the new claims the system is manufacturing. The framework translates the finding into its own terms without difficulty. The industry of trust is maintained from the fund and paid, in the last analysis, by the issuers of claims and the enterprises whose revenues support them, so its independence is a convention underwritten by the parties it polices, stable exactly as long as the cost of honesty is lower than the cost of the alternative. To describe the arrangement is not to allege universal corruption; it is to locate, in the circuit of payments, the reason the reckoning, when it comes, is always a surprise to the machinery built to foresee it.

## What the constraint cannot do

An article that has praised a framework's organising power owes its readers an equally explicit account of the framework's limits, lest the praise curdle into the impression of a universal key. The surplus constraint classifies revenues by source. It does not, by itself, explain the timing of any expansion or contraction, since the pressures it identifies, blockage in production, withdrawal of provision, elaboration of corporate structure, operate on timescales set by politics and technique that the classification does not govern. It does not explain the form any national financialisation takes, the relative weights of the household and corporate channels being set by institutional inheritances the theory treats as given. It does not evaluate the welfare effects of the transformation it classifies, since a redistribution can finance waste or finance the cheapening of circulation, and the classification is silent between them until further analysis is brought. And it does not prophesy: the tendency it organises can strengthen, stagnate, or recede under the pressures the reversibility section described, and the framework's composure in all three cases, which the article praised as portability, is purchased at the price of abstaining from prediction about which case will occur.

Those limits are the price of the framework's rigour, and the literature's best practitioners observe them scrupulously, presenting the constraint as the grammar of the subject rather than its encyclopaedia. Grammar does not write sentences, but no sentence in the subject can be well formed without it, and the confusions the article has spent its length correcting, the blended rows, the untraced revenues, the magnitudes without provenance, are grammatical errors in that precise sense: claims whose sources have not been declared. A reader who reaches the end of the article able to detect an undeclared source in a paragraph about finance, and to ask the question that forces its declaration, possesses the framework in the only form in which frameworks are actually possessed, as a habit of interrogation. The remainder of the article, its persona sections and its questions, is arranged to convert that habit into study, teaching, and further research, which is where habits of interrogation go to be exercised.

The limits of the constraint have one further implication that students in particular should carry away, since it governs the use of this article in argument. Because the constraint classifies rather than predicts, it cannot be refuted by an event, and a framework that no event can refute must be handled with a corresponding discipline by its own adherents, who are obliged to specify, for each application, the finding that would embarrass the application, as this article did for the redistribution answer. The obligation is the price of the framework's generality, and the tradition's internal critics perform a service when they exact it from the tradition's popularisers, in whose hands a classificatory scheme can degenerate into an incantation pronounced over any revenue whatever. The test of a Marxist analysis of finance, on the standard the article has tried to exemplify, is not whether it invokes the surplus but whether it completes the tracing, names the fund, states the discount for the accounting artefact, and attaches the defeater. Analyses that do those four things can be wrong in ways that teach; analyses that do none of them can only be applauded or denounced, and the literature's progress is measurable by the proportion of its output that submits to the first fate rather than courting the second.

## One constraint against a literature: the article's wager

Surveys of a field usually end by multiplying distinctions, and this one has multiplied its share. It is worth closing the analytical argument by recalling the wager on which the whole exposition was placed: that a single theorem, followed with sufficient obstinacy, would organise a field that presents itself as an unruly crowd. The theorem is that financial income is a share, a transfer, or a revaluation, and never a creation. Followed obstinately, it sorted the definitions by the objects they measure, derived the three answers as the only answers the framework permits, assigned each dispute to its proper tribunal of accounting, tracing, or archive, and graded the answers against evidence whose provenance the accounting controversy taught the field to declare. A reader who doubts the theorem's truth can still use the organisation it imposes, since the organisation is a set of questions, and questions do not require belief, only pertinence. Where does this revenue come from? Which fund pays it? What would the accounts look like consolidated? What finding would change the answer? A literature interrogated in that order yields its structure whether or not its premises are granted, which is the reason the article has treated the constraint as an instrument before treating it as a doctrine.

The wager has a second stake, on the reader's side of the page. Fields accumulate commentary the way harbours accumulate silt, and the student who approaches financialisation through its commentary meets a hundred positions before meeting the problem the positions answer. The article reversed the order deliberately, stating the problem before any position, on the principle that a problem understood makes positions legible at sight, while positions memorised make the problem invisible. That principle is the series' house method applied to its most contemporary subject, and its success here, if it has succeeded, is evidence for the method's portability: the same reversal, problem before positions, constraint before controversy, is available for every crowded field the reader will meet, in economics and far beyond it. The three answers will be revised; the magnitudes will be restated; authors not yet writing will enter rows of the table that this article could not foresee. The problem will remain where Marx left it, in the division of a surplus that production supplies and finance can only share, extract, or misrecord, and each new position will take its place as an answer to it, legible on arrival to any reader who learned to ask the question first.

There is a discipline of reading that the wager implies for the commentary still to come, and stating it may be the article's most durable bequest. New contributions to this field arrive monthly, in journals, in policy papers, in the financial press, and each announces itself in the vocabulary of novelty: a new channel, a new instrument of extraction, a new geography of claims. The wager's reader has a procedure for such arrivals. Before asking whether the novelty is important, ask which of the three sources it draws upon, and watch the announcement reorganise itself. A channel that moves surplus from enterprises to claimants is the first answer with new plumbing. A contract that intercepts wages is the second answer with a new counterparty. A magnitude that swells because the recording has changed is the third answer with a new convention. Genuinely new sources would be revolutionary, and the procedure's severity consists in how rarely the announcements survive it: nearly everything new in finance is new in the way a river's meanders are new, as rearrangements of a flow whose origin and destination the map already shows. The severity is not cynicism. It is what a theory is for, the conservation of attention for the arrivals that genuinely break the classification, which the defeater stands ready to recognise when one appears.

One more implication of the wager deserves a sentence before the article turns to its audiences. If the constraint is the right instrument, then the quality of a contribution to this literature can be assessed before its conclusions are known, by inspecting its method alone: whether its revenues are traced, its magnitudes anatomised, its rows kept separate, and its defeater stated. Methodological assessment of that kind is sometimes derided as formalism, yet in a field where the conclusions are contested and the data are constructions, the method is the part of a work that a reader can verify independently, and the part whose quality predicts whether the conclusions will survive the next revision of the accounts. The literature's own history is the demonstration. The contributions that still organise the field are those whose methods compelled their rivals' respect: a definition that could be operationalised, an anatomy that could be repeated, a channel that could be looked for in the loan books. Conclusions were revised around them. The article has arranged its exposition to honour that order of durability, presenting the constraint first, the answers second, and its own verdict last and most tentatively, in the expectation that the verdict will date faster than the questions it answers.

The article can therefore be read at two depths, and it is content to be judged at either. At the first depth it is a map of a literature: who holds which answer, on what evidence, against which difficulty, in a field whose journals will continue to rearrange the map's details. At the second depth it is a demonstration of a habit, the tracing of revenues to their sources, performed on the largest and most mystified body of revenues the modern economy produces. The map will date; the habit will not, because the economy will go on presenting impressive incomes whose forms conceal their origins, in sectors not yet named, under vocabularies not yet coined. A reader equipped with the habit meets those incomes the way this article met finance's: without hostility, without credulity, and with the one question that the whole tradition, from the third volume's chapters on interest to the newest consolidation exercise, exists to teach. Where does the money come from? Everything else in the field is commentary on the answers.

## What would settle the dispute: verdict and defeater

The article's verdict can now be stated with its defeater attached, in the form this series requires. The verdict: financialisation, as the Marxist literature establishes it, is a compound redistribution. Its largest component is the redirection of surplus value toward financial placements and claimants under conditions of slow accumulation, organised politically by a recomposed upper stratum and mechanically by shareholder oriented governance. Its second component is a genuine and growing extraction from wages through the financial contracts households must hold, real as a transfer whatever its final theoretical label. Its measured magnitude is inflated, substantially in the headline series, by the accounting practices of corporate groups, and every quantitative claim in the field should be read at the discount the consolidation exercises establish. The surplus constraint survives the whole enquiry untouched, because every party to the dispute, including the sceptics, argues within it: nobody in this literature has shown financial activity originating profit, and the apparent candidates dissolve on inspection into shares, transfers, or revaluations of claims.

### What evidence would defeat the redistribution answer?

Proof that aggregate profit expanded over a long stretch while the surplus generated in making commodities stood still. Redistribution only rearranges what production supplies, so a fund that grew without its source growing would falsify the answer outright, and the field would have to promote the expropriation account's stronger thesis or abandon the constraint that generates all three.

The defeater deserves its paragraph, because a verdict without one is a posture. The redistribution answer would be defeated by a demonstration that total profit rose in a period when the surplus arising from commodity production did not, a demonstration that is empirically checkable in principle, however demanding the measurement, and which the expropriation account implicitly asserts in its strong form. Short of that demonstration, the compound verdict stands on the conjunction of the syndrome's robust series with the mechanisms that explain them. Lesser findings would revise the verdict's weights rather than overturn it: a consolidation exercise that erased the entire profit shift would promote the artefact row from discount to headline; a tracing of financial firms' margins that found household counterparties dominant would promote expropriation from second component to first. The verdict is therefore a set of weights with revision rules attached, which is the most that honest social science usually achieves and considerably more than a slogan achieves.

For the reader who has travelled the whole argument, the remaining task is orientation among the sources, and the article closes its analytical work with a map in prose. Begin with the definitions and the sorting of the field in van der Zwan's survey, proceed to Krippner for the measurable claim and to Epstein's volume for the breadth of the phenomenon, then take the three answers in their canonical statements: Foster and Magdoff for stagnation and absorption, with Baran and Sweezy behind them; Lapavitsas for expropriation; Crotty and Orhangazi for the accounting correction; Arrighi and Duménil with Lévy for the historical and class strategy storeys. Behind them all stand the chapters of the third volume of Capital on interest bearing capital and fictitious capital, which reward the labour of reading them in the original sequence, since the literature's every move is a footnote to distinctions drawn there. The crisis connection, deliberately excluded from this article's scope, is developed in the marxist crisis theory guide, and the stagnation lineage in the account of [monopoly capital stagnation theory](/2014/02/01/monopoly-capital-stagnation-theory/).

Whether financialisation will be remembered as an epoch or as an episode depends on evidence no survey can supply in advance, and the article's structural discipline forbids it to pretend otherwise. What can be said is what each answer commits its holders to expect. The redistribution answer expects the financial share to track the fortunes of accumulation, subsiding if a new wave of profitable investment absorbs the surplus and swelling again at its exhaustion. The expropriation answer expects the household channel to persist as long as provision remains individualised, through booms and contractions alike, since its driver is institutional rather than cyclical. The artefact answer expects the measured phenomenon to jump at every revision of accounting standards and every settlement among jurisdictions over the taxation of groups, a prediction already repeatedly fulfilled. The class strategy variant expects the order's beneficiaries to defend its arrangements with a tenacity proportional to their income's dependence on them. A reader keeping score across the coming decades will find the four expectations a serviceable instrument, and the instrument, unlike a prophecy, improves with use, since every divergence between the expectations and the record identifies the row whose weights must be revised. That is the most a theoretical article can honestly bequeath: not a verdict on the future, but a table on which the future's evidence can be entered.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The surplus constraint teaches a transferable intellectual skill, and seminar leaders may find it the most useful export of the whole topic: it is a working example of what a framework does to a literature. Students arrive with a pile of positions and leave, if the teaching succeeds, with a question that generates the positions. The pedagogy that works, in the experience this series records, is adversarial in a specific direction: require each student to defend the row of the table they find least congenial, with Crotty's scepticism assigned to the enthusiasts and Lapavitsas's boldness to the orthodox, and grade the exercise on the accuracy with which the alien position's evidence is stated. A second exercise uses the anatomy method: distribute a published profit share figure without its provenance and require the seminar to reconstruct, from the statistical office's documentation, the imputations and boundaries inside it, an afternoon's work that permanently inoculates against the unexamined citation of aggregates. A third sets the tracing habit as fieldwork, each student following one revenue, a landlord's, a fund's, a platform's, from its appearance in an account to the fund that ultimately pays it, and reporting the chain with its uncertainties marked. The three exercises share a premise the article has argued throughout: the theory is a discipline of questions before it is a stock of answers, and it is learned by interrogating magnitudes, not by memorising the verdicts of those who interrogated them first.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The open problems of the field, stated as a research agenda, are these. The tracing of financial firms' margins to their counterparties, enterprises against households, remains the decisive unperformed measurement for the relative weights of the first two answers, and it awaits a researcher with access to the supervisory data in which the counterparty composition of the loan and fee books is recorded. The consolidation exercises need extension across economies and decades, so that the artefact discount can be stated as a schedule rather than as a warning. The generational accounts need construction on the same principles as the sectoral ones, allocating the claims and the debts by cohort, so that the transfer the article described can be measured rather than inferred. Journalists covering the sector can serve the agenda immediately, and serve their readers, by adopting the article's two rules of provenance: never publish a financial share without the treatment of non-financial groups' financial income, and never describe a financial income as created without naming the fund it was drawn from. Historians will find the archives of the recomposition, the governance reforms, the internal memoranda of the treasury departments, and the professionalisation of the claims industry, largely unworked, and the class strategy thesis will stand or fall, in its next decade, by what those archives show about the weight of design against the weight of pressure. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is financialisation?

It is the name for a long shift in how capitalist economies operate, in which financial motives, markets, institutions, and earnings carry steadily more weight. Gerald Epstein's widely used formulation dates from 2005 and stresses that rising role across domestic and international economies. Greta Krippner offered a tighter version the same year, pointing to accumulation in which profit arrives mainly through financial channels instead of through trade and making things. Marxist writers add two emphases: the swelling portion of aggregate profit booked as financial, and the spread of borrowing, pension, and insurance relations through the everyday finances of households.

### Q: Can financialisation create profit?

Within the theory this article expounds, no, and the negative is the hinge of the whole subject. A lender's interest is carved out of the borrowing enterprise's profit. A bank's margin, a fund's fee, a trader's gain: each is either a slice of surplus value that production generated, a sum taken from somebody's revenue, most consequentially from wages, or a paper revaluation of claims whose settlement awaits output still to come. Because those exhaust the possibilities, any Marxist treatment of the phenomenon is forced to declare which of the three it is describing, and the literature sorts itself accordingly.

### Q: What is fictitious capital?

Marx's label in the third volume of Capital for paper titles whose prices are set by capitalising an anticipated flow of payments, and which therefore trade as if they were themselves capital. A state bond illustrates the pure form: the money raised was spent long ago, nothing productive corresponds to the certificate, yet the certificate commands a price and yields its holder a revenue drawn from taxation. Shares mix the form with substance, since an enterprise's plant genuinely exists even though the share price, being a capitalisation of expected earnings, wanders far from the plant's value. Fraud is not implied; the fiction concerns what the paper represents.

### Q: What is financial expropriation?

Costas Lapavitsas's name for a pattern he argues became structural: financial institutions drawing income straight from workers, outside the workplace, through the terms of their personal borrowing and saving. His 2013 book Profiting Without Producing sets out the case. As employers and public schemes retreated from providing housing loans, retirement income, and insurance cover, households had to buy those things in the market, and the resulting interest, charges, and margins became a dependable transfer from pay packets to finance. Whether that transfer deserves promotion to an independent source of profit, alongside exploitation in production, is the point his Marxist critics dispute.

### Q: Is the shift to finance an accounting artefact?

In part, and the size of that part is the field's standing quarrel. Work by James Crotty and by Özgür Orhangazi demonstrated that a large slice of the financial earnings attributed to industrial groups is generated inside their own finance subsidiaries and treasury companies, or follows from where groups choose to book their profits, so the headline migration of profit toward finance exaggerates any underlying change. Yet the correction has limits. Debts owed by households are contractual facts, the sector's payroll is counted in people, and risks once carried collectively now sit on family balance sheets; none of that is a bookkeeping illusion.

### Q: How does Marx treat interest?

As a subdivision of profit rather than a reward for a separate factor. One capitalist owns money and advances it; another borrows it, sets it to work, and hands back a portion of the resulting profit as the price of the loan. What the borrower keeps, Marx calls profit of enterprise; what the lender takes is interest, and the boundary between them shifts with competition and custom, there being no natural rate. The arrangement also explains money's most misleading appearance, seeming to multiply by itself, when in truth the increment was produced by labour and merely assigned, by contract, to the owner of the funds.

### Q: What is the surplus absorption account?

It is the explanation descended from Baran and Sweezy's Monopoly Capital of 1966, later carried into the study of finance by John Bellamy Foster and Fred Magdoff. Concentrated industry, they argued, throws off a surplus larger than its profitable outlets: new capacity would glut markets, and neither owners' consumption nor public waste reliably closes the gap. Funds denied a home in expansion therefore accumulate as financial placements, bidding up claims and swelling the earnings booked in financial form. On this reading the swelling is a symptom, the visible sign of an underlying blockage, and its returns remain shares of a surplus the placements did nothing to create.

### Q: Why did the financial sector grow?

Different writers weight the causes differently, and the article separates the level at which each cause works. The deepest, for the stagnation school, is a surplus in search of placement as profitable investment lagged. The most concrete, for Lapavitsas, is the arrival of households as finance's direct customers once collective provision receded. The most sceptical, for the accounting critics, is that groups reorganised themselves so that more of their income was recorded in financial shape. Around those sources operated plain mechanisms: savings pooled in pension funds, balance sheets stretched across borders, loans repackaged for resale, managers paid in equity, and computing cheap enough to administer claims in vast numbers.

### Q: What evidence supports each account of financialisation?

Each leaves a different fingerprint, which is what makes the dispute empirical rather than rhetorical. Stagnation based redistribution predicts, and finds, feeble investment beside generous payouts to shareholders across the expansion decades in a number of advanced economies. Expropriation predicts, and finds, household indebtedness climbing against income and reaching down the income scale, together with retirement and insurance provision moving onto individual contracts. The artefact thesis predicts, and finds, that consolidating corporate groups shrinks the apparent migration of profit considerably. A finding that fits one fingerprint while violating another is the kind of result that rearranges the field's weights.

### Q: What would settle the financialisation dispute?

Chiefly two pieces of measurement that the published data still withhold. Analysts need the counterparty composition of the financial sector's earnings, how much margin comes from lending to and charging enterprises, which is redistribution of profit, against how much comes from households, which is deduction from wages. They also need consolidated accounts for corporate groups across economies and decades, so the bookkeeping component can be priced once and for all. Behind those stands a sterner test of the framework itself: proof that aggregate profit climbed through a long spell in which the surplus value arising from commodity production stagnated would overturn the redistribution reading outright.

### Q: How does the surplus constraint grade the accounts of financialisation?

It grades them the way an examiner grades scripts, against the question each was set: name the fund your financial profit is drawn from. Redistribution passes with the strongest evidence and a deduction for relying, in its early statements, on profit shares that consolidation later discounted. Expropriation passes on the existence of its channel and is marked down on its boldest claim, that the channel amounts to a new source of profit rather than a new route of transfer. The artefact account passes as a correction and fails as a complete explanation, since too much of the transformation survives its adjustments. All three marks are provisional, and the article names the findings that would change them.

### Q: What should a student separate between creating profit and redistributing it?

The habit to acquire is following money back to whoever ultimately pays it, and asking out of which fund they pay. A fee, a margin, a dividend, and an interest payment all arrive looking like earnings; the student's task is to refuse the appearance and trace the payment to profit made in an enterprise, to a wage already handed over, or to a valuation that only future production can redeem. Only the first of those journeys ends in creation, and even there the creating was done by labour in production, not by the financial operation that collected its share. Answers that perform the tracing explicitly, step by step, are the ones examiners reward.

### Q: Why must financial profit trace back to production?

Because, in this theory, production is the sole place where the value available for distribution comes into being. Labour set to work under capital yields more value than its own upkeep costs, and that excess is the fund from which every property income is subsequently paid. Lending assigns part of it to a creditor, fees skim part of it for services of circulation, dividends distribute part of it to owners, and asset gains merely reprice the right to receive parts of it later. Follow any financial receipt backwards through the hands it passed through and the chain ends at that excess, or at a wage, which is itself paid from an enterprise's proceeds.

### Q: How does the three answers table order the explanations?

By forcing every explanation to declare a birthplace for financial profit, and then seating them according to the declaration. One row gives redistribution: the money is surplus value that production supplied and stagnation diverted. A second gives expropriation: some of the money is pay that financial contracts intercepted on its way through household budgets. A third gives measurement: some of the apparent money never moved at all, being industrial earnings dressed in financial form by group accounting. A fourth row registers the class strategy variant, which accepts the first row's birthplace and adds an account of who arranged the diversion and pocketed its advantages.

### Q: What separates fictitious capital from productive capital?

Where the value resides. Productive capital is actually at work: wages advanced, materials consumed, machinery turning, commodities emerging to be sold at a profit. A fictitious capital is a deed to payments expected later, valued by dividing those payments by the going return on money, and it can be bought, sold, pledged, and inherited without anything being produced anywhere in consequence. The two can move in opposite directions for years, paper fortunes doubling while the stock of plant stagnates, which is precisely why the distinction matters: it explains how an economy's recorded wealth can balloon, and later deflate, with no corresponding change in its capacity to make things.
