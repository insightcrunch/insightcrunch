Most students write their Gatsby essay for the reader they imagine, and the reader they imagine is generous. That imaginary reader admires the effort, forgives the drift, notices the hours of revision behind the work, and finds something kind to say about every page. Understanding how examiners mark Gatsby essays begins by replacing that fantasy with the reality of the marking table. The real reader is a trained assessor working through a long stack of scripts in limited time, measuring each one against published criteria that name exactly what scores: argument, analysis, evidence, and context. The mindset of that reader decides the mark long before the final paragraph has been read, and it rewards a narrower set of things than most students expect.

![An examiner reading a student Gatsby essay at a desk, with the published mark scheme and annotated script pages showing how argument and analysis are rewarded](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The claim of this article is simple enough to state in one sentence and consequential enough to change how a student writes: writing to the mark scheme, not against it, is the single most reliable way to move an essay across a grade boundary. Everything else in these pages serves that claim. The gap between what feels impressive to a writer and what scores with a marker is the whole subject here, and the gap is wider than it looks. Effort is real and admirable, and the mark scheme does not pay for it. Length is visible and reassuring, and the criteria do not count words. A beautifully decorated paragraph that never argues will lose to a plain paragraph that argues cleanly, because the examiner is not judging the essay as a performance of diligence. The examiner is judging it as an answer to a question, weighed against a published standard that the student was always allowed to read.

That last fact deserves emphasis, because it is the most underused advantage in examination writing. The mark scheme is not a secret. The criteria that decide bands are published, and they describe in plain language what each level of response looks like. An examiner marking a Gatsby essay does not invent the standard on the spot, and does not reward essays for reminding the marker of the standard's existence. The examiner applies the standard. A student who knows the standard can therefore write toward it deliberately: put the argument where the criterion for argument looks for it, make the analysis do the work the criterion for analysis describes, choose evidence the way the criterion for evidence rewards, and handle context in the manner the criterion for context specifies. None of this is gaming the system. The system was designed to be legible. Writing to it is what it is for.

The complication that gives this article its shape is the stubborn assumption that marks reward effort or length. It is an understandable assumption. School rewards effort everywhere else: attendance, homework, revision hours, the visible labor of preparation. An examination essay looks like the place where all that labor should finally be paid out, and a long essay looks like proof that the labor happened. So students write long, cover everything, decorate the prose, and wait for the reward. The marker, meanwhile, is reading for something else entirely: a position taken on the question, defended through the text, with the writer's own thinking doing the heavy lifting. When the two readers meet, the writer's and the marker's, the mismatch costs marks that the writer never knows were available. This article exists to close that mismatch, by describing the marking mindset accurately, from the first read-through to the final band decision, so that every writing choice a student makes can be aimed at what is actually rewarded.

It helps to name what the examiner does not do, because the myths cluster there. The examiner does not compare scripts against each other, ranking the pile from best to worst; each script is measured against the published descriptors, so a strong essay is never punished for landing in a strong cohort. The examiner does not reward mystery, the sense that something clever is happening beneath unclear prose; unclear prose hides the thinking the criteria need to see, and hidden thinking is uncredited thinking. The examiner does not credit intention, the good idea the writer meant to express but did not; the mark scheme prices the sentence on the page, not the thought behind it. And the examiner does not, despite the suspicion that shadows every results day, mark to a quota or curve. The bands are absolute, described in words, and a script that meets the words gets the band. That absoluteness is the student's greatest ally. It means the target does not move with the competition, the marker's mood, or the time of day. It sits still, in print, waiting to be written toward.

## How Examiners Mark Gatsby Essays Against a Published Standard

The marking process has a shape, and knowing its shape explains half of what seems mysterious about results. An examiner does not read an essay the way a teacher reads coursework. The teacher knows the student, has watched the thinking develop across a term, and can credit the intention behind a muddled sentence. The examiner knows nothing except the script and the question, and credits only what the script achieves on the page. That sounds colder than it is. It is really a discipline: the mark must be defensible, which means it must be tied to evidence in the script and to language in the published criteria. Every mark an examiner gives has to survive a second reader, a moderation sample, or an appeal, and the only way a mark survives is if the examiner can point to the sentence where the argument tightened, the passage where the analysis deepened, or the moment where the answer stopped answering.

### How does an examiner read an essay differently from a teacher?

An examiner reads for what the script demonstrates against published criteria, not for what the student intended or how hard the student worked. The teacher reads with memory of the term; the marker reads with the mark scheme open beside the script, scanning for argument, analysis, evidence, and context, and awarding credit only where those appear on the page.

The difference shows up in the first minute of reading. A teacher reading coursework slows down at a promising sentence, makes a marginal note of encouragement, and carries a sense of the student's trajectory into the judgment. An examiner performing the first read-through is doing something narrower and more disciplined: forming a provisional band impression. The question in the examiner's mind is not whether the essay is good in some general sense. The question is which band of the published criteria the script inhabits at that point, and what would have to change for it to move. That framing sounds mechanical, and in one sense it is, because consistency across thousands of scripts demands a common frame. But it is not mindless. The best examiners describe the process as listening for the essay's thinking: does this writer have a position, or only a topic; does the evidence serve the position, or merely accompany it; does the analysis reach the language of the text, or stop at its plot.

The practical consequence for a writer is that the essay must make its qualities unmissable. A teacher will hunt for merit in a student's work, because the teacher's job includes developing it. An examiner will not hunt. If the argument is buried in the third paragraph, the examiner may never find the band the essay deserved. If the analysis is present but tangled inside summary, the examiner reads summary, because the criterion for analysis looks for the writer's own interpretive work and summary is not that work. This is why the standard advice about signposting and topic sentences matters so much more than students believe. It is not about pleasing a fussy reader. It is about making the script's thinking legible to a reader who is reading fast, reading fairly, and reading only what is on the page.

Picture the same essay landing on two desks. On the teacher's desk, a muddled sentence in the third paragraph gets the benefit of the doubt, because the teacher remembers the sharp comment the student made in class last Tuesday and credits the thinking behind the sentence. On the examiner's desk, the same sentence gets exactly what it contains, because there is no last Tuesday in the marking room. This is not unfairness. It is the condition that makes fairness possible across thousands of scripts written by strangers. The teacher's knowledge is an advantage for the known student and would be an injustice to the unknown one, so the examination removes it, and what remains is the discipline of the page. Students who understand this stop writing sentences that rely on being understood and start writing sentences that cannot be misunderstood, which is a higher standard and, paradoxically, the kinder one: the essay that needs no charitable reading is the essay no uncharitable reading can harm.

### What does the first read-through actually decide?

The first read-through decides the provisional band, the rough territory of the mark, and everything after it is adjustment rather than discovery. An examiner forms that impression from the opening, the shape of the argument, and the density of genuine analysis, then spends the rest of the script confirming, refining, or revising it.

Walk through the marking of a script the way examiners describe it. The examiner reads the question, then reads the opening paragraph, and by the end of that paragraph a hypothesis has formed. A clear, contestable thesis that answers the question suggests the upper bands. A restatement of the question, or a tour of the topic, suggests the middle. A paragraph of scene-setting with no position suggests the lower bands. The hypothesis is provisional, and good examiners hold it lightly, because a weak opening can recover and a strong opening can collapse. But the hypothesis organizes everything that follows. As the examiner reads on, each paragraph is weighed as confirmation or complication: here the argument sharpens, which confirms; here the writer drifts into retelling the Plaza confrontation without making it argue, which complicates; here the analysis of Daisy's voice as "full of money" reads the metaphor instead of repeating it, which confirms strongly.

The second pass, if the examiner makes one, is where the fine adjustment happens. Borderline scripts get the most attention here, because the difference between the top of one band and the bottom of the next is the difference that appeals and grade boundaries are made of. The examiner re-reads the passages that seemed strongest and asks the hard question the criteria demand: is this analysis or is it description wearing analytical vocabulary. A sentence can use all the right words, technique, effect, significance, and still only describe. The marker learns to hear the difference the way a musician hears intonation, and the second read is where that hearing is applied most carefully. For the writer, the lesson is that the opening buys attention but the middle of the essay decides the band. An essay that opens brilliantly and then coasts will be revised downward. An essay that opens plainly and then thinks harder with every paragraph will be revised upward, because the examiner's final decision answers one question: what level of thinking does this script sustain.

The physical trace of that decision is the annotation, and understanding what examiners write in the margin explains what they value. Markers do not annotate everything. They mark the moments where the criteria are met or missed: a tick or a brief note where the thesis sharpens, where a quotation gets read rather than repeated, where context lands inside the analysis. Long stretches of summary get no annotation at all, and the absence is itself the judgment. Experienced markers describe the pattern of a strong script's margins as busy in the right places, clusters of ticks around the analytical sentences, and the pattern of a weak script's margins as empty where it matters. The student will never see these marks, but writing as if the margin is watching changes drafting priorities. Every paragraph should contain at least one annotatable moment, a sentence where the thesis advances, a word read closely, a piece of evidence doing structural work. A paragraph with no such moment is a paragraph the examiner reads without the pencil moving, and paragraphs like that, accumulated across an essay, are what a middle-band mark looks like from the inside.

## The Criteria an Examiner Marks Against

Published criteria differ in their wording from one board and one specification to another, but the architecture underneath is remarkably stable, and it is the architecture that matters for a writer. Nearly every literature mark scheme assesses some version of the same five things: the quality of the argument, the depth of the analysis, the selection and use of evidence, the handling of context, and the clarity of expression. The labels move. One scheme calls the first criterion response to the task, another calls it conceptual argument, another calls it thesis and line of reasoning. The underlying demand does not move: the essay must take a position on the question and defend it. A writer who understands the five criteria as five jobs the essay must perform, rather than five hoops to jump through, has understood the marking mindset in its most useful form.

| Criterion | What the examiner rewards | What the examiner penalizes |
|---|---|---|
| Argument | A clear, contestable thesis that answers the set question; a line of reasoning that develops across paragraphs; each paragraph advancing the case rather than restating it | Restating the question as the thesis; listing points that never connect; paragraphs that could be reordered without loss; a conclusion that introduces a new argument |
| Analysis | Close reading of the writer's own: how specific words, images, and structures produce effects; technique connected to meaning; the novel's language doing argumentative work | Plot summary with analytical vocabulary sprinkled over it; technique named but not read; assertions about effect with no textual mechanism shown |
| Evidence | Well-chosen, precisely placed quotations or textual references; evidence embedded in the writer's sentences; each reference serving the argument of its paragraph | Long block quotations left to speak for themselves; quotation clusters with no commentary; evidence that illustrates a point the paragraph never makes |
| Context | Relevant background integrated where it sharpens the reading: the novel's concerns, its moment, its genre; context that changes what a passage means | Bolted-on historical paragraphs; context recited for its own sake; biographical detail that never touches the argument; context as decoration |
| Expression | Clear, controlled academic prose; precise vocabulary; sentences that carry complex thought without tangling; technical terms used accurately | Obscurity mistaken for sophistication; misused critical vocabulary; sentences that collapse under their own weight; errors that block meaning |

The table is worth studying the way an examiner studies the mark scheme: as a map of where credit lives. Notice what the two columns have in common. Every reward in the left-hand column is something the writer does, an action performed on the page. Every penalty in the right-hand column is the absence of that action, or its counterfeit. The mark scheme does not reward knowing things about the novel. It rewards doing things with that knowledge. That distinction, between possessing material and performing operations on it, is the single most important sentence in this article for a student to absorb. Everything the examiner credits is a verb: argues, analyzes, selects, integrates, expresses. The nouns, the facts about Gatsby, the quotations memorized, the context revised, are the raw material on which those verbs operate. An essay rich in nouns and poor in verbs is exactly the essay that feels impressive to write and disappoints at marking.

The band descriptors themselves reward study, because they describe the escalation from one level to the next in the language of verbs. Read any published set of descriptors from bottom to top and the movement is audible: lower bands describe scripts that describe, narrate, and assert; middle bands describe scripts that begin to argue, with some analysis and some use of evidence; upper bands describe scripts where argument is sustained, analysis is detailed and integrated, evidence is well selected, and the response engages with the question's full complexity. Notice that the escalation is never about more. No descriptor asks for more points, more quotations, or more context. Every step upward is about deeper and more connected: the same material, worked harder. A student who internalizes that movement stops asking what to add and starts asking what to deepen. The paragraph on the green light does not need a second symbol or a third quotation to move up. It needs its one quotation read more closely, its claim made more disputable, its development pushed one consequence further. Depth is the currency of the bands, and the descriptors are the exchange rate, published for anyone willing to read them.

### How do examiners weigh argument against analysis?

Argument sets the ceiling and analysis decides where inside the room the essay stands. A strong thesis with thin analysis cannot reach the top, and deep analysis without a thesis has nowhere to go; examiners reward the two together, with sustained argued analysis across the essay marking the highest bands.

This is the pairing students misunderstand most often, because the two words sound like synonyms and the mark scheme treats them as partners. Think of argument as the essay's skeleton and analysis as its muscle. The skeleton gives the shape: this essay claims that Gatsby's dream is self-defeating because the very intensity of his longing makes its object unreachable, and each paragraph will test that claim against a different part of the novel. The muscle does the work: the reading of "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us" that shows recession built into the grammar of desire, the account of the green light that makes it an emblem of permanent deferral rather than simple hope. An examiner rewarding argument is asking whether the essay knows what it thinks. An examiner rewarding analysis is asking whether the essay can show its thinking working on the text.

The band descriptors make the partnership explicit in the way they escalate. Lower bands describe essays that describe or narrate. Middle bands describe essays that begin to argue, with some analysis of language or structure. Upper bands describe essays where argument and analysis sustain each other across the whole response: the thesis is not just stated but tested, complicated, and refined, and the analysis is not just present but directed, serving the argument rather than wandering beside it. A writer aiming at the top therefore has two jobs, not one. The planning stage is where the argument gets built: the contestable claim, the paragraph order that develops it, the through-line that the conclusion can genuinely conclude. The drafting stage is where the analysis gets done: the slow reading of the chosen passages, the attention to what specific words do, the refusal to let a quotation pass without saying what its language achieves. Students who plan without drafting produce skeletons. Students who draft without planning produce muscles with no bones. The examiner rewards the body whole.

A concrete way to feel the partnership is to watch it fail in each direction. Take an essay with a sharp thesis, that Gatsby's parties are performances of longing rather than celebrations, but whose paragraphs never read a single line closely, only summarize the guest lists and the music. The examiner credits the argument and finds nothing for the analysis criterion to hold, and the script stalls in the middle bands, all skeleton. Now take the reverse: an essay that reads the shirts scene beautifully, lingering on the language of the cascading clothes and what the excess of fabric says about the excess of desire, but that never turns the reading into a position on the question. The examiner credits the analysis and finds no argument for it to serve, and the script stalls in the same place, all muscle. The top band belongs to the essay that does both at once, where the shirt reading proves the thesis about performance and the thesis gives the shirt reading its direction. Neither half is optional, and neither half can substitute for the other, which is why the mark scheme lists them as separate criteria and the examiner's pencil looks for each in turn.

### Where does evidence earn marks and where does it stop earning them?

Evidence earns marks when it is selected to serve an argument and integrated into the writer's own sentences; it stops earning the moment it merely proves the writer has read the novel. Examiners credit the choosing and the placing, not the possessing: a short phrase doing argumentative work outscores a long passage left uncommented.

Consider two uses of the same famous line. In the first, a paragraph about Daisy's charm opens with the observation that her voice is "full of money," and then the writer spends three sentences on what that fullness does: how the preposition makes wealth something contained and carried, how the synesthesia turns a moral fact about Daisy into a sensory one, how the image explains why Gatsby hears promise in a sound that Nick hears as corruption. In the second, the same quotation appears at the end of a paragraph that has already said Daisy is attractive to Gatsby because she is rich, and the quotation sits there like a stamp of approval on a point already made. The examiner rewards the first use generously and the second barely at least, because the first use shows the writer thinking with the text and the second shows the writer decorating a thought the text did not need to supply.

The practical rules follow from that distinction. Choose short over long: a phrase can be read closely, while a passage can only be gestured at, and close reading is what the analysis criterion pays for. Embed rather than drop: fold the quotation into the grammar of the writer's sentence so that removing it would break the sentence, which proves the evidence is load-bearing rather than ornamental. Never let a quotation end a paragraph's thinking: the sentence after the quotation should always say what the quotation's language does, because the examiner's eye, trained by thousands of scripts, goes straight to the sentence after the quote to check whether analysis follows or the writer has moved on. And select with the argument in mind: the best evidence for a paragraph is not the most famous line on the topic but the line whose specific wording lets the paragraph's claim be proved. Fame is not a criterion. Fit is.

Selection deserves one more principle, because students choose quotations the way collectors choose objects, for rarity and display value, when they should choose them the way lawyers choose witnesses, for what each one can prove. Before committing to a quotation, state in one clause what the paragraph needs proved: that longing is bodily, that wealth corrupts perception, that the narration both enchants and judges. Then ask which line's wording proves exactly that, and no more. The famous lines are famous because they prove many things at once, which makes them tempting and often wrong for the paragraph, since a quotation that proves everything proves nothing in particular. The unfamous line, the half-sentence from a party scene or a fragment of Nick's narration, often fits the paragraph's need precisely, and precision of fit is what lets the analysis be specific rather than general. Examiners notice the fit. A quotation that slots exactly into its paragraph's claim tells the marker the writer chose with the argument in mind, and the evidence criterion rewards that choosing as one of the highest skills in the subject. Collect lines for the argument, not for the collection, and the evidence will always earn.

## Why Effort and Length Do Not Score

This is the complication the article promised to address head-on, and it deserves its own room because the assumption it attacks is so deeply rooted. Students arrive at the examination hall carrying an economy of effort: hours in, marks out. The economy feels just. It is also, at the marking table, beside the point. An examiner cannot see the hours. An examiner sees only the script, and the published criteria describe what the script must do, not what the student must have suffered to produce it. None of this denies that effort matters causally. The student who revises thoroughly usually writes the better essay, because revision builds the knowledge and the fluency that argued analysis requires. But the marks are awarded to the analysis, not to the revision that made it possible. Effort is the cause; the criterion rewards the effect. Confusing the two is like expecting the examiner to pay for the scaffolding after admiring the building.

Length fails for a related but distinct reason. A long essay feels like a strong essay to the person writing it, because writing long is hard and the difficulty feels like quality. To the examiner reading the fortieth script of the day, length is neutral at best and suspicious at worst. The criteria contain no word count and no page target. What they contain are bands described by qualities of thought, and a quality of thought can be demonstrated in fewer words or obscured in more. Experienced markers develop a reliable instinct about padding: the paragraph that repeats the introduction in new words, the third example that adds nothing the second example did not establish, the historical detour that never touches the argument. None of it is penalized directly. It is simply not rewarded, and it costs the writer the time that could have been spent on the analysis the criteria actually price.

### How does an examiner spot padding in a long script?

An examiner spots padding by checking whether each paragraph performs a criterion verb: the restated introduction, the third example that adds nothing, the detour that never touches the argument. Thousands of scripts train the eye to see filler instantly, and none of it is rewarded, which is why length never impresses at the marking table.

There is a revealing exercise examiners sometimes describe: take a long mid-band essay and cut every sentence that does not advance the argument or analyze the text. What remains is usually a short essay, and its band is usually the band the long version received, because the band was always decided by the thinking, and the thinking was always the short version's size. The cut sentences were never earning. They were the writer thinking aloud, circling the point, restating the introduction, adding a third illustration where two had sufficed. All of that labor is understandable under time pressure. None of it is creditworthy. The student who learns to hear the difference between a sentence that works and a sentence that fills can convert length into density, and density is what the top bands describe: sustained, economical, every paragraph pulling its weight.

The positive version of the same lesson is that brevity is not penalized either. No examiner has ever lowered a mark because an essay finished early, provided the essay answered the question fully. A complete argument in fewer words is not a thin essay; it is an efficient one, and efficiency reads as control, which the expression criterion rewards. Students should therefore stop asking how long the essay should be and start asking whether each paragraph does a job. If a paragraph advances the thesis, analyzes its evidence, and connects to its neighbors, it has earned its place regardless of its length. If it does none of those things, no length will save it. The mark scheme is indifferent to volume. It is attentive to work.

### How should revision be aimed so effort becomes marks?

Aim every revision task at a verb the criteria price: turn a descriptive paragraph into an argued one, replace a dropped quotation with an embedded phrase, integrate context where it sharpens a passage. Effort aimed at nouns, more quotes memorized, more background learned, stays invisible; effort aimed at verbs becomes analysis on the page.

This is the kindest possible framing of a hard truth, and it matters because the alternative framing breeds resentment. A student who worked for weeks and received a middling mark often concludes that the marking was unfair, that the examiner failed to see the effort. The examiner saw everything the script contained. What the script contained was the effect of the effort, and the effect fell short of the criteria. Perhaps the revision memorized context that never got integrated, so the context criterion found nothing to reward. Perhaps the hours went into learning quotations that were then dropped in without analysis, so the evidence criterion paid little. The tragedy, if that is the word, is misdirected labor: effort spent on the things the mark scheme does not price, while the things it does price, argued analysis sustained across the essay, got the leftover hours.

The constructive response is to let the criteria direct the effort. Every hour of revision can be aimed at a verb the examiner rewards. An hour spent turning a descriptive paragraph into an argued one, finding the contestable claim inside the summary and rebuilding the paragraph around it, is an hour the mark scheme will repay. An hour spent memorizing a fifth quotation for a paragraph that already has four is an hour spent on a noun when the verbs are hungry. Students who internalize this do not work less. They work with the marking mindset beside them, asking of every revision task the question the examiner will ask of the script: what will this make the essay do that the criteria reward. Effort aimed that way stops being invisible, because it becomes analysis, argument, and evidence on the page, which is the only form in which effort can ever be marked.

The misdirected version has a recognizable inventory, and students should audit their own revision against it honestly. Hours spent memorizing long quotations that will be dropped in whole, rather than short phrases that can be embedded and read. Hours spent learning background that will be recited in a freestanding paragraph, rather than background paired with the passages it sharpens. Hours spent writing full practice essays that are never marked against the criteria, so the same unrewarded habits get rehearsed instead of corrected. Hours spent perfecting introductions while the middle paragraphs, where the band is decided, remain tours. None of this labor is lazy. All of it is aimed at the wrong target, at nouns instead of verbs, at possession instead of performance. The audit that fixes it is simple: for every revision task, name the criterion verb it serves. If the task serves none, replace it with one that does. An hour converting a descriptive paragraph into an argued one, finding the contestable claim and rebuilding around it, serves argument and analysis directly. An hour adding a fifth quotation to a paragraph that already has four serves nothing the mark scheme prices. The difference between those two hours is the difference between effort the examiner can see and effort the examiner never will.

## Two Paragraphs, Two Marks: A Marker-Readthrough Walkthrough

Abstract advice about criteria becomes concrete the moment it is applied to actual prose, so this section does what an examiner does: reads two paragraphs and decides what each earns. Both paragraphs answer the same imagined question about the green light, and both are written by competent students who know the novel. The difference between them is the difference between description that decorates and analysis that argues, and walking through the marking of each shows exactly where the marks are won and lost. Read them the way a marker reads: with the criteria open, crediting only what the prose performs.

The first paragraph is the kind that fills many scripts. It is fluent, knowledgeable, and sincere, and it demonstrates everything except the thinking the criteria price.

*The green light is one of the most famous symbols in the novel. It sits at the end of Daisy's dock and Gatsby stares at it across the water, reaching out his arms toward it in the opening chapters. The light represents Gatsby's hopes and dreams for the future, especially his dream of being reunited with Daisy. Fitzgerald describes it as "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us," which shows how Gatsby's dream keeps moving away from him. The green light therefore symbolizes the American Dream and how it is impossible to achieve, which is one of the main themes of the novel.*

Now mark it. The argument criterion finds no contestable position: the paragraph announces that the light is a famous symbol of hope and the American Dream, which no reader would dispute, so there is nothing to defend and nothing is defended. The analysis criterion finds the quotation deployed as illustration: "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us" arrives after the point has already been made, and the sentence following it, "which shows how Gatsby's dream keeps moving away from him," restates the quotation's meaning rather than reading its language. No word is examined. No technique is connected to an effect. The evidence criterion notes the quotation is relevant and accurately remembered, which earns the paragraph its modest credit, but the quotation is dropped in rather than embedded, and it proves a point the paragraph had already asserted without it. Context is absent, which is not a fault in a single paragraph, but the paragraph's reach for "the American Dream" gestures at context without integrating any. Expression is clear and controlled, the best-performing criterion here. An examiner would place this paragraph in the middle bands: competent, knowledgeable, fluent, and almost entirely undescribed by the verbs the top bands require. It tells the marker what the student knows. It never shows the student thinking.

The second paragraph answers the same question, and every sentence is doing criterion work.

*The green light matters less as a symbol of hope than as a mechanism for making hope permanent and satisfaction impossible. Fitzgerald builds that mechanism into the grammar of the novel's most famous sentence about it: "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us." The adjective "orgastic" makes longing bodily and urgent, while the verb "recedes" makes retreat the future's habitual action, year by year, so that the sentence's own rhythm enacts the deferral it describes. Gatsby's outstretched arms in the opening chapters reach toward a light whose defining property, by the novel's end, is distance: the closer the prose looks, the further the object moves. The light is therefore not an emblem of the American Dream but of the dream's structure, a desire organized around an object that must stay unreachable to stay desirable.*

Mark this one with the same criteria. Argument: the opening sentence takes a position a reader could dispute, that the light is a mechanism rather than an emblem, and every subsequent sentence serves that position. Analysis: the paragraph reads specific words, "orgastic" and "recedes," says what each does, and connects the sentence's rhythm to its meaning, which is technique tied to effect in the exact manner the criterion describes. Evidence: the quotation is embedded inside the writer's grammar, load-bearing rather than ornamental, and the reference to the outstretched arms is placed to serve the paragraph's claim about distance. Context: the closing gesture toward the dream's structure integrates the novel's larger concern without a bolted-on history lesson. Expression: the sentences carry complex thought without tangling. An examiner places this paragraph at the top, not because it is longer or more decorated, but because every criterion finds the verb it is looking for. The comparison is the whole lesson in miniature. Same knowledge, same novel, same question. Different verbs. Different band.

Set the two paragraphs side by side as the examiner experiences them, and the difference becomes a rhythm the marker feels in the pencil. In the first paragraph, the pencil lifts after the opening sentence and does not touch down again until the quotation, where it makes a small mark for relevance, then lifts for the rest. The margin at the paragraph's end holds one lonely tick. In the second paragraph, the pencil touches down at the opening claim, again at the embedded quotation, again at the reading of "orgastic," again at the reading of "recedes," and again at the closing development. The margin fills with the small, quick marks of criteria being met. No single tick decides the band. The accumulation does, because the accumulation is the visible form of sustained thinking, and sustained is the word the top-band descriptors keep using. Students sometimes imagine the examiner weighing the essay's intelligence in some holistic, mysterious way. The reality is more mechanical and more hopeful: the mark is the sum of the moments the pencil moved, and the writer controls how many such moments the essay contains. Every disputable claim, every embedded quotation, every word read closely is a touch of the pencil. Write an essay full of them, and the band takes care of itself.

### What does a rewarded paragraph look like to a marker?

A rewarded paragraph opens with a disputable claim, embeds its evidence inside sentences that would break without it, spends its middle reading specific words for what they do, and closes by spending the reading on the argument. The marker's pen moves in the margin at each of those moments, because each one matches language in the published criteria.

Notice the sequence of the second paragraph above, because it is a portable template rather than a lucky accident. The opening claim does two jobs at once: it answers the question and it creates an obligation, since a claim about mechanism must now show a mechanism. The evidence arrives inside the writer's sentence, which means the paragraph cannot retreat into summary; the quotation is structural, and the analysis must therefore engage with it. The middle sentences perform the slow reading the analysis criterion prices: one word at a time, each word's work named, each naming tied back to the paragraph's claim. The closing sentence does not restate the opening. It spends what the paragraph has earned, lifting the local reading up to the novel's larger concern, which is the development the top bands describe as sustained thought. A marker reading this paragraph is not being charmed. The marker is checking boxes that the paragraph keeps filling, and the ease of that checking is what a top-band mark feels like from the marking side: not a reward for brilliance but the recognition of a script that keeps doing the creditworthy things.

### What does a penalized paragraph look like to a marker?

A penalized paragraph opens with undisputed fact, narrates plot where analysis should be, drops in quotations that illustrate points already made, and closes by restating its opening. The marker's pen stays still, because stillness is what the absence of criterion verbs looks like from the marking side.

Return to the first paragraph and watch the pen not move. The opening, "one of the most famous symbols in the novel," is true and inert: no reader disagrees, so no argument exists. The narration of Gatsby staring across the water retells what the examiner already knows, and the criterion for analysis explicitly withholds credit for narration. The quotation arrives as a guest: the paragraph's point about the dream receding is complete before the quotation appears and unchanged after it, so the evidence criterion finds possession without use. The closing sentence, "one of the main themes of the novel," reaches for significance without earning it, because significance in a mark scheme is not a label applied at the end but a connection demonstrated in the middle. None of this makes the paragraph bad writing. It is clear, accurate, and fluent. It is simply writing that performs none of the verbs the criteria reward, and the mark reflects the verbs, not the virtues. Students who grasp this stop asking whether their paragraph is good and start asking whether it argues, analyzes, evidences, contextualizes, and expresses. The second question is the one the examiner is answering.

A third walkthrough completes the picture, because the conclusion is where examiners test whether the argument developed or merely repeated. Read this closing paragraph as the marker reads it, after an essay that has argued, with varying success, that Gatsby's dream defeats itself.

*In the end, the green light shows that Gatsby's dream was impossible from the start. He reached for something he could never have, and the novel's famous ending, with its image of boats against the current, confirms that his struggle was always going to fail. Gatsby is therefore a tragic figure, and the American Dream he represents is shown to be hollow.*

The pencil moves, but not in the way the writer hopes. The opening phrase, "in the end," followed by a restatement of the thesis, tells the examiner the essay is summarizing rather than concluding. Nothing in the paragraph spends what the analysis earned: the boats-against-the-current image, one of the richest in the novel, arrives as a gesture, its language unread, its famous paradox of striving against drift unexamined. The final labels, tragic figure and hollow dream, are applied rather than demonstrated, and the examiner, checking the argument criterion, finds the line of reasoning exactly where the introduction left it. Contrast what the paragraph could have done with the same material: taken the "borne back ceaselessly into the past" half of the ending's paradox and used it to complicate the thesis, showing that the dream's impossibility is not a flaw in Gatsby's planning but the structural condition the whole essay has been tracing. That would be development, the criterion's word for what conclusions should do, and the examiner rewards it because it proves the thinking did not stop when the drafting did. The lesson generalizes. A conclusion that restates confirms the band the essay had already earned. A conclusion that develops can lift it, because it gives the examiner one last annotatable moment, and markers remember the last thing a script does well.

## Reading Introductions the Way a Marker Reads Them

The introduction is the most over-invested paragraph in examination writing and the most misunderstood. Students spend a disproportionate share of their planning time on it, polishing the opening sentences while the middle paragraphs, where bands are actually decided, get whatever time remains. Examiners, meanwhile, spend less than a minute on it, and that minute is not spent admiring it. It is spent sorting. The introduction tells the marker which kind of essay this is going to be: one that argues, one that tours, or one that reports. That sorting is the provisional band hypothesis forming in real time, and no amount of polish on a touring introduction will change the sort, because the sort is about the kind of thinking, not the quality of the prose.

Read two introductions the way a marker reads them, both answering the same familiar question about whether Gatsby is great. The first is the kind that opens a large share of scripts.

*F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby is one of the most famous novels ever written. It tells the story of Jay Gatsby, a mysterious millionaire who throws lavish parties in the hope of winning back his lost love, Daisy Buchanan. This essay will really consider whether Gatsby is great, looking at his character, his dream, and the novel's themes.*

Watch the examiner's pencil during those three sentences. It does not move. The first sentence is true and tells the marker nothing about the writer's thinking; fame is not a position. The second sentence retells the premise, which the examiner knows, and the analysis criterion explicitly withholds credit for narration. The third sentence announces a tour: character, dream, themes, in that order, with no hint of what the essay will claim about any of them. The phrase "will really consider" is the giveaway the marker has seen ten thousand times. It promises consideration without committing to a conclusion, which means the essay is reserving the right to have no thesis. The provisional band forms around the middle, not because the writing is poor, it is perfectly fluent, but because nothing in the introduction performs the verbs the upper bands require. The examiner turns the page expecting a tour and will be pleasantly surprised if one does not arrive. Pleasant surprises are rare.

The second introduction is doing different work in the same space.

*Gatsby is great the way a cathedral is great: not because the structure stands, but because the aspiration that built it exceeds every material it uses. The novel measures him against his own dream and finds the dream larger than the man, larger than Daisy, larger than the world that contains them both. His greatness is therefore inseparable from his failure, since the same excess of imagining that lifts him above his circumstances is what makes any real outcome unworthy of him.*

The pencil moves here, and it moves early. The first sentence takes a position through an analogy that does argumentative work: greatness as aspiration rather than achievement, which a reader could dispute. The second sentence extends the position into a claim about the novel's own measuring, which gives the essay a textual anchor beyond the writer's opinion. The third sentence complicates the position with the paradox the whole essay can now test, that greatness and failure share a cause. Notice what the introduction does not do. It does not summarize the plot. It does not list the topics to come. It does not announce that consideration will happen. It considers, in the act of writing, and the marker watching that consideration happen forms the upward hypothesis. The essay that follows still has to sustain it, and many essays do not, but the introduction has done its one job: it has shown the examiner a mind with a position, which is the first thing the criteria ask the marker to find.

### What should an introduction do in its first two sentences?

An introduction should put the question's key terms inside a disputable claim, then imply the route the argument will take. Two sentences can do it: the first says what the essay thinks, the second says how it will show it. Everything beyond that is usually decoration, and decoration spends the reader's freshest attention on the least creditworthy prose.

The sorting happens fast because it has to, and it happens reliably because the signals are crude. An introduction that uses the question's key terms inside a disputable claim signals argument. An introduction that lists areas to be covered signals a tour. An introduction that establishes background and retells premise signals a report. Markers learn these signals the way experienced readers learn genre: within sentences, not paragraphs. The practical consequence is that the introduction's job is narrower than students believe. It does not need to be brilliant, original, or memorable. It needs to sort correctly. A plain sentence that takes a clear position on the question sorts the essay into the arguing pile more effectively than a dazzling sentence that takes no position at all, because the sort is about thinking, and thinking is what the criteria price. Students who grasp this stop performing the introduction and start using it, which is the moment it begins to earn.

There is a further subtlety the thirty-second read reveals, which is that examiners can hear the difference between a thesis and a topic wearing a thesis's clothes. "This essay will explore Gatsby's greatness through his dream, his parties, and his death" is a topic with a map. "Gatsby's greatness is inseparable from his failure" is a thesis. The map is not worthless; an essay needs structure, and the reader needs orientation. But the map without the thesis is a tour itinerary, and the marker sorts it accordingly. The strongest introductions do both in two moves: the position first, so the sort lands in the arguing pile, and then the briefest of maps, so the examiner knows the route the argument will take. Two sentences can do it. The first says what the essay thinks. The second says how the essay will show it. Everything beyond that in an introduction is usually decoration, and decoration in the opening is the most expensive kind, because it spends the reader's freshest attention on the least creditworthy prose.

### What separates an introduction that argues from one that tours?

An arguing introduction takes a disputable position on the question's terms and creates obligations the essay must then meet; a touring introduction lists topics without taking a position, so no paragraph that follows has anything to prove. The marker rewards the first because the criteria reward argument, and merely tolerates the second.

The anatomy of the arguing introduction repays close attention because it is buildable. It has three components, and each does a distinct job. First, the question's key terms appear inside the writer's own claim, which proves relevance from the first line and prevents the most expensive failure, answering a different question than the one set. Second, the claim is contestable: a reasonable reader could disagree, which means the essay has something to defend and the analysis has something to do. Third, the introduction implies a route, not by listing topics but by containing a tension the body must resolve. The cathedral sentence does this economically. Its analogy sets aspiration against material, and the essay's paragraphs now have their work cut out: show the aspiration, show the materials, show the excess. A touring introduction fails all three tests. It paraphrases the question instead of answering it, it asserts nothing disputable, and its list of topics creates no tension, so the paragraphs that follow can arrive in any order without loss. Examiners have a quiet test for this: if the introduction's topic list could be shuffled without damage, the essay is touring. If removing a paragraph would break the argument, the essay is arguing. Writers can apply the test to their own plans before writing a word, and the thirty seconds the examiner spends sorting will then sort in their favor.

## Where Context Earns Its Marks

Of the five criteria, context is the most consistently mishandled, and the mishandling follows a pattern so regular that examiners recognize it within a paragraph. The pattern is the bolted-on history lesson: a freestanding block of background, accurate and well revised, sitting beside the analysis without touching it. The student knows the context criterion exists and has prepared accordingly, often at great expense of revision time. The examiner reads the block, finds no connection to the textual argument, and moves on without crediting it. Both parties feel they have done their jobs. Only one of them is right about what the job was. The criterion asks for integration, background that changes what a passage means, and a paragraph of context that never touches a line of the novel is not integrated. It is adjacent.

Watch the difference in two handlings of the valley of ashes, the novel's great image of industrial desolation watched over by the eyes of T. J. Eckleburg. The first is the bolted-on version that fills many scripts.

*The novel's era was one of rapid economic expansion and industrial growth, when new fortunes were made quickly and cities spread outward into surrounding land. Factories produced goods on an unprecedented scale, and the gap between rich and poor widened dramatically. Fitzgerald would have seen this industrial landscape around him, and it influenced his depiction of the valley of ashes as a place of waste and desolation between West Egg and New York.*

Every fact in that paragraph may be correct. None of it earns more than minimal credit, because none of it does anything to a passage. The examiner's pencil stays still for a precise reason: the context criterion rewards background that sharpens a reading, and there is no reading here for the background to sharpen. The paragraph informs. The criterion asks the writer to analyze. A student who wrote this paragraph spent real revision time learning economic history, and the tragedy of the misdirected labor is that the same time, spent finding one passage where the industrial pressure changes the meaning of Fitzgerald's language, would have been repaid in full.

The second version integrates the same knowledge at the point where it does work.

*The valley of ashes is where the novel lets its industrial era write directly onto the landscape, and Fitzgerald makes the writing literal: the ash grows like wheat into ridges and hills, a harvest image perverted into waste. That perversion is the context doing its work inside the passage, because the simile borrows the language of agricultural plenty, the very plenty the era celebrated, and turns it into a measure of what the expansion discards. The eyes of T. J. Eckleburg watch over this field of waste, and the context sharpens their meaning: in an age that treated growth as a moral good, the novel sets a god made of advertising above the refuse growth leaves behind, and lets the contradiction sit unanswered.*

Here the background never appears as background. It arrives inside the reading of the simile, where "grows like wheat" needs the era's celebration of plenty to become ironic, and inside the reading of the billboard, where the advertising god needs the era's faith in growth to become an indictment. The examiner credits this because the context criterion's verb is integrate, and integration is visible on the page: remove the contextual sentences and the readings become poorer, which is the functional test the marker applies. Notice the economy. The integrated version uses less background than the bolted-on version, not more. It uses exactly as much as the passage requires, placed exactly where the passage requires it. That economy is the signature of a writer who understands that context is not a display of knowledge but a tool for reading, and it is the tool-use, not the knowledge, that the mark scheme prices.

### What does integrated context look like inside a paragraph?

Integrated context arrives inside the analysis of a passage, at the exact point where background changes what the language means, so that removing it would impoverish the reading. It is never a freestanding block; it is a sentence or two woven where the text needs it, doing visible work on specific words.

The placement is the whole technique, and it can be practiced mechanically. Draft the close reading first, attending only to what the passage's language does. Then ask where the reading leans on something outside the text: the irony of the wheat simile leans on the era's celebration of plenty, the force of the billboard leans on the era's faith in growth. Write the contextual sentence at that lean point, no earlier, and keep it to what the passage needs. The examiner recognizes the result immediately, because the context is load-bearing: the analysis depends on it, the way an embedded quotation is load-bearing inside its sentence. Writers who master this stop revising context as a separate topic and start revising it as a set of placements, a small collection of background points each paired with the passage it sharpens. That pairing is what the criterion rewards, and it converts the most misdirected revision hours in the subject into some of the most productive.

### What does bolted-on context cost the essay?

Bolted-on context costs the essay twice: once in the marks the unintegrated paragraph never earns, and once in the time and word budget spent on prose the criteria do not price. The freestanding history block is the most expensive paragraph in many scripts, because it feels like diligence while performing none of the rewarded verbs.

The double cost deserves spelling out, because students consistently underestimate the second half. The first cost is straightforward. The context criterion asks for integration, so a paragraph that never touches the text earns little under that criterion, and it earns nothing under the others, since it argues nothing, analyzes nothing, and evidences nothing. The second cost is opportunity. Every minute spent writing the history block is a minute not spent on the word-level reading the analysis criterion prices, and every line of it displaces a line that could have been doing criterion work. Under timed conditions, where the essay is a budget as much as a text, the bolted-on paragraph is therefore not neutral. It is actively harmful, consuming the scarcest resource, time, on the least rewarded prose. The repair is not to abandon context but to relocate it: cut the block, keep the knowledge, and spend it sentence by sentence inside the passages it sharpens. The same revision hours, aimed at integration rather than coverage, move the essay across the band boundary the block could never reach.

## Writing to the Mark Scheme Instead of Against It

Knowing the marking mindset changes nothing until it changes drafting decisions, so this section converts the criteria into writing moves. The conversion is direct: each criterion implies a question the writer asks of every paragraph, and a paragraph that can answer all five questions is a paragraph the examiner can reward. The discipline sounds laborious. In practice it becomes quick, because the questions are the same every time and the essay teaches the writer to hear them. After a few practice essays, the criterion check runs in the background, the way a driver checks mirrors without deciding to check them.

What changes in the writer's habits, once the check is internalized, is the order of operations. The old order was: write, then worry about whether it is good. The new order is: decide what the paragraph must do, then write it doing that. The decision comes first because the criteria are known in advance, and writing toward a known target is a different activity from writing into the unknown and hoping. Students describe the shift as the essay getting quieter: fewer flourishes, fewer hedges, fewer sentences performing cleverness, and in their place a steady sequence of claims, evidence, and readings, each doing its named job. The prose that results is less showy than the old kind and scores higher, which surprises students exactly once. After the first marked essay comes back with the band the checklist predicted, the surprise converts into trust, and the writer stops decorating for the imaginary reader for good. The mark scheme, consulted often enough, becomes not a constraint but a companion, the quiet voice beside the desk asking of every paragraph the only question that matters: what will the examiner's pencil do here.

Start with argument. Before drafting, write the thesis as a single sentence that a reasonable reader could dispute, and test every paragraph's opening against it: does this paragraph's claim advance the thesis, complicate it, or defend it against an objection. If a paragraph's opening could be moved to another essay without change, it is not arguing; it is touring. The fix is to rewrite the opening as a position on the question, which immediately gives the paragraph's evidence a job and its analysis a direction. Students resist this because a disputable claim feels riskier than a safe summary. The mark scheme resolves the dilemma: safety is what the middle bands describe, and risk, the willingness to take a position and defend it, is what the upper bands require.

Continue with analysis, which is where most of the writing time should go. For each quotation selected, write at least one sentence about a specific word's work before writing any sentence about the quotation's general meaning. The word-level sentence is the unit the analysis criterion prices, and paragraphs that lack it are paragraphs where the examiner's pen stays still. If a quotation resists word-level reading, that is information: the quotation may be the wrong evidence for the paragraph, chosen for fame rather than fit, and replacing it with a more workable phrase is a marking decision, not a retreat. The [guide to the highest band](/2030/09/30/gatsby-top-band-essay/) describes what this sustained argued analysis looks like when all five criteria fire together, and it is worth reading alongside this article as the companion that owns the top-band synthesis while this one owns the marking mechanics.

Handle evidence and context as servants of the argument, not as displays of knowledge. Evidence earns when it is short, embedded, and followed by reading; context earns when it is integrated at the point where it changes a passage's meaning. A paragraph on the valley of ashes does not need a freestanding history of industrial America. It needs one sentence, placed where the dust and the eyes of T. J. Eckleburg are being read, that lets the historical pressure sharpen the textual point. The examiner rewards that sentence because the context criterion asks for integration, not coverage. Coverage is the student's instinct. Integration is the criterion's demand. Every time the two conflict, the criterion wins, because the criterion is what the examiner is holding.

Expression deserves a final word because students misunderstand its place in the hierarchy. Clarity is not the garnish on the argument; it is the condition of the argument being marked at all. An examiner cannot credit thinking that the prose obscures, and the expression criterion exists to say so plainly. This does not mean ornate prose. It means sentences that say what they mean, vocabulary used accurately, and technical terms deployed only where the writer controls them. A misused term, symbolism invoked where image was meant, or irony claimed where the passage is sincere, costs more than plain language would have, because it tells the examiner the writer is performing analysis rather than doing it. Write plainly, argue sharply, and let the analysis carry the sophistication. The mark scheme has no criterion for impressing the reader. It has criteria for thinking on the page, and plain prose that thinks will always outscore fancy prose that does not.

Timed conditions change the application of all of this without changing any of it, and the distinction matters because students often treat the examination hall as a different country from the practice desk. The criteria do not relax under time pressure. The examiner marking the fortieth script applies the same descriptors as the first, and the verbs the mark scheme prices do not become cheaper because the writer was hurried. What changes is the budget: fewer words, fewer paragraphs, less room for recovery from a false start. That is why the criterion checklist belongs in planning, not just in revision. Ten minutes spent building the disputable thesis and ordering the paragraph claims is ten minutes the examiner repays across every band, because a planned essay cannot tour by accident. The unplanned essay, written sentence by sentence in the hope that an argument will emerge, is the one that drifts into summary and decoration, and no amount of fluent prose compensates for a missing line of reasoning. Practice under timed conditions should therefore rehearse the planning discipline, not just the writing stamina: thesis first, paragraph claims second, evidence matched to claims third, and only then the drafting. Students who plan this way find that the timed essay is shorter than the untimed one but denser, and density, as the length section established, is what the bands describe.

A final point belongs to the essay's opening paragraph, because it is the only paragraph the examiner reads twice: once in the thirty-second sort, once again when the provisional band is being tested. That double reading makes it the cheapest place in the whole essay to buy marks. A single sentence that sharpens the thesis after the opening claim, or a word-level reading tucked into the first paragraph's evidence, repays across both passes, because the sort rewards the promise and the second reading rewards the kept promise. Students should therefore draft the opening last, or at least revise it last, once the essay's real argument has emerged in the drafting. The introduction written before the argument is the introduction the examiner has to forgive. The introduction rewritten after it is the introduction the examiner uses to set the band.

### How do you turn each criterion into a writing decision?

Turn each criterion into a question asked of every paragraph while drafting: does it take a position, does it read language closely, is its evidence embedded and serving the claim, is its context integrated where it sharpens meaning, and is its expression clear. A paragraph that answers yes five times is a paragraph the examiner can fully reward.

The method works because it externalizes the examiner's checklist and puts it in the writer's hands before the script reaches the marking table. Draft a paragraph, then interrogate it. The argument question catches the touring paragraph and forces the disputable claim. The analysis question catches the quotation left uncommented and forces the word-level sentence. The evidence question catches the block quotation and forces the cut to the load-bearing phrase. The context question catches the bolted-on history and forces the integration or the deletion. The expression question catches the tangled sentence and forces the rewrite. None of these interrogations require talent. They require honesty, applied paragraph by paragraph, and they convert the abstract criteria into a concrete revision pass. Students who practice this on two or three essays find the questions start asking themselves during drafting, which is the moment the marking mindset has moved from the article into the writer.

### When should you stop decorating and start arguing?

Stop decorating the moment a sentence could be removed without weakening the paragraph's case, and start arguing by replacing it with a sentence that a reader could dispute. Decoration is any prose that performs knowledge without performing thought; argument is any sentence that takes a position and accepts the burden of defending it.

The test is practical. Read a drafted paragraph and strike every sentence whose removal leaves the argument intact. What falls is usually the throat-clearing opening, the plot reminder the examiner does not need, the extra example that repeats rather than extends, the elegant restatement of the introduction. What remains is the skeleton of the case, and it is almost always shorter than the draft, which alarms students until they see what the shorter version scores. The [catalogue of common mistakes](/2030/08/05/gatsby-essay-mistakes/) shows where decoration most often hides, in the very moves students trust most, and reading it beside this article turns the abstract warning into a recognizable set of habits to break. Then rebuild: for each struck sentence, ask whether the paragraph needs a disputable claim, a word-level reading, or a development of the consequence, and write that sentence instead. Decoration removed and argument installed is the entire revision in one move, and it is the move the mark scheme was written to reward.

The most stubborn decorations deserve naming, because students defend them as strengths. The elegant restatement of the introduction in the conclusion feels like closure, but the examiner reads it as repetition, and the argument criterion withholds credit for standing still. The third example, added after two have established the point, feels like thoroughness, but the examiner reads it as padding, and no criterion pays for the third instance of what two proved. The long quotation, dropped in whole to prove wide reading, feels like evidence, but the examiner reads it as possession without use, and the evidence criterion rewards the choosing, not the owning. The sophisticated vocabulary, deployed to sound analytical, feels like expression, but the examiner reads it as costume, and the expression criterion prices clarity, not ornament. Each of these feels like writing well. Each of them is the writer performing for the imaginary generous reader. The discipline is to catch each one in revision and ask the hard question: does this sentence argue, analyze, evidence, contextualize, or clarify. If the answer is none of the five, the sentence goes, however beautiful, and something that does one of the five takes its place. That is not the impoverishment of the essay. It is the essay becoming what the examiner is actually marking.

## Verdict

The verdict this article takes is unqualified: learn the mark scheme and write to it, because the examiner's mindset is not an obstacle to good writing but a description of it. The criteria reward exactly what strong literary thinking looks like when it is put on the page under time pressure: a position taken, defended with evidence, developed through close reading, situated where context sharpens it, expressed clearly enough to be followed. Nothing in that list is a trick. Nothing in it rewards gaming, padding, or performance. The students who score highest are not the ones who have cracked a code. They are the ones who understood earliest that the code was published, that it describes thinking rather than knowledge, and that every paragraph is a fresh chance to do the verbs the criteria price.

That verdict carries a practical corollary worth stating plainly. Stop writing for the imaginary generous reader and start writing for the real disciplined one. The generous reader forgives drift, admires effort, and rewards length. The real reader, the examiner with the mark scheme open, does none of those things, and does something better: rewards argued analysis wherever it appears, in whatever prose, at whatever length, from whomever wrote it. The fairness students sometimes doubt is built into the method. The criteria do not know whose script they are marking. They know only what the script does. Write the script that does the rewarded things, paragraph after paragraph, and the marking mindset that once seemed mysterious becomes the most predictable part of the examination. That is the whole of the insight, and it is enough.

There is one more consequence, and it concerns how a student should feel walking out of the examination hall. The old mindset leaves the hall anxious, because effort and length are wagers: the student has bet hours and pages on a reader who may or may not honor them. The new mindset leaves the hall with something closer to knowledge. The student who wrote to the criteria knows what the script did, paragraph by paragraph, because every paragraph was built to do it. Not every such script reaches the top band; execution under pressure is imperfect, and the criteria are demanding. But the student knows where the marks were aimed, which is a different and steadier feeling than hoping they were noticed. Examinations will always involve uncertainty. Writing to the mark scheme does not remove it. It moves the uncertainty from the marking, which is predictable, to the performance, which the student controls, and that is the only trade in the whole process that favors the writer. Take it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do examiners mark Gatsby essays?

Examiners mark Gatsby essays by measuring each script against published criteria that describe what each band of response looks like, rather than by ranking scripts against each other or rewarding general impressions. The process begins with a first read-through that forms a provisional band judgment from the argument's clarity, the analysis's depth, and the evidence's use, and continues with closer checking of borderline passages where the difference between bands is decided. Every mark must be defensible, which means the examiner ties it to specific achievements in the script and to language in the mark scheme, so the standard stays consistent across thousands of scripts. For the student, the practical meaning is that the target is visible in advance: the criteria name argument, analysis, evidence, and context as the rewarded verbs, and an essay that performs those verbs paragraph after paragraph is an essay the examiner can place with confidence. Nothing about the process rewards mystery, and nothing in it punishes a student who writes plainly but thinks rigorously.

### Q: What criteria do examiners use?

Examiners use published criteria that, across different boards and specifications, assess the same five qualities: the strength of the argument, the depth of the analysis, the selection and use of evidence, the integration of context, and the clarity of expression. Argument means a clear, contestable thesis that answers the set question with a line of reasoning developed across paragraphs. Analysis means close reading of the writer's own, connecting specific words, images, and structures to their effects and tying technique to meaning. Evidence means well-chosen textual references embedded in the writer's sentences, each serving its paragraph's claim. Context means relevant background integrated where it sharpens the reading, never recited for its own sake. Expression means controlled academic prose that carries complex thought without tangling. The labels shift between specifications, but the architecture is stable, and a writer who treats the five as five jobs the essay must perform has grasped the marking standard in its most usable form.

### Q: What do examiners look for first?

Examiners look first for whether the essay has an argument, because the thesis determines the provisional band more than any other single feature. In the first read-through, the examiner forms a hypothesis about which band the script inhabits, and a clear, contestable thesis that answers the question points that hypothesis upward, while a restatement of the question points it toward the middle. What follows the thesis matters as much: the examiner scans for whether the opening's promise is kept, whether paragraphs advance a case, and whether analysis appears early rather than summary.

There is a drill that makes this visible before the examiner ever reads the script. Read only the first and last sentence of each paragraph, the way a hurried marker skims for the argument's shape. If those sentences convey a developing position, the fast read finds the thinking immediately. If they convey a list of topics, the essay lands in the touring pile no matter how strong the middle sentences. The drill takes five minutes; the band it protects took weeks to earn.

### Q: How does what earns marks differ from what students think?

What earns marks differs from what students think because students tend to value the visible signs of labor, length, coverage, decoration, and effort, while examiners value only the thinking performed on the page, which is argument, analysis, evidence, and context against the criteria. A student often believes a long essay proves diligence, that mentioning many themes proves range, that elegant phrasing proves sophistication, and that obvious hard work deserves recognition. The examiner, reading only the script against the published standard, credits none of those beliefs directly: length without analysis is padding, coverage without argument is listing, decoration without thought is ornament, and effort is invisible except through its effects. The gap is not a trick of the system but its design, since the criteria describe what the essay does rather than what the student endured to write it. Students who close the gap stop asking whether the essay looks impressive and start asking whether each paragraph argues, analyzes, evidences, and contextualizes, which is the question the examiner is actually answering.

### Q: Do marks reward effort or length?

Marks reward neither effort nor length directly, and understanding why is the single most useful correction a student can make to the marking misconception. Effort is causally important, since thorough revision builds the knowledge and fluency that strong analysis requires, but the examiner cannot see the hours and the criteria describe achievements in the script, not labor behind it; the marks go to the analysis, never to the revision that made it possible. Length is even less relevant, since no mark scheme contains a word count and examiners read enough scripts to recognize padding instantly: the paragraph that restates the introduction, the third example that adds nothing, the detour that never touches the argument. A compact essay that argues and analyzes throughout will outscore a sprawling one that describes at length, and no examiner has ever lowered a mark because an essay finished early. The constructive move is to aim effort at the verbs the criteria price and to convert length into density, so every paragraph does criterion work.

### Q: Why is argued analysis rewarded over decoration?

Argued analysis is rewarded over decoration because the criteria are written to price thinking performed on the text, and decoration, however elegant, performs knowledge without performing thought. A decorated paragraph tells the examiner what the student knows: the symbols identified, the themes named, the plot accurately retold, the vocabulary deployed. An argued paragraph shows the student thinking: a position taken on the question, evidence selected to serve it, specific words read for what they do, technique connected to meaning, the reading developed into a consequence for the novel. The mark scheme's verbs are all in the second list, which is why a plain paragraph that argues cleanly will always outscore a beautiful paragraph that never takes a position. Decoration also carries a hidden cost, since ornate prose often obscures the very thinking it is meant to display, and an examiner cannot credit analysis that the sentences hide. Students should therefore treat every decorative sentence as a candidate for replacement by a sentence that disputes, reads closely, or develops, because those are the sentences the examiner's pen moves for.

### Q: How do examiners decide between two adjacent bands?

Examiners decide between adjacent bands by re-reading the borderline passages and asking which band descriptor the script's best sustained thinking most closely matches, weighing the whole response rather than averaging its parts. The decision rarely turns on a single brilliant sentence or a single weak paragraph; it turns on what level of argument and analysis the essay sustains across its length, since the criteria describe bands as qualities of the whole response. In practice, the examiner looks for the discriminating features the descriptors name: whether the thesis is merely stated or genuinely developed, whether analysis reaches the language of the text or stops at its plot, whether evidence is embedded and serving claims or dropped in as illustration. A script that shows flashes of top-band thinking inside a mostly middle-band essay usually stays in the middle, because bands reward sustained achievement. For the writer, the lesson is that consistency matters more than peaks: an essay that argues at one level all the way through will beat an uneven essay whose best paragraph is better but whose average is lower.

### Q: What does an examiner do during the second reading of a script?

During a second reading the examiner tests the provisional band formed on the first pass, focusing on the passages that seemed strongest and asking whether they are analysis or description wearing analytical vocabulary. Borderline scripts receive the most attention here, because the difference between the top of one band and the bottom of the next is where grade boundaries bite and where marks must be most defensible. The examiner re-examines the sentences after quotations to check whether genuine reading follows or the writer moved on, re-tests the thesis against the conclusion to see whether the argument developed or merely repeated, and weighs whether context was integrated or bolted on. This is also where the examiner's trained ear matters most: thousands of scripts teach a marker to hear the difference between a sentence that reads language and a sentence that restates meaning, even when both use the vocabulary of analysis. Writers should therefore draft as if every strong passage will be re-read skeptically, which means making the analysis explicit rather than implied and the argument's development visible rather than assumed.

### Q: How do examiners treat an original reading they disagree with?

Examiners treat an original reading they disagree with exactly as the criteria instruct: they reward it if it is argued, evidenced, and analytically developed, regardless of whether the marker would have read the passage the same way. The mark scheme assesses the quality of the thinking, not the conformity of the conclusion, so a well-defended minority interpretation of Gatsby's character or the novel's ending can score at the top while a conventional reading argued thinly stays in the middle. What the examiner will not reward is originality as mere contrarianism: a startling claim with no textual support, or a provocative thesis abandoned two paragraphs later, fails the evidence and argument criteria no matter how fresh it sounds. The practical guidance is to take interpretive risks only where the text can carry them, anchoring every bold claim in specific language and developing the consequences honestly. Markers are trained to recognize and credit genuine independent thought, and the descriptors for the highest bands explicitly describe it, so the bold, well-argued essay is playing to the criteria rather than against them.

### Q: Do examiners penalize misremembered quotations?

Examiners do not penalize misremembered quotations heavily as factual errors, but a misremembered quotation usually costs marks indirectly because the analysis built on wrong words cannot read the text accurately. The criteria reward what the writer does with language, so if the quoted words are wrong, the close reading attaches to phrasing Fitzgerald never wrote, and the analysis criterion finds less to credit. Small slips, a wrong article or a transposed word, matter little when the reading still engages the passage's real language and the examiner can see what was meant. Larger errors, attributing to the novel a line it never contained or reversing a phrase's meaning, undermine the evidence criterion, since the support no longer supports. The safest practice is to quote short: brief phrases are easier to remember exactly and easier to embed, and a short accurate quotation analyzed deeply will always outscore a long approximate one gestured at. Students should also prefer paraphrase with precision over quotation with doubt, since an accurately rendered idea in the writer's own words preserves the argument even when memory of the exact wording fails.

### Q: What happens when an essay answers a different question than the one set?

When an essay answers a different question than the one set, the argument criterion withholds most of its credit no matter how strong the writing, because every mark scheme makes response to the task the foundation the other criteria build on. An examiner can admire the analysis, note the well-chosen evidence, and still place the script low, since a brilliant answer to an unasked question demonstrates everything except the thing being assessed. This is the costliest of the common failures because it is invisible to the writer, who feels the essay's quality, while the marker measures its relevance. The usual cause is a pre-prepared argument dragged onto an unsuitable prompt: the essay on the American Dream answering a question about narration, or the character study answering a question about structure. Prevention belongs to the planning stage, where the question's key terms should be underlined, the thesis should use those terms, and every paragraph's claim should be testable against them. A student who writes the question's words into the thesis and returns to them in the conclusion gives the examiner continuous proof of relevance, which is the cheapest high-value move in examination writing.

### Q: How do examiners weigh a strong opening against a weak conclusion?

Examiners weigh a strong opening against a weak conclusion by judging the whole response, which means a weak conclusion costs less than students fear but reveals more than students hope. The opening forms the provisional band and the conclusion tests whether the argument developed or merely repeated, so a conclusion that restates the introduction without adding thought confirms the lower of the two impressions the essay created. A conclusion that genuinely concludes, drawing together the strands of the analysis into a final judgment on the question, can lift a script, because it demonstrates the sustained thinking the top bands describe. What examiners do not do is average the parts mechanically; a brilliant first paragraph cannot carry four weak ones, and a weak final paragraph cannot sink four strong ones. The practical lesson is to spend conclusion-writing time on synthesis rather than summary: one sentence gathering what the analysis proved, one sentence answering the question directly with the full weight of the essay behind it, and no new arguments introduced where they cannot be defended.

### Q: Do examiners expect students to know the mark scheme?

Examiners do not expect students to have memorized the mark scheme, and no criterion awards credit for naming its bands or quoting its language. What examiners expect, because the criteria describe it, is writing that does the things the scheme prices: argued positions, close reading, well-chosen evidence, integrated context, clear expression. A student who has never seen the mark scheme but writes that way will score well, because the scheme describes good literary thinking rather than imposing an arbitrary template. Knowing the scheme helps, though, and the help is practical rather than magical: it tells the student where credit lives, so revision aims at verbs instead of nouns and drafting decisions favor analysis over decoration. The scheme is published precisely so that students can write toward it, which means using it is not gaming the system but using the system as designed. The examiner's only expectation is the essay in front of them; the mark scheme is simply the shared language in which its achievement is described, available to the writer before a word is written.

### Q: How do examiners mark strong context with thin textual evidence?

Examiners mark strong context with thin textual evidence as an essay that knows a great deal about the novel's world and too little about the novel's language, which places it firmly in the middle bands. The context criterion rewards integration, background that sharpens a reading of a specific passage, but integration needs a passage to sharpen, and without textual evidence there is nothing for the context to work on. A script that discusses the novel's era fluently while quoting rarely is performing the wrong verb: it informs where the criteria ask it to analyze. Markers describe the pattern clearly, since it appears in many scripts, as knowledge displayed rather than knowledge used. The repair is structural rather than additive: the student does not need less context but more text, pairing each contextual point with the passage it illuminates and letting the quotation do the proving. One well-placed historical sentence that changes what "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us" means will earn more than a paragraph of background that never touches a line of Fitzgerald's prose.

### Q: What does examiner moderation check in marked essays?

Examiner moderation checks that marks are consistent with the published criteria and consistent between markers, by re-reading samples of marked scripts and testing whether the awarded band can be defended from the script's achievements. A moderator does not re-mark from scratch; the moderator asks whether the original examiner's judgment falls within the range the criteria support, looking for the annotated evidence, the marginal notes, and the band justification that show the mark was reasoned rather than felt. Scripts near grade boundaries and scripts with unusual features, striking originality or atypical structure, draw particular attention, since those are where inconsistency most often appears. For the student, moderation is invisible but reassuring: it is the mechanism that keeps the standard stable across markers, schools, and sessions, and it explains why examiners annotate as they go, since every mark may need to survive a second reader. The practical consequence is that the clearest scripts are also the safest, because an essay whose argument and analysis are unmissable gives both the examiner and the moderator the same easy path to the same band.

### Q: How do examiners keep the standard steady across many scripts?

Examiners keep the standard steady across many scripts through training, standardization meetings, and continuous reference to the published criteria and anchor scripts that exemplify each band. Before live marking begins, examiners mark practice scripts and compare their judgments, calibrating their sense of each band boundary against agreed examples until their marks align. During marking, the mark scheme stays open beside the script, and experienced markers return to the band descriptors whenever a script feels borderline, testing their impression against the published language rather than against the last script they read. The well-known danger is drift, where a run of weak scripts makes an average one look strong or a run of strong scripts makes a good one look ordinary, and the discipline of re-anchoring to the descriptors is the defense. Students benefit from understanding this because it confirms that the mark depends on the script's absolute achievement against the criteria, never on the quality of the other scripts in the pile, so there is no advantage in hoping for weak competition and no penalty in facing strong competition.

### Q: Does handwriting or presentation affect the mark?

Handwriting and presentation do not affect the mark directly, since no criterion prices neatness, but illegible writing can cost marks indirectly because an examiner cannot credit thinking that cannot be read. The distinction matters: a marker will not add credit for beautiful script or subtract it for plain script, and the criteria contain no presentation band, so students should not spend revision time on penmanship beyond legibility. The real risk is the sentence the examiner cannot decipher at the decisive moment, the thesis statement half-lost in a tangle or the analytical point obscured by crossings-out, since uncredited thinking might as well not be there. Examination technique therefore includes the humble disciplines of legible writing, clear paragraph breaks, and leaving space for the examiner's eye to follow the argument's shape on the page. Digital examinations remove the handwriting question entirely, but the underlying principle survives in every format: the script must make its thinking easy to find, because the examiner rewards what is demonstrated and demonstration requires legibility in the broadest sense.

### Q: How do examiners mark an essay that changes its thesis midway?

Examiners mark an essay that changes its thesis midway according to what the change reveals: a thesis refined through argument can show developing thought, while a thesis abandoned for a new one usually signals lost control. The argument criterion rewards a line of reasoning that develops across paragraphs, and development can include complication, where the essay discovers a tension in its own position and adjusts honestly. Markers credit that adjustment when the essay acknowledges it, showing the reader how the new formulation grows out of the evidence rather than contradicting the opening by accident. What costs marks is the unacknowledged swerve, where the third paragraph argues something the first paragraph denied and no sentence bridges the gap, because the examiner then cannot tell what position the essay defends. Students who feel their thesis shifting mid-essay should therefore make the shift explicit, adding the sentence that names the complication and restates the claim in its stronger form. An essay that thinks aloud and corrects itself reads as intelligence at work; an essay that contradicts itself silently reads as confusion.

### Q: Do examiners reward knowledge beyond the novel?

Examiners reward knowledge beyond the novel only when it serves the argument about the novel, which is the precise boundary the context criterion draws. Well-chosen background, the novel's literary moment, its genre pressures, the critical conversations it belongs to, earns credit when it is integrated at the point where it changes a passage's meaning. Knowledge displayed for its own sake, the freestanding paragraph of history or biography that never touches the analysis, earns nothing, however accurate and well written. The test the examiner applies is functional: remove the external knowledge and ask whether the reading of the text becomes poorer; if it does not, the knowledge was decoration. Students often over-invest here because background feels safe to revise, being factual rather than interpretive, but safety is not a criterion. The efficient approach is to revise a small set of contextual points deeply connected to likely questions, and to practice placing each one inside the analysis of a passage rather than beside it. Examiners describe the rewarded version as context in service of reading, and the unrewarded version as reading interrupted by context.

### Q: What makes an examiner pause and re-read a paragraph?

An examiner pauses and re-reads a paragraph when it shows the discriminating quality that decides a band: genuine analysis of language, an argument taking an unexpected but defensible turn, or evidence used in a way that changes the paragraph's claim. The pause is the marker's professional instinct firing, the recognition that something here may lift the script or complicate the provisional band, and it triggers the closer scrutiny of the second reading. Students cannot manufacture the pause with tricks, since examiners read too many scripts to be surprised by formatting or vocabulary, but they can earn it reliably by doing the things the criteria describe: reading a specific word closely, connecting technique to meaning in a fresh way, developing a point to its consequence. The pause is therefore a useful image for revision, because it asks the writer to imagine which paragraph of the essay deserves a second look and why. An essay with no such paragraph is an essay the examiner reads once and places without hesitation, which is rarely the placement the writer wanted.
