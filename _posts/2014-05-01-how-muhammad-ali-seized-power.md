The usual one-line account of how Muhammad Ali took Egypt is that he seized it by force, which is wrong in a specific and interesting way. He took it by being handed it. On 12 May 1805 the religious scholars and notables of Cairo, at the head of a population that had shut the city down in protest, deposed the Ottoman governor and proclaimed an Albanian officer in his place, invested him with the robe of office, and presented the empire with a decision it had not made. The sultan confirmed it two months later because there was nothing else he could usefully do.

![How Muhammad Ali seized power in Egypt after the French withdrawal - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-24.webp)

Six years later the same man invited the leaders of the Mamluk households to a ceremony at the Cairo Citadel, closed the gates on them in a narrow descending passage, and had them shot from the walls. The killing continued across the country for weeks. The elite that had governed Egypt in one form or another since the thirteenth century ended in an afternoon.

Those two events are the whole story and they point in opposite directions, which is why the popular account collapses into either the liberator or the butcher depending on which one it chooses to emphasize. The framework this article advances holds both and can be called the backed-then-purged thesis. Muhammad Ali acquired power through a genuine political alliance with the religious establishment and the population of Cairo, an alliance he spent four years constructing and which was not a fiction. He then secured that power by eliminating every group capable of withdrawing it, including the one that had given it to him.

That sequence is not a contradiction and it is not hypocrisy in any useful sense. It is the standard logic of a man who understood that a throne conferred by others can be taken back by others, and who acted on that understanding with unusual speed and thoroughness. The alliance was real while it was useful. Its dismantling began within four years of the appointment it had produced.

This article is about the mechanics. Not the man, who belongs to the article on [Muhammad Ali Pasha](/2014/04/01/muhammad-ali-pasha-of-egypt/), and not the state he went on to build, which belongs to the article on [Muhammad Ali and the birth of modern Egypt](/2014/03/01/muhammad-ali-and-modern-egypt/). What belongs here is how a subordinate officer in a foreign expeditionary force, arriving in a country he had never seen, converted four years of confusion into a governorship and then spent six more making it irreversible.

## The board in 1801

A seizure of power requires something to seize, and what Egypt offered after the French withdrawal was an unusually complete absence of authority.

The French had occupied the country since 1798 and capitulated at Alexandria in September 1801, an episode set out in the article on [Napoleon's invasion of Egypt](/2013/10/01/napoleon-invasion-of-egypt/). Their departure removed the only government the country had had for three years and put nothing in its place.

What remained were five parties with incompatible intentions.

The Ottoman expeditionary force had come to recover the province and expected to govern it, and it was commanded by officers appointed from Istanbul with the authority of the sultan behind them and very little else. It was a coalition of contingents raised across the empire, unpaid, and held together by the prospect of plunder.

The Mamluk households had survived the French occupation in weakened form. Their field army had been destroyed in 1798, their leaders had been hunted the length of Upper Egypt, and their tax farms and property had been confiscated, but the households still existed, still had cavalry, and still expected to resume the position they had held for two and a half centuries, described in the article on [the Mamluk beys of Ottoman Egypt](/2013/09/01/mamluk-beys-of-ottoman-egypt/). They were also divided into rival factions that hated each other.

The British had come to expel the French and had their own view of who should govern afterward, favouring an arrangement in which the Mamluks retained a share of power as a counterweight to Ottoman authority. They withdrew in 1803, leaving the argument unresolved.

The Albanian contingents within the Ottoman force were a distinct and dangerous element: cohesive, well armed, chronically unpaid, and loyal to their own commanders rather than to the expedition. Muhammad Ali was second in command of one of them.

And the population of Cairo, along with the religious establishment that led it, had learned something during the French occupation that changed everything: that it could riot, negotiate, and force the removal of a government. That lesson is the single most underrated factor in what followed.

### Why was there a vacuum at all?

Because the French removed the old order and the Ottomans could not replace it. Three years of occupation had destroyed the Mamluk system, dispersed the tax farms, and discredited imperial authority, and the force sent to recover Egypt was an unpaid coalition with no administration behind it. Authority had to be rebuilt from nothing, and several parties tried.

## 1802 and 1803: the years of nobody

The first two years after the French left were a sequence of short-lived governments, and following them is necessary because the pattern is what Muhammad Ali learned to exploit.

The Ottoman government appointed a governor, Khusrev Pasha, who attempted to do what every incoming governor of Egypt had attempted for three centuries: establish authority over the armed groups in the province. He tried to disband or reorganize the Albanian contingents, whose pay was in arrears and whose commanders had no intention of being disbanded.

In the spring of 1803 the Albanians mutinied. Their commander, Tahir Pasha, drove the governor out of Cairo and took control of the city. He held it for a matter of weeks before being killed by troops from another contingent in a dispute over, inevitably, pay.

At that point Muhammad Ali became the leader of the Albanian forces, and the manner of it is characteristic. He did not seize the command in a coup; he was the surviving senior officer of a cohesive body of troops whose other claimants had killed each other, and he took it up.

What followed over the next two years is a sequence of alliances that look cynical in summary and were entirely rational at each step.

He allied with the Mamluk beys against the Ottoman governor, and together they drove Khusrev Pasha from Cairo and pursued him to Damietta, where he was captured.

He allowed a new Ottoman appointee to arrive and did not protect him when the situation turned, and that governor was killed within weeks of taking office.

He then turned against his Mamluk allies, whose leaders al-Bardisi and al-Alfi were themselves rivals and could not combine against him, and positioned himself as the party of order against their exactions.

By the time the next governor, Khurshid Pasha, arrived in 1804, there was one armed force in Cairo with a single competent commander, and every alternative had been used and discarded.

### How did he outmanoeuvre the others so consistently?

By never being the strongest party and always being the indispensable one. He joined whichever coalition could remove the current threat, let his allies exhaust themselves and each other, and kept his own force intact and paid while everyone else's fragmented. Four years of that left him the last cohesive actor standing.

## Building the constituency

While the armed parties destroyed each other, Muhammad Ali was doing something none of them thought to do, and it is the reason the seizure took the form it did.

He cultivated the people of Cairo. The mechanism was not charm but restraint: in a city being plundered by unpaid troops from several contingents, he made a point of paying for what his men took, restraining them where he could, and being seen to do so. In the environment of 1803 and 1804 that was a remarkable and highly visible distinction.

He cultivated the merchants, who had capital, who financed troops, and who wanted above all a government that would stop the arbitrary exactions. A commander who could be dealt with predictably was worth supporting.

And he cultivated the religious establishment, which was the decisive relationship. The scholars of al-Azhar were the natural leadership of Cairo society, the only group with standing among the population after the beys had been broken, and the only institution with continuity through the French occupation and after it. They had discovered during that occupation that they could represent the city, negotiate for it, and withhold cooperation from a government.

The central figure was Umar Makram, the naqib al-ashraf, the head of the corporation of those claiming descent from the Prophet, a position that combined religious standing with real social authority and the capacity to mobilize the population. He had opposed the French, had been exiled and returned, and by 1804 was the most influential non-military figure in Cairo.

What Muhammad Ali offered this constituency was simple and it was what they wanted: an end to the exactions, a government that would not loot the city, and a commander who treated them as partners rather than as subjects to be taxed.

What he received in return was a mechanism for making a governor.

## What the French occupation had taught everyone

The three years before the vacuum shaped every actor in it, and the lessons each party drew explain the decade that followed.

The population of Cairo had learned that a government could be resisted. It had risen twice against the French, in 1798 and again in 1800, and had been bombarded and stormed for it, and it had also discovered that an occupying power negotiated, that the religious establishment could speak for the city, and that authority was not simply endured. In 1805 it applied that knowledge to an Ottoman governor.

The religious establishment had learned that it was the only surviving institution with standing among the population and that this gave it leverage. It had served on the French divan, been held responsible by the occupiers for keeping order, and been treated as the party that could deliver the city. Whatever it thought of the experience, it had been in the room.

The Mamluk households had learned that they could be beaten by a modern army and that the population would not defend them. The looting of bey properties by the Cairo crowd the morning after Embabeh was the lesson delivered most directly, and it is the reason their attempted restoration after 1801 had no popular element.

The Ottoman government had learned that it could not hold Egypt without an army it could not afford to keep there, which is why it accepted a governor it had not chosen four years later.

And Muhammad Ali, who arrived in 1801 and watched the last stage of all this, had the advantage of observing the whole situation from outside it. He had no stake in the old order, no assumptions about how Egypt worked, and no loyalty to any party in it, which allowed him to read the board accurately while everyone else was reading it through the expectations of the world before 1798.

That is the most underrated of his advantages. Every other actor was trying to restore something. He was the only one with nothing to restore.

## Cairo in these years

The city where all of this happened deserves a paragraph, because the conditions explain why the population acted as it did.

Cairo in 1803 and 1804 was a large city under armed occupation by several forces at once, none of which was being paid. Troops billeted themselves, requisitioned goods, and extorted money. Markets closed intermittently. Trade was disrupted. The quarters organized their own protection where they could.

On top of that came formal exactions. Each successive governor needed money and levied it on the merchants and the households of the city, which meant that legitimate taxation and outright plunder were experienced as a single continuous burden.

Plague and food shortage recurred, as they did in every decade of the period, against a background of no functioning administration.

The result was a population with an acute and specific grievance and a recent demonstrated capacity to act on it. The 1805 shutdown was not an outbreak of enthusiasm for a particular officer; it was a city at the end of its tolerance choosing the least bad of the available commanders and using the only instrument it had.

That distinction matters for the thesis. The backing was real and it was conditional, and Muhammad Ali understood the condition better than the people offering it did.

## May 1805

The event itself was a strike, and describing it as anything grander misses what made it effective.

Khurshid Pasha, the governor appointed in 1804, had the same problem as his predecessors and less skill in handling it. His troops, including a contingent of Albanians not under Muhammad Ali's command, were unpaid and taking what they wanted from the city. He attempted to raise money by fresh exactions on the population and the merchants.

In the spring of 1805 Cairo stopped. The markets closed, the shops shuttered, crowds assembled, and the religious establishment, with Umar Makram at its head, refused to cooperate with the governor and took the grievance to the streets. This was not spontaneous disorder; it was an organized withdrawal of consent by a city that had done it before against the French and knew the procedure.

The demand was the removal of Khurshid Pasha. The question that followed was who should replace him, and the answer supplied by the scholars was Muhammad Ali.

On 12 May 1805 the assembled notables and scholars proclaimed him governor and invested him with the robe of office, an act that had no basis whatever in Ottoman constitutional practice. Governors were appointed by the sultan, not acclaimed by provincial crowds. What the ceremony did was create a fact and dare the empire to reverse it.

The famous exchange preserved in the accounts has him affecting reluctance and the scholars insisting, which is the conventional form for such moments and should be read as ritual rather than as evidence about his state of mind. He had spent four years positioning for exactly this.

Khurshid refused to go. He barricaded himself in the Citadel with troops loyal to him and held out through the early summer, bombarding parts of the city, while Muhammad Ali governed the rest of it and waited.

### What did the scholars actually have the power to do?

They could close the city. The ulama could not command troops or collect revenue, and they could mobilize the population, withhold the cooperation that made administration possible, and confer or withhold legitimacy in a society where their standing was the only authority the beys had never destroyed. A governor they refused to work with could not govern.

## The firman

Legitimacy in the Ottoman system came from one place, and Muhammad Ali needed it whatever Cairo had decided.

The imperial confirmation arrived in July 1805. The sultan's government, presented with a province that had removed its governor and installed a replacement, issued a firman appointing Muhammad Ali wali of Egypt. Khurshid, deprived of his standing, gave up the Citadel and left.

The decision from Istanbul repays examination, because it looks like weakness and was closer to calculation.

The empire had no practical alternative. It could not garrison Egypt, as the expedition of 1786 had already demonstrated and as the events of 1801 to 1805 confirmed. It had no candidate with local support. The Mamluk households it might once have played against a strong governor were divided and weakened. And it had more urgent problems in the Balkans and with Russia.

Against that, confirming the appointment cost nothing immediately and bought something: a governor who owed his legal title to the sultan, who would pay tribute, and who was at least a known quantity rather than another rotation of appointees being killed on arrival.

There was also a reasonable expectation that he could be moved later, which the empire attempted in 1806 by ordering his transfer to Salonica. He refused, the Cairo notables petitioned against it, a substantial payment was arranged, and the order was rescinded. That episode is the moment the appointment became permanent, because a governor who can decline a transfer and survive it is not really a governor in the ordinary sense.

### Why did Istanbul confirm a governor it had not chosen?

Because refusing would have cost more than accepting. The empire could not garrison Egypt, had no candidate with local support, and was occupied in the Balkans and with Russia. Confirming the appointment bought tribute, a legal relationship, and a stable province, and the government reasonably expected it could move him later. In 1806 it tried and failed.

## 1807: the British test

Two years into the governorship, a European army landed and tried to remove him, and the failure of that attempt consolidated his position more than anything he did himself.

Britain, which had never accepted the settlement in Egypt and which had encouraged the Mamluk interest, dispatched an expedition in March 1807 under General Fraser. It landed near Alexandria and took the city without much difficulty, the population being unenthusiastic about defending a governor it barely knew and the garrison being weak.

The expedition then pushed inland toward Rosetta to secure supplies, and there it was destroyed. The column entered the town and was ambushed in the streets, and a second and larger relief force was cut up in the surrounding country. British losses were severe by the standards of the campaign, and the heads of the dead were displayed in Cairo in the traditional manner, which was noted by every observer and is the detail that most appears in the accounts.

The expedition held Alexandria until September and then negotiated a withdrawal.

The political effect was enormous and it ran in three directions at once. It demonstrated to Istanbul that its new governor could defend the province against a European power, which made him harder to remove and more valuable to keep. It demonstrated to the Egyptian population that he could protect them, which is the one thing the Mamluk beys had comprehensively failed to do in 1798. And it removed the British as a factor supporting a Mamluk restoration, since Britain had tried the direct approach and lost.

A ruler who has beaten a British landing in his second year is a ruler with an argument nobody in Egypt could answer.

## 1809: the ally removed

The dismantling of the coalition that had installed him began four years after it installed him, and the first casualty was the man who had led it.

The issue was taxation of land held under religious endowment and of the associated categories that had been exempt. These holdings represented a substantial share of agricultural property, their revenues funded religious institutions and the scholars who ran them, and their exemption was both a financial interest and a matter of principle for the ulama.

Muhammad Ali needed the revenue and moved to take it. Umar Makram objected, and he objected in the manner of a man who believed he had made a governor and could unmake one, which was a reasonable inference from 1805 and turned out to be four years out of date.

The governor's response was careful. He did not confront the religious establishment as a body; he divided it, cultivating scholars who were rivals of Umar Makram or who could be brought to accept the new arrangement, and isolating the man himself. In 1809 Umar Makram was removed from his position and exiled to Damietta. He never recovered his standing and died in 1822.

No riot followed. The city that had shut itself down in 1805 did not move for the man who had led it, which is the clearest evidence of how completely the position had changed in four years.

The lesson generalizes and it is the core of the thesis this article defends. The alliance was genuine, and it was also a ladder. A ruler who owes his position to a constituency is a ruler that constituency can remove, and the first business of such a ruler is to make himself independent of the people who made him.

### Was the coalition of 1805 sincere?

On both sides, yes, and sincerity is not the point. The scholars wanted an end to plunder and a governor who would deal with them, and they got one. Muhammad Ali wanted the province and got it. What ended the arrangement was not bad faith but the ordinary logic of power: he no longer needed them.

## The last rivals

Between 1805 and 1811 the Mamluk households remained the one armed interest in Egypt outside the governor's control, and their condition in those years explains both why he moved against them and why the move was so easy.

They had been broken as a field force by the French in 1798 and never rebuilt. Their tax farms had been confiscated during the occupation and were not returned. Their capital, carried on the persons of men who had died in the Nile at Embabeh or been hunted through Upper Egypt, was gone. The route that had supplied them with new recruits from the Caucasus had been disrupted by war and by Russian expansion in the region.

They were also leaderless in a way they had not been for decades. The two dominant figures of the post-French period, al-Bardisi and al-Alfi, were rivals who had spent the years after 1801 fighting each other as much as anyone else, and both died in 1806 and 1807 within months of one another. Their successors were lesser men commanding smaller followings.

By 1810 the households held territory in Upper Egypt, retained several thousand cavalry between them, and had no prospect of combining into anything that could take Cairo. They were a nuisance, a claim on the revenue of the south, and a standing invitation to any foreign power looking for a local partner.

That last point is what made them intolerable. Muhammad Ali was about to commit his forces to a campaign in Arabia at the sultan's request, which meant sending his army out of Egypt, and an armed rival interest in the south with a history of foreign backing could not be left behind him.

### Were the beys still a real threat in 1811?

Not as a military force. They could not have taken Cairo and they knew it. They were a threat as a permanent alternative: a body with legitimacy, cavalry, and territory that any foreign power or disaffected faction could rally around, especially with the Egyptian army about to leave for Arabia.

## 1 March 1811

The operation was planned, the pretext was ceremonial, and the execution took under an hour.

The occasion was the investiture of Muhammad Ali's son Tusun as commander of the expedition to Arabia. An event of that kind required the attendance of the province's notables, and invitations went to the Mamluk leaders, who had been treated with conspicuous courtesy in the preceding months and had been given reason to think an accommodation was being reached.

They came. The figures given for the number of beys and their retainers who rode into the Citadel that morning cluster around four to five hundred, and like all figures in this episode they derive from estimates rather than from a count.

The ceremony took place. Afterward the procession formed to leave, descending the narrow walled passage that led down from the Citadel toward the gate, a route with high walls on both sides and room for horsemen to pass only a few abreast.

The gate at the bottom was closed. The gate at the top was closed behind them. Albanian troops positioned on the walls opened fire into the passage.

What followed was not a battle. Men trapped in a walled lane on horseback, under fire from above, with no exit at either end, cannot fight back in any meaningful sense. The killing continued until it was finished, and the bodies were stripped.

The operation did not end at the Citadel. Orders went out the same day and in the following weeks for the killing of Mamluks and their retainers across Egypt, in Cairo, in the provincial towns, and in the households' properties. Estimates for the total run to a thousand or more, with some accounts putting the figure considerably higher when retainers and dependents are included. The households' property was confiscated.

### Why did the beys walk into it?

Because refusing a ceremonial summons from the governor was itself a declaration of hostility they were not strong enough to make, and because they had been courted for months and had reason to believe terms were being negotiated. The same logic had trapped elites in the Ottoman world before, and it worked because the alternative to attending was open rebellion.

## The place

The geography of the Citadel explains why the operation worked, and it is worth describing because most accounts leave it abstract.

The Cairo Citadel stands on a spur of the Muqattam hills above the medieval city, a fortress begun under Saladin and occupied by every regime since. It is a walled compound on high ground with a small number of gates, and the approaches from the city climb steeply.

The route the procession took that morning descended through a narrow way between high walls, wide enough for horsemen to ride a few abreast and no more, with a gate at the lower end. A mounted column in that space is strung out in single file or close to it, cannot turn, cannot spread out, and cannot charge.

Above it, the walls provide a firing position with complete cover and a clear view down into the passage.

Those features are why the location was chosen and why no fighting was possible. Cavalry in the open is formidable; cavalry in a walled lane under fire from above is a target. The beys were the finest horsemen in the world and their horsemanship was made irrelevant by a few hundred metres of masonry.

The site is still there and is shown to visitors, which is one reason the episode retains a physical immediacy that most massacres of the period have lost.

The mosque Muhammad Ali later built on the summit of the same Citadel, where he is buried, stands a short distance from the passage, which is a juxtaposition nobody arranged and which is difficult to overlook.

## The governors who failed

Between 1801 and 1805 the Ottoman government appointed a series of men to govern Egypt and each one failed, and the pattern of their failures is the negative image of Muhammad Ali's success.

Khusrev Pasha arrived with imperial authority and no local base, attempted to reorganize the Albanian contingents whose pay was in arrears, and was driven out by a mutiny within roughly a year.

His immediate successor lasted weeks before being killed.

Khurshid Pasha, appointed in 1804, was the most capable of them and lasted a year. He understood the situation better than his predecessors, attempted to build a force of his own, and failed for the same underlying reason: he had troops he could not pay, in a city he had to tax to pay them, which turned the population against him.

The common element is the absence of money and of any local constituency. An Ottoman governor of Egypt in these years arrived with a document, inherited unpaid soldiers, and had to extract revenue from a population that had just experienced three years of French exactions, in a province whose tax system had been dismantled. Every one of them turned to the same solution and every one of them was destroyed by it.

Muhammad Ali faced the identical problem and solved it differently, by paying his own troops from resources he had accumulated and by making restraint toward the city a deliberate policy. That is not a matter of character; it is a matter of having understood that the population was a resource rather than a revenue source, which none of the appointed governors grasped.

The article on [Ottoman Egypt](/2013/05/01/ottoman-egypt-complete-guide/) sets out how the province's governing arrangement had produced exactly this pattern of weak governors for three centuries, and the collapse of 1801 to 1805 is the final and most acute expression of it.

## The numbers and the survivors

Two questions are asked about the massacre more than any others and both have answers that are ranges rather than figures.

How many died at the Citadel itself is usually given as somewhere between four and five hundred, the number of Mamluk leaders and their immediate retainers who entered the passage. The figure comes from contemporary estimates by people who were in Cairo, including the chronicler al-Jabarti, rather than from any tally.

How many died in total across Egypt in the weeks that followed is much less secure. Numbers in the low thousands appear in the accounts when the households' retainers, servants, and dependents are counted, and the uncertainty is structural, since nobody was recording the deaths of men killed in provincial towns by soldiers acting on an order.

Survivors existed. A body of Mamluks escaped the general destruction, principally by not being in Cairo, and fled south. Several hundred made their way into Nubia and the Sudan, where they established themselves at Dongola and remained a factor until the Egyptian conquest of the Sudan a decade later swept through the region.

The story everyone knows is the leap. In the popular version a single bey, usually named, rode his horse off the Citadel wall, survived the fall when the animal did not, and escaped. It appears in guidebooks, in paintings, and in a great many retellings.

The evidence for it is thin and historians generally treat it as legend. The physical implausibility is considerable, the story attaches to different names in different versions, and it has the shape of the survival tale that attaches itself to every massacre. What is documented is that individual Mamluks survived because they were absent, late, or elsewhere, which is how people usually survive events of this kind and which makes a duller story.

The reason the legend persists is worth noticing. A massacre with no survivor is unbearable to narrate, and a single escape converts an atrocity into a story with a hero in it.

## Was the massacre necessary?

The question is worth asking directly, and the honest answer distinguishes between what it accomplished and what alternatives existed.

What it accomplished is clear. It removed the only organized armed interest in Egypt outside the governor's control, at the moment his army was about to leave for Arabia. It freed the households' remaining property and revenues for confiscation. It ended, permanently, a system of government by armed household that had shaped Egypt since the thirteenth century. And it demonstrated to everyone else in the country what opposition now cost.

Whether there were alternatives is the harder part. The beys were weak, divided, and leaderless, and a case can be made that they could have been absorbed, bought, pensioned, or simply left to decline, as similar groups elsewhere in the empire were. Against that, the Ottoman and Egyptian political culture of the period offered no mechanism for retiring a military elite peacefully, the beys had spent four centuries recovering from setbacks, and the precedent of 1798, when a defeated bey power had reassembled within a few years, was recent.

What can be said with confidence is that the method was chosen rather than forced. Treachery under the cover of ceremony was a recognized technique in the Ottoman world, used against elites who could not be defeated in the field, and Muhammad Ali used it because it was reliable and cheap rather than because nothing else was available.

The judgment on that choice belongs to the article on [whether Muhammad Ali was a reformer or a ruthless ruler](/2014/12/01/muhammad-ali-reformer-or-ruler/). What this article can establish is the mechanics: it was planned, it was effective, it was not a spontaneous act, and it was not his only option.

## How it landed at the time

The reaction in Cairo is documented and it is not what a modern reader expects.

Al-Jabarti, the chronicler, records the event with horror at the manner of it, which is to say at the treachery, the violation of the protection a host owes his guests, and the slaughter of men who had come under invitation. His moral vocabulary is about hospitality and good faith rather than about the loss of the beys as such.

What he does not record is grief. The Mamluk households had taxed Cairo hard, fought in its streets, failed to protect it in 1798, and had their properties looted by the city's own population the morning after that defeat. Their destruction was received as the removal of a nuisance by treacherous means rather than as a tragedy.

The religious establishment did not object, or did not object audibly, two years after the exile of Umar Makram had shown what objection cost.

The wider population appears to have registered it as one more violent event in a decade full of them, with the significant difference that this one was followed by a government that lasted.

European consuls reported it with a mixture of disapproval and calculation, noting that the province now had a single undisputed authority and adjusting their expectations accordingly.

And the Ottoman government accepted it. The sultan had lost nothing: the beys had been a rival power that paid the empire little and obeyed it less, and their removal by a governor who continued to forward the tribute was a net improvement from Istanbul's point of view.

The absence of consequences is the most telling detail. A ruler who massacres his rivals and faces no reckoning from any quarter is a ruler who has correctly read his position.

## The technique had precedents

Treachery under ceremonial cover was not invented in 1811, and placing the episode in its tradition explains why it worked.

The essential logic is the same in every case. An elite that cannot be beaten in open conflict, because it is armed, dispersed, and capable of raising forces, can be destroyed if it can be persuaded to assemble somewhere the host controls. The occasion must be one the targets cannot refuse without declaring war, which is why ceremonies, investitures, banquets, and negotiations are the standard settings.

The Ottoman world knew the method. Provincial governors and imperial authorities had used variations on it against troublesome notables, and the most famous later instance is the destruction of the janissary corps at Istanbul in 1826, when the sultan provoked and then annihilated the corps that had blocked military reform for a century. That operation was larger, bloodier, and more openly military, and it rested on the same insight: an entrenched armed interest can only be removed all at once.

There is a direct connection worth noting. Muhammad Ali's success in 1811 was observed in Istanbul, and the demonstration that a modernizing ruler could eliminate an obstructive military caste and survive it fed into the calculations that produced 1826. The pupil taught the master, which is one of the odder relationships in the period.

The technique has costs beyond the moral ones. It can only be used once against a given group, it makes every subsequent invitation suspect, and it establishes a precedent that the ruler's own successors and subordinates will remember. Muhammad Ali was never afterward able to summon anyone to a ceremony without the memory being present.

## The men who did it

The Albanian troops who had brought him to Cairo carried out the killing, and their position in the story is worth setting out because it ends shortly afterward.

They were the force he had inherited in 1803 and the base of everything he had built since: cohesive, effective, loyal to their commanders, and dangerous to anyone who could not pay them. They had removed governors, fought the Mamluks, beaten the British at Rosetta, and now destroyed the beys.

They were also, for exactly those reasons, the next problem. An armed constituency that had made a ruler could unmake one, which was the lesson of 1803 and of 1805 alike, and Muhammad Ali had no intention of remaining dependent on them.

His solution was to spend them. The Arabian expedition that provided the pretext for the Citadel ceremony was also the operation that removed the Albanians from Egypt, sending them across the Red Sea to fight a long, difficult, and attritional campaign in the Hijaz and the Najd from which many did not return. Others followed to the Sudan.

Over the following decade they were progressively replaced by the conscript army of Egyptian peasants that became the instrument of the reign. By the time that force existed, the men who had put him in power in 1805 and killed his rivals in 1811 were no longer in a position to matter.

That is the same pattern as the removal of Umar Makram, applied to a different constituency and executed by attrition rather than by exile. Every group that helped him rise was dismantled within a decade of helping.

## The spoils

A seizure of power is also a transfer of property, and what happened to the Mamluk holdings shaped Egypt for a century.

The households' remaining estates, urban properties, and revenue rights were confiscated after 1811 and taken into the state's hands, joining the tax farms already being dismantled through the fiscal reform of the preceding years.

That property became the pool from which the new regime rewarded its servants. Grants went to family members, officials, and officers on terms that obliged development and payment, and those grants hardened over the following decades into private ownership.

The large estates of the later nineteenth century, and the landed class that dominated Egyptian politics until 1952, originate substantially in this redistribution. A political elimination in 1811 produced a landholding structure that outlasted the dynasty that created it.

The revenue mattered as much as the land. Removing the households ended the leakage of Egyptian agricultural income into the maintenance of private cavalry establishments, and the money went instead into the treasury that funded the army, the schools, and the factories described in the article on [how Muhammad Ali modernized Egypt](/2014/06/01/how-muhammad-ali-modernized-egypt/).

## Money

Every stage of the seizure turned on cash, and following the money is the least romantic and most explanatory way to read the decade.

The basic problem in Egypt between 1801 and 1805 was that troops had to be paid and nobody could pay them. The French had stripped the country, the tax system had been dismantled, the Mamluk revenues had been confiscated and dispersed, and the Ottoman expedition had arrived without a treasury behind it. Every commander in the province was short, and shortage was what destroyed governments.

Muhammad Ali's advantage was that he understood this as a commercial rather than a military problem. He borrowed from Cairo merchants, who preferred a predictable commander to unpaid mobs in their streets. He took what revenue he could from districts under his control and accounted for it rather than simply plundering. And he kept his force small enough to pay, resisting the temptation to accumulate men he could not sustain.

After 1805 the pattern intensified. The confiscation of endowed property from 1809 and of Mamluk holdings from 1811 funded the expansion, and the dismantling of the tax farm channelled agricultural revenue to the centre for the first time in centuries. The Arabian campaign, the schools, the factories, and the conscript army all rest on that base.

The merchants who backed him early did well and were absorbed into the new order, and the monopoly system that followed eventually cut across their independent trade, which is another instance of the pattern: the constituency that helped him was useful until the state could do without it.

## The Arabian hinge

The campaign that provided the pretext for the massacre was also the operation that completed the consolidation, and the two functions were not a coincidence.

The sultan had asked for help against the Wahhabi movement, which had taken the holy cities and disrupted the pilgrimage, and the request was both an obligation and an opportunity. Refusing would have damaged the relationship with Istanbul that his position depended on. Accepting delivered three things at once.

It removed the Albanian irregulars from Egypt. Sending them to fight a long attritional war across the Red Sea solved the problem of an armed constituency that had made one governor and could unmake another.

It required the elimination of the Mamluks first, since the army could not leave with an armed rival interest in Upper Egypt, which supplied the timing for 1811.

And it delivered religious prestige of a kind no other action could. Restoring the holy cities and the pilgrimage was the most legitimating act available to any Muslim ruler, and for a governor of foreign origin with no religious credentials it was worth more than a victory.

The campaign ran from 1811 to 1818 and ended the first Saudi state. By the time it was over, the Albanians were spent, the beys were gone, the religious establishment had been subordinated, and the conscript army was beginning to form.

The seizure of power, properly understood, does not end in 1811. It ends somewhere around 1818, when there was no longer anyone in Egypt who had not been placed there by the man governing it.

## The method distilled

Read as a single operation from 1801 to 1811, the seizure shows a consistent method, and setting it out is the most useful thing this article can offer.

Never be the strongest party early. In 1803 and 1804 he was one force among several and he avoided the position of principal threat, which is the position that attracts coalitions. He let the Ottomans, the beys, and the rival Albanian commanders identify each other as the danger.

Let opponents remove each other. Khusrev Pasha was driven out by an alliance he joined rather than led. The beys' two dominant leaders destroyed each other's factions and then died. Successive imperial appointees failed without his having to break them.

Keep one force intact and paid. Through a decade in which every other contingent in Egypt fragmented over arrears, he kept his Albanians together. That is a matter of money and management rather than of charisma, and it was the material basis of everything else.

Build a constituency nobody else was courting. The armed parties competed for troops and revenue. He competed for the city, which cost little, took years, and produced the mechanism that made him governor.

Take legitimacy from wherever it is available and then secure the formal version. The acclamation of 1805 had no standing in Ottoman law and created a fact. The firman two months later converted the fact into a title.

Remove your creators before they remember they created you. Umar Makram in 1809, the beys in 1811, the Albanians through the Arabian campaign over the following years.

And convert every contingent asset into a structural one. A command became a governorship, a governorship became control of revenue, revenue became an army recruited from a population that had no independent power at all.

## The reading that gets it wrong

The popular account that he simply took power by force fails on the evidence and it fails in an instructive way.

He never won a battle for Cairo. There is no engagement in which his troops defeated the Ottoman garrison or the Mamluk households and thereby took the capital. The city was handed to him by its own notables, and the Citadel was surrendered to him because an imperial document arrived.

Force was present throughout and it was never sufficient. His Albanians were the most reliable body of troops in Egypt and they were a few thousand men in a country of three or four million, incapable of holding it against a united opposition. What prevented a united opposition was politics.

The force-alone reading also misses the timing. If it were a military seizure, it should have happened in 1803 when he took command of the Albanians and the province was at its most chaotic. It happened in 1805, after two further years of positioning, because he was waiting for a political opportunity rather than a military one.

And it makes the 1811 massacre inexplicable. A ruler who had taken power by force would have had no need to invite his rivals to a ceremony; he would have fought them. The treachery was necessary precisely because the beys could not be destroyed by the methods available to him.

The accurate reading is that he was a political operator who commanded troops, not a soldier who happened to govern, and the distinction runs through the whole career.

## The crowd

One element of 1805 deserves separate attention because it is genuinely new in Egyptian history and it is usually passed over.

The Cairo population acted as a political force. It closed the markets, assembled, sustained the pressure for weeks, and forced the removal of a governor. It had done something similar against the French in 1798 and 1800, and it did it again in 1805 with better organization and a clearer objective.

That capacity had not existed in any comparable form under the Mamluk beys, whose politics ran between households and had no place for the city as an actor. It developed during the French occupation, when the population discovered that a government could be resisted and that the religious establishment could speak for it.

The pattern recurs through the following two centuries of Egyptian history with a consistency that makes 1805 look like an origin rather than an incident: the city assembles, the religious or professional leadership articulates the demand, and the government either concedes or is removed.

The irony is that the first ruler produced by this mechanism spent his reign ensuring it could not be used again. The exile of Umar Makram, the destruction of the beys, and the construction of a state that dealt with its population through conscription and registration rather than through negotiation were all, among other things, answers to the question of what had put him in power.

## How we know

The evidence for the decade after 1801 is good by the standards of earlier Egyptian history and uneven in a way worth understanding.

The indispensable source is al-Jabarti, the Cairo scholar whose chronicle covers the French occupation, the chaos that followed, the rise of Muhammad Ali, and the early years of his government. He was present in Cairo throughout, moved in the circles that made the decisions of 1805, and recorded events year by year as they happened rather than in retrospect. His account of the massacre is the closest thing to eyewitness testimony that exists.

His limitations matter. He was a member of the religious establishment and his sympathies run with it, which colours his treatment of the taxation of endowments and the fall of Umar Makram. He grew increasingly hostile to the regime as it developed, and his later judgments are those of a man who had seen his own class dispossessed. And he is an urban scholar writing about urban politics, so the provinces appear only when something happens in them.

Ottoman records supply the imperial side: the appointment documents, the correspondence about the attempted transfer in 1806, and the chancery view of what was happening in the province. They are valuable for what Istanbul knew and decided, and they naturally record the formal version of events.

European consular correspondence is extensive, because every power had agents watching Egypt closely in these years and the French and British in particular were reporting on a situation they had been fighting over a year or two earlier. Consuls are useful for dates, for the movements of troops, and for commercial detail, and they are outsiders interpreting a politics they did not fully understand.

What is thin is the view from inside the losing sides. The Mamluk households left almost no written account of their own calculations between 1801 and 1811, so their decisions, including the decision to attend the ceremony in 1811, have to be inferred from what they did. The Albanians left nothing. And the population of Cairo, whose action in 1805 was decisive, appears entirely through the eyes of the scholars who led it.

## The chronology, tightened

Since the decade contains a great many short-lived governments, a compressed sequence helps.

In September 1801 the French capitulate and leave. Through 1802 an Ottoman force occupies the country while Mamluk households return and the British negotiate for their position.

In 1803 the Albanian contingents mutiny over pay, their commander seizes Cairo and is killed within weeks, and Muhammad Ali takes over the Albanian command. He allies with the beys, the Ottoman governor is driven out and captured at Damietta, a replacement is appointed and killed.

In 1804 Khurshid Pasha becomes governor. Muhammad Ali separates from the beys, whose leaders are at war with each other, and positions himself as the party of order in a city suffering from the exactions of unpaid troops.

In the spring of 1805 Cairo shuts down. On 12 May the notables and scholars depose Khurshid and invest Muhammad Ali. In July the sultan's confirmation arrives and Khurshid gives up the Citadel.

In 1806 Istanbul orders him to Salonica, he declines, a payment and a petition settle the matter.

In 1807 a British expedition lands, takes Alexandria, is destroyed at Rosetta, and withdraws in September.

In 1809 Umar Makram is exiled over the taxation of endowed land.

On 1 March 1811 the Mamluk leaders are killed at the Citadel and across the country in the weeks following.

Ten years, four phases, one outcome.

## Al-Jabarti's changing verdict

The chronicler who is our principal witness changed his mind about Muhammad Ali across the decade, and tracking that change is one of the best available ways to read the seizure from inside.

In the early entries the tone is neutral to mildly favourable. An Albanian commander who restrains his troops and pays for what he takes is, in the Cairo of 1803, a notable improvement on the alternatives, and al-Jabarti records it as such.

Around 1805 the tone is approving. The removal of Khurshid Pasha and the installation of a governor chosen by the notables is presented as the city acting properly through its recognized leadership, which is exactly how a scholar of that establishment would see it.

From 1809 the tone hardens. The taxation of endowed property struck directly at the material base of his own class and at a legal category the scholars regarded as inviolable, and the exile of Umar Makram removed the man who embodied the arrangement of 1805. The chronicle registers both as betrayals.

By 1811 the account of the massacre is one of horror, focused on the treachery rather than on the victims, and the later entries describe a government of arbitrary exactions and conscription with steadily increasing hostility.

Two things follow for reading him. The first is that his shift is evidence: a contemporary who supported the 1805 settlement and turned against the regime by 1811 is telling us something real about what changed. The second is that the hostility of the later entries colours his account of the earlier years when he revised them, which is a standard problem with chronicles written across decades.

He is still the best source we have, and the best source is not a neutral one.

## Did he plan it from the beginning?

The tempting reading is that the whole decade was a single design executed to schedule, and the evidence does not support it.

What the record shows is a series of decisions each of which makes sense on the information available at the time, and which together produce a coherent outcome. Staying in Egypt in 1801 when his commander left. Taking the Albanian command in 1803 when the alternative claimants had killed each other. Allying with the beys and then separating from them as their factions collapsed. Cultivating the city through 1804 as the governor's position deteriorated.

None of that requires a plan and all of it requires a method, which is the distinction worth holding. He was consistently good at identifying which asset mattered next and acquiring it, and that capability produces something that looks like foresight in retrospect without anyone having forecast anything.

The clearest evidence against the master-plan reading is the number of things that could have gone otherwise. Tahir Pasha's death in 1803 was not his doing. The deaths of al-Bardisi and al-Alfi in 1806 and 1807 were not arranged by him. The British decision to land in 1807 was outside his control and could have ended him. The sultan's confirmation in 1805 was a judgment made in Istanbul for Istanbul's reasons.

What was his was the positioning that meant each of those events benefited him rather than somebody else, which is a smaller claim than omniscience and a more interesting one.

## Why 1811 and not earlier

The timing of the massacre was not arbitrary and working out why it happened when it did explains the operation better than the operation itself.

He could not have done it in 1805 or 1806. The beys were still numerous, still held cavalry in strength, and were still a party the British and the Ottomans might have backed. His own position depended on a coalition, his troops were the same Albanians who had served alongside the Mamluks only months before, and a treacherous massacre would have destroyed the reputation for good faith on which his relationship with Cairo rested.

He did not need to do it in 1807, when the British expedition made the beys briefly relevant again and then removed them as a foreign-backed option by failing.

By 1809 the domestic situation had changed decisively. Umar Makram was gone and the religious establishment had been shown what opposition cost. The tax farms were being dismantled and the beys' financial base with them. Their leadership had died off.

And in 1810 and 1811 an immediate reason appeared. The sultan wanted the Wahhabi problem in Arabia dealt with, the campaign would take the Egyptian army out of the country for years, and leaving an armed rival interest in Upper Egypt behind a departing army was not acceptable.

So the timing had three components: the beys were weak enough to be destroyed cheaply, his own position was strong enough to absorb the reputational cost, and an operation abroad made the risk of leaving them intolerable. All three had to be true at once and all three were true in early 1811.

That is what distinguishes the massacre from an act of temper. It happened at the first moment it was both possible and necessary, which is the signature of a calculating operator rather than a cruel one, and which is a distinction with limited comfort in it.

## What the beys thought was happening

The losing side's perspective is reconstructible only by inference, and the inference is worth making.

They had spent a decade trying to recover a position that had been destroyed in 1798, and by 1810 the realistic assessment available to them was that it could not be recovered. Their leaders were dead, their revenue was gone, their recruitment was disrupted, and the governor in Cairo had beaten a British landing and outlasted three imperial appointees.

What was left was an accommodation. A negotiated settlement in which the surviving households kept a position in Upper Egypt, retained some revenue, and accepted the governor's authority was the reasonable outcome to work toward, and the courteous treatment they received through late 1810 and early 1811 was consistent with exactly that.

Attending the investiture of the governor's son as commander of a campaign was, in that reading, the visible acceptance of the new order and the occasion on which their own place in it would be acknowledged.

They were not fools. They were men reading the situation accurately, concluding correctly that they had lost, and walking into a ceremony that confirmed it, which is what makes the operation work and what makes it an ambush rather than a battle.

## Counterfactuals

Two are worth running because they isolate what was contingent and what was structural.

Suppose the Cairo notables had chosen someone else in 1805. There were other Ottoman officers in Egypt and other candidates could have been proposed. The strongest reason they did not is that nobody else had spent four years courting them or had kept a force intact, and any alternative would have faced the same problems with fewer resources. The likely outcome is another short governorship and a continuation of the cycle.

Suppose Istanbul had refused the confirmation in 1805 and sent a force. It had done exactly that in 1786 against the beys and the operation had succeeded militarily and failed politically, since the expedition could not stay. With the Balkans and Russia demanding attention in 1805, the empire had less capacity than in 1786, and the probable outcome is a prolonged conflict ending in the same accommodation reached three or four years later.

The structural conclusion from both is that somebody was going to consolidate Egypt after 1801, because the vacuum was real and the imperial centre could not fill it. What was contingent is that the person who did was unusually competent, which is why the consolidation produced a state rather than a warlord.

A third is worth running for its own sake. Suppose the Mamluk leaders had declined the invitation in 1811 and withdrawn to Upper Egypt instead. The likely outcome is a campaign in the south rather than a massacre in the north, conducted by an army that had already demonstrated in Arabia what it could do, against households with no capacity to combine. The beys would have lost, more slowly and at greater cost, and with the fighting spread over a region whose grain Cairo depended on. That comparison is the strongest version of the necessity argument: the alternative to the ambush was not the survival of the households but a longer and more destructive war to the same end. It is also the argument's limit, since a longer war would have been fought against armed men rather than against guests, and the objection contemporaries actually raised was about the treachery rather than about the deaths.

What all three counterfactuals share is that they change the route and not the destination. By 1810 the structural position of every party in Egypt except the governor was hopeless, and the interesting historical questions are about how that position was reached between 1801 and 1809 rather than about what happened in the passage on 1 March.

## What the seizure decided

The events of 1805 and 1811 settled several questions permanently, and listing them shows why the decade from 1801 rather than any other is the hinge of modern Egyptian history.

It settled who would govern Egypt for the next century and a half. The dynasty founded on the 1805 appointment and secured by the 1811 massacre held the country until 1952.

It settled that Egypt would be governed by a single central authority rather than by competing armed households. The system of government by bayt, which had operated in one form or another since the Ayyubids and Mamluks, ended in the Citadel passage. No comparable arrangement has existed since.

It settled that the revenue would be collected by the state. The elimination of the households removed the last defenders of the tax farm, and the fiscal reform that followed had no organized opposition.

It settled that the religious establishment would be a social authority rather than a political one. The exile of Umar Makram drew the line, and every subsequent Egyptian government has operated within it.

It settled that Egypt would be a military power. The Arabian campaign whose investiture provided the pretext for the massacre was the beginning of three decades of conquest, and it required the conscript army that transformed the country.

And it settled, without anyone intending it, that the population of Cairo would matter politically. The mechanism that produced the 1805 appointment was suppressed by the ruler it produced and has recurred in Egyptian history ever since.

## What survived of the Mamluks

The destruction was thorough and it was not total, and the remnants are a small but real part of the story.

Several hundred escaped south and established themselves in Nubia and the Sudan, principally at Dongola, where they held a position for roughly a decade. The Egyptian conquest of the Sudan from 1820, conducted by the same governor's sons, ended that refuge, and the survivors were dispersed, absorbed, or killed.

Individual Mamluks who had been absent or who made terms afterward were taken into Egyptian service, and a number appear in the records of the following decades holding minor positions. Their households, however, were gone: the property, the recruitment, and the capacity to maintain a private force had all been removed.

The word itself persisted for a while in Egypt as a description of household retainers, gradually losing any connection to the political institution it had named.

And the system's central technique, the purchase and training of imported military slaves, disappeared from Egypt entirely. The army that replaced the households was recruited from Egyptian peasants, which is the single largest social change of the reign.

The Mamluk phase of Egyptian history, running from 1250 in one form and from the Ottoman conquest in another, therefore ends with a precise date, which is rare in history and is the reason 1 March 1811 appears in every chronology of the country.

## The seizure among its contemporaries

Placing the episode beside comparable events elsewhere shows what was ordinary about it and what was not.

Provincial strongmen consolidating power as central authority weakened was the general condition of the Ottoman world in this period, and the techniques were shared: build a personal force, cultivate local notables, obtain imperial confirmation for a position already taken, and eliminate rivals when strong enough. Ali Pasha of Ioannina, in the western Balkans, followed almost exactly this sequence over the same decades.

What distinguishes Muhammad Ali is what came after the consolidation. Most such figures used their position to extract revenue and maintain a retinue, and left nothing behind when they were eventually destroyed. Ali Pasha was hunted down and killed in 1822 having built no institutions at all.

The difference lies in the choices made immediately after 1811. He abolished the revenue system that had funded his rise rather than exploiting it, raised an army from the population rather than from his own following, and built an administration that did not depend on personal loyalty. Each of those steps reduced his immediate power in exchange for durability.

The seizure, in other words, is not what makes the career remarkable. Seizures were common. What was uncommon was using one to build something.

## Outside Cairo

The seizure is usually told entirely as a capital-city story and the provinces were not passive, which matters for understanding how complete the consolidation was.

The Delta towns experienced the decade as a succession of armed parties demanding supplies and money, with no authority capable of adjudicating between them. Rosetta and Damietta, as ports, were particularly exposed and also particularly consequential: Khusrev Pasha was captured at Damietta in 1803, and the British expedition was destroyed at Rosetta in 1807.

Upper Egypt was where the Mamluk households retreated and reconstituted, as they had during the French occupation and as they had done after every setback for centuries. The south was never securely under the government's control between 1801 and 1811, and the grain that fed Cairo came through territory the beys could interfere with, which is a large part of why they remained a problem long after they had ceased to be a military threat.

Bedouin confederations operated through all of this as they always had, taxing the roads, raiding where profitable, and supplying cavalry to whichever party could pay for it.

The elimination of 1811 was therefore not simply an event in Cairo. Orders went out to provincial commanders and the killing was carried out across the country over the following weeks, which is what made it decisive rather than merely dramatic. A massacre of the leadership alone would have left the households in the south to regroup under new men, as had happened repeatedly before. Extending it nationally is what broke the system.

## What it cost people who were not in it

The decade's violence fell heavily on people with no stake in the outcome, and an account of a seizure of power owes them a paragraph.

Cairo was bombarded by its own governor in 1805, when Khurshid Pasha held the Citadel and shelled parts of the city below. Quarters were fought through repeatedly during the earlier mutinies and factional clashes.

The countryside supplied grain, animals, and money to every armed party in turn, under compulsion, with no prospect of redress and frequently more than once in a season. The tax system had collapsed, which meant extraction happened by requisition rather than by assessment, and requisition falls where the soldiers are.

Trade was disrupted for the better part of a decade, which in a city dependent on the grain trade and the Red Sea routes meant shortage and price volatility for people living close to the margin.

Plague and food shortage recurred against a background of no functioning administration and no reserves.

And the 1811 killings extended well beyond the beys themselves to retainers, servants, and household dependents who were killed because of whose household they belonged to, which was the ordinary logic of such operations and is no less an atrocity for that.

The period is usually narrated as a contest between commanders. It was also a decade in which a large population absorbed the costs of that contest and had no voice in it except once, in May 1805, when Cairo shut itself down and got a government.

## The long shadow

Two elements of the seizure kept recurring in Egyptian history long after everyone involved was dead.

The first is the pattern of 1805: a city assembling, a leadership articulating a demand, and a government conceding or falling. That mechanism appeared again in 1881 and 1882, in 1919, in 1952 in a military version, and repeatedly since. The first successful use of it produced Muhammad Ali, and every ruler since has had to reckon with it.

The second is the pattern of 1811: a ruler securing his position by removing an entire competing institution at once. The technique was watched in Istanbul and informed the destruction of the janissaries in 1826, and the general lesson that an entrenched armed interest can only be removed comprehensively has been applied in the region since with some regularity.

Those two patterns are in tension, which is the tension of the whole decade. A regime that came to power because a population could act, and which then spent sixty years and several reigns ensuring that populations could not, is the founding arrangement of the modern Egyptian state, and the contradiction has never quite resolved.

## How historians have read it

Interpretation of the seizure has shifted with the interpretation of the man, and the phases are the same ones described elsewhere in this cluster.

Nineteenth century European writing treated 1811 as the necessary act of a reformer clearing away a decayed feudal caste. The treachery was noted and excused on the grounds that the Mamluks were an obstacle to progress and that oriental politics operated by different rules, a framing that flattered the writer as much as the subject.

Egyptian nationalist historiography had a harder time with it, since a national founder who massacres his guests is difficult material. The usual solution was to emphasize the necessity, present the beys as a foreign military caste oppressing Egyptians, and treat 1811 as the removal of an alien occupation. That reading has some force, since the beys genuinely were a foreign caste, and it sits awkwardly beside the fact that the man who removed them was also foreign and also ruled a population he did not come from.

The developmentalist accounts of the mid twentieth century tended to fold the seizure into the reform narrative: the old order had to go for the new one to be built, and the method was regrettable.

The archival scholarship, working from the state's own records, has produced something different. It reads the decade as a study in political consolidation, with the alliance-building of 1803 to 1805 given as much weight as the violence of 1811, and it emphasizes that the operation was planned, timed, and executed as policy rather than improvised. Khaled Fahmy's work on the period is the principal reference for this reading.

Where argument continues is on the necessity question and on how much weight to give the popular element in 1805. Some accounts treat the acclamation as a genuine popular movement that Muhammad Ali rode; others treat it as an event he substantially engineered through four years of cultivation. The evidence supports something in between: he created the conditions and the city acted on its own grievances.

## The moral question, stated plainly

This article defers the verdict to the debate article and it should not pretend the question is not there.

The case against is straightforward. Four to five hundred men were invited to a ceremony under the protection an Ottoman host owed his guests, disarmed by the occasion, trapped in a passage, and shot. Killings across the country followed for weeks and took in retainers and dependents who had no part in any rivalry. The operation violated a norm that the society itself took seriously, which is why al-Jabarti's account is horrified specifically about the treachery.

The case for necessity is weaker than its defenders claim and not empty. The beys had regenerated after every previous defeat across five centuries. No mechanism existed in that political culture for retiring a military elite peacefully. And the army was about to leave the country.

The case that the question is anachronistic is the one to resist. Treachery against guests was condemned by contemporaries in their own terms, not merely by later readers, and al-Jabarti's horror is the evidence.

What this article establishes is the factual basis on which any judgment has to rest: it was planned rather than spontaneous, it was timed for advantage rather than forced by crisis, alternatives existed even if they were unattractive, and the man who ordered it faced no consequence of any kind.

## Why the story gets told badly

Three habits recur in popular accounts and each one loses something.

The first is starting at 1811. An account that opens with the massacre presents a ruler who took power by butchery, which inverts the actual sequence and removes the most interesting part of the story, which is the six years of politics that preceded it.

The second is romanticizing 1805. An account that presents the acclamation as a popular revolution installing a beloved liberator has to explain the exile of the man who led it four years later, and usually does so by ignoring him.

The third is the leap. A massacre narrated around a single improbable escape becomes an adventure story, and the legendary status of that escape is rarely mentioned.

The corrective is to hold the decade as one operation with two halves, in which a genuine alliance was built and then dismantled, and in which the same qualities produced both.

## Why nobody reversed it

A seizure is only complete when reversing it stops being worth anyone's while, and the reason that point was reached deserves its own examination.

The Ottoman government had the strongest formal claim and the weakest practical position. It tried a transfer in 1806 and failed. It could have sent an expedition, as it had against the beys in 1786, and the calculation that had governed then still held: the province could be reconquered and could not be held without a permanent garrison the treasury could not fund. After 1811 the argument for trying weakened further, because the governor was delivering the tribute, had beaten a British landing, and was about to recover the holy cities on the empire's behalf.

Britain had tried the direct approach in 1807 and lost a column at Rosetta. It had no local partner left after 1811, since the Mamluk interest it had favoured no longer existed, and its attention turned to the war with France. By the time Britain was in a position to think about Egypt again, the governor was an established power with an army.

France had been expelled in 1801 and had no appetite for a second attempt, and from the 1810s it found a technically minded ruler who bought French instructors and machinery a more useful proposition than a client regime.

Inside Egypt the answer is simpler: after 1811 there was nobody. The beys were destroyed, the religious establishment had watched its leader exiled without moving, the Albanian troops were being sent abroad, and no other institution with armed force or independent revenue existed. The conscript army that replaced the Albanians was recruited from a peasantry that had no political organization at all, which is precisely what made it safe.

The absence of any reversal is therefore not a mystery requiring explanation by the ruler's brilliance. It follows from a sequence in which every party that might have acted was either destroyed, discredited, bought, or distracted, and the sequence was constructed deliberately over ten years.

That is the final point this article can make about the mechanics. A seizure of power is usually described as the moment authority changes hands. It is more accurately described as the process by which every alternative to the new holder is removed, and by that measure the seizure of Egypt took from 1801 to somewhere around 1818 and the events of 1805 and 1811 are the two stages where it becomes visible.

## Errors worth avoiding

The specific mistakes that recur about these years are easy to list and worth fixing.

That he conquered Egypt. He fought no battle for Cairo and won the city politically. The only battle of the period he won was against a British expedition two years after he was already governor.

That the 1805 appointment was an Ottoman decision. It was a Cairo decision that Istanbul ratified two months later because it had no better option.

That the ulama were his lasting allies. They installed him in 1805 and their leader was exiled in 1809 over taxation, with no popular reaction whatever.

That the massacre was a battle. Men trapped in a walled passage under fire from above did not fight, and calling it a battle obscures what happened.

That it ended the Mamluks in a single day. The Citadel killings were followed by weeks of killing across the country, and several hundred escaped to the Sudan where they persisted for a decade.

That a bey escaped by leaping his horse from the walls. The story attaches to different names, is physically implausible, and is treated as legend by historians. Survivors survived by being absent.

That the massacre was unprovoked cruelty. It was a planned operation timed to a strategic requirement, which is a different and colder thing.

And that the beys were still powerful. They were weak, divided, leaderless, and financially broken, which is why the operation was cheap, and they remained a standing alternative that a foreign power could have used, which is why it happened at all.

## The verdict

The backed-then-purged thesis holds, and stating it carefully requires giving both halves their full weight rather than letting one qualify the other into insignificance.

The backing was real. Four years of deliberate work with the merchants and the religious establishment of Cairo produced, in May 1805, an event in which a city withdrew its cooperation from a governor and named a replacement. That had no basis in Ottoman practice and it worked, because the empire could not afford to contest it. Muhammad Ali became governor of Egypt by acclamation, which is a genuinely unusual route to provincial office in that system and which gave him a legitimacy no appointee had possessed.

He did not fake that alliance and he did not simply exploit a crowd. The grievances the city acted on were its own, the restraint of his troops that distinguished him was a real and visible policy, and the scholars who proposed him were making a considered choice among the available commanders.

The purge was equally real and it began almost immediately. Umar Makram, the man who had organized the acclamation, was exiled within four years over the taxation of endowments, and the city that had shut itself down for him in 1805 did not move. The Mamluk households, the last armed interest outside his control, were destroyed in 1811 by an ambush at a ceremony, followed by weeks of killing across the country. And the Albanian troops who had done both the fighting and the killing were spent in Arabia and the Sudan over the following decade and replaced by a conscript army drawn from a population that had no independent power at all.

Every group that helped him rise was dismantled within ten years of helping. That is not an indictment so much as a description of what he understood: power conferred by others is held at their discretion, and a ruler who intends to build anything has to remove the discretion.

The verdict on the method belongs to the debate article. What this article concludes is narrower and firmer. The seizure was not a coup, not a conquest, and not a popular revolution. It was a decade-long political operation in which an outsider with a small force and no standing built a coalition, converted it into an office, secured the office against the empire that granted it, and then eliminated in sequence every party capable of taking it back, finishing with the one that had given it to him.

Understood that way, 1805 and 1811 stop being a contradiction and become two stages of the same plan.

## Using this for study and revision

This is an event question and examiners want causation rather than narrative, so the structure matters more than the details.

Fix four dates and what each one settled. September 1801: the French leave and the vacuum opens. 12 May 1805: the Cairo notables depose Khurshid Pasha and invest Muhammad Ali, with the imperial firman following in July. 1809: Umar Makram is exiled and the coalition that made him is broken. 1 March 1811: the Mamluk leaders are killed at the Citadel and the old order ends.

Fix the mechanism at each stage: a vacuum nobody else could fill, a constituency nobody else was courting, an acclamation with no legal basis that the empire had to ratify, and an ambush timed to the departure of the army for Arabia.

Fix the correction: he did not take Egypt by force, and the force-alone reading fails because he never fought for Cairo and because the 1811 treachery would have been unnecessary if he had.

And fix the through-line, which is the thesis: backed, then purged, with every creator removed within a decade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali seize power?

Politically rather than militarily, over roughly a decade. Arriving in 1801 as second in command of a small Albanian contingent, he took over that force in 1803 after its commander was killed, allied with the Mamluk beys against the Ottoman governor, then separated from the beys as their own leaders fought each other. While the armed parties destroyed one another he did what none of them thought to do, cultivating the merchants and the religious scholars of Cairo by restraining his troops and paying for what they took. In May 1805 the city shut down against the sitting governor and the notables proclaimed Muhammad Ali in his place, and the sultan confirmed it in July. He then spent six years removing everyone capable of reversing the decision.

### Q: What was the massacre of the Mamluks?

The killing of the Mamluk leadership at the Cairo Citadel on 1 March 1811. The occasion was a ceremony investing his son Tusun as commander of an expedition to Arabia, and the Mamluk beys, who had been courted with conspicuous courtesy for months, were invited and attended. Somewhere between four and five hundred rode in. After the ceremony the procession formed to leave and entered the narrow walled passage descending from the Citadel, the gates at both ends were closed, and Albanian troops on the walls above opened fire into it. Men on horseback in a lane a few abreast wide, under fire from above with no exit, could not fight back. Killing of Mamluks and their retainers continued across Egypt for weeks afterward.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali destroy the Mamluks?

By ambush rather than battle, because they could not be destroyed any other way. They were already weak: their field army had been broken by the French in 1798, their tax farms confiscated, their Caucasus recruitment route disrupted, and their two dominant leaders, al-Bardisi and al-Alfi, had died in 1806 and 1807 after years of fighting each other. But they still held cavalry and territory in Upper Egypt and remained a standing alternative that any foreign power could back. With his army about to leave for Arabia, leaving them behind was unacceptable. The Citadel operation removed the leadership in an hour, orders for killings across the country followed, and their remaining property was confiscated into the state.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali become ruler of Egypt?

He was acclaimed, not appointed. In the spring of 1805 Cairo shut down in protest at the exactions of the governor Khurshid Pasha's unpaid troops, with the religious scholars under Umar Makram refusing cooperation and taking the grievance to the streets. On 12 May the assembled notables and scholars deposed Khurshid, proclaimed Muhammad Ali governor, and invested him with the robe of office, an act with no basis whatever in Ottoman constitutional practice. Governors were appointed by the sultan, not acclaimed by provincial crowds. Khurshid barricaded himself in the Citadel and held out until July, when the imperial confirmation arrived and left him no standing. The ceremony created a fact and dared the empire to reverse it.

### Q: Did the people support Muhammad Ali's rise?

Yes, and the support was conditional rather than devoted. Cairo in 1803 and 1804 was occupied by several unpaid forces that billeted themselves, requisitioned goods, and extorted money, on top of formal exactions from each successive governor. The population had also learned during the French occupation that a government could be resisted and that the religious establishment could speak for the city. What Muhammad Ali offered was restraint: he paid for what his troops took and was visibly seen to do so, which in that environment was a remarkable distinction. The 1805 shutdown was not enthusiasm for a particular officer but a city at the end of its tolerance choosing the least bad commander available and using the only instrument it had.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali outmaneuver his rivals?

By never being the strongest party early and always being the indispensable one. He joined whichever coalition could remove the immediate threat, let allies exhaust themselves and each other, and kept his own force intact and paid while every other contingent fragmented over arrears. The Ottoman governor Khusrev Pasha was driven out by an alliance he joined rather than led. A successor was killed within weeks without his intervention. The Mamluk leaders destroyed each other's factions and then died. Meanwhile he built a constituency nobody else was competing for, the city itself. By 1805 there was one cohesive armed force in Cairo with a single competent commander and every alternative had been used and discarded.

### Q: When did Muhammad Ali take control of Egypt?

The formal answer is 1805: acclaimed governor by the Cairo notables on 12 May and confirmed by imperial firman in July. The practical answer is longer at both ends. He became the leading armed figure in Cairo in 1803 when he took over the Albanian command, and his control was not secure until he had survived an attempted transfer to Salonica in 1806, defeated a British expedition in 1807, exiled Umar Makram in 1809, and destroyed the Mamluk households in 1811. Arguably the consolidation only finished around 1818, when the Arabian campaign had spent the Albanian irregulars and the conscript army was forming, leaving nobody in Egypt who had not been placed there by him.

### Q: How did Muhammad Ali get Ottoman recognition?

By presenting Istanbul with an accomplished fact it could not profitably reverse. The imperial firman appointing him wali arrived in July 1805, two months after the Cairo acclamation. The empire had no practical alternative: it could not garrison Egypt, as the failed expedition of 1786 had already shown, it had no candidate with local support, the Mamluk households were divided and weakened, and the Balkans and Russia were more urgent. Confirming cost nothing immediately and bought tribute, a legal relationship, and a quieter province, with the reasonable expectation that he could be moved later. In 1806 Istanbul tried exactly that, ordering him to Salonica; he declined, the notables petitioned, a payment was arranged, and the order lapsed.

### Q: Who was Umar Makram?

The naqib al-ashraf of Cairo, head of the corporation of those claiming descent from the Prophet, a position combining religious standing with real social authority and the ability to mobilize the population. He had opposed the French, been exiled and returned, and by 1804 was the most influential non-military figure in the city. He organized the withdrawal of cooperation that brought Cairo to a standstill in the spring of 1805 and led the notables who deposed Khurshid Pasha and proclaimed Muhammad Ali governor. He was, in the most direct sense available, the man who made him. Four years later he objected to the taxation of endowed land, was isolated by a governor who cultivated his rivals among the scholars, and was exiled.

### Q: What happened at the Citadel in 1811?

The Cairo Citadel stands on a spur above the medieval city, a walled compound with few gates and steep approaches. On 1 March 1811 the Mamluk leaders attended a ceremony there investing Tusun as commander of the Arabian expedition. Leaving afterward, the mounted procession entered the narrow walled way descending toward the lower gate, wide enough for only a few horsemen abreast, with high walls on both sides providing a covered firing position above. Both gates were closed and Albanian troops fired down into the passage. The finest cavalry in the world was made irrelevant by a few hundred metres of masonry. The site is still shown to visitors, and the mosque where Muhammad Ali is buried stands a short distance from it.

### Q: How many were killed in the massacre?

The figure for the Citadel itself is usually given as between four and five hundred, the Mamluk leaders and their immediate retainers who entered the passage, and it comes from contemporary estimates by people in Cairo including the chronicler al-Jabarti rather than from any tally. The total across Egypt in the weeks that followed is much less secure, with numbers in the low thousands appearing once the households' retainers, servants, and dependents are counted. That uncertainty is structural: nobody was recording the deaths of men killed in provincial towns by soldiers acting on an order. Any account giving a precise national figure is reporting an estimate as though it were a count.

### Q: Did anyone escape the killings?

Yes, and mostly by being somewhere else. Several hundred Mamluks who were not in Cairo escaped the general destruction and fled south into Nubia and the Sudan, establishing themselves at Dongola, where they held a position for roughly a decade until the Egyptian conquest of the Sudan from 1820 ended it. Individuals who were absent or late survived and some later entered Egyptian service. The famous story of a single bey leaping his horse from the Citadel wall and surviving the fall is treated as legend by historians: it is physically implausible, attaches to different names in different versions, and has the shape of the survival tale that follows every massacre. It persists because an atrocity with no survivor is hard to narrate.

### Q: Who was Khurshid Pasha?

The Ottoman governor appointed to Egypt in 1804 and deposed by the Cairo notables in May 1805. He was the most capable of the governors sent in this period and he faced the same structural problem as all of them: troops whose pay was in arrears, in a province whose tax system had been dismantled, which forced him to extract revenue from a population already exhausted by three years of French exactions and two of occupation by unpaid contingents. When Cairo shut down against him he refused to go, barricaded himself in the Citadel with loyal troops, bombarded parts of the city, and held out through the early summer until the imperial confirmation of Muhammad Ali arrived in July and left him without standing.

### Q: What role did the Albanian troops play?

They were the material base of everything. Muhammad Ali arrived as second in command of a Kavala contingent, took over the Albanian forces in 1803, and kept them cohesive and paid through a decade in which every other body of troops in Egypt fragmented over arrears. They drove out governors, fought the Mamluks, destroyed the British column at Rosetta in 1807, and carried out the Citadel killings in 1811. They were also, for exactly those reasons, the next problem: an armed constituency that had made a ruler could unmake one. His solution was to spend them, sending them to the long attritional campaign in Arabia from 1811 and to the Sudan, and replacing them with conscripted Egyptian peasants.

### Q: What was the Fraser expedition?

A British force under General Fraser that landed near Alexandria in March 1807 intending to remove Muhammad Ali and restore a Mamluk interest Britain had favoured. It took Alexandria without much difficulty and then pushed inland toward Rosetta, where it was ambushed in the streets and a larger relief force was cut up in the surrounding country. Losses were severe and the heads of the dead were displayed in Cairo. The expedition held Alexandria until September and negotiated a withdrawal. The political effect was enormous: it showed Istanbul that its new governor could defend the province against a European power, showed Egyptians he could protect them as the beys had not in 1798, and removed Britain as a backer of Mamluk restoration.

### Q: Why did Istanbul accept the appointment?

Because refusing would have cost more than accepting. The empire could not garrison Egypt, had no candidate with local support, faced divided and weakened Mamluk households it might once have played against a strong governor, and had more urgent problems in the Balkans and with Russia. Confirming an appointment the province had already made cost nothing immediately and bought tribute, a legal relationship, and a governor who was at least a known quantity rather than another rotation of appointees being killed on arrival. There was also a reasonable expectation that he could be transferred later. The empire tried in 1806, ordering him to Salonica, and the order lapsed after he declined, the notables petitioned, and a payment was arranged.

### Q: What happened to Umar Makram afterwards?

He was exiled in 1809 and never recovered. The issue was the taxation of land held under religious endowment and associated exempt categories, which represented a substantial share of agricultural property and funded the religious institutions and the scholars who ran them. Muhammad Ali needed the revenue and moved to take it; Umar Makram objected in the manner of a man who believed he had made a governor and could unmake one, which was a reasonable inference from 1805 and four years out of date. Rather than confronting the establishment as a body, the governor divided it, cultivating rival scholars and isolating the man, then removed him from his position and sent him to Damietta. He died in 1822. No riot followed.

### Q: Were the Mamluks still a threat by then?

Not militarily. By 1810 they could not have taken Cairo and knew it: the field army was gone since 1798, the tax farms confiscated, the capital that had been carried on the persons of men who died at Embabeh lost, the Caucasus recruitment route disrupted by war and Russian expansion, and both dominant leaders dead within months of each other in 1806 and 1807. What made them intolerable was different. They remained a body with legitimacy, cavalry, and territory in Upper Egypt that any foreign power or disaffected faction could rally around, and Muhammad Ali was about to send his army out of the country to Arabia.

### Q: What was the pretext for the Citadel ceremony?

The investiture of his son Tusun as commander of the expedition to Arabia, which the sultan had requested against the Wahhabi movement that had taken the holy cities and disrupted the pilgrimage. An event of that kind required the attendance of the province's notables, which is precisely why it worked as a trap: refusing a ceremonial summons from the governor was itself a declaration of hostility the beys were not strong enough to make. They had also been treated with conspicuous courtesy for months and had reason to believe an accommodation was being negotiated, so attending looked like the visible acceptance of a settlement rather than a risk. The same logic had trapped elites in the Ottoman world before.

### Q: Why did the scholars turn against the governor?

Because his unpaid troops were plundering the city and his solution was to tax the people they were plundering. Khurshid Pasha inherited contingents in arrears and a province whose revenue system had been dismantled, and his attempt to raise money by fresh exactions on the population and the merchants was the immediate trigger. Behind it lay something the French occupation had taught: the religious establishment had served on the French divan, been held responsible for keeping order, and discovered it was the only institution with standing among the population and could therefore withhold the cooperation that made administration possible. A governor the ulama refused to work with could not govern, and in 1805 they refused.
