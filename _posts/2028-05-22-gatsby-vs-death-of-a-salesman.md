Two men are sold the same promise and neither survives it. That is the pairing at the center of Gatsby vs Death of a Salesman, and the reason it has been assigned in American classrooms for seventy years is that it works at two social altitudes at once. Jay Gatsby buys a mansion across the water from the woman he wants and dies in his own pool without her. Willy Loman rents a small house in Brooklyn, cannot make his territory pay, and drives into a wall so his family can collect the insurance. One man is a bootlegger with a fabricated past and a private beach. The other is a sixty-three year old salesman with a broken car and two disappointing sons. Set their stories together and the promise that destroyed them turns out to be identical.

The comparison earns its place because it isolates a variable. If only Gatsby failed, the failure could be blamed on his methods: the crime, the lies, the absurdity of trying to repurchase a married woman with a light on her dock. Arthur Miller's play removes that defense. Willy Loman commits no crime worth the name, invents no Oxford year, and asks for nothing more exotic than to be well liked and to sell enough to keep the refrigerator. He fails anyway, and he fails through exactly the mechanism that destroys Gatsby: he believes a story about what personal magnetism can purchase in America, and the story is not true.

## What Gatsby vs Death of a Salesman Actually Compares

The pairing is not a comparison of plots, which barely resemble each other, and not a comparison of styles, which are not commensurable across a novel and a stage play. It is a comparison of two diagnoses of the same national belief, delivered from opposite ends of the class structure and separated by a quarter century.

Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby in April 1925, near the top of a boom, and set it in the summer of 1922 among people with yachts and stables and imported shirts. Miller's play opened on Broadway in February 1949, after a Depression and a war, and is set in a house that the mortgage has nearly outlasted, surrounded by apartment buildings that have blocked the sun. The distance between those two positions is the whole point. Gatsby's world is the promise at its most gilded, where a man can invent himself and buy a mansion and still be refused. Willy's world is the promise at its most ordinary, where a man does the job for thirty-four years and is fired by the son of the man who hired him.

What connects them is a single proposition: that in America, personal quality converts into material success, and that failure to succeed therefore indicates a failure of personal quality. Both men accept the proposition completely. Gatsby's version is romantic and specific, aimed at one woman and one restored past. Willy's version is social and diffuse, aimed at being known and liked in New England towns where the buyers now no longer remember him. Neither man ever questions the exchange rate. Both die because the rate does not exist.

### Are Gatsby and Willy Loman the same kind of dreamer?

They are not. Gatsby pursues a single object with monomaniacal precision and builds an entire criminal fortune as its instrument. Willy pursues a diffuse condition, wanting to be liked and respected, and has no instrument at all beyond his own manner. One dream is engineered; the other is inherited and never examined.

The pairing also compares two ways of ending a life and two ways of burying a man. Both works close on a funeral that fails to draw a crowd, and in both the failure of attendance is the verdict on the belief. That structural echo is the most quoted point of contact between the texts, and it is genuine, but it is also where most comparative essays stop. The more useful material lies in what the two funerals mean at different social levels, and that is where this article spends most of its attention. The wider field of books that test the promise is surveyed in [the American Dream novel tradition and where Gatsby sits in it](/2028/07/31/gatsby-american-dream-novels/); the present comparison stays on the two texts and what each shows about the other.
## The Genuine Points of Contact

Four things connect the two works substantively, and each is worth stating precisely so that an essay built on them does not slide into vagueness.

The first is the structure of the belief. Both men hold that success in America is a referendum on personal worth. Gatsby's version has an object: acquire enough, become impressive enough, and Daisy will unmake her marriage and the last five years will be cancelled. Willy's version has a method: be well liked, be known by name, and the orders will follow and the sons will inherit the same magic. The two versions differ in specificity and share their engine, which is the conviction that the market rewards charm. Neither man ever considers the possibility that the market is indifferent to charm and responsive to something else entirely.

The second is the fabricated self. Gatsby's construction is total and famous: the invented name, the fictional Oxford year, the practiced phrase, the curated photographs, the accent maintained under pressure. Willy's construction is smaller and more pathetic and therefore harder to watch, consisting of inflated sales figures reported to his wife, imagined intimacies with buyers, and a brother's mining fortune retold until it becomes proof of what boldness can do. Both men lie in the same direction, upward, and both lie primarily to sustain a version of themselves that the evidence no longer supports. The lies are not character flaws in either work. They are load-bearing.

The third is the corrupted inheritance passed to the next generation. Gatsby has no children, but he has a protege of sorts in his own younger self, and the schedule Nick's father finds in the boy's book after the death is the document of a self-improvement program. Willy has two sons and transmits the belief directly, and Miller's play is largely about the damage of that transmission. Biff was taught that being liked substitutes for being competent, learned the lesson thoroughly, and spends the play trying to unlearn it. Fitzgerald's novel has no equivalent because Gatsby's dream dies with him. Miller's has an equivalent because Willy's dream is a family business.

The fourth is the funeral. Gatsby's is attended by Nick, the father, a servant or two, the postman, and one former guest with owlish glasses who observes that the crowd that ate his food will not come to bury him. Willy's is attended by his wife, his sons, and his neighbor, and the play's Requiem scene turns on the absence of the buyers Willy spent thirty-four years believing were his friends. Both scenes deliver the same judgment, and neither work editorializes. The empty grass does the work.

## The Decisive Differences

The differences are where the comparison earns its keep, because similarity alone produces a list and a list produces a weak essay.

The first difference is class, and it is the largest. Gatsby's tragedy requires that he succeed materially and be refused socially. He has the money. He gets the house, the car, the shirts, the parties, the woman for an afternoon. What he cannot get is admission, and the novel is precise that the barrier is not wealth but origin: the people whose acceptance he wants have had money long enough to consider having gotten it recently a vulgarity. Willy's tragedy requires the opposite. He never succeeds materially at all. His refrigerator breaks, his insurance lapses, his commission is cut, and the house is paid off in the same week he dies. Gatsby is destroyed at the top of the ladder by a door. Willy is destroyed at the bottom by the ladder itself.

The second difference is agency. Gatsby designs his life with something close to engineering discipline. Every purchase is instrumental, and the man who "had come a long way to this blue lawn" chose that lawn for its sight line. Willy designs nothing. His life happened to him: he took a job, believed what he was told about it, raised sons on the same belief, and aged out of a territory he thought was his. Miller wants the audience to feel that Willy could not have done otherwise, which is a different tragic proposition from Fitzgerald's, since Gatsby manifestly could have done otherwise at several points and chose not to.

The third difference is knowledge. Gatsby dies without recognition. Whether he understood at the end that the dream had failed is one of the novel's permanent open questions, and Nick can only speculate about what he might have seen in a sky he found frightening. Willy achieves something closer to a recognition scene, though Miller complicates it: in the confrontation with Biff he glimpses that his son loves him and is astonished by it, and the astonishment is what sends him to the car. He dies still inside the belief but having been touched by something outside it. That difference in access to knowledge is the sharpest formal distinction between the two works and the best material for a strong essay.

The fourth difference is where the blame lands. Fitzgerald distributes it carefully: onto the Buchanans, onto the era, onto the "foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams", and never squarely onto Gatsby himself. Miller distributes it onto a system, and did so explicitly in his own critical writing, arguing in 1949 that the common man is as apt a subject for tragedy as kings. The play's targets include the employer, the commission structure, and the mythology of personal salesmanship. Gatsby's targets are people; Salesman's targets are arrangements. That distinction shapes everything about how the two works feel and how their politics read.
## The Dream at Two Altitudes

Naming the central claim precisely makes the comparison usable, and the claim of this article is that Gatsby and Death of a Salesman indict the same promise at two altitudes, and that reading them together produces a diagnosis neither text delivers alone.

At the gilded altitude, the promise fails through exclusion. The novel's argument is that America offers mobility and withholds arrival. A man can accumulate an enormous fortune, buy a house on the correct shore, and still be treated as an amusement by people who inherited theirs. Tom Buchanan's contempt is not about money, since Gatsby has more of it in ready cash than Tom probably keeps liquid. It is about lineage, and the novel is unusually clear that lineage is unpurchasable. The dream tells its believer that the door is open and the door turns out to be painted on the wall.

At the ordinary altitude, the promise fails through obsolescence. Miller's argument is that America offers dignity through work and then discards the worker. Willy has done what the promise required. He traveled, he sold, he made his numbers in his best years, he raised sons, he paid a mortgage for a quarter century. The system's response is to cut him to commission and then to fire him, and the specific cruelty is that the man who fires him is the son of the man who hired him, which converts a business decision into a generational betrayal. The dream tells its believer that faithful service compounds into security, and the compounding never happens.

Put the two failures together and you get the diagnosis. The American promise fails from both directions at once: it will not admit the striver who succeeds, and it will not keep the servant who complies. There is no position inside the arrangement that is safe, which means the failure is not a matter of choosing the wrong strategy. Gatsby chose the aggressive strategy and Willy chose the compliant one, and both are dead by the last page. The full treatment of what the novel claims about that promise appears in [the American Dream in The Great Gatsby](/2024/04/22/american-dream-great-gatsby/); the play supplies the control at the other end of the income scale.

### What does the everyman tragedy add to Gatsby?

It removes the excuse that Gatsby's downfall was caused by his crimes and his extravagance. Willy Loman breaks no laws, throws no parties, and pursues nothing exotic, and the same belief destroys him. The play therefore converts a story that could be read as personal folly into evidence of a systemic pattern.

There is a second thing the everyman version adds, less often noticed. It supplies duration. Gatsby's ruin is compressed into a summer, and the reader never sees the decades of grinding effort that preceded it, since the novel handles Gatsby's years with Dan Cody and his war and his climb in a few compressed pages. Miller's play is entirely about the decades. Willy is old, his body hurts, he cannot drive to Boston any more, and the audience watches a man who has already spent everything. Reading them together restores the missing time to Gatsby's story, because Willy shows you what those omitted years of climbing feel like from the inside.

## Two Funerals and What They Mean

The funerals deserve a section of their own, since they are the point of contact everyone notices and the point most often handled shallowly.

Gatsby's funeral is a study in the transactional nature of the crowd he assembled. The parties were mobbed. Nobody comes. Wolfsheim declines by letter, offering the principle that a man should show friendship while the friend is alive. The one guest who appears is the owlish man from the library, who had earlier been impressed that the books were real, and his presence is the novel's small joke: the only party guest who bothered to check whether Gatsby's display was genuine is the only one who returns. The funeral proves that the display bought attendance and not attachment, which is the exact failure of the belief that success purchases connection.

Willy's funeral operates differently because Willy never had a crowd to lose. The Requiem's force comes from the fact that Willy had predicted his own funeral would be enormous, populated by buyers from all over New England who would come to honor a man they had known for decades. The prediction was central to how he understood his own life. When the graveside contains only his family and one neighbor, the audience is not learning that his friends were false. It is learning that the friends never existed, that thirty-four years of believing himself known produced no witnesses at all.

The distinction matters for essay writing. Gatsby's empty funeral exposes a crowd; Willy's exposes a delusion. In the novel, real people who benefited from Gatsby chose not to come. In the play, the people Willy expected were never his to expect. The first is a judgment on a society; the second is a judgment on a belief. An essay that treats the two funerals as making the same point will be technically defensible and analytically thin. An essay that separates them has an argument.

Both works also give the last word to someone other than the dead man. Fitzgerald gives it to Nick, whose closing meditation moves outward from one man to a continent and ends with the image of boats beating against a current that carries them backward. Miller gives it to Linda and to Charley, and Charley's defense of Willy insists that a salesman lives on nothing but his own confidence and that no blame attaches to a man who runs out of it. Both endings refuse to condemn the dreamer while condemning the dream, and the parallel there is exact.
## The Comparison Table: Two Dreamers, One Promise

The table below is the findable artifact for this pairing. Each row is built so that a student can lift it and construct a comparative paragraph without further research.

| Dimension | Jay Gatsby (The Great Gatsby, 1925) | Willy Loman (Death of a Salesman, 1949) |
|---|---|---|
| Social position | New money, enormous and criminally acquired | Salaried salesman cut to commission, chronically short |
| The dream's object | One woman and one cancelled past, precisely specified | Being well liked and known, diffuse and never examined |
| Method | Engineered, every purchase instrumental | None, the manner itself was supposed to be the method |
| Point of failure | Exclusion, admission withheld after success is achieved | Obsolescence, discarded after decades of compliance |
| Relationship to lying | Total self-invention, sustained under pressure | Small inflations of sales figures and imagined friendships |
| Inheritance | No children, the dream dies with him | Two sons raised inside the belief, damage transmitted |
| Access to recognition | None certain, the novel leaves it open | Partial, he glimpses his son's love and is undone by it |
| Manner of death | Shot in his pool for another's act | Deliberate crash, staged to convert himself into insurance |

| Where blame falls | On people, chiefly the Buchanans and their carelessness | On arrangements, the commission structure and the myth |

Two rows carry most of the analytical weight. The point of failure row establishes that the promise breaks in opposite directions at different altitudes, and the where blame falls row establishes that the two writers had genuinely different politics about the same phenomenon. An essay that uses those two rows and ignores the rest will still have an argument.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The two works are not in competition, and pretending otherwise produces a false question. What a verdict can settle is which one gives the more complete account of the promise it attacks, and the answer is Death of a Salesman, for one reason: it includes the system.

Fitzgerald's novel identifies a barrier and personifies it. The obstacle to Gatsby is Tom Buchanan, and behind Tom a class, and behind the class an inherited arrangement that the novel gestures at without anatomizing. This is a legitimate choice and it produces enormous concentration, but it also means the novel's indictment terminates in people. If Tom were less brutal and Daisy braver, a reader can almost imagine a different outcome, and generations of readers have imagined exactly that. The novel's structure permits the fantasy that the failure was contingent.

Miller closes that door. There is no villain in Death of a Salesman. Howard, who fires Willy, is not cruel; he is distracted and mildly embarrassed and has a business to run. Charley is generous and offers Willy a job repeatedly. Linda is devoted. Biff and Happy are damaged rather than malicious. Every person in the play behaves within the normal range, and Willy is destroyed anyway, which forces the audience to locate the cause in the arrangement rather than in anyone's character. That is the more complete indictment, and it is why the play remains politically legible in a way the novel sometimes does not.

The deciding factor, named for essay use, is the locus of causation. Gatsby locates the failure in a class; Salesman locates it in a structure. A reader who wants to understand why individual Americans are destroyed by the promise of success should read Fitzgerald. A reader who wants to understand why the destruction keeps happening regardless of who is involved should read Miller.

None of which makes Gatsby the lesser work, and the reason is worth stating because it prevents the verdict from being misused. Fitzgerald is doing something Miller does not attempt: he is preserving the beauty of the belief while destroying it. Willy's dream is never made attractive; the audience pities it from the first scene. Gatsby's dream is made genuinely magnificent, credited with "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness", and the novel's difficulty is that the reader wants it to work. That double motion, admiring and demolishing at once, is a harder artistic problem than Miller's, and it is why the novel generates a century of argument while the play generates agreement.

### Which work is more critical of American capitalism?

Death of a Salesman, unambiguously. Miller places the cause in the commission structure, the disposability of aging workers, and the myth of personal salesmanship, and gives the play no villain so the audience must blame the system. Fitzgerald's indictment terminates in a class of people rather than in an economic arrangement.
## Is Willy Loman a Tragic Hero, and Is Gatsby?

The tragic-hero question attaches to both figures and is worth handling directly, because it is the single most common essay prompt built on this pairing.

The classical objection is the same in both cases: tragedy in the Aristotelian tradition concerns figures of consequence whose fall carries public weight, and neither a Brooklyn salesman nor a Long Island bootlegger has a kingdom to lose. Miller answered the objection himself in 1949, arguing in print that the common man is as apt a subject for tragedy as kings, on the grounds that the tragic feeling comes from watching a person lay down his life to secure his sense of personal dignity. That is a real critical position, publicly stated by the playwright, and it can be cited without hedging.

Applied to Willy, the argument holds. He has a fixed and total conception of what he is owed, he refuses every offer that would require abandoning it, including Charley's repeated job offer, and he finally converts himself into a payment to preserve his standing in his son's eyes. The refusal to accept the job is the hinge. A man who took the job would live and would be, in his own accounting, nothing. Willy chooses the accounting.

Applied to Gatsby, the argument holds differently and less cleanly. Gatsby has the stature that Willy lacks, in the sense that the novel takes enormous pains to make him magnificent, and Nick's language keeps reaching for the theological, describing the moment of commitment as one after which "the incarnation was complete". But Gatsby's fall is not caused by a choice made at a crisis. He is shot for something Daisy did, by a man who has been misinformed, while floating in a pool waiting for a phone call that was never coming. The death is arbitrary in a way that classical tragedy avoids. What makes it feel tragic is not the mechanism but the waste, and the case is argued in full in [the reading of Gatsby as a tragic hero](/2024/02/12/gatsby-tragic-hero/).

The comparison sharpens both cases. Willy's death is chosen and undignified; Gatsby's is unchosen and made beautiful by the prose surrounding it. If your definition of tragedy requires a decisive choice, Willy qualifies and Gatsby struggles. If it requires stature and the sense of a large thing destroyed, Gatsby qualifies and Willy struggles. That neither figure satisfies both criteria at once is the most interesting result the pairing produces on this question, and an essay that says so has a genuine argument rather than a summary.

## Fathers, Sons, and Witnesses

Both works route the dreamer's meaning through a younger man, and the two arrangements are instructively different.

Miller's arrangement is biological and direct. Biff Loman is Willy's son, was raised on his father's belief, and spends the play trying to tell him the truth about both of them. The climactic confrontation is not about business or money but about whether either man is willing to say what he is. Biff's insistence that they are ordinary people, and Willy's inability to accept it, is the play's actual crisis, and Willy's death follows from his misreading of the confrontation as proof of his son's love and therefore of the belief's validity. The transmission of the dream from father to son, and the son's attempt to refuse the inheritance, is the play's deepest subject.

Fitzgerald's arrangement is elective and oblique. Nick Carraway is not Gatsby's son and has no obligation to him. He is a neighbor, a distant cousin of Daisy's, and a Midwesterner in bond sales who spends one summer close to a stranger and then spends the rest of his life explaining him. The relationship has a filial quality without the family, and the novel underlines it by giving Gatsby an actual father who arrives too late, understands his son only through a schedule in a boyhood book, and is proud in the wrong register. Henry Gatz sees the mansion and is impressed. Nick sees the mansion and understands what it was for.

The comparison exposes what each choice buys. Miller gets a dramatic engine: father and son in a room, each holding the other's version of the past. Fitzgerald gets an interpretive one: a witness with no stake, whose account can therefore be trusted about facts and distrusted about judgment. The two arrangements are not interchangeable. A play needs conflict in the room; a novel of this kind needs a mind reconstructing a man from insufficient evidence.

Both works also place a competent alternative alongside the dreamer's family. Miller gives Willy the neighbor Charley and his son Bernard, who studied, became a lawyer, and argues cases before the Supreme Court, and Charley's quiet success is the play's standing rebuke to the theory that being well liked is what matters. Fitzgerald gives the reader Nick himself, who works, saves, declines the parties, and goes home. Neither work makes the sensible alternative appealing, which is a shared honesty worth noting: Charley is dull and Nick is cold, and the sensible life is presented as survival rather than as a prize.
## The Past as a Weapon in Both Works

Memory operates as an active force in both texts, and comparing the mechanisms is one of the sharpest available angles for a formal essay.

Gatsby's relationship to the past is a project. He does not remember 1917 so much as intend to reinstate it, and his notorious insistence that of course the past can be repeated is the novel's clearest statement of the belief. The novel treats this as a category error rather than as sentimentality. Nick observes that Gatsby's vision had been assembled over five years and could not survive contact with an actual afternoon, and the accounting metaphor for what happens when the fantasy meets the woman, that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one", registers the loss in the moment of achievement. Gatsby's past is a blueprint he is trying to build from.

Willy's relationship to the past is an intrusion. Miller stages memory directly, letting scenes from fifteen years earlier occupy the stage alongside the present, and the audience watches Willy slip between them mid-conversation. The past is not a project for Willy; it is a place he keeps falling into, and the falling accelerates as the play proceeds until the boundary collapses entirely. The dramaturgy was innovative in 1949 and remains the play's most striking formal feature, since it converts a psychological condition into a staging convention.

The contrast produces a usable claim. Gatsby is trying to return to a past he has improved in his imagination; Willy is being dragged into a past he cannot improve and cannot leave. One relationship is aspirational, the other pathological. Both are fatal, and the difference explains why the novel feels like a pursuit and the play feels like a collapse.

There is a shared insight underneath the difference. Both works hold that the American promise generates an unusual relationship to time, because a belief in unlimited self-transformation makes the past feel provisional. If a person can become anything, then what he has been need not bind him, and both texts show what happens when that logic is taken seriously by someone who has run out of room. Gatsby takes it seriously and tries to reverse five years. Willy takes it seriously and keeps returning to the summer when everything still seemed possible. Neither man can accept that the past is fixed, and the refusal is the same refusal.

## Novel and Play: What the Form Changes

Comparing across forms is where student essays most often go wrong, so it is worth being explicit about what the difference in medium actually does.

A novel can withhold. Fitzgerald's central technique depends on the reader having no access to Gatsby's interior, and that unavailability is enforced by the first-person narration. Nick cannot enter Gatsby's head, so neither can the reader, and the man remains partly unknown through the last page. The technique is examined in detail in [the narrative point of view in The Great Gatsby](/2026/01/12/gatsby-point-of-view/), and the essential point is that the withholding is not incidental. It is what makes the figure irreducible.

A play cannot withhold in the same way, because a character on stage is physically present for every minute he occupies. The audience sees Willy's body, hears his voice crack, watches him talk to people who are not there. Miller compensates by making the interior visible rather than hidden, staging the memories, so the play's method is the exact inverse of the novel's. Where Fitzgerald conceals to create mystery, Miller exposes to create pity. Neither is superior; they are the natural moves for their media.

This has a consequence for how the two dreamers land. Gatsby is magnificent partly because we cannot see him clearly, and a reader supplies the grandeur in the gaps. Willy is pitiable partly because we can see everything, including the tics and the repetitions and the moments when he contradicts himself within a page. A production can make Willy dignified, and great performances have, but the text does not permit mystery. The audience knows more about Willy Loman's inner life in two hours than a reader knows about Jay Gatsby after five rereadings.

The formal difference also governs the endings. A novel can end in meditation because prose can leave the scene entirely and speak in a narrator's voice about continents and time. A play must end with people on a stage. Miller's solution, the Requiem, brings the survivors to the grave and lets them speak, which is the closest a stage can come to Nick's closing paragraphs. Reading the two endings side by side is the best possible demonstration of what each medium can and cannot do, and it is a stronger essay topic than any thematic comparison because it is concrete and demonstrable from the texts.
## The Self-Made Ideal and Its Two Casualties

Both works are arguments about self-making, and separating what each says about it clarifies a great deal.

The self-made ideal holds that a person's origins do not determine his destination, and that effort plus character produces standing. Gatsby is the ideal's most complete fictional embodiment: he changed his name at seventeen, attached himself to a mentor, taught himself a manner, made a fortune, and bought a position. Every step succeeded. The ideal delivered on everything it promised except the last thing, which was acceptance, and the novel's cruelty is that the last thing was the only thing he wanted. The series treats this at length in [the self-made man myth in The Great Gatsby](/2025/01/13/self-made-man-myth-gatsby/), and the essential finding is that the myth is not shown to be false in its mechanics. It is shown to be false in its purpose.

Willy is the ideal's other casualty, and the mode of failure is different. Willy never made himself at all. He absorbed a version of the ideal in which the self was already sufficient and only needed to be liked, which converts self-making from a program into a temperament. His brother Ben is the play's emblem of the real thing, a man who walked into a jungle and walked out rich, and Ben's recurring appearances function as a reproach: that was available, and Willy declined it, and now the man who declined it is being fired.

The pairing therefore produces a distinction that neither text makes alone. The self-made ideal has an active form and a passive form. The active form works mechanically and fails socially, which is Gatsby. The passive form does not work at all, which is Willy. Both forms are sold in the same national vocabulary, and the vocabulary does not distinguish between them, which is exactly why so many believers cannot tell which version they have bought.

Notice what this implies about advice. If the ideal's active form succeeds mechanically, then telling Willy to be more like Gatsby would have made him richer and would not have made him safe. And telling Gatsby to be more like Willy would have made him ordinary and equally excluded. There is no adjustment available inside the belief that produces a good outcome, which is the pairing's harshest finding and the one most worth building an essay around.

## Five Misreadings of the Pairing, Corrected

The first misreading is that the two men are essentially the same character at different income levels. They are not. Gatsby is monomaniacal, disciplined, and secretive, with a single object and a plan for reaching it. Willy is diffuse, undisciplined, and confessional, with no object he could state precisely and no plan at all. Treating them as one figure flattens the comparison into a similarity list and loses the class argument entirely.

The second misreading is that both works condemn the American Dream. Neither does, quite. Fitzgerald's novel is scrupulous about crediting the dream's beauty while showing its cost, and the final page insists on the striving even while describing it as futile. Miller's play condemns a particular commercial version of the dream and explicitly leaves other versions standing, most obviously in Biff's discovery that he is happy working outdoors with his hands. An essay claiming blanket condemnation will be contradicted by both texts.

The third misreading is that the parallel funerals prove the same point. They do not. Gatsby's empty funeral exposes the transactional nature of a real crowd that chose not to come. Willy's exposes a network that never existed outside his own account of it. One is a judgment on people, the other on a delusion, and the difference is the most easily scored distinction available in a comparative essay.

The fourth misreading is that Willy is a failure and Gatsby a success. In the terms both works use, Gatsby fails more completely, because he achieved everything the belief promised and discovered it purchased nothing. Willy at least dies believing his death will provide, and the play refuses to tell us whether the insurance paid. Gatsby dies with the answer already delivered: he got the money and the house and the afternoon with Daisy, and none of it held.

The fifth misreading is that the comparison is only about the American Dream. It is also about narration, form, memory, inheritance, and the difference between prose that conceals and theater that exposes. Essays that stay on the dream theme write themselves into the same paragraph everyone else writes. Essays that pick the memory mechanism or the witness structure will say something the grader has not read forty times.

## How to Write a Comparative Essay on Gatsby and Death of a Salesman

Begin by rejecting the similarity list. The single most common failure is an essay whose thesis is that both works show the failure of the American Dream, which is true, uncontested, and worth no credit. A comparative thesis needs to claim something the comparison produces, not something both texts announce.

Pick the altitude argument if you want the safest strong option. The thesis runs: the two works indict the same promise at opposite ends of the class structure, and the fact that opposite strategies produce identical outcomes shows the failure is structural rather than personal. This thesis is defensible from both texts, generates three or four body paragraphs naturally, and pre-empts its own objection.

Pick the causation argument if you want the more ambitious option. The thesis runs: Fitzgerald locates the cause of failure in a class of people while Miller locates it in an arrangement, and the difference explains why the novel invites readers to imagine a happier outcome while the play forecloses that fantasy by giving itself no villain. This is a harder argument and worth more when it lands.

Structure each body paragraph as claim, primary evidence from Gatsby, primary evidence from the play, and the inference the contrast licenses. Keep Gatsby first if the assignment is a Gatsby course, and keep the play first if it is a drama course. The one thing to avoid is alternating whole paragraphs between texts, which produces two summaries running in parallel and no comparison at all.

Handle evidence with the asymmetry the forms require. From the novel, quote briefly and precisely. From the play, cite scenes and stage situations rather than lines, since paraphrasing a dramatic moment accurately is stronger than a half-remembered line and avoids the risk of misquotation. Name the Requiem, the confrontation with Biff, the scene in Howard's office, and the recurring appearances of Ben. Those four situations will support almost any thesis this pairing generates.

Close on the pairing's finding rather than on a restatement. The finding available here is unusually clean: there is no position inside the promise that survives it. Gatsby played aggressively and Willy played by the rules, and the outcome was identical, which means the game rather than the strategy is the problem.

## What Each Work Illuminates in the Other

A pairing is worth keeping only if it changes how each text reads. This one changes both.

Reading the play makes Gatsby's omitted years visible. The novel handles the climb in compressed fragments: a boy with a schedule in the back of a book, a stretch on Dan Cody's yacht, a war, an unexplained arrangement with Wolfsheim, and then a man with a mansion. What those years cost is never dramatized. Miller dramatizes exactly those years, spread across a career, and once you have watched Willy age inside them you cannot read Gatsby's compressed backstory as lightly. The novel's ellipses acquire weight.

Reading the play also makes Gatsby's magnificence look like a choice rather than a fact. Willy is never made beautiful. Miller declines to give the audience anything to admire in the belief itself, and the play is more efficient as a critique because of it. Fitzgerald could have written the Willy version of Gatsby, a shabby striver whose delusion is merely sad, and he chose instead to make the delusion "the colossal vitality of his illusion" and to have his narrator half endorse it. That choice is what makes the novel hard and permanent, and it becomes visible only when set against a writer who declined it.

Reading the novel, in the other direction, makes Miller's refusal of beauty look like a cost as well as a strength. The play is unanswerable and it is also, in a specific sense, finished. Audiences do not argue about whether Willy Loman was right; they agree that he was wrong and pity him. Gatsby generates a century of disagreement precisely because Fitzgerald contaminated the critique with admiration. The novel therefore illuminates what Miller gave up to gain his clarity, which is the reader's implication in the belief.

The novel also exposes what the play does not attempt: the aesthetics of aspiration. Fitzgerald's prose makes wanting feel like the highest human activity, and the closing movement extends one man's wanting to a whole continent's, moving from the island as the first sailors saw it to the observation that the striving continues even as the object recedes, in the language of "the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us". Miller has no equivalent register and does not want one. Comparing the two endings shows that the same critique can be delivered as elegy or as verdict, and that the choice determines the work's afterlife more than its argument does.

## Where the Two Works Sit in the Tradition

Both texts belong to a lineage of American writing that tests the promise of success against a particular life, and knowing the lineage helps place the comparison.

The novel arrives near the end of a long nineteenth-century argument about mobility, and it inherits the machinery of the rise-and-fall narrative while inverting its moral. The older form rewards the striver. Fitzgerald's version lets the striver succeed at every measurable step and then withholds the reward, which is why the novel felt modern in 1925 and still does. Its immediate contemporaries were making similar moves, and the placement of Gatsby among those books is handled across this series rather than repeated here.

The play arrives after the Depression has already discredited the confident version of the promise, which means Miller is writing into a culture that half agrees with him before the curtain rises. That is a different rhetorical situation and it shapes the work. Miller does not have to persuade the audience that the promise can fail; he has to persuade them that its failure is tragic rather than merely sad, and the 1949 essay on tragedy and the common man exists precisely to make that argument outside the play.

The two works therefore stand at opposite ends of an argument's life. Fitzgerald writes while the belief is still ascendant and produces a book whose critique is smuggled inside a romance. Miller writes after the belief has been publicly damaged and produces a work whose critique is explicit and whose difficulty is emotional rather than intellectual. That difference in rhetorical situation explains more about the two works' methods than any comparison of their authors' temperaments, and it is a reliable way to make a comparative essay sound informed rather than impressionistic.

## Closing Verdict

Jay Gatsby and Willy Loman are killed by the same sentence, and the sentence is that a person's worth in America is measured by what he can sell, including himself. Gatsby believed the aggressive version and executed it flawlessly, acquiring everything the belief promised and discovering that the acceptance it was supposed to buy was never on the market. Willy believed the compliant version and executed that flawlessly too, serving one company for thirty-four years and discovering that service purchases nothing that survives a man's usefulness. Two opposite strategies, one outcome, two nearly empty funerals.

The comparison's finding is that no strategy inside the promise works. That is a harder claim than either text makes alone, and it is available only from the pairing, because a single failure always looks like a personal mistake and two opposite failures look like a rule. Fitzgerald supplies the beautiful version of the mistake and Miller supplies the ordinary one, and the fact that beauty and ordinariness end in the same grass is the whole argument.

What separates them in the end is what each writer wanted the audience to carry out of the room. Miller wanted anger at a system and produced a work that generates agreement. Fitzgerald wanted something more difficult and more permanent: a reader who understands that the dream is false and still cannot stop admiring the man who believed it. The novel ends with boats beating against a current and being carried backward, which is defeat and effort held in the same image and ranked in neither direction. That refusal to rank is why the argument about Gatsby has not ended, and it is the one thing Miller's clearer, angrier, more complete indictment does not have.
## Two Wives and the Cost of Belief

The women in these works absorb the cost of the men's belief, and comparing their positions produces one of the pairing's least worked and most productive angles.

Linda Loman knows. That is the play's quiet devastation. She has found the rubber tubing Willy has hidden behind the heater, she understands the sales figures do not match the reports, and she says nothing, because saying it would take from her husband the only thing holding him upright. Her defense of Willy against her sons, insisting that attention must be paid to such a person, is the play's moral center and its most quoted speech. Linda's tragedy is that she chose sustaining the delusion over puncturing it, and Miller makes clear the choice was made from love rather than from cowardice.

Daisy Buchanan does not know, and the novel is careful about the asymmetry. She has no access to how Gatsby made his money, no idea of the scale of the construction aimed at her, and no warning that a stranger has organized five years around a light on her dock. When the reunion happens she is moved, genuinely, and the shirts scene registers something real. What she cannot do is sustain the weight of what she has been made to mean, and the novel says so directly, observing that "No amount of fire or freshness can challenge what a man can store up in his ghostly heart". She retreats into her marriage because the alternative is to become a fixed point in someone else's cosmology.

Set the two together and a distinction appears. Linda is a participant in the belief who protects it. Daisy is a conscript into a belief she never enlisted in and cannot carry. Miller's woman chooses complicity; Fitzgerald's is drafted. Both works therefore show that the dreamer's conviction costs someone else, and the cost is paid by a woman in each case, but the mechanisms differ enough that an essay can build a real argument on the gap rather than a similarity list.

There is a further point about voice. Linda speaks at length, argues, and is given the Requiem's most important lines. Daisy speaks in fragments filtered through a narrator who is more interested in the sound of her than in the content, and the novel gives her exactly one moment of undisguised self-knowledge, when she reports hoping her daughter would grow up "a beautiful little fool" because that is the safest available condition. One woman is heard; the other is described. That difference belongs to the forms as much as to the writers, since a stage requires speech and a first-person novel can subordinate anyone to its narrator's ear.

## A Practical Reading Order and Time Budget

If you are working to a deadline, order and scoping decisions matter more than most study advice admits.

Read Gatsby first if the assignment sits in an American literature course, which is the usual case. The novel is under fifty thousand words and takes most readers four to six hours. Arriving at the play afterward means you already hold the gilded version of the promise and can register Miller's ordinary version as a deliberate lowering of altitude.

Read or watch the play second, and prefer watching if you can, because Death of a Salesman is written for staging and its memory sequences read as confusing on the page while landing immediately in performance. The running time is roughly two and a half hours, which is less than the novel demands, and the scenes an essay needs are all vivid in production: Howard's office, the restaurant, the confrontation with Biff, and the Requiem.

Do not attempt to read them in alternating sections. The novel's images accumulate force only when the recurrences are close enough together to register, and the play's memory intrusions depend on continuous immersion in Willy's deteriorating attention. Interleaving destroys both effects.

Budget your evidence gathering asymmetrically. From the novel you will want exact wording, since graders reward precise quotation and the prose rewards close attention. From the play you will want scene situations, since a correctly described dramatic moment is worth more than a half-remembered line and carries no risk of misquotation. Roughly two hours of note-taking on the novel and one on the play produces enough material for a standard comparative essay, and the theme of hope curdling into disillusion, which the series treats at length across [hope and disillusionment in The Great Gatsby](/2024/08/05/hope-disillusionment-gatsby/), gives you a ready spine if the altitude argument feels too ambitious for the word limit.
## What the Pairing Settles

Three things become settled by this comparison that neither text settles alone, and naming them gives an essay its final paragraph.

The first is that the failure is not a matter of strategy. Gatsby played the aggressive game with total discipline and Willy played the compliant one with total fidelity, and both are buried in front of almost nobody. If either strategy worked, the other man's failure could be attributed to having chosen wrong. Because neither works, the choice is revealed as irrelevant, and the belief itself moves into the position of cause. This is the finding no single work can produce, since one failure always looks like a mistake and two opposite failures look like a rule.

The second is that the promise is not falsified by its mechanics but by its purpose. Gatsby's self-invention succeeded at every measurable step. He changed his name, acquired the fortune, built the house, and got the afternoon. Nothing in the program broke. What broke was the assumption about what the program was for, since the acceptance it was designed to purchase turned out not to be for sale at any price. Willy's version failed earlier and more visibly, but the structure of the disappointment is identical: the thing the effort was supposed to buy was never available.

The third is that the two writers had genuinely different politics about the same phenomenon and that the difference determined each work's afterlife. Miller wrote an indictment of a system and produced a play that generates agreement, which is why it functions so cleanly as a teaching text and why audiences leave angry rather than puzzled. Fitzgerald wrote an elegy contaminated by admiration and produced a novel that generates argument, which is why the question of whether Gatsby was admirable has not closed in a hundred years. Clarity finishes a work. Contamination keeps it running.

### Which text should I lead with in an essay?

Lead with whichever text the course belongs to, and keep the order stable throughout. In an American literature course lead with the novel and use the play as the instrument that exposes the class argument. In a drama course reverse it. The one fatal move is switching the lead halfway through.

## Closing Verdict

Two men are killed by one sentence: that a person's worth in America is measured by what he can sell, including himself. Gatsby believed the aggressive version, executed it flawlessly, and discovered that the door he had bought his way toward was painted on the wall. Willy believed the compliant version, executed that flawlessly too, and discovered that thirty-four years of faithful service compounds into nothing that survives a man's usefulness. Two opposite methods, one outcome, two nearly empty graves.

Miller gives the more complete indictment because he includes the system and refuses the audience a villain to blame instead. Fitzgerald gives the more difficult performance because he makes the belief beautiful while destroying it, crediting his protagonist with an extraordinary gift for hope and then relocating the blame onto the foul dust in the wake of the dream. Willy's delusion is pitiable from the first scene; nobody in the theater wants it to work. Gatsby's is magnificent, and the reader's problem is that some part of him is still rooting for the man to get the girl and cancel the five years.

That difference is what the pairing finally teaches. A critique can be delivered as verdict or as elegy. The verdict is more useful, more persuasive, and more easily agreed with, and it ends when the audience agrees. The elegy is harder to write, admits the very thing it condemns, and never finishes, because a reader who has been made to admire what he has been shown to be false has been given a permanent problem rather than a conclusion. Miller wanted the audience to see what the system does. Fitzgerald wanted the reader to feel what it costs to stop believing, and to be unable to stop entirely. Both got what they wanted, and only one of them is still being argued about.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to Death of a Salesman?

Both works indict the same American belief from opposite ends of the class structure. Fitzgerald's 1925 novel places a self-made millionaire on Long Island who acquires everything the promise offers and is refused admission by the class he wanted to join. Miller's 1949 play places an aging salesman in Brooklyn who complies with every requirement of the promise for thirty-four years and is discarded when his usefulness ends. Both men die by their belief, both funerals are nearly empty, and both works give the final word to a survivor who refuses to condemn the dreamer. The comparison matters because opposite strategies produce identical outcomes, which converts what could look like personal folly into evidence of a structural pattern.

### Q: How are Gatsby and Willy Loman alike as dreamers?

They share the engine rather than the shape of the dream. Both hold that in America personal quality converts into material success, and that failure to succeed therefore indicates a failure of personal worth. Both sustain fabricated versions of themselves: Gatsby through an invented name, a fictional Oxford year, and a maintained manner, and Willy through inflated sales figures, imagined intimacies with buyers, and a brother's mining fortune retold as proof of what boldness achieves. Both organize their lives around a projected future that the present cannot supply. Neither man ever entertains the possibility that the market is indifferent to charm, and the refusal to entertain it is what kills them both.

### Q: How do the two indict the dream at different class levels?

At the gilded altitude the promise fails through exclusion, and at the ordinary altitude it fails through obsolescence. Gatsby succeeds at every measurable step, acquiring an enormous fortune, the correct house, and one afternoon with the woman he wanted, and is still treated as an amusement by people whose money is older. The barrier is lineage, and the novel is clear that lineage cannot be purchased. Willy never succeeds materially at all: his commission is cut, his refrigerator breaks, and he is fired by the son of the man who hired him. One man is stopped at the top of the ladder by a door; the other is destroyed at the bottom by the ladder itself.

### Q: What does the everyman tragedy add?

It removes the defense that Gatsby's downfall was caused by his methods. A reader can attribute Gatsby's ruin to the bootlegging, the fabrications, and the absurdity of trying to repurchase a married woman. Willy Loman breaks no laws, invents no exotic past, and asks only to be well liked and to sell enough to keep the household running, and the same belief destroys him. The play therefore converts a story that could read as personal folly into evidence of a pattern. It also supplies duration, dramatizing across a full career exactly the decades of grinding effort that the novel compresses into a few pages of backstory about a schedule in a boyhood book and a stretch on a yacht.

### Q: Are Gatsby and Salesman simply alike?

No, and the differences carry more analytical weight than the similarities. Gatsby is monomaniacal, disciplined, and secretive, with a single precisely specified object and an engineered plan for reaching it. Willy is diffuse, undisciplined, and confessional, with no object he could state and no method beyond his own manner. Gatsby's death is unchosen, arbitrary, and made beautiful by the prose around it. Willy's is deliberate, staged to convert himself into an insurance payment. Fitzgerald locates blame in a class of people, chiefly the Buchanans, while Miller gives his play no villain at all and forces the audience to blame an arrangement. Treating the two as one figure loses the class argument entirely.

### Q: How does each man die by a false promise of success?

Gatsby dies having received everything the promise offered and discovered that acceptance was never on the market. He is shot in his own pool for something Daisy did, by a man who has been misinformed, while waiting for a call that was never coming, and the arbitrariness underlines that the fortune bought him no protection. Willy dies having complied with the promise's every requirement and discovered that service purchases nothing surviving a man's usefulness. He drives into a wall so his family can collect, and the play declines to tell us whether the insurance paid. In both cases the belief remains intact at the moment of death and the world has already refused it.

### Q: When was Death of a Salesman first performed?

Arthur Miller's play opened on Broadway in February 1949 at the Morosco Theatre and won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama that year. The date matters for any comparative essay because it places the play after the Depression and the Second World War, in a culture that already half agreed the confident version of the American promise could fail. Fitzgerald published The Great Gatsby in April 1925, near the top of a boom, writing into a culture where the belief was still ascendant. That difference in rhetorical situation explains more about the two works' methods than any comparison of the writers' temperaments, since Miller did not need to persuade his audience that the promise fails, only that its failure is tragic.

### Q: Who is Willy Loman in Arthur Miller's play?

Willy Loman is a sixty-three year old traveling salesman who has worked the New England territory for the same company for thirty-four years and can no longer make it pay. He lives in a Brooklyn house nearly paid off and hemmed in by apartment buildings, with his wife Linda and two grown sons, Biff and Happy, who have both absorbed his belief that being well liked is what produces success. Over the play's two days he is cut loose by his employer, confronts his elder son about the lies both of them have been telling, and finally kills himself in a staged car accident to leave his family the insurance money. He is Miller's argument that the common man is fit material for tragedy.

### Q: Is Willy Loman a tragic hero in the classical sense?

Miller argued in print in 1949 that the common man is as apt a subject for tragedy as kings, on the grounds that the tragic feeling comes from watching a person lay down his life to secure his sense of personal dignity, and that is a real critical position stated publicly by the playwright. Applied to Willy the argument holds well. He has a total conception of what he is owed, he refuses every offer that would require abandoning it, including his neighbor Charley's repeated job offer, and he finally converts himself into a payment to preserve his standing in his son's eyes. The refusal of the job is the hinge: a man who took it would live and would be, in his own accounting, nothing.

### Q: How do the funerals in the two works compare?

Both are nearly empty, and the emptiness delivers the verdict in each case, but they expose different things. Gatsby's funeral is attended by Nick, the father, a servant or two, the postman, and the owlish guest from the library, while the crowds who ate his food stay away and Wolfsheim declines by letter. That exposes a real crowd that chose not to come, which is a judgment on a society. Willy's Requiem contains only his family and one neighbor, and its force comes from Willy having predicted an enormous graveside populated by buyers from all over New England. That exposes a network that never existed, which is a judgment on a delusion. Essays that treat the two as making one point stay thin.

### Q: How does the form of a play change the tragedy?

A novel can withhold and a play cannot. Fitzgerald's central technique depends on the reader having no access to Gatsby's interior, enforced by first-person narration through a witness who cannot enter the man's head, and the resulting unavailability is what makes the figure irreducible and magnificent. A character on stage is physically present for every minute, so Miller inverts the method: rather than concealing the interior he stages it, letting scenes from fifteen years earlier occupy the stage beside the present. The result is that a reader knows more about Willy Loman's inner life in two hours than after five rereadings of Gatsby. Concealment produces mystery; exposure produces pity, and each is the natural move for its medium.

### Q: How do Biff Loman and Nick Carraway function as witnesses?

Miller's arrangement is biological and direct, Fitzgerald's elective and oblique. Biff is Willy's son, raised on his father's belief, and the play's crisis is his attempt to make both of them admit they are ordinary people. The confrontation is the dramatic engine because two people who share a past are in one room holding incompatible versions of it. Nick is a neighbor and a distant cousin of Daisy's with no obligation to Gatsby at all, which gives Fitzgerald an interpretive engine instead: a witness with no stake whose account can be trusted about facts and distrusted about judgment. The novel underlines the difference by giving Gatsby an actual father who arrives too late and is proud in entirely the wrong register.

### Q: Does either work blame the individual or the system?

Fitzgerald blames people and Miller blames arrangements, and the difference is the pairing's most productive contrast. The obstacle to Gatsby is Tom Buchanan, and behind Tom a class, so the indictment terminates in human character. That structure permits readers to imagine a contingent alternative in which a braver Daisy or a less brutal Tom produces a different ending, and generations have imagined exactly that. Miller forecloses the fantasy by giving his play no villain. Howard, who fires Willy, is distracted rather than cruel. Charley is generous. Linda is devoted. Everyone behaves within the normal range and Willy is destroyed anyway, which forces the audience to locate the cause in the structure.

### Q: What role does memory of the past play in each work?

Gatsby's relationship to the past is a project and Willy's is an intrusion. Gatsby does not merely remember 1917; he intends to reinstate it, and his insistence that of course the past can be repeated states the belief plainly. The novel treats this as a category error rather than sentimentality, registering the loss at the moment of achievement when his count of enchanted objects diminishes by one. Willy is not building toward a past but falling into one, and Miller stages memory directly, letting scenes from fifteen years earlier occupy the stage beside the present until the boundary collapses entirely. One relationship is aspirational and the other pathological, which explains why the novel reads as pursuit and the play as collapse.

### Q: How do I write a comparative essay on Gatsby and Death of a Salesman?

Reject the similarity list first. A thesis claiming both works show the failure of the American Dream is true, uncontested, and worth no credit. Take the altitude argument for the safest strong option: the two works indict the same promise at opposite ends of the class structure, and opposite strategies producing identical outcomes shows the failure is structural rather than personal. Take the causation argument for the more ambitious option, contrasting Fitzgerald's blame on a class with Miller's blame on an arrangement. Structure each paragraph as claim, novel evidence, play evidence, inference, and never alternate whole paragraphs between texts, which produces two summaries running in parallel and no comparison at all.

### Q: How do fathers and sons figure in the two works?

The play makes the transmission of the belief its deepest subject and the novel largely declines to. Biff was taught that being liked substitutes for being competent, learned it thoroughly, and spends the play trying to unlearn it, so Willy's dream is effectively a family business whose damage passes down. Gatsby has no children and his dream dies with him, but the novel supplies an inverted version through Henry Gatz, who arrives after the death, understands his son through a self-improvement schedule in a boyhood book, and is proud of the mansion for the wrong reasons. Miller shows a father damaging a son; Fitzgerald shows a father who never understood what his son was building.

### Q: Which work is more critical of American capitalism?

Death of a Salesman, without ambiguity. Miller places the cause of Willy's destruction in the commission structure, the disposability of aging workers, and the mythology of personal salesmanship, and by giving the play no villain he requires the audience to blame the arrangement rather than any person in it. Fitzgerald's indictment terminates in a class of people, and the novel is more interested in exclusion than in economics, saying almost nothing about how Tom's money is maintained or what it does to anyone outside the story. The play is therefore the more complete political statement. The novel is the more difficult artistic achievement, because it preserves the beauty of the belief while destroying it.
