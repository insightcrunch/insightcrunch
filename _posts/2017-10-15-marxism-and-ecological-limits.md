The question whether Marxism can accommodate ecological limits is usually asked as though it were a single question, and the confusion begins there. Behind the familiar wording sit two quite different accusations, aimed at two different parts of the framework, with two different bodies of evidence behind them and two very different prospects of being answered. One accusation concerns the theory of value: it says that a framework built to analyse how value is produced has no analytical place for natural conditions, and that nature therefore disappears from its economics at exactly the point where an ecological critique would need it to appear. The other accusation concerns the destination: it says that the society Marxism describes as its goal, a society able to distribute according to need, presupposes a level of material plenty that ecological constraint, if the findings of earth-system science and ecological economics hold, renders unavailable. The first accusation has been examined closely for three decades and has received a substantial answer. The second has been stated clearly, by writers inside the tradition as often as outside it, and has received nothing that deserves the name of an answer. An article that keeps the two apart performs a service that most treatments of this subject fail to perform, because most treatments spend their energy on the accusation that can be answered and leave the one that cannot in a footnote.

That separation governs everything that follows. The reader who has met the charge that Marxism is productivist, that it celebrates the growth of productive forces without regard to natural consequence, has met a slogan that bundles together claims of very unequal strength. Some of those claims concern what Marx wrote, and they can be tested against texts. Some concern what the framework can coherently say, and each can be checked against its categories. Some concern what happened in states governed in the tradition's name, and they invite testing against a historical record that has its own article elsewhere in this series. And one claim, the strongest, concerns the material presuppositions of the future society the tradition holds out as its destination, and it can be tested only by asking what that destination requires of the physical world. The method of this article is to unbundle the slogan into its components, to give each component its strongest formulation and its named proponent, to record the reply where a reply exists, and to mark honestly, in a ledger the reader can inspect, which components land, which partly land, and which have been answered. Where the verdict is that an objection stands unanswered, the article says so in those words, because a critique conducted largely from within a tradition deserves the respect of an exact report rather than the comfort of a vague reassurance.

A discipline about the science must be stated before any of the argument can begin, because the whole discussion depends on it. This article does not establish that natural limits exist. It reports, where reporting is needed, that bodies of the earth sciences and ecological economics present findings and contested findings about the throughput of materials and energy, about the capacity of sinks, and about the conditions under which human production can be sustained. Those findings belong to their disciplines, and their disputes belong there too. The question pursued here is conditional and theoretical: if constraints of the kind those literatures describe are real, could the Marxist framework accommodate them without ceasing to be itself? A conditional question can be answered rigorously even while its premise remains the property of another science. It also has the advantage of permanence. Verdicts about particular measurements age quickly and belong to journals that revise them. A verdict about whether a conceptual scheme has room for a class of facts belongs to the slower literature of theory, and it is that verdict this article attempts to reach, component by component.

## Two charges under one slogan: ecological limits and the shape of the critique

Begin with the architecture of the accusation, because its architecture explains its strange career. The ecological criticism of Marxism arrived in several waves, and each wave fastened on a different layer of the edifice. An early wave, associated with the environmental movements that grew in the decades after the Second World War, treated Marxism as one more variety of industrial optimism, a doctrine whose heroes were blast furnaces and whose promised land was a consumption standard matching the richest capitalist societies. That wave produced rhetoric rather than arguments, and its targets were often the official ideologies of states rather than any text a careful reader could open. A second wave, more scholarly, went to the texts and the categories. It asked whether the concept of the labour process, the concept of value, and the concept of wealth left nature visible or rendered it invisible, and it produced the most cited single essay in this entire literature, Ted Benton's examination of Marxism and natural limits, published in New Left Review in 1989. A third wave, running alongside the second and drawing on archival recovery, assembled the passages in which Marx himself attended to soil exhaustion, to the robbery of the earth, to the conditions of reproduction of natural processes, and argued that the master had seen more than his followers used. A fourth concern, quieter and harder, runs through the work of writers who accept much of the recovery and still ask whether the destination survives it: whether a society distributing according to need can be described at all in a world where the findings about limits are taken seriously.

The four waves are usually reported as one rising tide of criticism, and that reporting flatters the criticism, because the waves do not reinforce one another as much as they appear to. The textual recovery of the third wave answers the rhetorical charge of the first almost completely. The categorical analysis of the second wave is answered, in its value-theoretic form, by a reply that draws strength from the very recovery the third wave produced: Paul Burkett's Marx and Nature, published in 1999, showed how the distinction between value and wealth, present in Marx's text from the beginning, explains why capitalist accounting is blind to nature without implying that the theory analysing that accounting shares the blindness. But neither the recovery nor the reply touches the fourth concern. A Marx who worried about soil fertility in 1867 might still have described, in 1875, a higher phase of society whose distributive principle presupposes a material condition that constrained throughput makes unattainable. The worry and the presupposition are compatible, because they live in different parts of the theory and answer to different tests. Recognising that compatibility is the beginning of seriousness on this subject. It is also, in the judgement recorded in the ledger below, this article's central finding, and it has a name that the article will use throughout: the abundance premise is the real difficulty.

Why has the debate spent so long on the weaker components? Part of the explanation is disciplinary habit. Textual questions can be settled by scholarship, and scholars prefer questions that can be settled. Categorical questions can be argued with the tools of philosophy, and philosophers prefer arguments with premises they can inspect. The abundance question belongs to neither craft. It asks a scholar of texts to reason about the physical presuppositions of a distributive principle, and it asks a philosopher to treat a passage about the future as a claim about material conditions rather than as a piece of rhetoric. Faced with a question that fits no seminar, the literature did what literatures do: it returned to the questions that fit. Another part of the explanation is polemical convenience. Defenders of the tradition found the productivism charge easy to resist with quotations, and a defence that can be conducted with quotations is pleasant to conduct. Critics found the same charge easy to repeat, and a charge that can be repeated without reading the reply is pleasant to repeat. Between the two pleasures, the hard question waited. This article is written in the belief that the waiting should end, and that the tradition is better served by a ledger that records one component as unanswered than by another decade of victories over a charge that was answered in 1999.

### What exactly does the productivism charge allege?

It alleges that the tradition values production's expansion as an end in itself, measuring progress by output and treating nature as an inexhaustible storehouse and sink. In crude form it targets rhetoric and record. In refined form it targets categories: the concepts through which production is analysed are said to contain no place where a natural limit could register.

The refined form deserves the emphasis, and the crude form deserves a brief hearing before it is set aside, because the crude form explains why the refined one is so often missed. The rhetoric of productivism certainly existed. Movements and governments speaking in the tradition's name promised to catch up and overtake, celebrated tonnage, dammed rivers as trophies, and treated a rising output index as the headline proof of a superior system. Any honest account concedes that material, and an account of the record of particular states belongs to the companion study of the [socialist states environmental record](/2017/11/01/socialist-states-environmental-record/). But rhetoric is not theory, and a government slogan is not a category. The question that matters for the framework is whether its analytical machinery, used correctly, can see a natural limit when one is present. A machinery might be operated badly by every government that owned it and still be well designed. Conversely, a machinery might be defective in design while its operators spoke beautifully about forests. The crude charge confuses the two enquiries and thereby lets both sides off the hook the refined charge puts them on. Defenders answer the crude charge with better quotations and believe they have answered the refined one. Critics repeat the crude charge and believe the refined one needs no further work. The ledger method refuses both shortcuts by grading the components separately.

There is also a matter of fairness in how the criticism is sourced. Much of the sharpest ecological criticism of Marxism was written by Marxists: by scholars who had worked inside the categories for years, who knew which passages cut which way, and whose aim was reconstruction rather than demolition. Benton wrote as a socialist attempting to repair a theory he regarded as indispensable and damaged. Writers on degrowth who press the abundance question frequently write as heirs of the tradition who want its analysis of capitalism without its nineteenth century expectations about plenty. Treating this literature as an external attack, as something the tradition faces rather than something the tradition contains, misdescribes it and weakens the response to it. An internal critic cannot be answered by reciting the founder's ecological passages, because the internal critic has usually read them, cited them, and explained why they do not settle the point being pressed. The strongest versions of the criticism considered here are internal in exactly that sense, and the article presents them that way: as the tradition arguing with itself about whether its destination remains describable, with the argument so far unresolved.

## The charge in five components

Unbundling produces five components, and the article takes them in order before grading them. The first component is analytical: the framework studies the production of value, and in value accounting nature contributes nothing, since value is congealed labour time and a waterfall, a seam of coal, and a fertile field add no labour until labour is applied to them; therefore, the charge runs, nature enters the theory only as a free gift, a precondition registered and then ignored, and a theory in which nature is a free gift cannot think a natural limit. The second component is conceptual and close to the first: the labour process concept, the transhistorical account of how human beings work on nature that Marx sets out in the first volume of Capital, describes intentional transformation of natural material by labour using instruments, and contains, Benton argued, no structural place for ecological conditions that labour must respect rather than overcome. The intentionality built into the concept, the picture of purpose realised in matter, supposedly fits manufacturing and misfits agriculture and every process where success depends on tending conditions that unfold on their own timescale. The third component is the abundance objection, reserved for extended treatment below: the higher phase of communist society, as described in the Critique of the Gotha Programme, distributes according to need, and that distributive principle is presented as becoming possible when the springs of wealth flow abundantly, a material presupposition that ecological limits, if the findings hold, directly contradict. The fourth component is the historical record of states ruling in the tradition's name, which is poor in ways extensively documented; it is graded here only in passing, because its full treatment belongs to its own article and because a record, however poor, cannot by itself convict a theory that predicted failures of quite other kinds. The fifth component concerns the agent: the social force the tradition expects to carry its programme has historically been organised most strongly in mining, steel, energy, transport, and heavy manufacturing, the very sectors whose expansion a serious limits programme would restrain, so the bearer of the programme has a material interest arranged against it.

Each component has a different logical shape, and the shapes matter for what an answer would even look like. The first two components are claims about a conceptual scheme, and they can be answered by showing that the scheme contains resources the critics overlooked: distinctions that do register nature, passages that assign it a role, categories whose correct use points at the degradation rather than past it. The fourth component is an empirical claim about states, answerable only by historiography and by argument about what the record shows concerning the theory as opposed to the circumstances. The fifth component is a sociological claim about interests, answerable by showing agents whose interests run the other way or by rethinking who the agent is. The third component is unlike all of these. It is a claim about consistency between a normative destination and a set of physical conditions. It cannot be answered by finding better passages, because the passage at issue is the most famous programmatic passage in the whole corpus and its wording is not in dispute. It cannot be answered by historiography, because it concerns a society that has never existed. It can be answered only by reinterpretation of what the destination requires, or by revision of the destination, or by showing that the physical conditions do not in fact forbid it. Each of those routes has been attempted in miniature. None has been carried through. That is the state of the question the ledger records.

Before the components are examined one by one, a warning about a seductive error in the opposite direction. Because the first and second components have received strong answers, there is a temptation to treat the ecological criticism as a whole as a misunderstanding that scholarship has dissolved. The temptation should be resisted for a reason internal to the criticism itself: the critics who developed the third component often grant the answers to the first two. Benton's later work engaged the recovery literature seriously. Burkett's reply on value was absorbed by the very writers who continued to press the question of plenty. A criticism whose strongest form survives the refutation of its weaker forms is not a misunderstanding; it is a criticism that was initially misstated by its own early proponents and then refined by debate into the form that matters. The history of this debate, read whole, is a story of a criticism improving under fire while its target's defences improved against the earlier versions. Both improvements are real. Only one of them reaches the destination passage.

### Why did the blind spot essay set the terms of debate?

Benton's essay earned its influence by relocating the argument from quotations to structure. Rather than collecting passages where Marx neglected nature, it examined that founding concept of production itself and argued that the concept's built in intentionality, its model of purpose imposed on passive material, left no room for the ecological conditions on which whole classes of production depend.

That relocation explains the essay's durability and also its vulnerability, since a structural claim invites a structural reply, and the reply when it came was of the same order. But notice what the relocation left untouched. An essay about the labour process concept is an essay about the analysis of production under any social form. It says nothing, and was never meant to say anything, about the material presuppositions of the higher phase. Readers who treat the subsequent debate about the labour process as the whole ecological debate inherit a map on which the destination does not appear. Much of the secondary literature still works from that map. Students meet the blind spot argument, meet the reply, and conclude that the ecological question has been asked and answered, when in fact they have watched a preliminary skirmish conducted entirely on the analysis side of the theory, while the objection concerning the future society stood outside the field of fire. The disambiguation matters for study as much as for scholarship, and the exam guide for this cluster, which treats the whole field, returns to it: the [ecological marxism complete guide](/2017/07/01/ecological-marxism-complete-guide/) sets out the field's questions in their full range, of which the abundance question is one and the labour process question another.

## Nature in the theory of value: the invisibility charge

The first component repays slow statement, because its plausibility comes from a genuine feature of the theory that careless statement turns into a caricature. In Marx's economics, value is a social form. A commodity has value because socially necessary labour time is embodied in it, and the magnitude of that value is measured by labour, not by usefulness, scarcity of material, or natural productivity. A diamond found by chance and a diamond mined at great cost differ in the labour their production commands; the fertility of the soil on which wheat grows affects how much labour a quarter of wheat represents, and thereby affects value indirectly, but fertility itself creates no value. Nature, in the strict vocabulary, is a source of use values, of wealth in the material sense, while labour is the source of value. From these premises the critic constructs the charge: an economics whose central magnitude systematically excludes nature's contribution will systematically fail to register nature's depletion. What does not count cannot be missed. The depletion of a fishery, the exhaustion of an aquifer, the degradation of a soil, none of these appears in the accounts the theory teaches its users to keep, and a ruling class guided by such accounts, or a planning bureau guided by them, would sail past every limit with its instruments reading normal.

Stated that way, the charge has the shape of an argument about capitalism that Marx himself pioneered, turned against his own categories. And that is precisely where the reply begins, because the blindness of value accounting is not an oversight Marx committed but a phenomenon he analysed. The theory of value is a theory of a specific social form: it describes how production is regulated when producers are separated and their labours are socially validated only through exchange. That such a form registers only labour, and treats natural conditions as free gifts to whoever commands the labour, is for Marx an indictment of the form, not a recommendation of it. Capital does in fact treat nature as a free gift; it does in fact externalise depletion; the theory that explains why it must do so is not refuted by the observation. The critic who says the theory cannot see nature has confused the object level with the analytical level: the blindness is in the object, and the theory is the account of the blindness. This is the core of Burkett's 1999 reply, and it is a strong core, because it rests on the architecture of the whole critique of political economy rather than on scattered ecological quotations. The quotations support it, since Marx repeatedly insists that labour is not the source of all wealth and that nature is just as much the source of use values, a insistence directed against the very slogan, work is all wealth's source, that he criticised in the Gotha Programme itself. But the architecture carries the argument even where the quotations are set aside.

The reply extends further. Marx's treatment of rent is unintelligible unless natural conditions register powerfully inside the analysis: differential rent exists precisely because fertility and location differ, and the entire analysis of ground rent is an account of how a non produced condition of production is monopolised and made to yield revenue. The analysis of the working day registers the natural limits of the human body with exacting attention. The discussion of machinery and large scale industry attends to the metabolic relation between town and country and to the disturbance of the circulation of matter between human beings and the earth, a theme the recovery literature developed into the account of metabolic rift treated in its own article in this series. A theory containing rent, the bodily limits of labour power, and an analysis of soil nutrient circulation is not a theory where nature is simply absent. What the theory lacks, the careful critic should say, is something narrower: it lacks a category of natural limit as such, a place where the finitude of a stock or the capacity of a sink enters as a binding constraint on the system as a whole rather than as a condition of this or that branch. Whether that narrower lack matters depends on what the theory is asked to do, and that dependence is where the first component's verdict becomes graded rather than simple. As an analysis of capitalism, the framework registers nature abundantly and explains its degradation. As a complete apparatus for governing production under constraint, something may still be missing. The ledger records the component as answered in its strong analytical form, with the narrower residue noted and transferred, in effect, to the third component's account.

### How does the value and wealth distinction do its work?

Value names a social relation measured by labour time, while wealth names material usefulness, to which nature and labour both contribute. Capitalist accounting tracks the first and ignores the second. The theory explains that asymmetry as a property of the value form itself, so nature's invisibility in the accounts becomes evidence the theory interprets rather than a gap it suffers.

Hold on to the phrase in the accounts, because it locates the remaining dispute with precision. The reply shows that invisibility in capitalist accounts is compatible with visibility in the theory of those accounts. It does not by itself show that a post capitalist administration, using categories inherited from the critique, would keep the right accounts. A planned economy that measured its success by labour embodied, or by any single magnitude inherited from the critique of value, could reproduce the blindness in a new institutional dress, not because the theory commanded it but because the theory's positive guidance for administration is thin. That thinness is a fair criticism, and some ecological Marxists have made it, proposing that physical accounting in units of energy and materials supplement or discipline value categories in any society attempting to steer by limits. The proposal concedes the narrower residue while denying the original charge, which is the pattern of this whole debate at its best: a charge stated absolutely, answered in its absolute form, and surviving as a narrower, more interesting problem. The narrower problem, however, concerns the instruments of a future society's housekeeping. It is serious and soluble in principle by supplement. The third component concerns whether the future society's defining distributive act is materially possible at all. No supplement answers that. Keeping the two apart is once again the whole discipline.

One more consideration completes the treatment of the first component, concerning the word free. When the theory calls natural conditions a free gift of nature to production, the adjective describes their status within value accounting: they cost no labour to bring into existence, however much labour their extraction or access may cost. Critics sometimes read the phrase as a valuation, as though the theory pronounced nature worthless, and defenders sometimes answer as though the phrase were merely technical and could do no harm. Both readings miss the phrase's function in the argument about capitalism. The point is that capital receives the gift and behaves accordingly, depleting what it does not pay to replace, and that any social form whose accounting imitates that reception will deplete likewise. The theory is a warning against the accounting, delivered in the accounting's own vocabulary. That a warning can be misread as an endorsement is a fact about reading, not about the theory. The first component, prosecuted with the texts open, collapses into the warning it mistook for the crime. What does not collapse, and what no reading of the value chapters can dissolve, is the question of what the higher phase needs from the physical world. To that question the article now turns by way of the ledger that grades all five.


## The labour process concept and the blind spot argument

The second component is subtler than the first and was always the more philosophical of the two. Marx's account of the labour process in general, abstracting from every social form, describes a process in which a human being, through an instrument, works upon an object according to a purpose conceived in advance. The architect, in the famous comparison, raises the structure in imagination before raising it in material, and that priority of conception over execution distinguishes the worst of architects from the best of bees. Benton's 1989 essay argued that this apparently innocent piece of conceptual scene setting carries an ecological consequence. A concept modelled on intentional transformation, on the paradigm of making, fits production in which the worker imposes form on passive material. It fits badly, Benton argued, production in which the worker's skill consists in establishing and tending conditions within which a natural process unfolds according to its own laws and timetable: agriculture, animal husbandry, forestry, much of what would later be called care of living systems. In such eco-regulatory labour processes, as he named them, the decisive conditions are not imposed but respected, and a conceptual scheme that foregrounds intention and transformation systematically underdescribes them. The blind spot is epistemological rather than moral: not a failure to care about nature, but a structure of concepts in which certain relations to nature cannot easily be thought.

The argument's strength is that it identifies a real asymmetry. There is a difference between assembling a chair and growing a crop, and the difference is not merely technical. The crop grows while the farmer sleeps; the timetable belongs to the organism; interventions are timed to processes the farmer did not design and cannot hurry beyond narrow margins. A conceptual vocabulary that treats every production process as the realisation of a prior design will tend to narrate the crop as though the farmer manufactured it, and will tend to evaluate agricultural improvement as the progressive replacement of natural rhythms by intentional control. That tendency can be documented in parts of the tradition's rhetoric about agriculture, where the industrialisation of farming was long celebrated as the subordination of backward natural process to rational plan. Benton's essay gave that documentation a theoretical spine: the celebration was not an accidental prejudice of particular writers but a drift encouraged by the master concept of production itself. Critics who had felt that something was off in the tradition's treatment of farming acquired, in 1989, a precise account of what and where it was.

The replies came in two kinds. The first kind disputed the reading: the labour process chapter, it was pointed out, abstracts deliberately, and the same body of work contains extended attention to processes where natural conditions dominate, from the analysis of rent founded on soil differences to the discussion of the seasons' governance of agricultural labour time and the distinction between production time and labour time, a distinction meaningless except where natural processes proceed unworked. Marx knew that in agriculture the product ripens during periods when labour is withdrawn, and built the category of turnover around the fact. On this reply, the blind spot belongs to a selective reading of one chapter rather than to the corpus or the framework. The second kind of reply accepted a portion of the charge and rebuilt: Benton himself, in later work, moved toward a reconstruction in which the labour process concept is supplemented by an explicit acknowledgement of the conditions production does not produce, and other writers developed accounts of the metabolic relation intended to occupy exactly the conceptual space Benton found empty. The debate, in other words, converged on a scholarly settlement: the original concept, read alone, underdescribes eco-regulatory processes; the wider framework contains more resources than the concept alone suggests; reconstruction drawing on those resources is possible and has been partly carried out. The ledger grades the second component as partly landing: a genuine conceptual weakness, localised, substantially addressed, with the addressing still incomplete in the sense that no reconstructed concept has won general adoption across the tradition.

### Why does the production time and labour time distinction matter here?

Because it records, inside the economics itself, that natural processes occupy stretches of the production period during which no labour is applied and none can usefully be applied. A framework containing that distinction has already admitted that production waits on nature in whole classes of activity, which qualifies any claim that its master concept recognises only imposed form.

Notice, once more, what this settlement does not reach. Grant the strongest version of every reply: grant that the corpus attends to natural timetables, that rent registers natural difference, that reconstruction can supply a concept of metabolic condition adequate to agriculture and beyond. The abundance question stands exactly where it stood, because it does not concern the description of production processes under capitalism or in general. It concerns a society defined by a distributive achievement. A theory could describe every production process with perfect ecological sensitivity and still promise, as its destination, a level of provision that the physical world cannot underwrite. Sensitivity in description and modesty in expectation are different virtues, and the first does not confer the second. The literature's habit of treating the Benton debate as the ecological debate thus commits a specific error: it exports a settlement reached on the terrain of concepts into a verdict on the terrain of presuppositions, where no battle was fought. This article insists on trying the destination question on its own terrain, with its own evidence, which consists of one short text and the readings that have been offered of it.

## The reply on value, and what a good reply looks like

It is worth pausing over Burkett's reply to the first component, not only for its content but for its form, because the form shows what a settled question in this literature looks like, and the contrast with the abundance question is instructive. Marx and Nature proceeded by reconstruction of an argument rather than by anthology. Burkett did not merely assemble green passages, though the book contains many. He showed that the allegedly missing consideration, nature's contribution to wealth and capital's systematic disregard of it, follows from the theory's central distinctions when those distinctions are handled carefully. The charge that nature is invisible in the economics was answered by demonstrating that the economics is, among other things, a theory of why nature must be invisible to the agents inside the system it describes, and that an analyst using the theory sees the invisibility, its causes, and its consequences with unusual clarity. The book also engaged the strongest opposing statements rather than the weakest, treated Benton's arguments as serious contributions to be answered rather than errors to be exposed, and conceded the points that had to be conceded about the tradition's later rhetoric. Within a few years, the value form of the ecological charge had largely retired from front line use among scholars who had read the exchange, surviving mainly in summary literature that repeats charges without tracking their replies. That is what an answered objection looks like from a distance: the specialists move on, and only the textbooks lag.

Compare the abundance objection's career. It has been stated in strong form by writers sympathetic and unsympathetic. It has been noticed in passing by defenders who promise that needs might be redefined or that abundance might be relative. It has never received a book length treatment that reconstructs the destination passage, states the physical presupposition exactly, tests the reinterpretations one by one, and reaches a verdict the specialists then argue about for a decade. The absence is not evidence that the objection is unanswerable in principle; absence of a treatment is evidence only of absence. But the contrast in careers is evidence about where the tradition's confidence actually lies. Scholars write the book they believe can be written. The value reply was written because its author saw that the materials for it existed. The abundance reply remains unwritten because the materials are not obviously there, and each scholar who approaches the passage discovers the difficulty privately and turns to a more tractable assignment. A literature can thus know something collectively that none of its members states: the shape of its silence is data. The ledger in the next section converts that silence into an explicit verdict, which is the only honest way to handle it.

There is a further asymmetry worth naming before the ledger grades the components. The value objection, even at its strongest, alleged a defect in an analytical instrument. Analytical instruments can be supplemented, and the history of the tradition is full of supplements that were absorbed without identity loss. The abundance objection alleges a defect in the destination itself, in the description of the society toward which the whole analysis is oriented. A destination cannot be supplemented in the same way; it can only be reinterpreted or revised, and both operations raise the question of continuity, of whether the revised doctrine is the same doctrine. That is why the abundance objection frightens in a way the blind spot argument never did, and why it attracts euphemism where the blind spot attracted monographs. Writers facing it reach for the softening vocabulary of relative abundance and transformed needs, phrases that gesture at an answer while postponing the moment when someone must say what the higher phase requires, in physical terms, and whether a constrained world supplies it. The sections after the ledger refuse the postponement and examine the two serious reinterpretations on their merits. First, the grading itself, set out in the artifact this article exists to deliver.

## The charge and verdict ledger

The charge and verdict ledger is the findable artifact of this article, built to be lifted out and used wherever the ecological criticism is summarised. It lists each component of the charge with its strongest proponent, the reply the component has received, and a verdict graded on a three point scale: lands, for a component that stands substantially as stated; partly lands, for a component that identifies something real but overstates its scope or has been partly answered; and answered, for a component whose strong form has been met. The grades are the article's own, defended in the sections around the table, and a reader who disagrees with a grade can locate the disagreement precisely, which is the purpose of a ledger as against a narrative. Narratives blur the difference between the components by telling one story of criticism and response. A ledger forces the differences into columns. Beside the table, the article names its governing finding once more, because the table exists to support it: the real difficulty is the abundance premise, the one row in the ledger whose verdict reads lands with no substantial reply entered against it.

Read the table from the verdict column inward and a pattern emerges that no summary of this debate should be allowed to hide. The two components graded answered or nearly so are the two that concern the analysis of capitalism and the concepts used in that analysis. The components graded as landing are the pair that concern the future: what the destination presupposes and who might carry a programme of restraint. The tradition's ecological problem, on the evidence assembled here, is not located where the famous arguments located it. Its analysis of the existing order survives ecological scrutiny remarkably well, arguably better than the analytical frameworks of its rivals, since it alone among major theories treats the disregard of natural conditions as a systemic compulsion of the economy it criticises rather than as a correctable failure of information or virtue. Its difficulty begins at the point where the analysis turns into a promise. A movement can be right about why the existing order degrades nature and still be wrong, or simply unexamined, about whether the order it proposes is physically available. The ledger's service is to make that sentence checkable, row by row.

A word about the grading of the fourth component is required, since its row compresses an argument conducted elsewhere at length. The environmental record of the states that ruled in Marxism's name includes episodes of severe damage, and no purpose is served by minimising them. The question the row's verdict addresses is narrower: whether that record convicts the framework's categories of an ecological defect. Here the article follows the discipline applied elsewhere in this series, in the companion treatment devoted to [that record](/2017/11/01/socialist-states-environmental-record/), where the disasters are stated plainly in their own right before any explanation is attempted. Explanation offered there includes the inheritance of underdevelopment, the priority of military competition, the suppression of independent environmental organisation, and the official productivism of ruling parties, which was a doctrine of governments rather than a theorem of the framework. A reader may find those explanations convincing or may find them exculpatory in the bad sense; either way, the record component belongs to historical judgement, and its grade of partly lands reflects that it establishes something about the tradition in power without settling anything about the theory's analytical adequacy. It is also, notably, the component least connected to the abundance question: a state can degrade its environment through haste and coercion without its crimes proving anything about the material presuppositions of a needs based distribution that no state attempted.

The fifth row's grade will be defended in its own section below. Its presence in the ledger alongside the abundance row is deliberate, because the two rows interact in a way the debate has begun to notice. Even if the abundance objection were answered conceptually, by a worked out account of sufficiency, the agent question would remain: which organised force adopts sufficiency as its programme when its historical strength lay in the industries of expansion? And even if the agent question were answered by the emergence of movements organised around care, repair, and public services, the abundance objection would remain: a movement of the right kind could still inherit a destination description that its own ecological commitments contradict. The two unanswered rows are independent, and a defence of the tradition needs both. At present it has neither in developed form. That is the ledger's finding, and the rest of the article is devoted to the harder of the two rows, the third, examining what the destination passage actually says and what the proposed reinterpretations can and cannot bear.


## The higher phase and the passage on which everything turns

Every road in this debate ends at a short text written in 1875 as marginal notes on the programme of a German workers' party, published after Marx's death as Marx's Gotha critique. The passage that matters describes two phases of a society emerging from capitalism. In the first phase, distribution follows contribution: each producer receives back from society, after deductions for common funds, the equivalent of the labour contributed, certified and exchangeable for means of consumption. Marx is unsparing about this arrangement's defects, calling it a bourgeois right persisting in the new society, since equal measurement by labour treats unequal individuals, with unequal needs and unequal dependants, by an equal standard. In the higher phase, the horizon changes. Labour has become life's prime want rather than a mere means of life; the productive forces have increased together with the all round development of the individual; and only then, the passage concludes, can society inscribe on its banners the principle of distribution according to need, in the famous formula that each contributes according to ability and each receives according to needs. The whole discussion is available in the series' companion treatment, the [critique of the gotha programme guide](/2013/09/01/critique-of-the-gotha-programme-guide/), which examines the document's argument about distribution in detail.

For the ecological question, the operative words are the preconditions Marx strings together before the banner sentence. The higher phase arrives after the enslaving subordination of individuals to the division of labour has vanished, after the antithesis between mental and physical labour has disappeared, after labour has become a prime want, and after the springs of common wealth flow more abundantly. Those clauses are usually read as a bundle, a rhetorical crescendo, and the abundance clause is absorbed into the music. The abundance objection performs the unmusical operation of isolating that one clause and asking what it commits the theory to. Distribution according to need, without the rationing logic of contribution, is presented as possible when wealth's springs flow abundantly. If the flow is instead constrained, durably and structurally, by limits of the kind the ecological literatures describe, then the condition Marx attached to his destination fails, and the destination as described cannot be reached. The objection does not say that Marx was careless about nature. It says that the one passage in which the destination is specified makes the destination conditional on plenty, and plenty is what a limited world refuses to guarantee. No passage about soil chemistry answers it. No reconstruction of the labour process concept touches it. It is an objection about a conditional sentence, and it can be met only on the ground of that sentence: by disputing what its condition requires, or by revising the destination, or by contesting the limits premise that the article, by its stated policy, treats as the property of other sciences.

### Does communism presuppose abundance?

the higher phase described in the Critique of the Gotha Programme involves distribution according to need, which appears to presuppose material sufficiency. Whether that requires unlimited output or only sufficiency relative to need is disputed, and it is the strongest ecological objection to the tradition.

Linger on the modesty of the question's grammar, because the objection is often overstated by its own proponents and then dismissed on the strength of the overstatement. The claim is not that Marx promised infinite consumption, a suburb of limitless goods. The claim is that a specific institutional arrangement, distribution unhooked from contribution, was said to become possible under a material condition described with a word meaning plenty, and that the removal of that condition removes the described possibility. Defenders reach too quickly for the rebuttal that Marx was no prophet of consumerism, which is true and beside the point. A society need not be consumerist for its distributive principle to presuppose abundance; it needs only to distribute without contribution accounting, which means it must be able to meet the aggregate of presented needs from its product without the discipline that scarcity imposes through rationing, prices, or queues. Whether presented needs stay modest in such a society is a question about the formation of needs, examined below. The presupposition stands in the text regardless: the phase transition is dated to a material condition, and the condition is flow described as abundant. Critics who press the point, including writers formed inside ecological Marxism who would prefer the objection to fail, are reading the sentence correctly. The question is what follows.

What follows depends on a distinction the passage itself invites between two kinds of scarcity. The scarcity that contribution based distribution answers is the scarcity of a society still poor in the relevant sense: product insufficient, relative to the needs its members present, to allow distribution without measurement. That scarcity is historical and was expected to recede as productive forces developed under social ownership. The scarcity the ecological objection introduces is of a different logical type: not a stage on a rising curve but a boundary on the curve itself, set by the throughput a finite planet can sustain and by the sinks that absorb production's residues. A boundary scarcity does not recede with development; development under the wrong form approaches it faster. If the findings reported by the ecological sciences hold in their general shape, and the article repeats that it asserts no findings, then the higher phase's condition meets an obstacle its author did not contemplate: not that wealth's springs flow too slowly in the early decades of a new society, but that beyond a certain rate of flow the springs themselves, and the systems receiving the outflow, are damaged. The passage's conditional was written in a world where more flow was unambiguously the direction of emancipation. A conditional whose antecedent has become ambiguous is a serious inheritance problem, and no amount of respect for the text dissolves it.

It is equally important to state what the objection does not claim, since the article's neutrality discipline applies to critics as much as to defenders. The objection does not show that a needs based distribution is impossible under all conditions; it shows that such distribution cannot be underwritten by the specific expectation the passage encodes, the expectation of abundant flow. A defender could in principle reconstruct the destination: distribution according to need within a bounded throughput, sustained by needs formed under the discipline of the boundary. Whether that reconstruction is still Marx's destination, or a new destination wearing its vocabulary, is a genuine question the tradition has not faced squarely. The objection also does not touch the critique of capitalism, which stands or falls on the analysis of the existing order and has passed its ecological examinations, as the ledger records, in good order. And it does not license the inference, popular in hostile summary, that the tradition is indifferent to nature: the criticism is strongest precisely in the hands of writers who affirm the analysis and lament the destination, and whose work is unintelligible as indifference. Precision about an objection's scope is part of stating it at full strength, since a criticism that claims everything can be answered by refuting anything, while a criticism that claims exactly one thing must be met exactly there.

## Sufficiency relative to need: the first reinterpretation

The most promising reply begins by interrogating the word abundance. What if the flow the passage requires is not unlimited output but sufficiency: a level of provision adequate to the needs the society recognises, where needs themselves are understood historically rather than as infinite appetite? On this reading, defended in various forms by writers in the ecological Marxist literature who resist the productivist interpretation of the destination, the condition for needs based distribution is not that production can expand without end but that production has passed the threshold at which distribution must be policed by contribution. Below the threshold, scarcity forces measurement; above it, measurement becomes an anachronism, an accounting habit surviving its necessity. The threshold is relative to the needs in question. A society whose needs centred on nourishment, shelter, health, education, leisure, and the material bases of culture might cross its threshold at a level of throughput far below the level a society organised around competitive consumption would require, and might cross it within boundaries that a constrained planet can sustain. The passage's abundant flow, on this reading, names the crossing of a socially defined threshold rather than a physical maximum, and ecological limits obstruct only readings that equate emancipation with the indefinite growth of the product.

This reinterpretation has real textual support, and the article records it fairly. Marx's needs are never natural constants in his writing; they are formed historically, and the working class's standard of life in his analysis contains a moral and historical element that varies by country and epoch. The realm of freedom passage in the third volume of Capital, discussed in its own section below, distinguishes the realm of necessity, which persists in every society, from the freedom that begins beyond it, and locates the rational regulation of the metabolic relation with nature as the enduring task rather than its abolition. A thinker who held that necessity persists and must be regulated rationally was not dreaming of a world without material constraint in every sense. The sufficiency reading gathers these threads and proposes that the destination was always compatible with constraint, provided the constraint binds at a level above the sufficiency threshold for historically formed needs. If that proviso holds, the abundance objection fails, because limits bind total throughput while the destination requires only sufficient throughput, and the two can coexist across a wide range of assumptions.

### Can sufficiency relative to need be defined without circularity?

Only with difficulty. If needs are whatever a society of that kind happens to present, the threshold moves with the presentation and sufficiency becomes true by definition. The reading needs an independent account of which needs count, grounded in human capacities and their development, and that account must be stated rather than assumed by the defenders who rely on it.

The difficulty named in that answer is where the reinterpretation stalls, and honesty requires the stall to be described rather than decorated. Three problems accumulate. First, the threshold problem: nobody has produced the independent account of needs the reading requires, specifying which presented needs the destination undertakes to meet and which it may decline, in terms that neither smuggle in the examiner's preferences nor collapse into whatever a population demands. Writers gesture at basic needs, at capabilities, at the distinction between need and manufactured desire, and the gestures are promising; a gesture, however, is not the account, and the account's absence means the sufficiency reading functions, as matters stand, as a promissory note drawn on future theory. Second, the formation problem: needs in a society freed from contribution accounting would be formed by that society's institutions, and the objection's proponents ask what prevents the threshold from rising with provision, as it has risen under every previous expansion, so that sufficiency recedes exactly as fast as production grows. The historical record of consumption under capitalism cannot settle this, since capitalist institutions manufacture need as a condition of accumulation, but neither can the record be waved away: it is the only evidence about need formation at scale that anyone possesses, and it is not encouraging without an institutional story about how formation would differ. Third, the dynamics problem: the passage imagines the higher phase as a settled condition, banners inscribed, while a sufficiency society at a boundary would require permanent, active management of the margin between provision and limit, a politics of the threshold without end. Nothing in the destination passage contemplates a standing politics of restraint as the higher phase's normal condition. The sufficiency reading may yet be developed into a genuine answer; the materials for it are the most promising in the whole debate. In its present state it is a direction, not a destination, and the ledger's verdict of lands against this component reflects the distance between the two.

A defender might respond that demanding a finished account of needs sets a standard no other tradition meets, and the response deserves its sentence. Liberal theories of distribution also presuppose material conditions they do not examine, and the growth assumptions embedded in most welfare economics would fail an abundance audit more quickly than Marx's single clause. That is true, and it establishes that the objection is not a unique disgrace. But the debate under examination is internal: the question is whether this framework can accommodate limits, and pointing at other frameworks' unexamined premises does not examine this one. Moreover, the destination's centrality in the framework makes its presupposition more consequential here than a background growth assumption is in a theory that promises less. A tradition that asks its adherents to orient their politics by a described future society owes that description a physical audit. The audit has been requested by the tradition's own ecological writers for a generation. The promissory note remains the position.

## The transformation of labour: the second reinterpretation

The second reinterpretation fastens on a different clause of the passage. Before the springs of wealth are mentioned, Marx lists the vanishing of the division of labour's enslaving form, the disappearance of the opposition of mental to physical labour, and labour's becoming life's prime want. Defenders of this reading argue that the passage's centre of gravity is qualitative: the higher phase is defined by a transformation in what work is and in what producers need from consumption, rather than by any quantity of output. A society in which labour is self directed activity, varied and expressive, generates satisfaction through production itself that a society of stunted jobs must purchase through consumption. On this account, the demand side of the abundance equation transforms along with the supply side: people whose days are spent in activity they value do not present the same profile of compensatory needs, and the product required to satisfy them changes in composition, toward goods and services whose provision is light in throughput, education, culture, care, conviviality, and away from the heavy material flow that compensates for impoverished activity. The abundant flow, then, might be a flow of a different kind of wealth, and a planet that cannot sustain endless tonnage might sustain the deepening of activities whose material footprint is small.

This reading also has textual roots, and they run deep. The early manuscripts describe unalienated labour in terms of free conscious activity; the critique of the division of labour persists across the corpus; the third volume's realm of freedom passage locates the true realm of freedom in the development of human powers as an end in itself, beyond the sphere of material production proper. A reconstruction emphasising the quality of activity is no invention of embarrassed defenders; it recovers a genuine strand of the destination's description that sloganised versions of the tradition, with their tonnage and their overtaking, themselves suppressed. Indeed, an irony of the debate is that the productivist rhetoric of the tradition in power betrayed exactly the clause of the passage that might have helped it most, trading the transformation of labour for the expansion of output and thereby leaving the destination defenceless when the expansion premise was challenged. The reinterpretation thus has a critical edge against the tradition's own history as well as against its critics, and the article counts that in its favour.

### What changes if labour itself is transformed?

Activity valued for its own sake alters what people ask consumption to provide. Where work supplies expression, variety, and social recognition, the compensatory appetite for goods weakens, and the product a society must generate to satisfy its members shifts toward education, culture, and care, whose material demands are comparatively light, easing the pressure on any throughput boundary.

Why, then, does the ledger not record the abundance component as answered by this reading either? Because the transformation of labour, however fully granted, operates on the composition of needs and the demand for compensatory consumption, while the objection concerns the aggregate material basis of distribution unhooked from contribution. Even a population of fulfilled producers eats, is housed, is heated or cooled, is transported, is doctored, and uses instruments and infrastructures whose production draws on stocks and sinks. The physical floor of provision for a large population living at a decent standard is not trivial, and whether it fits within the boundaries the ecological literatures describe is an empirical question the reinterpretation does not address, because addressing it would require the findings and the figures this debate brackets as belonging to other sciences. The transformation reading changes the question's terms in a fruitful direction; it lowers the threshold the sufficiency reading must defend; the two readings together are stronger than either alone. But together they still deliver a research programme rather than a result: a specification of transformed needs, a physical audit of the floor those needs imply, and a comparison of the floor with the boundaries, each stage contestable and none completed. The defenders who cite the transformation clause as though it closed the question mistake a clause for a calculation. The critics who dismiss it as evasion make the opposite mistake, since the clause does genuine work in lowering what the destination must demand from the world. The exact verdict is the one the ledger gives: the component lands, the replies mitigate, and the question stands open.

There is a pattern here that the article's series framing names the wrong target correction, and the abundance debate exhibits it in an unusually pure form. The famous argument, the productivism charge in its textual and categorical versions, was conducted for decades at great length and considerable learning against the components of the criticism that had answers. Meanwhile the component without an answer waited in a short passage that everyone had read and nobody audited. Relocating the objection, which is this article's whole ambition, is worth more than another adjudication of the familiar version, because it directs the tradition's considerable theoretical energies at the joint where they are needed. Whether those energies can produce the missing account is a question the next generation of the literature will settle. The article's contribution is the insistence that the account is missing, stated in a form specific enough to be refuted by anyone who can produce it.


## The realm of freedom under durable constraint

The passage most often recruited to reconcile the framework with limits comes not from the Gotha critique but from the third volume of Capital, and it deserves treatment as the strongest single text on the defenders' side. Marx writes there that the realm of freedom begins only where labour determined by necessity and external expediency ends, that it lies outside the sphere of material production strictly so called, and that within the sphere of necessity itself, freedom can consist only in the associated producers rationally regulating their interchange with nature, bringing it under common control instead of being ruled by it as by a blind power, and achieving this with the least expenditure of energy under conditions most worthy of human nature. He adds that the shortening of the working day is the basic prerequisite of the realm beyond. Defenders of compatibility make much of this passage, and they are entitled to: it contains no promise that necessity will be abolished, it describes regulation rather than conquest as the rational relation to nature inside necessity, and it measures progress by disposable time rather than by product. A destination whose glory is free time, and whose necessary labour is governed by common rational control attentive to conditions worthy of human beings, sounds like a destination designed by someone who could have accommodated constraint.

The abundance objection's proponents reply that the passage, examined closely, helps them at the decisive point. Rational regulation of the metabolism with nature is a description of how the associated producers would manage their interchange; it presupposes that the interchange can be managed at a level sustaining the society, and it says nothing about what happens when the sustainable level falls below the level at which the society's distributive arrangements were premised. Regulation is a virtue under scarcity as under plenty; a rationing board also regulates. The question is never whether the producers would regulate, but what their regulation would have to distribute, and whether distribution according to need survives as the operative principle when regulation must say no as a permanent condition rather than as a transitional discipline. On the passage's own terms, moreover, the realm of freedom beyond necessity expands as necessity's sphere is efficiently organised, and the historical motor of that organisation in Marx's account is the development of the productive forces, the very development whose physical career the limits findings interrogate. If the findings hold, the motor meets a boundary the passage does not contemplate, and the expansion of free time stalls at whatever level the boundary permits. That might still be a considerable level, and a humane society might flourish within it; but it would flourish under a description different from the one the tradition inherited, in which the boundary is an external accident rather than an internal feature of the destination. The passage thus gives defenders a vocabulary of regulation and gives critics the observation that vocabulary is not arithmetic. Both gifts are genuine, and the section's verdict mirrors the ledger: compatibility is assertible in the passage's language, undemonstrated in the passage's or anyone's calculations.

### How much freedom survives inside a bounded realm of necessity?

Potentially a great deal, since the passage measures freedom by time released from necessary labour rather than by volume of goods, and a society could convert efficiency into leisure at a fixed material standard. What it does not supply is assurance that the standard compatible with the boundary equals the standard the destination's distributive principle requires.

That final clause is the hinge of the whole article, and it is worth stating the hinge in the plainest available form. The abundance objection survives every textual reply because it is not, at bottom, a textual objection. It is the observation of a gap between two magnitudes nobody has measured in commensurable terms: what needs based distribution requires from the physical world, and what the physical world, on the contested findings of the relevant sciences, can durably supply. Textual scholarship cannot close a gap between magnitudes. Only a theory that specifies the first magnitude and a science that specifies the second could close it, and then only if the first came out smaller. The tradition has produced neither the specification nor, consequently, the comparison. Its defenders who announce compatibility are announcing a hope about the comparison's outcome. Its critics who announce incompatibility are announcing the opposite hope, and the article's discipline, imposed by its scientific claims policy, refuses both announcements. What can be stated without hope is the asymmetry of the burden. The destination passage asserts the possibility of the higher phase under a material condition. Whoever affirms the destination under changed information about the condition owes the reconciliation. The critics' role is discharged by pointing at the condition and the changed information. That is why the ledger grades the component as landing despite the genuineness of the interpretive work arrayed against it: the work has not yet reached the point where the burden sits.

## The agent problem: who carries a programme of restraint?

The fifth component leaves the texts entirely and asks about people. A programme that takes limits seriously must, at some point, restrain the expansion of the material sectors: extraction, heavy industry, freight, construction, industrial agriculture. The social force the Marxist tradition historically organised, and from which it drew its confidence that history had a bearer, was the industrial working class concentrated in exactly those sectors. Miners, steelworkers, railway workers, dockers, and engineers formed the organised core; their unions defended their industries' expansion as the condition of their livelihoods and their communities' survival; their parties measured progress by the sectors' growth. The criticism concludes that the tradition's agent has a structural interest against the tradition's ecological correction, whatever the corrected theory says: a movement may revise its destination on paper while its bearer votes, strikes, and campaigns for the expansion the revision forbids. The point is sociological rather than textual, and it has been pressed hardest in the dispute over degrowth, where writers proposing planned reduction of throughput in wealthy economies confront the question of whose organised power could enact such a reduction against the resistance of the sectors selected for contraction.

The replies available are real but programmatic. The class composition of wealthy societies has changed since the tradition's industrial zenith: employment has shifted toward services, care, education, logistics of a different kind, and public provision, so that the bearer of a contemporary programme would not be the miner of the classic texts but a heterogeneous population of workers whose daily labour is less entangled with material expansion and in many cases, care and repair and teaching, directly oriented to the activities a sufficiency society would expand. Theorists of the agent have proposed coalitions organised around reproduction and public goods rather than around the point of extraction. Environmental movements have demonstrated mobilising capacity outside the classic structures altogether. Each reply sketches a possible bearer; none identifies an organised force with the durability, discipline, and material leverage the tradition's theory of agency demands, committed to restraint as its programme rather than to expansion of its own sector under greener management. The historical unions that might have negotiated a contraction from strength largely spent that strength defending the sectors as they were. The newer movements mobilise episodically and dissolve. The agent question, like the abundance question, is therefore graded as open: not refuted, but unanswered in the currency the theory itself respects, which is organised collective capacity rather than sympathetic sentiment.

### Who is supposed to carry a restraint programme?

The honest answer is that the tradition has not settled the question. Candidate bearers have been proposed from the changed composition of labour, from movements organised around care and public provision, and from alliances between unions in contracting sectors and ecological campaigns, yet none commands the organised durability the theory of agency requires.

The two open rows of the ledger illuminate each other at this point. A destination revised toward sufficiency requires a bearer for whom sufficiency is a material interest rather than a sacrifice, and such a bearer is conceivable: workers whose livelihoods lie in the expanding sectors of a bounded economy, in health, education, restoration, and maintenance, have interests aligned with the programme in a way the classic industrial core did not. Conversely, a bearer of that kind, arising from labour's changed composition, would exert pressure on the destination's description from below, demanding that the promise be restated in terms its members can verify. The tradition's best hope for answering the abundance objection may therefore be sociological before it is textual: a movement of the new composition, theorising its own conditions, could produce the account of needs and thresholds that seminar scholarship has not. The degrowth literature is the arena where that production is being attempted, under the pressures of an actual programme, and the companion article on [degrowth and marxism](/2017/09/01/degrowth-and-marxism/) follows the attempt in detail. This article records the arena and the stake without joining the advocacy, since its assignment is the critique and its verdicts, and since the neutrality checkpoint governing it requires that the attempt be reported as an attempt, neither crowned nor dismissed in advance of its results.

## A criticism conducted from within

Return now to a fact flagged at the outset, because it changes how the whole debate should be read and taught. The ecological criticism of Marxism, in the forms that matter, is substantially an internal production. Benton worked as a Marxist sociologist seeking the theory's reconstruction. The scholars who recovered Marx's ecological passages were, in the main, Marxists rescuing their own tradition's resources from its official interpreters. The writers pressing the abundance objection include ecological Marxists whose complaint is that their own camp prefers the comfortable victory over the value charge to the uncomfortable audit of the destination. Even the degrowth dispute is a family quarrel, conducted between writers who share the critique of accumulation and divide over its implications. External critics exist, and some of the record based indictment comes from scholarship with no allegiance to the tradition, but the theoretical core of the criticism, the blind spot argument and the abundance objection alike, was forged inside. This matters for three reasons. It explains the criticism's precision: internal critics know where the load bearing passages are. It explains the criticism's persistence: internal critics cannot be excommunicated from a conversation they are helping to constitute, and their questions return with each generation of students the tradition trains. And it disciplines the response: a defence addressed to internal critics must meet the argument rather than the allegiance, since the allegiance is shared.

### Why does the internal origin of the criticism matter?

Because it removes the easiest defensive move, the dismissal of the critic as hostile or uninformed, and because it certifies the questions as ones the tradition's own methods generate. A framework that trains its adherents to audit material presuppositions will eventually be audited by them, and the ecological criticism is that audit arriving on schedule.

Presenting the criticism as internal also protects the article's verdicts from a misreading to which ledger grading is prone. To record that the abundance objection lands is not to record a defeat inflicted from outside; it is to record the state of an argument the tradition is having with itself, in which the article's sympathies, insofar as a reference article may have them, lie with the continuation of the argument rather than with either premature closure. The wider survey of criticisms of the framework, the [critiques of marxism complete guide](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/), places this debate among the other major challenges the tradition faces and shows a similar pattern in several of them: the criticisms that endure are disproportionately the ones developed by writers who learned the framework well enough to find its joints. There is a compliment buried in that pattern, though not a comfortable one. A theory sufficiently powerful to generate its own most searching critics has a resource that theories surviving on apologetics lack. Whether the resource suffices for the abundance question depends on work not yet done, and the article's final sections turn to what that work would have to look like, by distinguishing the limits a framework of this kind can absorb from those it cannot.

## Which limits could the framework absorb, and which could it not?

The conditional question at the article's centre, whether the framework could accommodate ecological limits if the findings hold, admits a more precise answer once limits are sorted by their relation to the framework's own dynamics. Some conceivable limits are internal to processes the framework already analyses. A resource whose depletion raises costs within a branch, a fishery whose exhaustion reorganises an industry, a soil whose degradation registers in rent and in the price of food: constraints of this kind enter the analysis through categories that exist, and the framework absorbs them the way it absorbs any change in the conditions of production, by tracing their effects through competition, rent, and the pressure on wages and profits. Other conceivable limits operate at the level of the system's reproduction as a whole: a boundary on aggregate throughput, a sink capacity whose breach degrades the conditions of all branches at once, a stability condition for the climate within which agriculture and settlement were organised. Constraints of that second kind pose the harder problem, not because the framework cannot describe them, it plainly can describe any physical fact once the fact is given, but because its destination and its theory of historical direction were formed in abstraction from them. A system boundary interacts with the promise of the higher phase in the way traced above, and with the expectation that the productive forces' development progressively enlarges the sphere within which human purposes can be realised. Absorbing a boundary at the level of the whole therefore requires more than adding a chapter on resources; it requires the destination audit that has not been performed and, possibly, a restatement of what historical progress means inside a bounded metabolism.

### What would count as an answer to the abundance objection?

A worked account, not a gesture: a specification of the needs the higher phase undertakes to meet, an argument that those needs can be durably met inside the boundaries described by the ecological literatures, and a treatment of need formation showing why the specification would hold in the society described rather than inflating with provision.

Set beside that standard, the present state of the literature can be summarised without cruelty. The specification of needs exists in fragments, in the basic needs literature, in capability approaches developed outside the tradition and partially imported, in the ecological Marxist journals where versions of the sufficiency argument circulate. The physical comparison exists nowhere, partly because it requires the findings to be stated with a confidence the bracketing policy of this article declines and partly because the first term of the comparison, the destination's requirements, remains unspecified, so there is nothing yet to compare. The treatment of need formation exists as a plausible institutional conjecture, that needs manufactured by accumulation would subside under institutions that gain nothing from manufacturing them, awaiting both elaboration and any evidence that could bear on it. Assemble the fragments and the shape of an answer is visible; the shape is the best that can honestly be reported. It follows that the article's verdict on the master question is a discriminating one. Marxism can handle ecological limits of the first kind within its existing categories, and demonstrably has. It can describe limits of the second kind without conceptual strain. What it has not shown is that its destination survives limits of the second kind, and the handling of a limit, for a doctrine oriented by a destination, finally means the destination's survival. On the question in its full sense, the tradition's position is unproven in a specific, locatable way, and locating it is preferable to the fog of general reassurance or general condemnation in which the subject is usually left.

One further discrimination protects the verdict from exaggeration in the critical direction. The failure to answer the abundance objection is a failure about the higher phase as described, not about every conceivable society the tradition's adherents might aim at. Political programmes of public ownership, decommodified provision, and planned investment operate at a level where limits can be incorporated as constraints on planning without any appeal to the higher phase at all; the first phase of the Gotha schema, distribution by contribution within a bounded product, is perfectly describable under boundary conditions, and much actual ecological socialist proposal lives there without noticing the demotion. What the objection puts in question is the eschatological storey of the doctrine, the further horizon where measurement ends. A tradition can survive the questioning of its furthest horizon, as many have, by quietly relocating its centre of gravity to the nearer arrangements. Whether the relocation is a maturation or a loss is a judgement this article leaves to its readers, noting only that the relocation, if it occurs, should occur knowingly, with the ledger open on the table, rather than by the slow pretence that the horizon was never load bearing.


## The strongest version of the ecological criticism, assembled

It is a discipline of this series that a criticism be assembled at full strength in one place before the verdicts are allowed to soften it, and the abundance objection has so far been examined piecemeal, as a clause, as a burden, as a gap between magnitudes. Assemble it now as its most capable proponents would present it, in a single connected statement. The higher phase of communist society is defined, in the tradition's founding programmatic text, by the possibility of distributing according to need. That possibility is not presented as a moral aspiration floating free of material conditions; it is presented as the outcome of conditions, among which the abundant flow of the springs of shared wealth is named in the sentence itself. Distribution according to need, institutionalised, means that no mechanism rations access by contribution, by price, or by entitlement: the society meets presented needs from its product as a matter of course. Such an arrangement is stable only where the product reliably exceeds the aggregate of presented needs by a margin wide enough to absorb harvest failures, demographic shifts, and the friction of an economy without a rationing signal. The ecological findings, taken at the strength their scientific literatures claim for them, describe a durable boundary on the material throughput from which any product must be drawn, a boundary that does not recede with technical progress in the manner of a depleting seam but binds the scale of production as such. If those findings are even approximately right, the margin the higher phase requires cannot be guaranteed by development; it can only be consumed. The destination is therefore not merely unbuilt but misdescribed: it promises, as the end of a historical process, a distributive condition whose material presupposition is the one thing a bounded world cannot promise. Its conclusion is that the tradition's account of its own destination is incompatible with the constraint the tradition now says it recognises. A doctrine may revise its destination; doctrines have survived worse revisions. What it may not do, without ceasing to argue honestly, is hold both the destination as described and the constraint as recognised, while declining to perform the reconciliation. The objection's proponents include writers who would welcome the reconciliation and writers who regard its impossibility as the tradition's epitaph, and the objection is identical in both mouths, which is the mark of a good objection. It does not depend on hostility. It depends on a sentence in 1875 and a body of findings in the physical sciences, and it will stand until a reconciliation is produced or the destination is revised in words.

Against that assembled statement, measure the standard defensive responses and record why each falls short, since a reference article owes its readers the full exchange. The response that Marx wrote before the findings existed is true and irrelevant: the objection concerns the compatibility of the inherited destination with present knowledge, not the culpability of a nineteenth century author who could not possess it; indeed the internal critics make this point themselves, since their project is the tradition's survival under new knowledge, not its founder's indictment. The response that other doctrines fare worse is true of several and likewise irrelevant to the internal question. The response that needs would transform is the sufficiency and labour readings in slogan form, and the sections devoted to them show both their promise and their unfinished state; a sloganised version of an unfinished argument is not an answer but a reminder that an answer was begun. The response that the higher phase is a regulative ideal rather than a prediction confuses genres at the decisive point: the passage does not present the phase as an asymptote to be approached forever but as a condition that arrives when its preconditions are met, and a tradition that reclassifies its destination as a regulative ideal has performed the revision the objection demands while declining to announce it. None of this makes the objection a refutation of the whole framework, and its best proponents do not claim that. It makes the objection what this article has called it throughout: the real difficulty, localised at the destination, unanswered at the time of writing, and more important than the famous quarrels it has outlived.

## Misreadings the debate keeps recycling

Reference articles in this series close their analytical sections by clearing away the misreadings that competing coverage recycles, and this subject has accumulated a thicket of them. The first misreading treats the productivism charge as the whole critique, so that answering it, by quotation or by the value argument, is taken to close the ecological file. The ledger exists to refute that compression: the file contains five components with three different verdicts, and the two that land concern matters the famous charge never mentions. The second misreading runs in the opposite partisan direction, treating the value objection as unanswered and the theory of value as ecologically blind by design; the reply reconstructed in 1999 met that objection on its own ground, and repeating the charge without engaging the reply is a failure of scholarship rather than a position. The third misreading confuses the textual question with the theoretical one: whether Marx personally attended to nature, a question with a rich documentary answer treated in the companion article on [was marx an ecological thinker](/2017/08/15/was-marx-an-ecological-thinker/), is distinct from whether the framework built from his work can house a destination compatible with limits, and a favourable answer to the first, which the evidence supports, does not transfer to the second. The fourth misreading equates abundance with unlimited consumption and then either attacks the destination as a consumerist fantasy or defends it by denying that anyone could want so much; the passage's condition is about the material basis of a distributive principle, and consumption fantasies are beside it. The fifth misreading, already corrected above, imports the criticism from outside, imagining an environmental movement indicting a tradition it never read, when the indictment's theoretical core was drafted by the tradition's own scholars in its own journals.

### Why did the answerable charge consume the debate for so long?

Because it sat where the scholarly tools were. Textual and categorical questions reward the methods academics are trained in, yield publications with settled conclusions, and allow both camps the satisfactions of victory. The destination question rewards none of those methods, so the literature orbited the questions it could close and called the orbit the debate.

There is a sixth misreading, subtler than the others and worth separate notice because it infects even careful summaries. It assumes that the ecological criticism and the ecological defence exhaust the positions, so that a reader must enrol with Burkett or with the critics. The ledger's graded verdicts demonstrate the inadequacy of enrolment: a reader can hold, coherently and on evidence, that the value charge is answered, the blind spot partly lands, the record partly lands, the agent question lands, and the abundance question lands hardest of all. That composite position is not fence sitting; it is the actual topography of the debate, and it is available only to readers who grade components instead of choosing teams. The scholarly literature's centre of gravity has been moving toward the composite position for some years, as the recovery work and the value reply have been absorbed and the destination question has been left standing in clearer isolation. Students meeting the subject through summaries written before that movement often inherit a battlefield that no longer exists, with trenches dug along lines the specialists have abandoned. The exam oriented sections below are written against that inheritance: they teach the composite position as the defensible one and show how its components are argued, since an examination rewards the candidate who can distinguish verdicts over the candidate who can recite a camp.

## The textual recovery and its proper use

Because the recovery of Marx's ecological writings does real work in this debate, and because its proper use is so often exceeded, a section must fix its scope. The recovery assembled, from published volumes, notebooks, and excerpts, a body of material in which Marx attends to the exhaustion of soil under capitalist agriculture, to the robbery system by which nutrients are extracted and not returned, to the antagonism between town and country in the circulation of matter, to forestry, to climate in its regional varieties, and to the conditions under which natural processes reproduce themselves. Scholars associated with the monthly review tradition and with the edition of the complete works made this material unavoidable; after their work, the portrait of a Marx indifferent to the earth became untenable among people who read. In the ledger's terms, the recovery powers the reply to the first component and supports the mitigation of the second: the corpus attends to natural conditions pervasively, and only a reading confined to a few canonical chapters could miss it. The recovery also matters to the cluster's larger project, since the metabolic rift literature built an influential account of capitalist ecology on the recovered passages, an account with its own companion article in this series.

The recovery's limit is generic, and stating it is no disrespect to the scholars who produced it. Documentary abundance about capitalism's treatment of nature does not address the material presuppositions of the post capitalist destination, because the two subjects share no premises. A notebook entry on soil chemistry bears on what Marx thought about agriculture under capital. It bears not at all on whether distribution according to need is compatible with a bounded throughput, a question whose terms, need, throughput, distributive principle, appear nowhere in the notebooks. Defenders who deploy the recovery against the abundance objection commit a category error that the objection's proponents, who typically know the recovered material well, are quick to name. The error also harms the recovery itself, by associating careful scholarship with a polemical use it cannot sustain, so that when the use collapses the scholarship is unfairly doubted. The article therefore assigns the recovery its exact honours: it transformed the textual question, it arms the value reply, it grounds the rift literature, and it leaves the destination question untouched. A defence of the tradition that observes those boundaries is stronger than one that ignores them, because it concedes nothing the evidence forces and claims nothing the evidence cannot carry.

### Which separations protect the recovery literature from misuse?

Keep distinct the question each body of evidence answers. Recovery scholarship settles what the texts say about soil, forests, and metabolism. The destination dispute asks something else entirely: whether a distributive principle can be honoured inside a bounded product. Evidence gathered for the first enquiry has no jurisdiction over the second, and honouring that boundary is the mark of careful work.

That separation also clarifies what the cluster's later articles inherit. The article on the record examines what states did; the article on degrowth examines the attempt to build a programme on the far side of the abundance problem; the exam guide examines how the whole field is assessed. Each inherits the separation maintained here: texts, theory, record, programme, and assessment are distinct enquiries with distinct evidence, and the habit of letting a verdict in one stand in for a verdict in another is the single error this cluster most labours to correct. A reader who carries only that habit of separation away from this article carries away its method entire, and the method will serve on subjects far from ecology, since most large criticisms of large doctrines unbundle the same way into components of unequal strength, defended by camps with unequal interest in the unbundling.


## The destination across the wider corpus

A defender might hope that the Gotha passage is an aberration, a marginal note unrepresentative of the destination as the wider corpus describes it, and that the wider corpus describes a destination innocent of the abundance condition. The hope does not survive a survey. In The German Ideology, written three decades earlier, the young Marx and Engels make the growth of productive powers an absolutely necessary practical premise of communism, on the explicitly material ground that without it want is merely made general, and with want the struggle for necessities begins again and all the old filthy business is reproduced. The sentence is remarkable for the abundance debate because it states the objection's logic from inside the founding texts: distribution beyond contribution is impossible under generalised want, since scarcity reinstates competition and hierarchy; therefore the destination presupposes that want, in the relevant sense, has been overcome by the development of production. In the Grundrisse, the mature notebooks return to the theme in a different register, imagining capital's tendency to reduce necessary labour and to create disposable time, and sketching a condition in which wealth is measured by free time rather than by labour time, a sketch whose splendour has attracted generations of readers. In the Gotha Programme critique the condition is stated as a clause. In each period of the corpus, early, middle, and late, the destination is tethered to a material development that removes the scarcity which makes contribution based distribution necessary. The tether is not a slip of 1875. It is among the most consistent commitments in the entire body of work.

That consistency cuts both ways, and the article weighs both edges. For the defence, consistency shows that the abundance condition belongs to the destination's logic rather than to a careless phrase: Marx reasoned, correctly within his premises, that a distributive principle is only as available as its material basis, and he refused the utopianism of decreeing needs based distribution amid want. The refusal is a mark of the theory's materialism, its insistence that institutions answer to conditions. For the criticism, the same consistency shows that the condition cannot be edited out as an embarrassment: remove the abundance premise and the destination loses the very mechanism by which its distributive principle becomes possible, leaving a banner without an argument. The objection is thus not an external imposition on a text that meant something gentler. It follows the text's own reasoning to the point where the reasoning meets a body of knowledge the text predates. Internal critics of the tradition make exactly this point when they insist that fidelity to the method, the relentless auditing of material presuppositions, requires revising conclusions the founder reached when the presuppositions seemed safe. A materialism that exempts its own destination from material audit has, on their account, stopped being materialist at the border of its hopes. The sentence deserves to be quoted in paraphrase wherever this debate is taught, because it converts the abundance objection from an attack on the tradition into a test of the tradition's consistency with itself.

The Grundrisse material deserves one further paragraph, since its vision of disposable time is sometimes offered as proof that the destination was always about time rather than throughput, and the offer deserves a precise reception. It is true that the notebooks imagine wealth redefined as disposable time and the working day contracted as machinery absorbs necessary labour. But the mechanism of that contraction, in the notebooks' own analysis, is the advance of the productive forces under and then beyond capital: automation, the application of science to production, the general intellect embodied in a system of machinery. That mechanism is a throughput story before it is a time story, since the machinery system that liberates time is built, powered, and maintained from materials and energy drawn from nature. Whether a machinery system adequate to liberate time at the scale of a whole society can be operated within bounded throughput is, once more, the unaudited comparison. The notebooks deepen the destination's attractiveness; they do not lighten its material basis. Readers who cite the disposable time passages against the abundance objection have usually not asked what the general intellect runs on. The question is not rhetorical and not hostile; it is the audit question, and the tradition will be stronger when some of its scholars answer it in detail rather than resenting it in principle.

## The productive forces and the direction of history

Beneath the destination passage lies a deeper stratum of the framework that the ecological criticism also touches, and a complete treatment must descend to it. The framework's philosophy of history assigns productive force development a directional role: productive powers accumulate across generations, strain against the relations of production that fetter them, and drive the succession of social forms. Ecological critics of a philosophical bent have asked what becomes of that directionality in a bounded world. If the forces cannot develop indefinitely in their material dimension, does the motor of history stall, and does the succession of forms lose its engine? The question is sometimes pressed as though it were fatal, and sometimes waved away as a confusion between quantitative growth and qualitative development. The truth, as this article's method would predict, is graded. The framework's directional claim can be restated in terms of knowledge, technique, and organisational capacity, dimensions in which development is not obviously bounded by throughput: a society can know more, coordinate better, and waste less without moving more matter. Several ecological Marxist writers have performed exactly that restatement, distinguishing the expansion of the productive forces from the growth of material throughput and accusing the tradition's official versions of conflating them. The restatement is coherent. It is also, once more, a revision in the strict sense, since the historical texts move between the two meanings without marking the distinction, and the official movements built their legitimacy on the quantitative meaning, in steel tonnage and electrification. A restated directionality may be the framework's future; it is not yet its settled doctrine, and the difference between a coherent restatement available in the journals and a doctrine a tradition actually holds is a difference this article has insisted on throughout.

### Does the development of productive powers have to mean material growth?

Not by logical necessity. Development can name gains in knowledge, technique, coordination, and the sparing use of materials, all compatible with a constant or falling throughput. The difficulty is historical and doctrinal: the texts slide between the qualitative and quantitative senses, and the movements that governed in the tradition's name institutionalised the quantitative sense as their measure of success.

The sliding between senses has a consequence for how the abundance objection should be taught. Students are often presented with a choice between a productivist Marx, doomed by ecology, and an ecological Marx, innocent of productivism, and invited to pick the real one. The sliding shows the choice to be malformed. The corpus contains a thinker who attended to nature with unusual penetration for his century and who also tethered his destination to the overcoming of want through production's development, without distinguishing the senses in which that development might proceed. Both facts are in the texts, and an education that hides either produces graduates who will be ambushed by the other. The ambush pattern is familiar to examiners: a candidate trained on the recovery literature meets the Gotha clause in a question and has nothing to say; a candidate trained on the productivism charge meets the soil chapters and has nothing to say. The composite position this article defends is precisely the refusal of the ambush, the insistence that both bodies of fact be held together and their tension named as the tradition's open problem. Open problems are not disgraces in a living tradition; they are its agenda. The disgrace, where there is one, lies in the decades during which the problem's sharpest form was available in the literature and the textbooks continued to examine only the forms that had been settled.

## Needs, their formation, and the threshold problem restated

Since the sufficiency reading carries the defence's best hopes, its central concept deserves a section of direct examination. A need, in the usage the debate requires, is not a passing desire and not a biologically fixed minimum; it is a requirement whose frustration damages a human life in ways the society itself recognises, and the list of such requirements changes as societies change what a human life can be. Marx's own treatment honours that historicity: the value of labour power contains a historical and moral element, the worker's recognised necessities expanding with civilisation's general level. The higher phase generalises the principle: distribution according to need takes the historically formed schedule of needs as its input. The threshold problem can now be stated in its hardest form. The schedule of needs is generated by the society doing the distributing. A society that has abolished contribution accounting generates its schedule under institutions with no built in brake: no price signals scarcity, no wage bargain disciplines claims, no contribution ledger shames the extravagant. What, in that institutional setting, keeps the schedule within a bounded product? The defenders' answers converge on culture and democracy: needs would be formed deliberatively, in public argument about what a good life requires, and a society conscious of its boundaries would deliberate its way to a schedule it can meet. The answer is attractive, and it is an answer about politics, which means it relocates the difficulty rather than removing it. A politics of the schedule is a politics in which some presented needs are refused, and refusal by deliberation is rationing by another name, exercised through assemblies rather than markets or ledgers. The higher phase was supposed to be the condition beyond rationing's necessity. A destination that requires permanent deliberative rationing to survive its boundaries is describable, perhaps even attractive, but it is not the destination the Gotha passage describes, and calling it by the passage's banner performs the quiet revision again.

Press the point one step further, because the objection's proponents do, and the article's charge is to state the strongest form. The deliberative answer assumes that a society can know its boundaries accurately enough to deliberate against them, and can enforce deliberated schedules against the perennial human tendency to experience one's own needs as necessities and other people's as luxuries. Every historical attempt to administer needs by committee has generated a politics of classification, with officials deciding whose requirements count, and the tradition's own critique of bureaucracy supplies the analysis of how such classification hardens into privilege. None of this shows the attempt must fail in a society of transformed labour and common ownership; the variables genuinely differ. It shows that the sufficiency reading, pursued honestly, leads out of distributive theory and into the hardest questions of institutional design under permanent constraint, questions on which the founding texts are nearly silent, since their author expected the constraint to lift. The silence is understandable in 1875. A defence that relies on the sufficiency reading in the present owes the institutional sequel, and the sequel is unwritten. The article returns the verdict it has returned at each stage: promising direction, missing account, objection standing.

There is, for completeness, a harder line available to the defence that few take, and its scarcity in the literature is itself informative. A defender could bite the bullet and argue that the boundaries described by the ecological sciences, whatever their validity for a wasteful civilisation, lie far above the throughput required by a rationally organised sufficiency, so that the audit, if performed, would vindicate the destination easily. That argument requires the figures the debate brackets, and requires them to come out a particular way, and scholars who have glimpsed the magnitudes involved have generally declined to rest the tradition's destination on the bet. The declension is prudent. It also concedes the article's central claim from the defending side: the compatibility of the destination with limits is an empirical wager that the tradition has not placed, not a theorem it has proved. Between the critics who assert incompatibility and the defenders who assert compatibility, the evidence supports only the party that asserts the question is open and locates the burden. That party's position is the one this article occupies, and the occupation is not neutrality for its own sake but the only verdict the assembled material sustains.


## The opening of the Gotha critique: labour is not all wealth's source

An irony sits at the head of the very document the abundance objection prosecutes, and a thorough article must exhibit it, because it disciplines both camps. The Critique of the Gotha Programme opens not with distribution between phases but with an attack on the draft programme's first sentence, which had declared labour to be all wealth's source and culture. Marx objected with unusual heat. Nature, he insisted, is equally the source of use values, of material wealth itself, as labour, which is only the manifestation of a force of nature, human labour power. Labour becomes a source of wealth and culture only in due proportion as it relates to nature as its material. The bourgeois, he added, have very good reasons for attributing supernatural creative power to labour, since precisely from the fact that labour depends on nature it follows that the human being who has no other property than labour power must, in all social and cultural conditions, be the slave of the human beings who own the material conditions of labour. The passage is the founding text's own author, in the founding text's own opening, rebuking his followers for a productivist slogan and grounding the rebuke in labour's dependence on nature. Critics who present the Gotha document as the charter of a nature blind doctrine have some explaining to do, and the explaining usually consists in not mentioning the first page.

Yet the irony does not acquit the document, and the article's discipline requires saying why with the same directness. The opening rebuke concerns the source of wealth; the higher phase passage concerns the conditions of a distributive principle. A thinker can hold, with complete consistency, that nature is a co source of all wealth and that the springs of that wealth must flow abundantly before needs based distribution becomes possible. Indeed the two positions interlock: precisely because wealth flows from nature as well as labour, its abundance is a material condition no institutional rearrangement can substitute for, and its constraint would be a material veto on arrangements premised on its plenty. The abundance objection, far from being embarrassed by the opening passage, can recruit it: the document's author understood that distribution answers to material sources, and the objection merely asks what follows when one of the two sources is bounded in ways his century had not registered. Defenders recruiting the same opening commit the mirror error, treating a rebuke about sources as a verdict about conditions. The document, read whole, supports neither camp's slogan. It supports the article's composite position with embarrassing neatness: a founder attentive to nature, a destination conditioned on plenty, and an open question where the two meet.

The episode also illuminates why the debate's textual phase lasted so long and satisfied so many. The corpus is genuinely double on this subject, not in the sense of contradiction but in the sense of addressing different questions with different answers that polemic can pry apart. Passages about nature's role in wealth answer the question whether the theory values nature's contribution. Passages about the springs of wealth answer the question what the destination requires. Each camp stocked its anthology with the passages answering its question and professed bewilderment at the other camp's anthology. The scholarly settlement of the textual phase consisted, at its best, in sorting the passages by question, which is the operation the ledger performs for the whole debate. Sorting is unglamorous work, and it ends arguments that anthology wars keep alive indefinitely, which is why the wars preferred anthologies. A student equipped with the sorting habit can dismantle most popular versions of this controversy in minutes, by asking of any quoted passage which of the five components it bears on and declining to let it testify about the others.

## Rent, machinery, and the soil: the analysis side revisited

The ledger graded the analytical components kindly, and kindness in a verdict invites the suspicion of favouritism unless the evidence is exhibited at some length. Exhibit it, then, from three regions of the analysis where natural conditions do decisive work. Ground rent first. In the third volume's treatment, the price of agricultural produce is regulated by the worst soil under cultivation, and the owners of better soils appropriate a differential rent grounded in nothing but natural differences of fertility and position, monopolised through property. The analysis makes a natural condition the pivot of a revenue category and of a class relation; it is impossible to expound rent, a load bearing part of the system, without expounding nature's unequal gifts and their social capture. Whatever the theory of value excludes, the theory as a whole places natural difference at the origin of one of its central distributive phenomena. Critics who charged the economics with nature's absence rarely engaged the rent chapters, because the chapters are technical and late and resist anthology; scholars who did engage them, in the reply literature, found the charge difficult to state in their presence.

Machinery and large scale industry supply the second exhibit. The first volume's longest chapter follows the factory system's development and pauses, in its section on modern industry and agriculture, over the disturbance of the metabolic interaction between human society and the earth: the concentration of population in towns prevents the return to the soil of the elements consumed from it, hinders the operation of the eternal natural condition for the lasting fertility of the soil, and robs the worker and the earth in parallel, since capitalist production develops technique by robbing both sources of wealth. The passage became the seed of the metabolic rift literature, and its presence in the most canonical chapter of the most canonical volume is a standing embarrassment for the invisibility charge in its crude form. The third exhibit is the working day. The analysis of capital's drive to extend labour beyond every natural and moral limit, and of the countervailing struggle that fixed a normal working day, treats the human body's natural limits as a material premise of class conflict: the werewolf hunger for surplus labour meets the physical boundary of a creature that must sleep and eat, and the boundary, defended collectively, becomes law. A theory whose central conflict is staged at the boundary of a natural limit is an odd candidate for the charge of having no concept of natural limits. What it lacks, to repeat the article's narrower finding, is a concept of an aggregate boundary on throughput binding the system as a whole and bearing on the destination. That lack is real. It is also specific, and specificity is what separates a finding from a slogan.

Why, then, did the crude invisibility charge flourish for so long outside the specialist literature? Partly because the theory of value is taught first and the rent chapters last, so that generations of students formed their impression of the economics from its most abstract stratum. Partly because the charge flattered the self image of a young ecological movement eager to distinguish itself from every inherited doctrine. And partly because the tradition's own popularisers had little use for the rent chapters either, preferring the drama of factory and crisis to the agronomy of differential rent, so the defence's best evidence sat unread by both armies. The episode generalises into a warning the series has issued before: controversies about large bodies of thought are usually controversies about their syllabuses, about which parts are assigned and excerpted, and a change in the syllabus can do what a decade of polemic could not. The ecological debate changed when the syllabus changed, when syllabuses began to include the value and wealth distinction, the agriculture section, and the rent chapters alongside the labour process. It will change again when syllabuses add the Gotha passage read as a material conditional, which is the addition this article exists to promote.

## What a revision would have to look like

Suppose the tradition accepts the ledger's verdict and undertakes the revision the objection demands. What would the work consist in, concretely, and what would count as its completion? The article's neutrality forbids prescribing the tradition's future, but describing the shape of the missing work is a legitimate act of reference, and the description sharpens the verdict by showing it is falsifiable. A completed revision would contain four elements. It would state the destination's material requirements as a schedule: the schedule of needs the higher phase promises to meet, expressed in terms definite enough to be costed in physical units. It would perform the costing: an account, conducted with the ecological sciences rather than against them, of the throughput a society meeting that schedule would require, conducted openly enough that the assumptions could be disputed line by line. It would compare the costing with the boundaries those sciences describe, accepting their findings as the best available representation rather than as verdicts to be welcomed or resisted according to convenience. And it would draw the consequence in public: if the schedule fits, the destination stands vindicated and the objection is answered at last; if it does not fit, the destination is restated, with the restatement owned as a restatement, the banner rewritten to name the distributive principle that bounded conditions can sustain, whether that is needs within deliberated schedules or some hybrid of need and contribution that the founding text would have classified as belonging to the first phase.

Each element has a partial precedent somewhere in the wider literature, which is why the article calls the objection unanswered rather than unanswerable. Needs schedules have been drafted by theorists of basic needs and by the capability approach, though not by writers aiming them at the Gotha passage. Physical costing of social arrangements is the daily work of ecological economics and industrial ecology, though not yet of the destination of a socialist society described in the tradition's own terms. Boundary comparison is the genre of the planetary boundaries literature itself. Restatement with ownership has precedents in the tradition's own history, which revised its expectations about the location and sequence of revolutions when the evidence required, and survived the revisions. The missing thing is the conjunction, performed by scholars who hold both literatures at once and accept the discipline of the comparison. Until the conjunction exists, statements that the tradition has answered the ecological challenge are premature in a precise sense: the answer's ingredients are scattered across four literatures that do not cite one another, and an answer whose ingredients have never met is a hope, not a result.

### Which limits bind a branch, and which bind the whole?

Local scarcities arrive inside single industries and register through prices, rents, and rivalry among producers, so the inherited categories digest them without strain. An aggregate ceiling is a different species of fact: it fences the social metabolism entire, and the promised destination was drawn on the assumption that no such fence exists, which is why digesting it demands fresh theoretical labour.

The description of the missing work also clarifies the relation between this article and the cluster's degrowth article, which examines the one contemporary literature that attempts something like the conjunction from the programme side. Degrowth writing begins from the boundary and argues backward to the arrangements a bounded economy requires, and its Marxist participants bring the critique of accumulation to that argument. Whether their programme amounts to the destination restated or to a different destination altogether is the liveliest form of the abundance question in print, and it is being conducted with a concreteness the older debate lacked, because programmes force schedules and costings in a way that commentary does not. The article commends the reader to that treatment for the attempt itself, while maintaining this article's distinct assignment: the grading of the criticism, which must not be hurried by sympathy for the attempt's early drafts. A programme under construction is not yet an answer, and the ledger will be revised when it becomes one. Ledgers, unlike banners, are meant to be revised.


## A short history of the argument, in the order it actually happened

Chronology disciplines controversy, and this controversy's chronology is frequently scrambled in summary, with replies described as preceding the charges they answered and settlements projected backward onto periods of confusion. Restore the order. Through the middle decades of the twentieth century, the ecological question was barely posed inside the tradition, whose environmental attention ran to conservation rhetoric and the planning of resource extraction; the objection that later mattered existed only as scattered unease. The environmental movements of the nineteen sixties and seventies posed the productivism charge from outside, in the period's polemical register, and the tradition's responses were mostly dismissals that treated ecology as a distraction from class. The scholarly phase opened when critics with theoretical training took the categories themselves as the target, culminating in the 1989 blind spot essay, which remains the period's landmark because it argued from the structure of a concept rather than from the movement's slogans. The nineteen nineties brought the counter movement: archival recovery accelerated with new editions of the manuscripts and notebooks, ecological readings of the published volumes multiplied, and the decade closed with the 1999 reconstruction of the value argument, which shifted the debate's centre by demonstrating that the most influential form of the charge rested on a confusion between the accounts capital keeps and the theory that explains those accounts. The metabolic rift literature consolidated in the following years, giving the recovery a positive research programme rather than a merely defensive brief.

Within that consolidation, the abundance question's modern form took shape. Writers who had absorbed the recovery and the value reply, and who in many cases had contributed to both, began to state in print that the destination remained unexamined: the higher phase's material condition, the agent's interests, the absence of a needs schedule. The degrowth movement's emergence gave those statements a programmatic edge, since a movement proposing deliberate contraction in wealthy economies needed to know whether the tradition it drew on could follow it to its own conclusions, and the ensuing dispute, between degrowth communists, ecomodernist Marxists, and defenders of the classical destination, is the abundance debate wearing organisational clothes. Meanwhile the earth-system sciences consolidated their findings into boundary frameworks that travelled widely beyond their disciplines, which changed the objection's rhetorical situation: the premise the article brackets, that durable aggregate limits exist, acquired institutional sponsors of a weight that seminar scepticism could no longer wave aside. The tradition's journals now host the argument in its refined form routinely. What the chronology shows, read whole, is a criticism improving in precision across four decades while the defences of its target improved against each successive version a few years behind it, a lag structure that explains the present position exactly: the defences have caught up with every version except the newest, and the newest is the abundance objection.

The lag structure deserves emphasis because it predicts, and prediction in a reference article should be marked as such. If the pattern holds, the coming literature will produce the missing audit or the owned restatement, since the refined objection is now too visible inside the tradition to be left where it lies, and the scholars formed by the recovery have both the textual command and the ecological literacy the conjunction requires. The alternative is that the pattern breaks: that the destination question proves to be the one the tradition cannot metabolise, and the debate's next phase is a quiet sorting of adherents into those who revise the destination, those who defend it unrevised against the findings, and those who relocate their politics to the first phase arrangements and let the higher phase recede into liturgy. All three groupings already exist in embryo. Which becomes the tradition's centre is a question about institutions and generations, not about arguments, and it lies beyond this article's competence and its remit. The ledger records the argument's state. History will record the rest.

## The record component, graded with care

One component of the five has so far been handled by reference to its companion article, and the grading it received, partly lands, deserves its reasoning in this article too, since the ledger's credibility depends on no grade resting on a gesture. The record in question is grave. States ruling in the tradition's name diverted rivers until seas contracted, pursued output campaigns that stripped forests and exhausted soils, suppressed the independent associations through which ecological damage is usually first publicised, and treated warnings from their own scientists as obstacles to plans whose fulfilment defined the regime's legitimacy. Any grading must begin by refusing to soften that catalogue, and this article refuses: the disasters were real, they were large, and they were inflicted by governments claiming the doctrine this series examines, a fact no analysis of categories can launder. The question the component poses to the framework, however, is not whether the disasters happened but what they prove. Three candidate conclusions compete. The strong critical conclusion holds that the record expresses the doctrine: productivist categories in power produce productivist outcomes, and the uniformity of the pattern across dissimilar countries convicts the shared doctrine rather than the differing circumstances. The strong defensive conclusion maintains that the record expresses circumstance: encirclement, backwardness, war and the threat of war, and the bureaucratic form of the regimes, would have produced ecological damage under any doctrine, as indeed comparable damage under capitalist auspices in the same decades suggests. The graded conclusion this article adopts holds that the record convicts the tradition in power of an operative productivism that its official interpreters chose and enforced, while the inference from that operative doctrine to the framework's analytical categories is precisely what the other components exist to test, and they return kinder verdicts. A record can damn a movement's practice without settling a theory's adequacy, and the refusal to grade it as settling either is the same discipline, applied to history, that the ledger applies to texts.

There is a corollary about evidence that students of this debate should carry away. The record is the component most often used in popular argument, because it requires no reading, and the component with the weakest bearing on the theoretical questions, because it underdetermines them. Its prominence in summary treatments is therefore a reliable index of a treatment's unseriousness, in either direction: the hostile summary that leads with the shrunken sea as though it refuted the theory of value, and the defensive summary that explains the sea entirely by encirclement as though explanation were absolution, are the same failure wearing different colours. The companion article's method, stating the disasters plainly, on their own terms, before any comparison or explanation, and never using comparison as an offset, is the standard this article endorses and has followed in miniature. Judgement about the record belongs to readers equipped with its full treatment; the ledger's row exists to mark the component's place in the criticism and the limits of its evidential reach.

## How the findings enter an article that asserts none of them

A word is owed about the handling of the science, since the article's policy of asserting no findings and quantifying nothing may strike some readers as evasive, and the policy's rationale belongs on the record. The debate examined here is conditional in structure: its interesting question is what follows for a doctrine if the findings of other disciplines hold. Performing that examination requires the findings to be represented accurately in their character, as findings, contested in places, revised in the normal course of their sciences, and owned by the institutions that produce them. It does not require, and would be corrupted by, the article's own adjudication among them, since a reference work on Marxism has no competence to certify earth-system science and its certification would add nothing but the appearance of authority. The policy also protects the argument's durability. Verdicts that rest on the destination passage and on the structure of the criticism survive revisions of any particular boundary estimate; verdicts that rested on a number would expire with the number. Earlier articles in this cluster adopted the same policy in their own forms, from the guide's treatment of the field to the study of whether Marx himself was an ecological thinker, and the consistency is deliberate: a cluster about the meeting of a doctrine with a science should model, in its own practice, the respect for disciplinary boundaries that it recommends to the doctrine. Where the article has spoken of the findings' general shape, the shape invoked is the one the relevant literatures themselves present as their working picture, that aggregate material throughput faces durable constraints, and even that shape is carried through the argument inside the conditional mood. The abundance objection, note, does not need more than the conditional. Its force comes from the destination's own presupposition meeting a possibility the sciences have made impossible to dismiss. A possibility that cannot be dismissed is enough to transfer the burden, and the transfer of the burden is the objection's whole achievement so far.


## The free gift in classical economics, and what Marx inherited

The doctrine that nature contributes no value was not invented by Marx; it was inherited from the classical economics he criticised, and understanding the inheritance clarifies both the charge and the reply. William Petty had declared labour the father and land the mother of wealth, a formula whose charm outlived its precision. Adam Smith held that the rent of land is a monopoly price, the work of nature in agriculture adding to the product without adding to the value the market measures. David Ricardo, whose labour theory Marx reconstructed, treated the original and indestructible powers of the soil as a free gift, a condition of production that commands no price until property appropriates it. Marx took over the classical insight that nature's contributions, unlike labour's, do not create value, and he made that insight the basis of a criticism: a society whose accounts register only value will treat what it cannot price as though it had no cost, and will degrade it. The ecological charge that the theory renders nature invisible therefore inherits a classical feature and attributes it to the critic of classical economics as though he had invented it. The reply's strength is exactly the strength of that observation: the invisibility is the phenomenon under investigation, and the theory's contribution is to explain why a market society cannot see what it is destroying.

There is a second classical inheritance worth naming, concerning the word free itself. In the economics of the period, free meant without exchange value, not without importance: air and water were the standard examples of useful things with no value, precisely because their abundance made them unpriceable. Marx's use of the term for natural conditions carries the same technical meaning, and the ecological critic who reads it as a moral valuation, nature is worthless to this theory, misreads a technical vocabulary in the same way a critic of physics would misread a technical use of work. The technical vocabulary does, however, contain a historical irony that the article records without exploiting. A vocabulary built to describe abundance becomes, in a century that discovers limits, a vocabulary that conceals them: what was free because it was abundant looks, under boundary conditions, like something whose price should have been counted all along. The theory's defenders are entitled to the technical reading; its critics are entitled to observe that technical readings age badly when their background conditions change. Both entitlements coexist, and the ledger's graded verdicts are the instrument for holding both at once. What the classical inheritance cannot supply, once more, is anything about the destination. Ricardo and Smith offered no account of a needs based society, and the Gotha passage's condition belongs to Marx's own normative imagination, not to his classical inheritance. The objection's locus stays where the ledger puts it.

## Two kinds of productivism, and why the charge misfires as often as it lands

A further distinction sorts the debate's parties and explains why they so often talk past each other. Productivism can name a doctrine about production's desirability, the proposition that more output is good and its growth is progress, or it can name a disposition of institutions, the habit of measuring success by tonnage and defending expansion regardless of consequence. The classical texts contain the first in the qualified form discussed above, since development of the productive forces is the engine of emancipation in the framework's history. The tradition's governing parties certainly exhibited the second, as the record component documents. The ecological criticism frequently accuses the doctrine of the disposition, and the defence frequently answers the disposition charge by defending the doctrine, and the two parties pass each other like ships. Sorting the senses is an act of charity to both sides: the disposition charge against the governing movements stands, and the doctrine's development thesis can be restated, as shown above, in terms that do not require indefinite material growth. A tradition can thus repudiate its productivist disposition while retaining a developmental doctrine, and the distinction matters for the abundance question too, since the disposition's repudiation is a political act a movement can perform overnight, while the doctrine's restatement is theoretical work of the kind the ledger finds undone. The degrowth dispute, on this analysis, is a quarrel about whether the doctrine's restatement is possible, not about whether the disposition should be abandoned, on which most participants agree. The clarity that follows from sorting the senses is worth the section's space, since the summary literature's confusion on this point is a major source of the debate's apparent interminability.

## Distribution in the first phase under a boundary

The first phase of the Gotha schema deserves a section of its own, because it is the part of the destination the abundance objection does not touch, and its immunity carries a practical lesson. In the first phase, the individual receives from society a certificate for the labour contributed, minus the deductions for common funds, for administration, for education and health, for the fund for those unable to work, and then exchanges the certificate for means of consumption. The arrangement is measurement in its purest form, and it is designed for a society still operating under the scarcity the objection describes. Nothing in its structure requires expanding throughput: a bounded product is certified and distributed by contribution, with the deductions doing the work of providing common goods. A socialist society of the first phase could, in principle, administer a bounded metabolism competently, even elegantly, by adjusting the deductions and the certificates to the product available and by debating the deductions in public. The first phase's honesty is that it does not promise to have transcended the problem of allocation. Its defect, by the passage's own lights, is that it perpetuates a bourgeois right, treating unequal persons by an equal measure and preserving the defect of equal rights for unequals. The abundance objection's full force is thus confined to the further step, the claim that society can move beyond that defect to a principle it describes as depending on plenty. A movement that located its ambitions in the first phase, and treated the higher phase as an aspiration whose conditions had not been met, would face no abundance objection at all. It would face instead the political question of whether a society that never graduates beyond contribution accounting can sustain enthusiasm, equality, and freedom, and that question is difficult enough without the ecological complication. The tradition's attachment to the higher phase is understandable, since the first phase's defects are the very inequalities a socialist programme exists to overcome, and the attachment explains why the objection bites: the banner principle is the promise that makes the doctrine more than a scheme for administering scarcity fairly. Without it, the doctrine promises fairness under constraint, a real good, but not the emancipation from measurement that made it distinct. The choice between a doctrine that keeps its banner and owes an audit, and a doctrine that quietly relocates to the first phase and lets the banner lapse, is the choice the tradition faces, and it should be faced in those words rather than through the usual vocabulary of denial and dismissal.

## The question as it is examined

Readers of this series include students whose encounter with the material is an examination, and the subject's examinability is part of its discipline. A strong examination answer on this topic begins by separating the two charges and naming the value reply, proceeds to the blind spot argument with its date and its author, states the abundance objection as the strongest form, distinguishes the sufficiency and labour readings from a settled answer, and concludes with the ledger's graded verdicts rather than with a verdict on the tradition as a whole. The commonest failing answers either recite the recovery literature as though it disposed of the destination question, or recite the productivism charge as though the value reply did not exist, and either failing reveals the same defect: an answer organised by camps rather than by components. Mark schemes that reward component grading, in this cluster and beyond, select for the article's central habit, and the exam guide for the cluster trains candidates in it explicitly. There is also a matter of examination tactics worth recording, since the subject tempts a candidate into asserting the science. A candidate should never assert a measurement or a boundary as fact, and never rest a verdict on a number, since the numbers belong to other disciplines and their exam relevance is nil. The defensible move is the conditional: if the findings hold in their general shape, the destination's presupposition is in difficulty, and the difficulty's locus is the higher phase. Conditional reasoning is not evasive reasoning; it is the only reasoning that respects both the science's authority over its own domain and the theory's responsibility for its own presuppositions. Students who master the conditional will find the same move serviceable across the series, wherever a doctrine meets a science, and the ecological cluster is the place where the move is taught most sharply, because the stakes of getting it wrong are visible in every paragraph of the debate.


## The degrowth dispute as the abundance question in organisational form

The abundance objection acquired a practical arena when a movement arose proposing the planned contraction of material throughput in wealthy economies. Degrowth, as a programme, begins where the ledger's third row ends: it treats the boundary as given for planning purposes and asks what arrangements a wealthy society needs in order to contract its metabolism deliberately while improving security and equality. Its Marxist participants bring to that programme the critique of accumulation, the observation that the existing order must expand or crisis follows, and the consequent argument that contraction under capitalist relations would be a catastrophe of unemployment and debt deflation rather than an ecological policy, so that contraction requires social transformation of exactly the kind the tradition describes. Its critics from within the tradition answer with two objections that reproduce, in programmatic dress, the article's two unanswered rows. The first is the destination objection inverted: a programme of contraction, they argue, abandons the promise of plenty that made the tradition a doctrine of emancipation for the world's poor, and substitutes an austerity whose appeal is confined to the already comfortable. The second is the agent objection in organisational dress: the coalition for contraction does not exist at the required scale, its natural constituency is educated and metropolitan, and the workers in the sectors marked for contraction will defend those sectors against any party that names them. The dispute is conducted with genuine learning on both sides and with an urgency the seminar debate lacked, because programmes cannot indefinitely bracket their presuppositions: a movement must say what it will do about the sectors, the needs schedule, and the destination, and its answers expose exactly the gaps the ledger records.

This article takes no side in that dispute, and its reasons belong to its assignment: the grading of a criticism, not the endorsement of a programme. But the dispute's existence is evidence in the grading, and the article enters it as such. A criticism that has generated a programmatic literature attempting to answer it is not a criticism that has been answered; it is a criticism that has been promoted, from the journals of theory into the manifestos of movements, where its unanswered status costs more and shows more clearly. The degrowth literature's best work can be read as the missing audit conducted piecemeal under political pressure: schedules of needs proposed in the form of policy platforms, costings attempted in the form of transition plans, bearers sought in the form of coalition building. Whether the piecemeal audit converges on an answer is the question the companion article pursues. For the ledger's purposes, the promotion of the question confirms the grading of the third and fifth rows: they are the live questions of the tradition's ecological wing, and their liveness is the strongest evidence that the famous quarrels they displaced are dead.

## The ecomodernist answer and its cost

Completeness requires the one reply to the abundance objection that does attempt to deny its premise from within the tradition, rather than reinterpret the destination or relocate to the first phase. A current of Marxist writing, sometimes called ecomodernist, argues that the boundary is not where the objection needs it to be: that human ingenuity, applied through the very development of the productive forces the tradition celebrates, can decouple provision from throughput so thoroughly that the springs of wealth can flow abundantly in use value terms while material throughput falls. On this view, the destination's condition is a condition about wealth, and wealth is not throughput; a society rich in services, knowledge, care, and digitally mediated goods can be abundant by every measure that matters to distribution while its material metabolism contracts. The argument deserves its place in the exchange because it is the only reply that meets the objection on the physical ground, accepting that the destination needs a material basis and claiming the basis is compatible with the boundary. Its cost is that it depends entirely on decoupling of a completeness and durability that the ecological literatures treat as unproven at the required scale, which returns the argument to the article's bracketed premise: the reply asserts, about the findings, exactly what this article's policy forbids asserting in either direction. The ecomodernist reply is therefore best understood as a wager placed on the science coming out a particular way, placed openly by its best proponents and evasively by its worst. A wager is a legitimate thing to place; movements place them constantly. But a wager is not an answer, and the ledger records the row accordingly, while noting the reply as the one whose fortunes would change fastest if the underlying science shifted, in either direction.

Notice the symmetry the last two sections have produced, because it is the debate's deepest structural feature and the article's final analytical gift to the reader. The abundance objection and its two programmatic replies, degrowth and ecomodernism, form a triangle in which each position accuses the other two of evading a term of the problem. Degrowth accepts the boundary and revises the destination. Ecomodernism keeps the destination and contests the boundary's bearing. The classical defence keeps both and owes the reconciliation. Every participant can state the other positions' evasions fluently; the literature is rich in such statements. What no participant has produced is the position with no evasion, the one that states the needs schedule, the costing, the boundary, and the bearer, and shows them consistent. The triangle is therefore the honest map of the territory, and a reader who can draw it understands the debate better than a reader who has memorised any single camp's bibliography. The article's verdict stands at the triangle's centre, which is not a compromise position but a report: the consistency proof has not been exhibited by anyone, and the presumption that it exists, in whichever camp's keeping, is faith rather than scholarship.

No article in a series stands alone, and this one's neighbours define its borders. The present article's assignment within that division of labour is the criticism and its grading, and its borders are the places where its questions hand over to its neighbours': the textual question hands over at the point where documents end and presuppositions begin; the record question hands over where historiography begins; the programme question hands over where advocacy begins. ## The ledger as a research agenda

A ledger is a static thing, and the article closes its analytical work by converting this one into the agenda it implies, since the difference between a criticism that paralyses and a criticism that directs is whether the work it demands is specified. The agenda has five items, one per row, in ascending order of difficulty. The value row demands maintenance: the reply of 1999 should be taught wherever the charge is taught, and summaries that omit it should be corrected as a matter of scholarly hygiene. The blind spot row demands completion: the rebuilding of the labour process concept, begun from within, should be carried to the point where a successor concept commands enough assent to be taught, so that students stop meeting the 1989 objection without its 1990s sequel. The record row demands the continued separation of historiographical judgement from theoretical verdict, with the disasters neither minimised nor conscripted. The agent row demands sociology: an account, grounded in the changed composition of labour, of the bearer of a bounded programme, stated with the concreteness the theory of agency requires rather than the warmth of coalition rhetoric. The abundance row demands the audit: the schedule, the costing, the comparison, and the owned consequence, performed by scholars literate in both the founding texts and the ecological sciences. None of the five items is utopian; each names work that identifiable people could begin tomorrow with existing skills. The agenda is the article's constructive residue, and it is offered in the belief that a tradition is judged, in the long run, by whether its open problems attract its best minds or repel them into safer topics. The abundance problem has repelled long enough. Its statement in a form this specific is an invitation to the scholar who will close it, in whichever direction the evidence closes it.


## The deductions: the common funds as an instrument the destination forgets

Return to the Gotha passage once more, this time to its least quoted machinery. Before the individual producer receives anything, in either phase's accounting, the total product is subject to deductions: a fund for replacing the means of production consumed, a fund for expanding production, a reserve against accidents and dislocations, the costs of administration, the funds for schools and health and the other common satisfactions, and the provision for those unable to work. The list is usually read as bookkeeping, the fine print before the banner. Read ecologically, it is something else: a standing institutional admission that the social product is finite, that claims on it compete, and that reproduction, including the reproduction of the material basis, has first call. The expansion fund is the item whose size the whole abundance question concerns. In the passage's imagination, expansion proceeds until the springs flow abundantly and the higher phase's condition is met; the deductions are the throttle by which a society conscious of constraint could govern its metabolism, enlarging the reserve and reproduction funds, shrinking the expansion fund toward maintenance, and holding the product inside a boundary while still distributing it by contribution or, in the common funds' domain, already by need. Schools and health services, note, are distributed by need in the schema's first phase already, since the common satisfactions are consumed without certificates. The needs principle thus operates inside the bounded phase for the collective goods, and the historical experience of public provision shows it operating there without abundance in the passage's grand sense: societies have distributed health care and education by need, imperfectly, from products nobody would call abundant. The observation does not answer the abundance objection, which concerns the generalisation of the needs principle to the whole product. It does refine the objection's target: the principle is not unworkable under constraint in the domains where it has been tried, and the objection's force concentrates on the margin, the generalisation to private consumption goods under conditions where presented needs grow. A precise objection is a more useful objection, and the deductions passage, of all unlikely places, supplies the precision.

The refinement also supplies the sufficiency reading with its best institutional material, better than the passages its defenders usually cite. A society that distributes its collective goods by need, funds its reproduction before its consumption, and deliberates the size of its expansion fund in public is a society that has built the organs a sufficiency politics would require. Whether those organs could govern the private margin is the open question, and the founding text does not ask it, because its author's attention was on the direction of travel, from measurement toward need, rather than on the steady state at any point along the road. The tradition that inherited the text inherited the direction without the steady state problem, and much of its ecological difficulty can be redescribed as the absence of a theory of the steady state: of how a society of this kind behaves when it is not travelling, when the product is what it is and the task is to reproduce a civilisation inside it indefinitely. Classical political economy had a stationary state and feared or welcomed it; the Marxian schema has phases of travel and a destination, but no worked account of rest. The abundance objection, translated into this vocabulary, says that the destination was described as though rest at the top of the climb were guaranteed by the climb's momentum. A bounded world asks what the society does at rest. The question is so natural, once posed, that its absence from a century of commentary is the debate's most surprising feature, more surprising even than the absence of the audit.

## Population, subsistence, and the Malthus inheritance

One variable haunts every calculation the missing audit would perform, and the tradition's treatment of it is a study in how a justified polemic can disable a necessary analysis. The variable is population: the number of needs presenters whose claims the product must meet. Marx's polemic against Malthus was fierce and largely justified on its own ground. Against the doctrine that poverty follows from a geometrical law of population pressing on an arithmetical food supply, Marx argued that every mode of production has its own law of population, that capitalism produces a relative surplus population as a function of accumulation, and that Malthusian natural law served to excuse a social arrangement by blaming the poor's reproduction for the poor's condition. The polemic entered the tradition's bloodstream, and with it entered a reflex: any attention to population as a variable in material calculations smells of Malthus, and the smell is treated as a refutation. For the analysis of capitalism the reflex does little harm, since the surplus population argument is powerful there. For the destination audit it does real harm, because the audit cannot proceed without the variable. The product required by needs based distribution is a function of how many people present needs and at what schedule, and a tradition that cannot discuss the first term without accusing itself of Malthusianism cannot cost its own destination. Ecological Marxist writers who have attempted the audit's preliminaries report the reflex as a practical obstacle inside their own camp, a conversation stopper available to any defender who prefers the destination unaudited. The article records the obstacle as part of the explanation for the audit's absence, alongside the disciplinary and polemical causes already given. A polemic won in the nineteenth century should not be allowed to levy a tax on the twenty first century's arithmetic, and saying so is a service to the tradition's own materialism, which is supposed to follow quantities wherever they lead.

The point generalises into a principle about how doctrines age. Every tradition accumulates reflexes from its founding polemics, shortcuts that once identified an enemy and now identify a topic as forbidden. The ecological debate exhibits several: population smells of Malthus, limits smell of austerity, restraint smells of moralism, and each smell test spares the defender the labour of the argument the topic requires. The critics inside the tradition have learned to name the reflexes as they encounter them, and the naming is among their most useful contributions, since a reflex named is a reflex weakened. The abundance objection's proponents had to pass all three smell tests to state their case, which is one reason the case was stated late and by writers with unusual standing inside the camp. A reader learning the debate should learn the reflexes alongside the arguments, because the reflexes predict which parts of the literature will be thin: wherever a smell test operates, the shelf will be empty, and the emptiness will be defended as principle. The ledger method is the antidote this article offers. A ledger has no nose. It asks what the components are, what the replies are, and what the verdicts are, and it records an empty reply column without embarrassment and without allowing the embarrassment to be disguised as a refutation.

## The barrier passage: limits inside the theory of capital

The corpus contains a famous sentence about limits that belongs to neither camp's usual anthology, and its placement in the argument about capitalism clarifies, by contrast, the destination's difficulty. In the third volume, Marx writes that the true barrier of capitalist production is capital itself: the system develops the productive forces without regard to the limits of value's self expansion, and periodically collides with the contradiction between the material expansion it has achieved and the valorisation it requires. The sentence shows the framework reasoning fluently about a system's internal barrier, a limit generated by the system's own logic rather than imposed from outside. Ecological writers have sometimes borrowed the sentence's cadence to suggest that the framework can think limits in general. The borrowing should be resisted, and the resistance sharpens the article's finding. The barrier in the passage is a barrier of the value form, a limit capital sets itself through its own measure; it is not a barrier set by nature, and the framework's fluency with the first does not constitute a treatment of the second. Indeed the contrast is instructive. Where the framework possesses a concept of limit, in the theory of crisis, the limit is internal, relational, and overcome by restructuring; the history of capitalism is a history of such barriers surmounted at higher levels of destruction and creation. A natural boundary of the kind the ecological sciences describe is external, non relational, and not overcome by restructuring but only respected or breached at cost. The framework's habits of thought about limits, formed on the internal kind, dispose it to expect the external kind to behave likewise, to present as a barrier that development will restructure its way past. That disposition, more than any single passage, may be the tradition's deepest ecological liability, because it operates below the level of doctrine, in the reflexes of its theorists, assuring them that limits are the sort of thing history dissolves. The assurance is a habit, not an argument, and habits are corrected by being named. This article names it as the final component of the explanation of why the audit is missing: the tradition's theory of limits taught it to misclassify the one limit that mattered.

Against that liability, set the framework's one great countervailing resource, which the article has used throughout and now acknowledges as its own method's source. The founding texts model, better than any rival tradition of their century, the practice of taking a doctrine's material presuppositions seriously: of asking what a proposed arrangement requires from the world and refusing to be charmed by its vocabulary. Marx applied the practice to the Gotha programme's slogans, to the economists' categories, to his rivals' utopias, with a severity his followers have often found uncomfortable when applied to anyone else. The abundance objection is that practice applied to the tradition's own destination, by the tradition's own students, with the tradition's own severity. To call the objection external, or hostile, or a misunderstanding, is to exempt from the practice the one text the practice's author would have insisted on auditing first, since he audited every other programme put in front of him, including the programme of the party that claimed his name while he lived. The sentence is the article's deepest ground for its verdicts, deeper than any row of the ledger. The abundance premise is the real difficulty because the tradition's own method says it must be faced, and a tradition that honours its method will face it in the open, with the schedule, the costing, and the comparison on the table, rather than in the seminar's corridor with the smell tests and the anthologies. The article has done what a reference work can do toward that facing. The rest is the literature's to write.


## The Podolinsky episode: an early attempt at a physical accounting

The tradition's difficulty with physical magnitudes has a founding episode, little known outside specialist scholarship, that repays attention because it shows the difficulty present at the creation. In the early eighteen eighties, the Ukrainian socialist Sergei Podolinsky sent Marx his attempt to reconcile the labour theory with the physics of energy: human labour, he argued, could be understood as the mechanism by which the energy of food, itself stored solar energy, is applied to retain on the earth's surface more energy than would otherwise remain, and a theory of production might be rebuilt on the energetics of that process. Marx read the work with interest and made notes; Engels, in correspondence after Marx's death approached and in letters that became famous among historians of ecological thought, rejected the reduction, arguing that energy accounting could not express the economic relations the labour theory exists to capture, that the productivity of labour in different branches cannot be commensurated in energy units without losing exactly what economics studies, and that attempts to found value on a physical magnitude repeat the errors of every naturalistic economics. The exchange is usually cited by the recovery literature as a missed opportunity, the moment the tradition declined the physical turn that ecology would later require. It is better cited, on this article's reading, as the moment the tradition chose, with open eyes and good arguments, the analytical path whose strengths and limits this whole debate has exhibited. Engels's objections were not stupid: energy units genuinely do not capture social form, and a theory that dissolved value into joules would lose the critique of political economy entire. The choice was defensible. Its consequence was that the framework developed without a native physical accounting alongside its social one, so that when the boundary question arrived, the tradition had to borrow instruments from ecological economics that its own development had not produced. A borrowed instrument can be used well; the degrowth literature is learning to use several. But the borrowing explains the audit's difficulty from the inside: the costing the destination requires must be done in units the framework does not supply, by methods its training does not teach, and traditions rarely rush to audits they are not equipped to perform.

The episode also corrects a simplification in the criticism's popular form, the picture of a founder serenely indifferent to the physical basis of production. The correspondence shows the physical basis arriving on the founder's desk, in the form of a disciple's manuscript, and being considered at the highest level of the theory, before being declined for reasons internal to the theory's design. One may regret the decision; the ecological Marxists who excavated the episode plainly do. But a considered decision is a different thing from a blind spot, and the difference matters to the ledger's second row, which grades the conceptual component as partly landing partly because the corpus and its custodians demonstrably engaged the physical questions and answered some of them in ways later critics must argue against rather than merely expose. Argument against a considered position is the normal condition of scholarship. The blind spot metaphor, with its implication of an unseeing organ, survives because it is vivid; the article's grading prefers the less vivid truth, that the framework saw the physical dimension, assigned it a place outside its central accounting, and thereby made a wager whose bill the destination debate is now presenting.

## What the critics concede, and what the concession pattern shows

Fairness to the defence requires a register of the concessions the serious critics make, since polemical summary usually strips them out and leaves the criticism sounding more total than its authors intend. Benton's reconstruction project presupposed the framework's indispensability: the point of repairing the labour process concept was that no rival account of production and class could do the work the repaired concept would do. Burkett's opponents in the value debate conceded, most of them, that the textual record was richer than the charge suggested, and moved their fire to the narrower ground this article identified. The abundance objection's proponents within ecological Marxism concede the analysis of capitalism wholesale; their complaint is precisely that a framework so good at auditing others should audit itself. The degrowth critics concede that contraction under existing relations would be a social catastrophe, which is the tradition's own point about the system's growth dependence turned into a premise of the ecological programme. Even the harshest record based indictment, in its scholarly forms, concedes that the comparison class, the capitalist states of the same decades, compiled records of damage that forbid any simple moral geometry in which one doctrine degrades and the other preserves. The pattern across these concessions is consistent and significant: the criticism, at its serious level, is not a rival doctrine's attack but a set of localised findings produced inside a shared enterprise, and its totalising versions exist mainly in summary and slogan, where the tradition's defenders and its enemies collaborate, for opposite reasons, in exaggerating it. A reader who understands the concession pattern understands why the ledger's verdicts are graded rather than absolute, and why the article has treated the totalising versions, in both directions, as misreadings to be cleared rather than positions to be answered.

There is a matching register on the defending side that honesty requires in the same breath. The serious defenders concede the destination's audit is missing; they concede the agent question is open; they concede the record in power was disgraceful in the particulars the historiography establishes; they concede the official movements' productivism as a disposition whatever they argue about the doctrine. What they do not concede is the inference from those findings to the framework's abandonment, and their ground for refusing the inference is the analysis's unmatched explanatory power over the existing order, the very power the critics' concessions acknowledge. The debate's mature form is thus a negotiation between parties who agree on most of the findings and divide over the weight of the destination in the whole. How much does an unproven destination matter to a theory whose analysis works? Answers to that question are partly temperamental, and the article, whose assignment is grading rather than temperament, leaves it open with the findings assembled. It notes only that the question's importance grows as the tradition's practical ambitions grow: a reading group can carry an unaudited destination indefinitely, while a movement proposing to reorganise production on the framework's authority will be asked for the audit by its opponents, its recruits, and eventually its own ecological wing, in ascending order of discomfort.

## The shape of the literature a reader will meet

A reference article should end its analytical work by equipping the reader to navigate the literature independently, and this literature has a shape worth describing because its organisation explains its gaps. The debate lives in four kinds of venue. The journals of the tradition itself, the ecological Marxist quarterlies and the socialist reviews, host the internal argument in its most precise form, with the recovery, the replies, and the abundance statements appearing as articles whose references map the field. The academic monograph literature, university press books on Marx and nature, on ecological Marxism's schools, and on degrowth's theory, consolidates positions at intervals and supplies the citations examinations reward. The earth-system and ecological economics literatures proceed in parallel, in their own journals, largely innocent of the doctrinal debate and indispensable to its missing audit, a separation of audiences that is itself one of the audit's obstacles, since the scholars who cite the Gotha passage rarely read the boundary literature and the scientists who write the boundary literature have no occasion to cite the Gotha passage. The summary literature, textbooks, companions, and encyclopaedia entries, lags the journals by a decade or more and is responsible for most of the misreadings this article cleared, since it compresses graded verdicts into camp slogans for the convenience of the survey chapter. A reader entering the field should therefore read in the order the venues' reliability suggests: the survey for orientation, with its compressions discounted; the monographs for positions; the journals for the live argument; and the scientific literatures, in their own summaries, for the bracketed premise whose conditional role this article has maintained. ## Scarcity inside the theory of transition

The abundance objection gains depth when set against the framework's own treatment of scarcity in the transition between social forms, because the treatment shows the founder reasoning about exactly the variable the objection presses, in the cases where he expected it to bind. The German Ideology passage already cited makes the productive forces' development an absolutely necessary practical premise, on the ground that, without such development, want is generalised and the struggle for necessities returns with all its old consequences. The reasoning deserves expansion, since it is the objection's logic stated as the founder's own. Distribution is not, in this materialist treatment, a policy choice that a determined movement can sustain against material conditions. It is a dependent variable. Where the product is insufficient relative to presented needs, competition for the insufficient product reasserts itself through whatever institutions exist, corrupting egalitarian arrangements from within regardless of the intentions inscribed in their constitutions. The founder drew from this the conclusion that a communist society could be established durably only where production had developed enough to lift the struggle for necessities out of social life. The ecological objection draws the same inference with a new premise: if the development that lifts the struggle has an aggregate ceiling, then the condition for durable needs based distribution may be unattainable not temporarily, as in a backward country attempting the transition early, but permanently, as a feature of the planet's economy. The parallel between the two cases is exact, and it is the parallel that gives the objection its peculiar authority inside the tradition. The defender who dismisses the objection must explain why the founder's scarcity reasoning, honoured in the case of the premature transition, should be suspended in the case of the bounded one. No such explanation has been offered in the literature, and the article records the absence as the objection's strongest single support, stronger even than the Gotha clause itself, since the clause might be read as rhetoric while the scarcity reasoning is the theory's working machinery.

The parallel also clarifies what a revision would have to surrender, which is not nothing, and the clarification serves the neutrality the article owes both camps. The founder's scarcity reasoning performs a function in the doctrine beyond the destination: it explains why egalitarian experiments under want fail, why the tradition counsels against equalitarian levelling amid poverty, and why its politics emphasises production's development as the precondition of its distributive ambitions. A revision that reconciles the destination with permanent boundedness must rebuild that whole complex, supplying an account of how needs based distribution, or its successor principle, avoids the corruption the founder predicted when want is, in the boundary sense, a permanent condition rather than a stage. The sufficiency reading's deliberative politics of the schedule is the embryo of such an account, but the embryo must survive the founder's own scepticism, since deliberation amid want is precisely the arena in which he expected the old filthy business to return, wearing the robes of the assembly. Whether a society of common ownership, transformed labour, and common funds could deliberate its schedule without reproducing competition in political form is the deepest form of the abundance question, deeper than the costing, because it would remain even if the costing came out favourably. Costs can be met; the politics of permanently administering the margin is the harder inheritance. The article flags the depth without pretending to plumb it: here the objection passes from economics into political theory, and the literature has barely followed it there.

## The logical form of a presupposition objection

Step back from the case and examine the kind of objection the abundance objection is, because its kind explains its career, its resilience, and the standards an answer must meet. A presupposition objection does not dispute a doctrine's claims; it disputes the conditions those claims require, showing that a commitment the doctrine treats as background cannot in fact be supplied. Such objections are unusually hard to answer for three structural reasons. They cannot be met by improving the doctrine's arguments, since the arguments may be impeccable given the presupposition. They cannot be met by accumulating evidence for the doctrine's other claims, since the objection grants those claims for the sake of its point. And they cannot be met by revising the presupposition quietly, since the objection's whole force lies in making the revision explicit and charging it to the doctrine's account. Doctrines that meet presupposition objections successfully do so by one of two routes: demonstrating that the presupposition, properly understood, is weaker than the objector supposed, which is the sufficiency reading's strategy and the reason it is the defence's most serious work; or demonstrating that the condition can after all be supplied, which is the ecomodernist wager's strategy and the reason it depends on the bracketed science. The third route, revising the destination, concedes the objection while denying its importance, and its honesty depends on the concession being announced. The fourth route, ignoring the objection, is the route of the summary literature and of every tradition in its decadent phase, and its presence in a debate is diagnostic of the phase rather than of the objection's weakness.

The form's generality should also be registered, because it protects the verdict from the charge of special pleading against this one tradition. Every ambitious doctrine carries presuppositions about the world that its founding period could not audit: doctrines of endless frontier, of inexhaustible soil, of progress as a law, of a species exempt from the conditions it studies in others. The nineteenth century was generous with such presuppositions, and the sciences since have been auditing them one by one, with results that have humbled every inheritance impartially. Marxism's distinction is not that it carries an unaudited presupposition but that its own method supplies the audit's tools and its own students have begun the work, which is why this debate, alone among the period's reckonings, is being conducted with the doctrine's participation rather than merely against it. The article's verdicts should be read in that light. To enter the abundance objection as landing amounts to to record that one more nineteenth century presupposition has met its auditors. To record that the auditors are the tradition's own is to record the ground for thinking the tradition may survive its audit better than the inheritances that refused theirs.

## Three questions to ask of any summary of this debate

Because most readers will meet this debate through summaries more often than through the literature, the article equips them with an inspection kit: three questions that expose a summary's reliability in minutes. Ask first whether the summary distinguishes the charges. A summary that speaks of the ecological criticism as one thing, to be accepted or rejected entire, has failed before it begins, since the components carry different verdicts and the distinction between them is the debate's chief finding. Ask second whether the summary engages the strongest form of whichever side it opposes. A defence that answers the crude productivism charge and stops has not met Benton, let alone the abundance objection; a criticism that repeats the invisibility charge and halts has not met Burkett, and a reader should discount both performances equally, since the debate's serious level is set by the strongest forms and anything below that level is theatre. Ask third whether the summary states plainly what remains unanswered. Every honest treatment of this subject ends with an open file: the destination's audit missing, the agent unsettled, the needs schedule unwritten. A summary without an open file is selling something, either reassurance or demolition, and the reader who has learned to look for the file will find its absence more informative than the summary's conclusions. The kit is generalisable, and the series offers it as part of its method: large criticisms of large doctrines always unbundle, always contain a strongest form that partisans avoid, and always leave a residue that honest accounts disclose. The reader who applies the kit to this very article will find it passes its own tests, which is as it should be: the charges are distinguished in the ledger, the strongest forms are stated before the replies, and the open file is the article's most repeated sentence.


## Scale, locality, and the metabolism of a bounded society

A strand of ecological writing inside and around the tradition locates the answer to limits neither in the destination's reinterpretation nor in new accounting, but in scale: the bounded society, on this view, would be a society of smaller and more local circuits, in which production and consumption are rejoined closely enough for their material consequences to be visible to the people who cause them. The strand claims textual ancestry, and the claim is not baseless. The founding texts treat the separation of town and country as a wound in the metabolism, with the town draining the soil's elements and returning nothing; the Civil War in France praises the Commune's form as the political shape of a society reconstituted from below; the critique of labour's division imagines associated producers whose varied activity overcomes the fixity that makes large scale coordination require command. Ecological readers have assembled these materials into a vision of federated local production, in which the metabolic rift is healed by proximity and the throughput problem is eased by the shortening of circuits. The vision has genuine attractions and a genuine ancestry. It also has, on inspection, the familiar structure of the debate's other replies: it answers a question adjacent to the abundance objection rather than the objection itself. Local circuits change who sees the consequences of production and how quickly feedback arrives; they do not, by themselves, change the aggregate relation between a population's needs schedule and the boundaries of its territory's ecology, except where relocalisation genuinely reduces the product's material cost, as it does for some goods and emphatically does not for others, since local production of the wrong thing in the wrong place can cost more matter and energy than distant production done well. The scale reply, like the sufficiency and transformation replies, is a research direction wearing the clothes of a conclusion in its popular versions, and a serious scholar's treatment of it would cost the local product against the imported one, category by category, in the physical units the audit requires. That costing, too, is unwritten. The article's pattern of verdicts thus extends to this fourth reply without strain: direction genuine, account missing, objection standing. The pattern itself is worth a sentence of reflection, since four independent replies converging on the same state, promising and unfinished, is evidence about the debate's maturity. The tradition has generated the lines along which an answer would run, in unusual abundance. What it has not generated is the finished traverse of any one of them, and the difference between having bearings and having arrived is the difference the ledger exists to record.

There is also a political caution the scale literature's critics raise, which the article registers for completeness. Localism in distribution can protect a bounded metabolism, and it can also protect local privilege, hoarding a fertile territory's product against the claims of the less favoured, in a world where needs based distribution was supposed to be the principle of the whole associated society and not of each valley. The tension between metabolic locality and distributive universality is real, and it is another form of the founder's scarcity problem: arrangements that work inside a favoured locality fail as a general principle when generalised want, or generalised boundary pressure, sets localities against one another. A bounded federation would need its common funds and its equalising transfers exactly as the Gotha schema's deductions provide, which returns the discussion, by a new road, to the same machinery and the same open questions about administering the margin. The recurrence of the same questions at the end of every road is the surest sign that the article has located the debate's true centre. Roads differ; the margin is one.

## Measure and administration: the calculation problem's ecological cousin

The missing audit has a cousin in an older debate, and recognising the kinship equips the reader to predict where the audit's difficulties will concentrate. The socialist calculation debate of the twentieth century asked whether a planned economy could allocate rationally without market prices, and its participants discovered that the hard problem was not arithmetic but information: the knowledge of what is scarce, what is wanted, and what things cost one another, dispersed through a society and expressed, under markets, in a unit nobody designed. The ecological version of the problem is structurally similar and materially harder. A bounded society must know its throughput in physical units, must know the state of the stocks and sinks its production draws on, and must compare claims denominated in needs against capacities denominated in matter and energy, two vocabularies with no natural exchange rate between them. The tradition's critics in the calculation debate doubted that any administration could gather the first kind of knowledge; its ecological critics can doubt, with better evidence, that any administration has yet gathered the second. The replies available also parallel the older debate's. Where the market socialists proposed simulated prices, the ecological literature proposes physical indicators, budgets of carbon and materials administered as quotas, dashboards of boundary variables reviewed by assemblies. Where the older debate's defenders of planning pointed to the wartime economies' ability to mobilise by physical targets, the ecological defenders point to the same episodes as proof that societies can steer by quantities when they decide to. The kinship is illuminating in both directions. It suggests that the audit's completion depends less on philosophy than on the unglamorous development of measurement institutions, a task at which the tradition's historical record is mixed and its theory largely silent. And it suggests that the abundance objection, in its administrative form, will be answered if it is answered at all by practitioners of accounting rather than by interpreters of texts, a prospect the textual scholars who dominate the debate have been slow to welcome. The article welcomes it on their behalf: the destination will be audited by whoever builds the instruments, and the tradition's choice is whether its own people build them or receive them from outside as a verdict.

One further consequence of the kinship deserves statement, because it reframes the agent problem in a way the literature has begun to explore. The bearer of a bounded programme, on the administrative view, is not only a class or a movement but a competence: the stratum that can run the measurement, staff the assemblies that deliberate the schedule, and maintain the infrastructures a bounded metabolism requires. The tradition's classical sociology has no ready category for a bearer defined by competence, and its suspicion of technocracy, well grounded in its critique of bureaucracy, makes it reluctant to invent one. Yet every serious sketch of the bounded society smuggles the competent stratum in through a side door, as planners, as indicator keepers, as the federation's accountants of matter. Naming the smuggling is a precondition of governing it, and the failure to name it is one more entry in the ledger of unfinished work, this time on the fifth row. The agent question, pursued honestly, thus divides into two: who wants the bounded programme, and who can run it. The tradition has candidate answers to the first and silence on the second, and the silence is the more dangerous, since a programme whose runners are unexamined will be run, in the event, by whoever volunteers, with the consequences the tradition's own analysis of bureaucracy predicts. The article enters the division into the record as its final analytical contribution, and turns to its closing assembly.


## Time as wealth: the destination's most durable intuition

If the destination must be reimagined under bounded conditions, the material for the reimagining will come disproportionately from one intuition that runs through the corpus and survives every audit so far applied to it: the intuition that wealth, properly understood, is time. The Grundrisse sketches an economy in which the measure of wealth ceases to be labour time and becomes disposable time, the hours released from necessary production for the development of the individual's capacities. The realm of freedom discussion in the third volume builds its architecture on the same intuition, locating freedom beyond the sphere of necessity and making a shortened working day its basic prerequisite. Even the Gotha passage, for all its springs of wealth, defines the higher phase partly by labour's transformation into life's prime want, a change in the experience of time as much as in the volume of goods. The ecological promise of this intuition is considerable. A society that measures its advance in released time rather than in product can continue to advance, by its own measure, at a constant or even a contracting throughput, converting every gain in efficiency into leisure rather than into output. The history of the wealthy economies shows the opposite conversion as the norm, efficiency gains harvested as more goods and more services with working time nearly stationary, a conversion the framework's analysis explains by accumulation's compulsion: capital must expand the product because its measure is value, not time. Under common ownership, the conversion could in principle be chosen the other way, and the choice would reconcile the destination's spirit with the boundary's letter in a manner no other reply quite achieves, since it changes the destination's metric rather than merely its level.

The article's discipline requires the difficulties to be stated with the same care as the promise, and they are three. First, the conversion to time presupposes that necessary labour can be contracted by efficiency at the required rate, and necessary labour includes the labour of maintaining the material basis itself, the repair of infrastructures, the tending of soils and systems, the care of the young, the old, and the ill, much of which resists the productivity gains that machinery brought to manufacturing; the realm of necessity has a floor set by care's temporality, and the floor's height under bounded conditions is another unaudited magnitude. Second, disposable time is not automatically ecologically innocent: free hours are filled, and a society could spend its released time on material consumption as readily as on development, so the time metric reconciles the destination with the boundary only if the culture of free time is itself formed toward light activities, which brings the argument back to the formation of needs and its unwritten institutional sequel. Third, the time intuition, for all its textual depth, was in the corpus a consequence of the productive forces' development rather than a substitute for it: disposable time was to be created by machinery's advance, and the advance's material basis returns the discussion to throughput by the back door. The intuition is thus best valued as the destination's most promising line of revision, the place where a restated higher phase would begin, rather than as a proof that no revision is needed. A restatement built on time would say: the higher phase is the condition in which the social product, held within its boundaries, is converted preferentially into free time and into the collective goods distributed by need, with contribution accounting retired first from the domains where need is plainest. That sentence is describable, attractive, and recognisably descended from the founding texts. It is also, word for word, a revision, and the article's standing demand is that revisions travel under their own name.

## The philosophical stake: what a need is, and who may say

Beneath the costing and the politics lies a philosophical question that the debate keeps approaching and retreating from, and a complete treatment must at least mark its depth. Distribution according to need presupposes that needs can be known: stated, compared, and recognised as claims, by a procedure the claimants accept as legitimate. The philosophy of needs, developed largely outside this tradition in the later twentieth century, distinguishes needs from wants by their relation to harm and to the conditions of a recognisably human life, and it has wrestled with the authority problem the distinction creates: if needs are objective, someone must be authorised to specify them, and the specification is a power; if needs are whatever people sincerely report, the schedule has no brake and the abundance problem returns in its purest form. The tradition's own resources for this problem are thinner than its resources for the critique of capitalism, a thinness its ecological writers have noted with some embarrassment. The corpus treats needs historically and critically, exposing manufactured wants and the poverty of a society that multiplies desires while stunting capacities, but it offers no procedure for certifying the genuine schedule its destination would distribute by, because its author expected the material condition to make certification unnecessary: where the springs flow abundantly, the schedule can be generous without being exact. Remove the abundance, and the certification problem becomes the destination's central institution, the place where its politics will actually live. The abundance objection, followed all the way down, thus terminates in a question about authority over the definition of need, and the tradition's silence at that depth is the objection's final piece of evidence. A doctrine that must, under bounded conditions, empower someone to say which needs count has to reconcile that empowerment with its critique of every previous authority that classified human requirements, from the priest to the bureaucrat. The reconciliation is conceivable; democratic specification under public argument is the sketch most often offered. It is also, once more, a sketch. The article's method ends where it began, with the distinction between a sketch and an account, and with the verdict that the account, at every level from the costing to the philosophy, remains to be written.

## What to watch: the debate's next phase

A reference article that has insisted on the difference between a question's statement and its settlement owes its readers a guide to recognising the settlement if it arrives. Four developments would signal that the abundance objection is being answered rather than restated. The appearance, in the tradition's own journals, of a needs schedule for the higher phase definite enough to be costed, defended against the authority problem rather than merely asserted, would be the first. A costing exercise conducted jointly by scholars of the tradition and ecological economists, with the boundary literature engaged at its own level of rigour, would be the second. An owned restatement of the destination by a significant current of the movement, banners rewritten in words, would be the third, and it would count as an answer by concession, the objection having forced the revision it demanded. A sociology of the bounded programme's bearer that names both the wanting constituency and the competent stratum, and confronts the bureaucracy problem the tradition's own theory predicts, would be the fourth. Absent those developments, readers should expect the debate to continue in its established rhythm: the recovery literature and Burkett's reply consolidated in the textbooks, the abundance objection restated at intervals by new internal critics, the programmatic literatures of degrowth and ecomodernism pressing their wagers, and the ledger's grades holding. The article's own shelf life is tied to that expectation, and it is written to age gracefully under either outcome: if the settlement arrives, the ledger will stand as the record of the position before it; if it does not, the ledger remains the position. Either way, the reader who has absorbed its distinctions will not mistake the next decade's restatements for news.


## Other destinations, other audits: the comparative light

The article has kept its focus narrow by design, since its assignment is one criticism of one tradition. A brief comparative glance at the end is nevertheless warranted, because it locates the abundance objection in the general history of doctrines meeting the physical sciences, and the location is clarifying for both camps. The liberal tradition's classical destination, a commercial society of indefinite improvement, carried its own abundance presupposition, and its audit arrived in the form of the stationary state debate, in which Mill, to his credit, welcomed the stationary state as a condition for moral improvement a century before the ecological sciences gave the idea its modern form. The audit did not destroy the liberal inheritance; it divided it, into currents that absorbed the stationary prospect and currents that defined themselves against it. The Keynesian settlement of the twentieth century rested on growth as the solvent of distributive conflict, the presupposition that a growing product lets every claim be met without any claimant losing, and the ecological audit of that settlement is the degrowth debate's other parent, since the politics of a non growing product is precisely the politics the settlement was designed to avoid. Religious eschatologies, by contrast, carry destinations explicitly independent of material conditions, located outside the order whose limits the sciences describe, and are correspondingly immune to the audit, at the price of the destination's worldly irrelevance. The Marxian destination occupies the exposed middle position: worldly enough to be audited, central enough that the audit matters. The comparative glance yields the article's most general sentence on its subject. A doctrine's vulnerability to the ecological audit is proportional to the worldliness of its promises, and the traditions that promised the most transformation in this world have the most auditing to do. That Marxism's audit is furthest advanced, and conducted most by its own adherents, follows from the same proportion: it promised the most, in the most material terms, with the most exacting method. The finding is not a consolation prize; it is a structural fact about the debate that any fair summary should carry.

The comparison also disciplines the critic's triumphalism, where it appears. A critic from a tradition whose destination was never worldly enough to audit, or whose growth presupposition is still unexamined in its own house, indicts the Marxian destination from a glass house, and the literature contains such performances in number. The internal critics are free of the taint, which is another reason the article has sourced the objection to them wherever possible. Their standing to press the audit comes from having submitted to it: the ecological Marxists who state the abundance objection most sharply are the same writers who have revised their own expectations under the findings, abandoned the tonnage rhetoric, and accepted the political costs of the boundary inside their own movement. Criticism with that biography behind it cannot be answered by pointing at anyone's glass house. It can only be answered by the audit. The article closes its analytical sections by returning the reader to that single practical conclusion, from which all the grading follows and to which all the sections have been converging: produce the schedule, perform the costing, own the consequence. Until then, the ledger stands as written, and the objection stands with it.

## Why this article grades rather than condemns or absolves

A methodological confession belongs at the end, since the article's form, a ledger of graded verdicts, is itself a position in the debate and should be defended as one. The alternatives were available. The article could have been written as a prosecution, assembling the criticism into a verdict on the tradition entire, or as a defence, assembling the replies into an acquittal. Both forms are common in the literature this article surveys, and both share a defect the series exists to correct: they treat a large doctrine as a single defendant, when the evidence concerns distinguishable components with distinguishable fortunes. Grading is slower and less stirring than verdicts on wholes. It is also the only form that tells the reader something portable, since a reader who knows which components stand and which have fallen can reason about new cases, new programmes, and new summaries without waiting for the next polemic. The graded form has a further virtue specific to this subject. The ecological debate's pathology, documented throughout, is the export of verdicts across components: acquittal on value smuggled into acquittal on abundance, conviction on the record smuggled into conviction on the categories. A ledger is a machine for preventing smuggling, since each row must carry its own evidence and its own grade, and the rows cannot lend one another their verdicts without the transfer showing. The article commends the machine to the debate's future participants, including its own critics: a reply that grades this article's grades, row by row, will have engaged it; a reply that pronounces on its tendency will not have. With the method confessed and the machine handed over, the article turns to its three standing audiences, in the series' customary order, and then to the questions by which the whole subject is most often searched.


## The Gotha clauses read one by one

The destination passage rewards the slowest reading the article can give it, since the whole debate turns on a handful of clauses and the clauses are usually quoted as a single breath. Take them in sequence. The first condition is the vanishing of the enslaving subordination of the individual to labour's division. The condition is social and organisational: it describes a reordering of work, not a quantity of product, and a bounded world obstructs it not at all, since varied and self directed activity is compatible with any throughput. The second condition is the disappearance of the mental and physical labour antithesis, again a condition about the organisation of activity and its prestige, costing nothing in matter. The third condition, labour's becoming life's prime want, is psychological and cultural: it describes a population for whom activity is fulfilment rather than toll, and it is the condition on which the transformation reading builds, since a population meeting its prime want in production presents a different schedule of consumption needs. Only the fourth condition, the abundant flow of common wealth's springs alongside the individual's all round development, is a condition about the material product itself. The clause by clause reading thus refines the objection to its sharpest form: of the passage's four conditions, three are compatible with bounded conditions on their face, and the destination's difficulty is concentrated in a single clause. Concentration is not dissolution. The fourth clause is not decorative; it is the condition that dates the distributive principle, the point at which the passage says the banner may be inscribed. A destination three of whose four conditions a bounded world permits, and whose fourth it forbids, is a destination that cannot arrive as described, however much of it can. The precision matters because it tells the revision where to work: the revision does not need to rebuild the destination's account of work, of the division of labour, or of human development, all of which survive the boundary intact. It needs to replace one clause, the material condition of the distributive transition, with a condition a bounded world can meet, and to show that the distributive principle survives the replacement. That is a far more tractable assignment than the wholesale reconstruction the polemical versions imagine, and stating it exactly is the article's gift to whichever scholar undertakes it.

The clause by clause reading also explains a feature of the debate that puzzles newcomers: why defenders and critics can quote the same paragraph at each other with equal confidence and equal textual warrant. They are quoting different clauses. The defender quotes the transformation clauses, which are genuine and central; the critic quotes the springs clause, which is genuine and decisive for the distributive question; and the paragraph, read as a breath, seems to vindicate both. Reading it in sequence dissolves the standoff into an allocation of work: the transformation clauses describe what the destination's producers are like, the springs clause describes what the destination's world must supply, and the audit concerns the supply. A seminar that performs the sequential reading once will not again be able to hear the paragraph as music, and the loss of the music is the gain of the analysis. Examiners know the difference between the two hearings and reward the second, which is why this article has laboured the point at a length its simplicity might seem to excuse.

## The two phases in the doctrine that followed

The schema of two phases had a long doctrinal afterlife, and the afterlife matters to the objection because it fixed the destination's description in the movement's catechism at exactly the point the objection audits. Later doctrine named the phases, assigned the first to a long epoch of transition with its own laws, and treated the second as the horizon that justified the first's disciplines. In that codification, the higher phase's conditions receded from examination: a horizon does not get audited, since auditing is for the road, and the springs clause survived as liturgy, recited at the points where the doctrine described its ultimate aim and questioned nowhere. The ecological critics inside the tradition are, among other things, scholars who read the liturgy as a text again, and their offence, in the eyes of the codified doctrine's custodians, was the offence of treating a horizon as a claim. The offence is the method's essence, and the article sides with the readers. A claim does not cease to be a claim by being promoted to a horizon; it merely ceases to be checked, and the history of this debate is the history of the check being resumed. The resumption is incomplete, as the ledger records, because the codification's habits persist in the summary literature and in the movement's self description, where the higher phase still functions as the unexamined warrant for present arrangements. Every time the destination is invoked to justify a discipline, the audit's absence does practical work, and the objection ceases to be a seminar matter. It becomes a question about the honesty of the warrant. That is the final reason the article has graded the component as it has and stated the grading without varnish: the clause is load bearing in the living doctrine, not an antiquarian curiosity, and loads are audited or they fail.


## Care, repair, and the labour a bounded society would expand

One concrete picture helps the abstract argument land, and the picture has the merit of drawing the article's threads together. A society steering its metabolism inside a boundary does not merely contract; it reallocates. The activities that grow in such a society are disproportionately those whose product is a maintained condition rather than a new object: care of children, of the ill, of the old; repair and maintenance of the built stock; restoration of degraded land and water; teaching; the arts of conviviality and culture. These activities share properties that matter to every row of the ledger. Their throughput per unit of human satisfaction is low, which speaks to the abundance row, since a needs schedule weighted toward maintained conditions costs less matter than one weighted toward accumulated objects. Their labour resists the productivity acceleration that manufacturing underwent, which speaks to the time discussion, since the realm of necessity in a care weighted economy has a high floor of hours and the realm of freedom expands slowly there. Their workforce is the population the agent row seeks, workers whose material interest lies in the bounded programme's expansion rather than in the heavy sectors' defence, which speaks to the fifth row with the first concrete bearer the debate has produced. And their product is largely distributed, in the societies that have them, through the common funds and by need, which speaks to the deductions discussion, since care and education are the historical beachhead of the needs principle inside bounded products. The picture is not a programme and this article does not offer it as one. It is a diagnostic instrument: a reader who wants to test any proposed answer to the abundance objection can ask how the proposal treats the care and repair economy, since a proposal that cannot say where maintained conditions come from, who staffs them, and how their hours are counted has not reached the level at which the objection operates. The serious literatures increasingly pass the test; the degrowth programme is substantially a proposal to reallocate toward exactly these activities, and the feminist economics of social reproduction, treated elsewhere in this series, supplies the analysis of the labour involved. The convergence of the ecological and the social reproduction literatures on the same activities, from opposite directions, is one of the field's most encouraging developments, and the exam guide maps it for candidates. For this article's purposes the convergence is entered as the strongest available hint about the shape an eventual answer would take: a destination restated around maintained conditions, staffed by the care workforce, costed in hours as much as in tonnes, and honest that its banner names a schedule deliberated under a boundary rather than a spring flowing without end.


## The objection and the replies, weighed together one last time

Before the article addresses its three audiences, the whole exchange deserves one consolidated weighing, in which the objection and the replies are set side by side at their strongest and the balance is struck without the shelter of sequence. The objection, at its strongest, is a conditional with an undisputed antecedent text and a bracketed scientific premise: if durable aggregate limits bind material throughput, then the condition the founding text attaches to needs based distribution cannot be guaranteed by development, and the destination, as described, cannot be relied upon to arrive. The replies, at their strongest, are three reinterpretations and one wager. Sufficiency relative to need weakens the condition the destination must meet, at the price of an account of needs nobody has supplied and a politics of the schedule nobody has designed. The transformation of labour weakens the demand the destination generates, at the price of leaving the physical floor of provision uncosted. The conversion of efficiency into free time changes the destination's metric, at the price of being a revision that its proponents rarely announce as one. Decoupling wagers that the scientific premise will soften at exactly the point the destination needs it to, at the price of resting a doctrine's eschatology on an empirical bet its own method would audit in any rival. Weighed together, the replies reduce the objection's scope considerably: the destination they collectively describe, needs oriented, time rich, care weighted, deliberated at its margins, is a far more defensible inheritance than the tonnage eschatology of the movement's official period, and a world that adopted their combined programme would have travelled most of the distance the objection demands. What the weighing cannot honestly report is arrival. Each reply ends in a promissory note, and the notes are drawn on different banks: a philosophy of needs, a physical costing, an institutional design, an empirical wager. The objection stands until at least the first three are honoured, because the destination needs its schedule, its costing, and its politics together, and a tradition that has produced the notes should be the last to mistake them for the payment. That is the balance, struck as exactly as the article can strike it, and it is the position the ledger's third row encodes in a single word.

There is a final consideration of intellectual honour that belongs in the weighing, addressed to the tradition's defenders rather than to its critics. The defenders' strongest asset in this debate has never been any single reply; it is the analysis of capitalism itself, whose ecological power the critics concede and whose explanatory reach no rival framework matches. That asset is not diminished by conceding the destination's audit is missing. It is diminished, visibly and repeatedly, by the defensive performances that deny the audit is missing, that answer the abundance objection with the soil passages, that treat the question as settled by victories won on other rows of the ledger. Every such performance teaches the audience that the tradition's custodians cannot tell its strengths from its weaknesses, and an audience that learns that lesson discounts the strengths along with the weaknesses. The internal critics, by contrast, enhance the asset they criticise beside, since their precision about the destination certifies their testimony about the analysis. A movement that wants its analysis believed should publish its ledger, open file and all. This article has published the file for one cluster of the doctrine, in the belief that the example generalises. The persona sections that follow translate the file for the readers who will use it in examinations, in seminars, and in print.

One sentence of orientation should accompany the file on its way to those readers, because the file's most important entry is the one most likely to be misreported. To say that the abundance objection is unanswered is not to say that it is unanswerable, and it is not to say that the tradition is finished, ecologically or otherwise. Unanswered is a statement about the literature at a date, and literatures change: the value objection was unanswered for a decade before 1999, and the blind spot stood unqualified for a similar span, and both now carry grades that record serious work done. The abundance objection's present grade records work not yet done, by scholars many of whom are already trained and reading the same journals in which the objection appears. Nothing in the ledger predicts whether the answer, when it comes, will arrive as a vindication of the destination, as its owned restatement, or as the discovery that the destination was the first phase all along wearing the higher phase's banner. Each outcome would be a legitimate close to the question, and the article's neutrality consists precisely in refusing to prefer one before the evidence exists. What the article does prefer, openly and as its only advocacy, is the asking: that the question be put in its sharpest form, in the tradition's own venues, by readers equipped with the ledger, until the literature is forced to move from restatement to settlement. A question that has waited in a famous paragraph for well over a century can wait a little longer to be answered well. It should not wait to be asked properly, and asking it properly is the whole of what this article has attempted.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination risk on this topic is compression: the syllabus tempts you to deliver one verdict on the ecological criticism, and the marks live in refusing it. Learn the ledger as your answer's skeleton. Open by separating the value charge from the destination charge and naming Burkett's 1999 reply as the answer to the first, with the value and wealth distinction doing the work. Give Benton's 1989 blind spot argument its date, its target in the labour process concept, and its graded verdict, partly lands, with the production time distinction as the corpus's internal qualification. Then spend the answer's centre where the marks are densest: the Gotha passage read clause by clause, the springs condition isolated, the sufficiency and transformation readings stated fairly and marked as unfinished, and the verdict that the abundance objection stands unanswered. Never assert a scientific measurement, never let the recovery literature testify about the destination, and keep every claim conditional where the science is concerned. The cluster's [ecological marxism exam answers](/2017/11/15/ecological-marxism-exam-answers/) trains exactly this structure against specimen questions, and the VaultBook companion card for this article reproduces the ledger of charges and verdicts in revision form, so that the five rows and their grades can be rehearsed until the separations are automatic under time pressure.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The abundance objection is the strongest evaluative point available on this topic, and a seminar built around it teaches more than the subject: it teaches students how doctrines carry presuppositions and how presuppositions are audited. Stage the session in the article's order. Begin with the productivism charge in its crude form and let the group discover, through the value and wealth distinction, why the crude form fails. Introduce Benton next and require the strongest statement of the blind spot argument before any reply is admitted, since students who steelman first argue better afterward. Reserve the final third for the Gotha passage, distributed in advance and read aloud clause by clause, and set the exercise the literature has avoided: each student drafts the needs schedule the higher phase would require, then costs one item on it in physical terms with the ecological economics readings alongside. The exercise reliably converts a polemical seminar into an analytical one, because the difficulty of the assignment is the objection's content. Close by returning the ledger and asking which grade the group would change and on what evidence, a question that sends students back into the texts with a purpose. The record component should be handled under the discipline of its own article: stated plainly, never used as an offset or a shortcut.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this subject is the missing audit, and its shape is now specific enough to organise projects around. Textual work remains to be done on the destination's clauses across the corpus's periods, particularly the relation between the Grundrisse's disposable time and the Gotha conditions, where a careful genealogy would clarify what a restatement may claim as ancestry. Conceptual work is owed on the successor to the labour process concept, where Benton's reconstruction programme stalled short of a teachable result. The largest gap is interdisciplinary: no scholar has yet costed a schedule of needs for the higher phase against the boundary literatures with both sides' methods respected, and the project requires a collaboration between historians of the tradition together with ecological economists that neither field's institutions reward, which is itself a finding about why the audit is missing. Journalists covering the degrowth dispute should note that it is this article's third and fifth rows in organisational form, and that the parties' statements grade exactly as the ledger grades their theoretical originals; the companion treatment of [degrowth and marxism](/2017/09/01/degrowth-and-marxism/) follows the attempt at the length it deserves. Historians of the record should maintain the separation this article has observed, since the conflation of record with theory remains the summary literature's chief defect and the easiest error for a deadline to commit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Can Marxism handle ecological limits?

Branch level limits, yes: depletion, soil degradation, and scarcity within a sector enter the analysis through existing categories of cost, rent, and competition, and the framework demonstrably registers them. Aggregate limits are another matter. The framework can describe a boundary on total throughput without any conceptual strain. What it has yet to show is that its destination, the higher phase distributing according to need, survives such a boundary, because that destination was premised on abundant flow. The honest answer is therefore divided: the analysis handles limits well, the destination's compatibility with them is unproven, and the unproven point is the one the tradition's own ecological critics press hardest.

### Q: What is the epistemological blind spot argument?

The argument, advanced by Ted Benton in a much cited essay of 1989 on Marxism and natural limits, holds that the labour process notion is built around intentional transformation: a purpose conceived in advance and imposed on passive material. That structure fits manufacturing and misfits the eco-regulatory processes, agriculture above all, where success depends on setting and tending the conditions within which natural processes unfold on their own timetable. The blind spot is conceptual rather than moral: not indifference to nature, but a conceptual scheme in which a whole class of relations to nature is difficult to think. Later scholarship qualified it with the corpus's own attention to natural timetables, and reconstruction was attempted from within.

### Q: Does communism presuppose abundance?

On the founding text, the higher phase does. The Critique of the Gotha Programme makes distribution according to need conditional on a set of changes that include the springs of co-operative wealth flowing more abundantly, and the earlier German Ideology argues that without developed production want is simply generalised and the struggle over necessities resumes. Defenders reply that the abundance required may be sufficiency relative to historically formed needs rather than unlimited output, and that the transformation of labour changes what producers need from consumption. Both readings are genuine and neither has yet been worked into a settled account, so the presupposition stands in the text with its reinterpretations acknowledged as unfinished.

### Q: Is nature analytically invisible in Marx's economics?

In capital's accounts, yes, and that is the theory's point rather than its defect. Value measures labour time alone, so nature's contribution to wealth registers nowhere in the magnitudes a market economy keeps, and capital consequently treats natural conditions as a free gift and depletes what it pays nothing to restore. The theory analysing those accounts sees the invisibility and explains it: nature and labour are joint sources of use value and wealth, while value is labour's social form alone. Paul Burkett's reconstruction of 1999 made this reply systematic, and the strong form of the invisibility charge has not survived it among scholars who engaged the exchange.

### Q: How does Burkett answer the value objection?

By reconstruction rather than anthology. In Marx and Nature, the 1999 study, Paul Burkett showed that the separation of value from wealth runs through the entire critique of political economy: value is a social form measuring labour, wealth is material usefulness to which nature contributes as much as labour does. Capitalist accounting tracks only the first, so nature's invisibility is a property of the object under analysis, and the theory is the explanation of that property, not another instance of it. He supported the architecture with the corpus's treatments of rent, of the working day's natural limits, and of soil exhaustion, and conceded the tradition's later productivist rhetoric while denying that the rhetoric convicts the categories.

### Q: Is the realm of freedom compatible with limits?

The passage in Capital's third volume describes freedom within necessity as the associated producers regulating, rationally and in common, their interchange with nature under common control, and places the true realm of freedom beyond material production, with the working day's contraction as its prerequisite. That vocabulary is compatible with constraint, since regulation is exactly what a bounded society would practise. Compatibility of vocabulary is not, however, a demonstration: the passage does not ask what happens when the sustainable level of the interchange falls below the level the destination's distributive principle requires, and it assumes the productive development that the boundary interrogates. The passage gives defenders their best language and leaves their arithmetic undone.

### Q: Is the Marxist political base productivist?

Historically, substantially yes, and the point is sociological rather than textual. The tradition's organised strength lay in mining, steel, energy, transport, and heavy manufacturing, and the unions and parties built there defended their sectors' expansion as the condition of livelihood and community survival. A programme of restraint therefore faces a bearer whose material interests were formed against it. Replies point to the changed composition of labour, toward care, education, repair, and public services, whose workers' interests align differently, and to movements organised outside the classic structures. Those replies sketch a possible bearer without yet identifying an organised force possessing the durability the theory of agency demands, so the component is graded as landing.

### Q: What is the strongest version of the ecological criticism?

The abundance objection. The higher phase is defined by the possibility of distribution according to need without the constraints that make distribution according to contribution necessary, and that possibility is stated in the founding text as conditional on material plenty. Ecological limits, if the findings of the relevant sciences hold, directly contradict the condition. The objection is strongest because it survives the answers that defeated the criticism's other forms: it grants the value reply, grants the textual recovery, and asks only whether the destination's presupposition can still be supplied. Its proponents include ecological Marxists arguing from inside the tradition, and it has received reinterpretations but no settled answer.

### Q: Has the tradition answered the abundance problem?

No, and the article states that plainly because the literature's condition permits no softer wording. Two serious reinterpretations exist. The sufficiency reading holds that the destination requires abundance relative to historically formed needs, not unlimited output, but it lacks the independent account of needs that would keep it from circularity. The transformation reading holds that labour become life's prime want changes the composition of demand toward light activities, but it does not cost the physical floor of provision. Together they form a promising research programme. A programme is not an answer, and no book length treatment has yet performed the audit of schedule, costing, and comparison that an answer requires.

### Q: Is this question settled?

In its parts, unevenly. The ecological charge in its value form is settled: answered in 1999 and retired from serious use. The blind spot argument is settled to a graded verdict, a genuine conceptual weakness, localised and partly repaired. The historical record is a matter for historiography, grave in content and limited in what it proves about categories. The abundance question and the agent question are not settled at all, and they are the questions that concern the tradition's future rather than its past. A reader who reports the whole subject as settled, in either direction, has compressed five verdicts into one and should be asked which component the compression is hiding.

### Q: How does the abundance premise locate the real difficulty for the tradition?

By relocating it from the analysis to the destination. The famous ecological quarrels concerned the theory of value, the labour process concept, and the founder's attitude to nature, and on that terrain the tradition's position proved strong: its analysis explains why capitalism degrades nature better than most rivals explain anything. The abundance premise sits in the one place those victories do not reach, the material condition attached to the higher phase's distributive principle. A framework can therefore be vindicated as an account of the existing order and unexamined as an account of its successor at the same time, and the difficulty's location explains why decades of successful defence left it untouched.

### Q: What should a student separate between the textual recovery and the abundance objection?

Three things: the questions, the evidence, and the verdicts. The recovery answers a documentary question, what Marx wrote and thought about nature, with documents, and the answer is rich and favourable. The abundance objection raises a theoretical question, whether the destination's material presupposition can be met under bounded conditions, and documents about soil, forestry, or metabolism cannot answer it because its terms are needs, throughput, and a distributive principle. Students who let the recovery testify about the destination repeat the debate's commonest error and will be caught by any examiner who separates the components. Keep the recovery for the textual question, the value reply for the categorical question, and the Gotha audit for the destination question.

### Q: Why does the higher phase raise the hardest problem?

Because it is the one component of the framework whose defence cannot proceed by scholarship. The value charge fell to a distinction recovered from the texts; the blind spot charge fell partly to passages the critics had underweighted. The higher phase's difficulty is not a missing passage but a present one, the springs clause, whose wording is undisputed and whose condition is material. Meeting it requires either a new account of needs, a physical costing no one has performed, or an owned revision of the destination, each of which is work of a different order from exegesis. Problems that cannot be solved by reading are the hardest problems a text based tradition faces, and this is the ecological debate's instance.

### Q: How does the charge and verdict ledger order the critique?

By refusing to let the components borrow one another's verdicts. The ledger lists five components, the value invisibility charge, the blind spot argument, the abundance presupposition, the historical record, and the agent's interests, each with its strongest proponent and the reply it has received, and a grade of lands, partly lands, or answered. The ordering reveals the pattern that narrative summaries hide: the components concerning the analysis of capitalism are answered or nearly so, while the components concerning the future, the destination and its bearer, are the ones that stand. A reader can lift the table out of the article and use it to inspect any summary of the debate, asking which row a given claim belongs to and whether its grade matches the evidence.

### Q: What separates a limit the framework can absorb from one it cannot?

The level at which the limit binds. A limit binding a branch of production, a depleting seam, an exhausted fishery, a degrading soil, registers inside the analysis through cost, rent, and competition, and the framework absorbs it as it absorbs any change in production's conditions. A limit binding the aggregate metabolism, a boundary on total throughput or on the sinks that receive production's residues, binds the destination itself, because the destination's distributive principle was premised on the metabolism's expansion. Absorption at that level requires the missing audit, a needs schedule costed against the boundary, and possibly an owned restatement of what the higher phase promises. The separation is the article's final distinction, and it converts a slogan about limits into a researchable question.
