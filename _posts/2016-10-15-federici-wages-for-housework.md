Silvia Federici is an Italian-American feminist theorist formed in the autonomist current of Marxism, and her reputation rests on two contributions that this profile keeps separate because they stand or fall separately. The first is a political campaign of the nineteen seventies, wages for housework, which she helped to build on two continents and whose logic is persistently misread as a proposal to pay housewives. The second is a historical thesis, set out in her book Caliban and the Witch of 2004, which argues that the early modern persecution of women as witches belonged to the process by which capitalism took control of reproduction. Both contributions are contested, and the contests are of different kinds: fellow feminists disputed the campaign's demand at the time, on grounds this profile states from named critics, while historians of the early modern period dispute the book's chronology, geography, and casualty scale on evidence that can be checked in the trial records.

A reader who meets her name on a reading list usually meets it without either contest attached, and that omission does real damage in both directions. Detached from its political logic, the wage demand sounds like a scheme for pensioning domesticity, which is close to the opposite of what its authors designed it to do. Detached from the specialist literature, the witch hunt thesis circulates as settled history, complete with a casualty figure that descends from a discredited eighteenth-century extrapolation rather than from the archives. This profile therefore proceeds in a deliberate order. It expounds the demand first, through the pamphlet in which Federici stated its logic most sharply, and gives the objection to the demand at full strength before turning to the history. It then expounds the historical thesis at length, component by component, and gives the historians' objections at equal length, attributed to the scholars and the books in which the findings appear. What survives each contest is assessed only after both sides are on the page.

Three terms recur throughout and are treated in depth in their own articles in the series, so they are linked rather than re-derived here. The historical thesis is an extension of the account of primitive accumulation, the violent separation of producers from the means of subsistence with which Marx closed the first volume of Capital. The campaign's theoretical claim, that housework reproduces the worker and so belongs inside the analysis of capital rather than outside it, belongs to the wider argument about social reproduction theory, where Federici's version is one position among several and not the consensus one. And the technical question underneath the campaign, whether domestic labour produces value, was fought out in the domestic labour debate, whose papers supply the vocabulary in which her pamphlet argues. The profile's own business is narrower: one thinker's formation, one demand and its logic, one book and its evidence.

One discipline governs the judgements that follow, and stating it in advance is a protection for the reader as much as for the subject. Claims about Federici's work are sourced in this profile to the work itself: to the pamphlet for the campaign's logic, to the book for the historical thesis, to the essays for the concepts. Claims about the world the work describes are sourced to the literatures that study that world, which is why the historians appear with their books in their hands rather than as a chorus. No evaluative claim is made about Federici as a person; the profile has no access to her person and no business with it. What can be graded, and is graded, is the design of a demand and the evidence for a thesis, and those two gradings use different instruments because the objects are different kinds of thing.

## Federici in profile: an autonomist formation

### Why does the profile begin with a campaign rather than a book?

Because her theory was built inside organising and cannot be read off the page alone. Federici co-founded a feminist collective in Italy in 1972 and a campaign committee in New York the year after, and the pamphlet that made her name was written as an intervention in a live dispute among feminists. The book came three decades later and grows out of the campaign's questions.

The order matters for a further reason. Profiles of thinkers usually move from biography to bibliography, as though the writing explained itself once the life is known, and that habit is especially misleading in this case. The 1975 pamphlet is not a youthful sketch of positions the mature historian later filled in. It is the sharper of the two major works, the one whose central argument can be stated in a sentence and tested against everything she wrote afterwards: that a demand can be built to expose a relation rather than to settle a grievance, and that wages for housework was built to expose the relation between capital and the unwaged work of women. Readers who start with the history book and work backwards routinely mistake the campaign for an eccentric prelude. Readers who start with the campaign see the book for what it is, an attempt to give the campaign's central category, the control of reproduction, a four-century pedigree.

Her formation explains the design. Federici was born in Parma in 1942 and came to the United States in 1967 to pursue a doctorate at Buffalo, which placed her inside two political worlds at once. Italy in those years was the site of the workerist current known as operaismo, whose theorists had rebuilt Marxist politics around the wage and around refusal: the wage, on this reading, is not a fair price for labour but the form in which capital commands living labour, and the working class's power shows itself less in seizing the wage system than in refusing the work it organises. The feminists of the Italian extra-parliamentary left took that apparatus and turned it on the terrain the male workerists had left unexamined. If the wage marked the boundary of recognised work, then everything outside the wage, the cooking, cleaning, childbearing, and care performed overwhelmingly by women, stood in a peculiar relation to capital: necessary to it, invisible to it, and paid for by nobody. Mariarosa Dalla Costa, working in Padua within the orbit of Potere Operaio, drew the conclusion in an essay of 1972 written with Selma James, arguing that housework produces not merely meals and clean rooms but labour power itself, the one commodity capital cannot do without. Federici's part in that milieu was organisational as much as authorial. In 1972 she was among the founders of the International Feminist Collective, the body that carried the wages demand across national borders, and in 1973 she helped to found the New York Wages for Housework Committee, which anchored the campaign in the United States.

### What did the feminists of her circle take from the factory struggles?

A method rather than a programme. Italian workerism treated the wage as the terrain on which the capital relation could be made visible and contested, and treated refusal, the withdrawal of labour, as the working class's characteristic weapon. Her circle applied both moves to the household: name the unwaged work as work by demanding its wage, and let the demand's horizon be refusal of the work's present form.

Translating a factory method into a domestic one required a specific theoretical step, and it is worth pausing on because everything else in her work depends on it. The workerists had argued that the strike's power lies not in the wage it wins but in the relation it interrupts. Dalla Costa's essay argued, in parallel, that the housewife's labour is not a private service rendered to a husband but a production process whose product, refreshed and reproduced labour power, enters the factory every morning. On that account the home is not outside the economy awaiting description by it; the home is a hidden department of it, and the housewife a worker whose wage has been set at zero and disguised as a natural duty. Federici's pamphlet radicalised the step into a strategy. If the work is work, then demanding its wage is not charity and not symbolism. It is the classic move of making a relation appear by pricing it, undertaken in the knowledge that pricing it begins to dissolve the natural disguise that keeps it cheap.

The decades after the campaign gave her a second formation, in teaching and in solidarity work rather than in a party or a movement of the earlier scale. She spent part of the nineteen eighties teaching at the University of Port Harcourt in Nigeria, an experience that fed her later writing on structural adjustment and on the new enclosures imposed on African communities, and in 1991 she co-founded the Committee for Academic Freedom in Africa with the philosopher George Caffentzis. She taught for many years at Hofstra University in New York. The historical research that became Caliban and the Witch grew alongside this work rather than after it, drafted across the nineteen eighties and nineties in dialogue with the Midnight Notes collective, whose studies of enclosures old and new supplied the frame into which the witch hunts were fitted. Her later books extend the two contributions rather than replacing them: Revolution at Point Zero, published in 2012, collects the campaign essays and the later work on reproduction and the commons; Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women, of 2018, restates the historical thesis in compact form; Re-enchanting the World, also of 2018, gathers the writings on commoning; and the essays collected as Patriarchy of the Wage, in 2021, return to the campaign's central concept and give it the title.

The American half of the formation deserves the same weight as the Italian, since the campaign was built in both places and its texts bear both accents. In the United States she encountered a women's movement arguing about the relation between housework, race, and wages from inside a different history: the welfare rights movement, led substantially by Black women, was already contesting the state's management of mothers in those years, and the analysis that unwaged domestic labour is labour had an American lineage in the organising of women who had performed domestic work in other people's homes for pay. The New York committee's work sat at that crossing. Its demand was framed to refuse the division between the housewife and the welfare mother, between the woman maintained by a husband and the woman maintained, punitively, by the state, on the ground that both were paid or unpaid managers of reproduction whose labour the economy consumed and declined to count. That refusal of the division, the insistence that the demand be made for all women or it would reproduce the hierarchies among them, is one of the campaign's most distinctive positions, and it was argued for in Federici's committee work as much as in her named texts.

The African years added a third element, which the later writing wears openly. Teaching in Nigeria through the nineteen eighties coincided with the imposition of structural adjustment programmes across the continent, whose cuts to public provision transferred the costs of social reproduction, health, schooling, water, food, onto the unwaged labour of women in a form her analysis recognised immediately: an enclosure conducted by budget, driving households back onto subsistence work while the commons that had supported that work were themselves enclosed, privatised, or destroyed. The Committee for Academic Freedom in Africa, which she co-founded in 1991 with George Caffentzis, campaigned against the World Bank's remaking of African universities and published the research of African scholars resisting it. When her later books turn to the commons, to land grabs, and to what she calls the new enclosures, the Nigerian experience is the bridge: the campaign's category, the unwaged reproduction on which capital silently draws, had shown itself to her operating at the scale of whole economies under adjustment, not only at the scale of the household under the wage.

Her genre as a writer follows from these formations and should be registered before the works are graded. Federici writes interventions: pamphlets, essays for movement journals, lectures reworked into chapters, and one large book that began as a collective research project. The prose is argumentative and present tense in its concerns even when its subject is four centuries old, and its citations run to the literatures of the movements as well as of the academy. Judging the pamphlet by the standards of a monograph, or the monograph by the standards of a pamphlet, misgrades both. The profile therefore grades the 1975 text as what it is, a strategic statement whose test is the coherence and consequence of its strategy, and the 2004 text as what it is, a work of history whose test is the archive. That division of standards is not indulgence. It is the precision the brief of the series requires, and it is what allows the criticisms in this profile to be as sharp as they are: each work is being held to the test appropriate to its own claim.

The intellectual parentage of the pamphlet's strategy can be named precisely, and naming it protects the reader against a common misreading in which the refusal language sounds like rhetoric. The workerist theorist Mario Tronti, in Workers and Capital, published in 1966, had argued that the working class's refusal of work is not a reaction to capital's development but its motor: capital restructures itself in response to refusals it cannot absorb, and political strategy should therefore begin from the refusal rather than from the programme. The feminists of Federici's circle performed on this doctrine the same operation they performed on the wage analysis, extending it to the labour Tronti's factory centred account had left at home. If refusal is the motor, then the unwaged labour of reproduction, once named as labour, becomes refusable on a continental scale, and the housewife becomes a strategic figure of the first rank rather than a residual category of the transition to employment. The pamphlet's confidence, its tone of addressing not a grievance but a power, descends from that extension. So does its characteristic method, inherited from the Italian inquiry tradition associated with Romano Alquati, of beginning analysis from the experience and the composition of the workers concerned rather than from the categories of the economists: the pamphlet reads, in places, like a factory inquiry conducted in the kitchen, asking what the work consists of, who commands it, and where its refusal would bite.

A word belongs here to the collaboration that structured her intellectual life across these decades, because the books bear its mark and a profile that omitted it would misdescribe how the work was made. George Caffentzis, a philosopher of the same autonomist formation, was her partner in the Midnight Notes collective and in the Committee for Academic Freedom in Africa, and the two wrote together, over many years, on the subjects where their projects joined: on debt as an instrument of discipline, on the enclosure of knowledge in the restructured university, on the commons as the surviving form of the world the enclosures attacked. The joint work matters for reading Federici because it locates the origin of her characteristic scale. Her essays move between the kitchen and the world market without changing vocabulary, and that movement, which hostile readers take for metaphor, was the collective's deliberate method: the claim that the same logic of enclosure and unwaged extraction operates at both scales, and that an analysis which cannot travel between them has understood neither. Whether the travel is earned in the historical case is the question the objections press; that it was method and not carelessness is the biographical fact the profile records.

The later work on debt and the university, undertaken largely with Caffentzis, shows the same analytical instrument being carried to new terrain and is worth a paragraph as evidence of the formation's durability. The restructured university, in their joint essays, appears as a factory of a familiar kind: knowledge enclosed, its producers casualised, its students converted into debtors whose future labour is claimed in advance, the debt functioning as the wage's mirror image, a payment in reverse that disciplines before the work begins. The analysis of student debt as a technology of command over the young, and of the university's enclosures as a chapter in the long history this profile has been tracing, is the autonomist method operating on its authors' own workplace, and its appearance in her bibliography beside the witch hunts is not eclecticism. It is the same claim at three scales, that capital's order is built by enclosing a commons, naturalising the extraction that follows, and punishing the figures who embody the alternative; and a reader who has grasped the claim's structure in the pamphlet will recognise it in the essays on the campus without assistance. The profile's grading of the claim in its historical application stands as recorded. Its fertility as an instrument of analysis, its capacity to generate readings of institutions its author had not previously considered, is a separate property, and on the evidence of the bibliography it is the property her work most reliably displays.

The Italian formation closed in repression, and the date belongs in the profile because it explains the geography of everything after. The movement of 1977, in which the autonomist currents and a new feminist generation briefly converged in the universities and the streets of Bologna and Rome, was the last season of the world that had produced the campaign; the state's answer culminated in the mass arrests of April 1979, when the theorists of autonomia were swept into prison on charges that collapsed only after years of proceedings. Federici, by then based in the United States, watched her first formation dismantled from the country of her second, and the double position, insider to a defeated Italian movement and organiser in an American one that was itself dispersing, marks the writing of the following decades. The turn to history, the long withdrawal into the archives of the sixteenth century, is often read as a retreat from that defeat. The profile's reading is less psychological and better evidenced: the defeat posed the question the book answers, since a movement that had been broken needed to know whether the order that broke it had been built once and could be unbuilt, or was simply the weather. Caliban and the Witch is the answer of a militant who went looking for the construction site.

## The Wages for Housework campaign as it was organised

The campaign that the International Feminist Collective launched in 1972 was international in structure from the start, which distinguished it from most feminist initiatives of the period. National committees formed in Italy, the United States, Britain, Canada, and elsewhere, coordinated through the collective and through a shared set of texts, of which Dalla Costa and James's essay, published in English in 1975 as The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community, served as the founding statement. The demand itself was addressed to the state rather than to individual husbands, a choice with two grounds its proponents stated plainly. Payment by husbands would have privatised the relation, turning each household into a negotiation; payment by the state named the real beneficiary of unwaged reproduction, which on the campaign's analysis was capital as a whole, dependent on labour power it did not pay to produce. The demand was also framed from the beginning as a demand made on behalf of all women rather than of wives alone, since the analysis held that every woman's social position was set by the expectation of unwaged domestic service, in the home she kept or in the service jobs that replicated its terms for a wage too low to live on.

### Who else built the campaign, besides Federici?

Mariarosa Dalla Costa supplied its founding theoretical text, arguing in 1972 that housework produces labour power and therefore belongs inside the critique of political economy. Selma James carried the argument into English-language organising and insisted that the demand be made for every woman. Federici's distinctive contribution was the strategic statement of 1975, which explained what the demand was for.

The campaign's practical life was conferences, pamphlets, and argument inside the women's movement rather than legislative lobbying, and measuring it by bills introduced mistakes its genre. Its organisers understood themselves to be propagating a perspective: a way of seeing the wage relation whole, from the factory gate back into the kitchen, that would change what feminists took their demands to be demands upon. Within that frame the wage demand functioned the way a union's first wage claim functions in an unorganised shop. It gave women who had been told their exhaustion was a private failing a public name for what they did and a collective addressee for their refusal of its terms. The committees also took up the material edges of the analysis, campaigning around welfare rights and against the disciplining of mothers by benefit rules, where the state's management of unwaged reproduction was most directly visible.

It should be recorded, because her later admirers sometimes flatten it, that the campaign was contested inside feminism from its first years and never spoke for the movement as a whole. Many feminists heard the demand as a proposal to pension women into the kitchen, and said so in print; the sharpest statement of that objection, and Federici's reply to it, occupy a later section of this profile, since the objection and the reply between them define what the demand actually was. A second line of dispute ran through the socialist feminist theory of the period and concerned the campaign's central economic claim. Whether housework produces value, and if so in what sense, was the subject of the domestic labour debate, whose exchanges in journals through the nineteen seventies worked over Dalla Costa's claim with technical care; the series treats that debate in its own article, and the profile notes only that Federici's side of it was the maximal one. Housework, on her account, does not merely support the production of surplus value at one remove. It produces the commodity labour power, and is therefore productive labour in the strict sense, disguised by its lack of a wage. That maximal claim is the point on which her position later separates from social reproduction theory, and it matters for grading the campaign: the demand's force depended on the claim being true, or at least on its being arguable in the terms the debate had set.

The demand's universality was a considered position and one of the campaign's most argued points, worth setting out because summaries usually miss it. The wage was demanded for all women, not for wives as a category: for women in waged employment, who performed a second shift of unwaged work at home; for women on welfare, whose mothering the state already treated as a charge on public funds while denying it was work; for lesbians, whose households the movement's rhetoric often failed to picture; and the campaign's literature refused, pointedly, to separate the housewife from the prostitute, arguing that the division of women into respectable and disreputable was itself one of the mechanisms by which all women's labour was cheapened. Selma James's writings of the period, including the 1975 pamphlet Sex, Race and Class, built the wider frame: a single analysis in which the unwaged work of the home, the low waged work of the service economy, and the work of the colonised world formed one structure of command, divided by the wage into visibilities. The demand was the wedge driven into that structure at its largest joint.

The campaign's organisational texture matched the ambition imperfectly, as the texture of campaigns does. Its centres of gravity were the Padua and Milan feminists around Dalla Costa, the London Power of Women Collective with its Falling Wall Press in Bristol, and the committees in New York and other North American cities, linked by conferences, by the circulation of pamphlets in translation, and by a shared insistence that the analysis be tested in organising rather than merely published. Its journals and bulletins carried reports of welfare struggles, of strikes by women workers, of campaigns against sterilisation abuse, each read through the wages perspective. By the end of the decade the formation was dispersing: the Italian movement of which the collective had formed part was broken in the repression that followed the convulsions of 1977, the committees thinned, and the argument migrated from campaign literature into academic feminism, where the domestic labour debate carried its technical questions forward. The dispersal matters to the profile because it fixes what kind of object the campaign was: not an institution that failed to survive, but a position that was stated, fought over, and absorbed, in fragments, into the common sense of later feminism, often without attribution. Several of its theses, that care is work, that the economy runs on unwaged labour, that the wage conceals as much as it pays, are now banal in mouths that have never heard the campaign's name. That pattern, victory by absorption and defeat by caricature at once, is the background against which the lever argument should be read.

The demand's birthplace fixes its original accent. It was first raised inside Lotta Femminista, the feminist organisation formed in Padua and Mestre at the start of the nineteen seventies, in a region where the Italian movement's encounter between factory struggle and feminist revolt was at its most intense; Dalla Costa's founding essay was a product of that milieu before it was a text of the international campaign. The Veneto committees agitated the demand among the women of the industrial districts, where the double presence, waged shifts in the small factories of the region and unwaged shifts at home, was the ordinary female condition and the analysis's Exhibit A. From that base the International Feminist Collective, founded in Padua in the summer of 1972, federated the demand outward: to Florence and Rome, to London, to Toronto and Montreal, to the American committees, each national group translating the founding texts and reissuing the argument against its own welfare state and its own family law. The collective's founding meeting drew the analysis's international conclusion explicitly: since the unwaged relation was a feature of capital as such and not of Italian backwardness, the campaign against it had to be as international as its object, a claim the movement's subsequent geography partly honoured.

The perspective's reach can be gauged by one of its organisational descendants, which the summaries of the campaign rarely mention. The English Collective of Prostitutes, founded in 1975 within the campaign's London milieu, took the analysis at its word: if the division between the respectable unwaged wife and the disreputable waged prostitute was a device for cheapening both, then prostitutes were workers whose work the law criminalised precisely in its capacity as work, and their demands, decriminalisation, an end to police persecution, the right to work in safety, belonged inside the same frame as the housewife's wage. Selma James's long association with that work carried the campaign's logic into a field where its central claim, that the moral classification of women's labour is an economic instrument, could be tested against police courts rather than seminar rooms. The episode matters to a profile of Federici for a structural reason. A perspective, as distinct from a programme, proves itself by the range of cases it reorganises, and the wages perspective reorganised, within its own decade, the analysis of domestic service, of welfare, of sex work, and of the double day, which is a record few programmatic demands of the period can match. It also clarifies what the campaign's principals thought they were building: not a lobby for a benefit but an optics, a way of seeing in which the whole organisation of women's work, in bed and kitchen and factory and street, became visible as one system with one accounting trick at its centre, the trick of the wage that pays for some of it and thereby renders the rest natural.

The campaign's American interface with the welfare rights movement deserves record, because it shaped the demand's address to the state and because the encounter was not frictionless. The National Welfare Rights Organization, built from 1966 by the mothers who received Aid to Families with Dependent Children, had already established the premise the campaign needed: that the state's treatment of unwaged mothers was a labour question. Johnnie Tillmon, the organisation's leading voice, stated the premise in 1972 in terms the campaign could have adopted entire: welfare, she argued, is a women's issue, because the rules that policed recipients, the man in the house rules, the midnight inspections, the perpetual suspicion of fraud, amounted to a system for disciplining women through their motherhood. The two movements agreed on the diagnosis and divided on the instrument. Welfare rights organising fought for the adequacy and dignity of benefits as they existed, for the claimants in front of it; the wages campaign held that the benefit form itself, discretionary, stigmatised, and policed, was part of the machinery, and that only the wage form, a right attached to work recognised as work, broke the discretion. The division is instructive for the profile's grading because it repeats, inside the movement of the poorest women, the same structure as the Davis exchange: a dispute between those who would improve the existing administration of reproduction and those who would reclassify the activity the administration administers. Federici's committee work in New York moved between the two camps, and the pamphlet's silence about welfare's specifics is one of its genuine limits, a place where the lever's abstraction cost it the knowledge the claimants' movement had accumulated.

The theoretical argument underneath the campaign had its own public career in the journals, and a compressed account of it belongs here because the campaign is often credited or blamed for positions the debate refined. The opening move inside Marxist economics was Wally Seccombe's paper of 1974, "The Housewife and Her Labour under Capitalism", which argued that the housewife's labour creates value and transfers it to the husband's labour power, so that capital draws a subsidy from the household measurable in the difference the unwaged work makes to the wage. The replies came quickly and established the lines the debate would keep: that the housewife's labour, lacking the social form of exchange, cannot be value creating in the theory's strict sense; that it produces the use values among which labour power is daily remade without itself entering the value relation; that the subsidy, if any, runs to the individual employer or to the husband rather than to capital in general. Dalla Costa's and Federici's position stood at the maximal end of this argument, and the campaign's fate in the academy was to watch the debate's centre settle well short of it, in the family of positions that social reproduction theory would later organise. The profile's separation of the demand from the theorem should be recalled at exactly this point. Himmelweit and Mohun's objection, recorded in the objections to the demand, bites on the theorem; the lever, as a device for changing what housework is perceived to be, does not require the theorem to be true, and the campaign's historical importance may rest on its having built a political instrument stronger than the economics it was bolted to.

The campaign's organisational afterlife outlasted its decade in one traceable line. The International Wages for Housework Campaign persisted as a network after the committees of the seventies thinned, with Selma James as its international coordinator, and in 2000 it issued the call for a Global Women's Strike, observed on the eighth of March in dozens of countries, in which women were asked to stop, for the day, all the work they do, waged and unwaged, in home and workplace and market, and to make the stopping itself the demonstration. The strike call is the campaign's doctrine in its purest organisational form, the lever generalised from a demand addressed to the state into an action performed by the demanders: no invoice this time, but the withdrawal itself, priced by its absence. That the call could be taken up, a quarter century after the pamphlet, by movements that knew the campaign chiefly through it, is the strongest evidence for the profile's earlier observation about victory by absorption. The analysis had become a repertoire.

The campaign's existence is also a standing correction to the periodisation by which the feminism of its decade is usually taught. The standard narrative of the second wave is populated by liberal campaigns for formal equality and by radical feminist analyses of patriarchy as a system of male power, with Marxist feminism admitted as a subordinate clause. The wages campaign fits none of the three shelves. It was international in structure when the textbook movements were national; it was materialist in method while refusing the orthodox Marxism of the parties; it addressed the state as an adversary to be exposed rather than a legislature to be petitioned; and its central category, reproduction, has since become the organising concept of the field's most vital research programme, which the textbook narrative now presents as a discovery of a later generation. A profile is not the place to rewrite the periodisation, but the fact should be flagged for the student who will meet it: the campaign was not a curiosity at the margin of its decade's feminism. It was one of that feminism's theoretical engines, and the subsequent absorption of its questions by scholars who cite its conclusions without its name is the mechanism by which engines disappear from the histories they powered.

The wage was the campaign's signature, not its whole platform, and the surrounding demands fix the signature's meaning. The committees campaigned for free contraception and abortion on request, against the coerced sterilisation of poor women and of women in the colonised world, which the decade's scandals, from the sterilisation programmes imposed on Puerto Rican women to the cases litigated in the American courts, had exposed as the state's other instrument for administering reproduction: the same authority that refused to pay for the work of raising children claimed the right to prevent the wrong women from bearing them. The pairing of the demands is the analysis in miniature. A state that will neither wage the labour nor refrain from policing its performance is administering reproduction as a resource while disclaiming it as a cost, and the campaign's platform was built to leave that administration nowhere to hide: pay for the work, or stop governing it. The sterilisation campaigns also bound the wages committees to the movements of Black, Latina, and indigenous women for whom the policing was not theoretical, and the alliances formed there are the practical root of the perspective's insistence, against its critics' composition charge, that its subject was never the housewife of the suburbs alone.

## The demand as lever

### Did Federici actually want wages for housework?

not primarily. She argued the demand makes unpaid domestic work visible as work, since unwaged labour appears as love or nature rather than labour, and wrote that wanting money for housework is a first step toward refusing it. The demand was a political lever, not a policy proposal.

The pamphlet behind that answer is Wages Against Housework, published in 1975 by the Power of Women Collective and the Falling Wall Press, and its title already contains the argument: the wages are against the housework, not a decoration of it. Its most quoted sentence runs, "They say it is love. We say it is unwaged work." The compression is the theory. Housework escapes the category of labour by being renamed as devotion, and the renaming is not a mistake but a mechanism, since work classified as love is work that cannot be refused, timed, organised, or struck against. A wife who stops cooking is not withdrawing labour, in the eyes the pamphlet is attacking; she is failing at love. The demand for a wage breaks the renaming at its weakest point. To demand money for an activity is to assert, in a form no one can mistake, that the activity is work, and to assert it in the one vocabulary, the vocabulary of payment, that the society being addressed cannot pretend not to understand.

From visibility the pamphlet moves to refusal, and this second move is the one hostile summaries omit. Federici argues that the demand's purpose is not the income but the transformation of the worker's relation to the work: the woman who has established that she works, and works unwaged, has acquired the standpoint from which the work can be renegotiated, reduced, collectivised, or refused, in the same way that the wage claim in a factory is the precondition of the strike rather than its opposite. Money here is a lever placed under a naturalised arrangement. The demand is designed so that winning it and losing it both advance the same end, since a state that pays for housework has conceded that housework is labour, and a state that refuses has been forced to say, in public, that the labour on which its workforce depends is to remain unwaged because it is women's. Either answer denaturalises. That is the structure this profile names, following the brief of the series, the demand as lever, and it is the standard against which objections to the campaign should be graded: an objection aimed at the policy consequences of a wages scheme is aimed at a proposal the campaign did not make, while an objection aimed at the lever itself, at whether the device works as designed, reaches the argument.

### Why a wage, of all instruments?

Because the wage is the form in which this society certifies that an activity is work. Demanding recognition, gratitude, or a fairer division of chores leaves the certification untouched and asks for kindness inside it. Demanding money forces the question of what the activity is. The wage was chosen as the one claim whose granting or refusal would both expose the relation it named.

Consider the alternatives the campaign had before it, since the choice of instrument was deliberate. A demand for men's equal participation in housework addresses the distribution of the burden between the sexes and leaves its social character intact: the work remains unwaged, uncounted, and performed in private households, merely shared more equitably within them. A demand for the socialisation of domestic work, for public laundries, nurseries, and canteens, addresses the work's organisation and was the older socialist answer, running back through the Russian revolutionary tradition; Federici's circle did not dismiss it, but observed that it left the women who would staff the socialised services in the same unwaged or low-waged relation, and that it could be granted as a welfare measure without conceding anything about what housework is. A demand framed in the language of rights and recognition asks the state to honour the work rhetorically. The wage demand alone was constructed to make the state's answer, whatever it was, into evidence. That construction is what distinguishes a lever from a request, and it explains the otherwise puzzling energy the campaign spent on a demand its organisers did not expect any government to grant.

### What does denaturalising housework mean in practice?

It means stripping the activity of its alibis one by one. Work done from love can be timed, counted, costed, and compared once it is called work; work that produces the workforce can be discussed as production; work that exhausts can be refused without the refuser being cast as unnatural. Denaturalisation is the precondition of every later move, from sharing the burden to abolishing its present form.

The pamphlet's analysis of time belongs to this practical core. Waged work in a factory is bounded by the shift; the working day has edges, and beyond them the worker's hours are her own in a sense the law recognises. Housework as the pamphlet describes it has no edges. It fills the day and the night, it is on call, and its product is consumed in the moment of its production, so that its performer can rarely point to what she has made. Federici's insistence that this boundlessness is a political arrangement rather than a fact of nature is the analytical heart of the text: what has no edges cannot be struck against, and the first act of organisation is to draw the edges in, by naming the tasks, pricing the hours, and treating the whole as a job that someone has been assigned. The wage demand was the instrument for drawing those edges publicly. That the same demand could be heard, by other feminists, as a proposal to gild the arrangement it was meant to expose, is the objection to which the profile now turns.

Two further mechanisms complete the lever's design, and both are usually left out of the summaries. The first concerns the relation between the unwaged sphere and the waged work that resembles it. Cleaning, cooking, nursing, and caring are performed in the market as well as in the home, and they are among the worst paid work in the market. The campaign's analysis explains the connection: labour that is done for nothing in the household sets the ceiling on what the same labour can command outside it, since an employer can always point to the wife and mother performing it from love as proof of its natural cheapness. The wage demand was therefore aimed at the waged sphere too. Price the work at home and the social valuation of the work everywhere moves; leave it priceless and the nurse's aide and the cleaner inherit its pricelessness. This is the sense in which the campaign called its demand a perspective rather than a claim: a way of seeing the whole of women's work, waged and unwaged, as one system whose parts set one another's terms.

The second mechanism concerns the addressee. Demanding the wage from the state was not a belief that the state is benevolent; it was an accusation drawn up as an invoice. The state already administered reproduction in the campaign's analysis: through family law that assigned the work to wives, through welfare rules that inspected the homes of mothers, through the provision or withholding of nurseries, clinics, and schools, through population policies that treated women's fertility as a national resource. Administration without payment was the existing arrangement, in which the costs of raising the workforce were privatised onto women while the direction of the process remained public. The demand proposed to make the arrangement declare itself. A state that pays has admitted the work is work and the worker a worker; a state that will not pay has been made to defend, in terms, the proposition that the labour on which every other labour depends shall be extracted as a natural duty. Either way the privatised silence in which the arrangement operated is broken, and breaking it was the objective. Demands built on this pattern do not stand or fall by enactment, which is precisely what their critics found, and find, so difficult to answer and so important to resist.

That refusal had a real world demonstration inside the campaign's own decade, which its literature noticed. In October 1975 the women of Iceland observed a day off, withdrawing for one day from waged employment and from housework alike, and the country's life, its offices, its newspapers, its fish processing, its households, visibly stopped; fathers carried children into workplaces suddenly revealed as dependent on an infrastructure of care that had never been counted. The Icelandic action was not the campaign's doing and its organisers framed it in their own terms, but it performed the campaign's thesis in public: the unwaged and underwaged work of women, withdrawn for a day, disclosed itself as the load bearing structure it had always been. Refusal, the pamphlet's horizon, turned out to be practicable, and its practicability is part of the evidence that the lever was a strategy and not a metaphor.

The lever belongs to a family of political devices that the profile should distinguish it from, because later debates have repeatedly confused its members. One relative is the strike, which reveals by withdrawal: the necessity of labour is invisible while it is performed and undeniable the day it stops, so the strike is an epistemological instrument as much as an economic one. The wage demand reveals by pricing, performing the same disclosure in advance and on paper: it does not wait for the work to stop before showing that the work is work. A second relative is the transitional demand of the older socialist tradition, formulated to be ungrantable within the existing order so that its refusal radicalises the demanders; the lever shares the structure but differs in aim, since its object is not to expose the state's limits in general but to change the perceived nature of one specific activity. A third relative, and the source of the most confusion, is the income policy: the proposals, recurrent in later decades, for a care income, a participation income, or a basic income justified by the social value of unpaid work. Those proposals mean what they say. They are drafted to be enacted, costed, and administered, and their advocates write the costing. The campaign's demand was drafted to be understood. Grading an income policy by whether it shifts perception, or the lever by whether it could be administered, repeats in mirror image the error the next section's critics made from the other direction.

There is a final feature of the design that the exposition should not leave implicit, because it explains the demand's otherwise strange combination of maximalism and modesty. The lever does not specify the world it wants. It does not say whether housework, once visible as work, should be socialised, shared, mechanised, reduced, or abolished, and the campaign's literature is correspondingly plural about the destination while being single minded about the disclosure. That abstinence was strategic: any specified destination would have divided the women the demand was meant to unite, since the socialisers, the sharers, and the abolitionists of housework disagreed with one another as much as with the existing order, whereas the disclosure, your work is work and its wagelessness is a political fact, could be signed by all of them. Davis's objection, it will be seen, is partly an objection to this abstinence: a strategy that unites by refusing to specify the destination may find, at the moment of victory, that it has organised a coalition for a disclosure and not for a world. The profile flags the point here and leaves its weight to the reader's judgement of the exchange as a whole.

The campaign's mature name for its own standpoint deserves recording, since Federici's later collection took its title from it. Reproduction, in the essays of the later nineteen seventies, is named the point zero: the ground on which every analysis of capital must silently stand, the labour that produces the labourer before any working day can begin, assigned the value zero in the accounts of the system that consumes it. The phrase compresses the whole trajectory this profile has traced. The demand of 1972 was the point zero made audible, a claim filed on behalf of the activity the accounts set at nothing; the history of 2004 was the point zero given a past, an account of the centuries in which the zero was imposed. A reader who holds the phrase can navigate the corpus without a map, since each text is the same operation at a different scale: find the work the form prices at zero, and refuse the pricing. The operation's limits are the ones the objections record. Not everything priced at zero is thereby shown to be work in the value sense, and not every silence in an account is a concealment by design; the phrase is a searchlight, and searchlights illuminate by narrowing. But as the name of a standpoint from which to begin, rather than a conclusion at which to arrive, point zero is the campaign's most portable bequest, and it is the term under which her tradition now teaches its own history.

The demand also intersected, in its decade, with the labour movement's argument about the social wage, and the intersection sharpened both. Union economists of the period defended public services, health, education, housing, as a social wage, income in kind that raised the working class's real standard beyond the pay packet. The campaign's rejoinder exposed the presupposition inside the arithmetic: the social wage's services are consumed by households, and a household is not a passive vessel but a workplace, in which the clinic's prescription becomes nursing, the school's day becomes supervised evenings, and the council flat becomes a maintained home, all through labour the accounting leaves unwaged and female. The point was not that public services are worthless; the campaign's women used them and fought their cuts. The point was that the wage, in both its individual and its social form, is paid to a unit whose internal labour it refuses to see, and that any politics of the wage which stops at the unit's boundary has accepted the concealment the concept of the patriarchy of the wage exists to name. The argument's afterlife in the austerity debates of a later century, where the transfer of services from state to household could be measured in women's hours, needs no underlining here.

One campaign text completes the exposition of the lever, because it shows the demand's authors refusing, in advance, the charge that their horizon was a better administered household. "Counter-Planning from the Kitchen", written by Federici with Nicole Cox in 1975, takes the standpoint the wage demand establishes and draws the consequence the pamphlet had left implicit: if reproduction is the point zero, then planning the economy from the kitchen outward, from the needs of the reproducers rather than from the requirements of accumulation, is not a welfare proposal but a rival rationality, against which the existing order's planning, which treats the kitchen as a free input, stands exposed as the partial one. The essay matters to the grading of the campaign because it supplies the destination the lever's abstinence had seemed to omit. The destination was not the waged household; it was a society whose economic plan begins where life is made. Critics are entitled to find the destination gestural, and the socialist planning tradition it invoked had its own record to answer for; but the text forecloses the simplest version of the confinement objection, the version in which the campaigners had not thought beyond the kitchen door. They had thought beyond it in print, in the campaign's first years, and the profile's record should show the thought.

## The objection to the demand, and the reply

The objection that matters was made from inside the feminist movement at the time, and it deserves to be stated at the strength its authors gave it, because Federici's profile is often written as though the campaign's only enemies were conservatives. The objection runs as follows. Whatever its authors intend, a demand is received in the world as the thing it appears to be, and a demand for wages for housework appears to be a demand that the state pay women to remain in the home performing the labour that defines their subordination. Grant it, and the state acquires a budgeted interest in the arrangement: the paid housewife becomes a recognised occupation, staffed by women, supervised in principle by the payer, and fenced off from the waged economy in which collective power is actually accumulated. Refuse it, and the campaign has still spent its energies teaching the public to associate women's emancipation with a cheque for domesticity. On this reading the lever is an illusion produced by confusing the intention of a demand with its career. Demands do not carry their interpretations with them. They are interpreted by governments, newspapers, and husbands, and the interpretation that survives will be the policy proposal, not the strategy of refusal that the policy proposal was meant to smuggle in.

Angela Davis gave the objection its most durable form in Women, Race and Class, published in 1981, in the chapter that argues for the approaching obsolescence of housework as a private activity. Davis's counter-proposal was the older socialist one, restated with the authority of a different experience: housework should be socialised, drawn out of the individual household into public nurseries, laundries, and kitchens, and women should be drawn into social production on equal terms, rather than confirmed in domestic isolation by a wage. Two features of her statement give it weight beyond the general objection. The first is that it speaks from the history of Black women in the United States, for whom work outside the home was never an emancipation to be demanded but an exploitation to be survived, and who had performed domestic labour in other women's houses for wages that conferred no liberation at all; from that vantage, the discovery that housework is work, and the demand that it be waged, could sound like a very partial experience generalised. The second is that Davis located the degradation of housework in its privatised, unmechanised, individual form rather than in its unwaged status, from which it follows that paying for the form leaves the degradation standing.

### What did Angela Davis argue should happen to housework instead?

That it should cease to be a private burden at all. In Women, Race and Class she argued for socialising domestic labour through collective nurseries, laundries, and kitchens, and for women's full entry into social production, holding that a wage for work performed alone in individual households would pay for the arrangement to continue rather than organise its abolition.

Other critics pressed adjacent points that the profile should not blur into Davis's. Some socialist feminists objected that the campaign's economics were wrong before its politics were reached: if housework does not produce value, as the technical side of the domestic labour debate largely concluded against Dalla Costa's maximal claim, then the wage demand lacked the foundation its authors built on it, and a political strategy premised on a contested theorem inherits the theorem's fate. Others objected on tactical grounds that a demand addressed to the state for all women dissolved the differences among women, between those who employed domestic workers and those who were employed, that any serious organising would have to confront. These objections are not interchangeable, and the campaign's literature answered them separately; the point for a profile is that the demand was fought over, on its merits and at the time, by feminists who shared its ends.

Federici's reply is the one already given in the exposition, and it is a real reply to part of the objection. The demand, she argued, does not ask for the work to continue under better pay; it is the first step toward refusing the work, because the woman who has won recognition that she labours has won the standpoint of a worker, and workers can strike. The wage is demanded precisely as the form that dissolves the natural disguise; nobody, she observed, supposes that factory workers who demand higher wages are asking to be confined to the factory forever, and the supposition that women demanding wages are asking to be confined to the kitchen applies to women a reading that would be laughed at if applied to men. To the extent that the objection assumes the demand's authors wanted the domestic arrangement preserved and merely repriced, the reply is complete, because they did not.

### Does restating the intention answer the reception objection?

No, and the profile records the concession plainly because the brief of the series requires it. An argument about what a demand is designed to do does not settle what the demand will be heard to propose. Reception is governed by the hearers, and the objection that a wages demand would be received as a policy for paying housewives stands unanswered by any restatement of the lever.

The concession has a shape worth noting, since it recurs across this series wherever a demand-as-lever pattern appears. The objection divides into a part about intention and a part about reception, and the two parts have different fates. The intention part is answerable, and Federici answered it: the texts are unambiguous that refusal, not institutionalisation, was the design. The reception part is not answerable by texts at all, because it is a prediction about audiences, and predictions about audiences are settled by events rather than by exegesis. The honest verdict is therefore split. As an account of what the campaign proposed, the hostile summary is false, and correcting it is this profile's first duty. As a prediction about how the proposal would travel, the objection identified a genuine exposure that the campaign's strategy could not design away, because the lever's whole mechanism depended on the demand being publicly understood, and public understanding was the one variable the designers did not control. Federici's later writing on the campaign tends to restate the design rather than to revisit the prediction, which is why the concession has to be supplied by the profile rather than quoted from its subject.

The technical wing of the objection arrived from within Marxist economics and carried its own names and papers. Susan Himmelweit and Simon Mohun, in "Domestic Labour and Capital", published in the Cambridge Journal of Economics in 1977, subjected the campaign's founding claim to the categories of the value theory and concluded that it failed them: domestic labour, they argued, is performed outside the capital relation, produces use values for direct consumption rather than commodities for exchange, and therefore does not produce value or surplus value in any sense the theory can sustain, whatever its necessity to the system. The consequence they drew was precise. A politics that grounds the demand for wages in the claim that housework is value producing has mortgaged the demand to a theorem, and when the theorem falls the demand's warrant falls with it. The campaign's defenders answered that the political force of the demand never depended on winning a seminar in value theory, which is true and also beside the point Himmelweit and Mohun were making, since the campaign's own texts had chosen to argue in the theorem's terms. The exchange matters for grading Federici's position because it locates the exact seam where her tradition later divided from social reproduction theory: Vogel's reconstruction can be read as the attempt to keep the analysis of reproduction while surrendering the value claim, a retreat, or a clarification, that the autonomist line refused to make.

Michèle Barrett's book on women's oppression, published in 1980, pressed a different scepticism from within socialist feminism. Barrett's subject was the family household system and the ideology of familialism that constructed women as wives and mothers by nature; her doubt about the wages demand was that a wage paid into the household leaves that construction intact and may reinforce it, since the payment certifies the role it pays for. The objection is close to Davis's but its ground is ideological rather than organisational: where Davis feared the arrangement's perpetuation in brick and budget, Barrett feared its perpetuation in the heads of the paid, the state's cheque arriving as confirmation that domesticity is indeed women's work, now with a payslip. Between them, and together with the reception argument, the contemporary critics had assembled a case with three independent layers, economic, organisational, and ideological, any one of which the campaign had to survive and all of which its literature engaged. The profile's purpose in stacking them is not to bury the demand under its decade. It is to establish that the demand was tested, by serious opponents who shared its ends, at the moment of its making, and that its survival as an analytical instrument was earned against that testing rather than conferred by nostalgia.

The campaign's principals did not lack replies at the time, and fairness requires their presence in the record alongside the concession already entered. James answered the policy reading by insisting that the demand be judged as a perspective on all women's work rather than as a legislative draft, and that critics who treated it as the latter were answering a leaflet of their own invention. Federici answered the confinement charge with the factory comparison already quoted in the exposition: wage demands are not read, in any other industry, as requests for permanent confinement to the work demanded upon. What neither reply touches, the profile has already conceded, is the prediction about audiences. But the concession should be entered with its boundary marked. The prediction was partly falsified by the campaign's actual career: the demand was indeed heard as a policy by many, and it was also heard, by the women who built movements on it and by the scholarship that absorbed it, as the lever it was designed to be. Reception, it turned out, was divided rather than single, and a divided reception is a more complicated verdict than either the designers hoped or the critics forecast.

Two more contemporary objections complete the record and locate the campaign in its decade's real field of forces. The first came from the feminism of the workplace. The nineteen seventies were the decade of the equal pay and equal treatment campaigns, of women's entry into the organised labour movement in force, symbolised in the United States by the founding of the Coalition of Labor Union Women in 1974 and by the long fight for the Equal Rights Amendment. From that terrain the wages demand could look like a defection: a call to price the home at the moment when the movement's leverage lay in the workplace, addressed to women as housewives when the strategic task was to organise them as workers. The campaign's answer, that the demand was for all women and indicted the double day that employed women worked, was logically sufficient and organisationally unavailing, since movements are built from the identities they address and the two feminisms were addressing different ones. The second objection was a charge about composition: that the campaign's typical theorist was a woman educated enough to experience housework as a prison of the mind, while the women for whom domestic labour was a wage, the cleaners and nursemaids employed in other women's houses, appeared in its texts more as evidence than as authors. The campaign's universality was designed against exactly this charge, and its committees included the argument that the employed domestic worker's wage is set by the housewife's wagelessness, so that the two women's interests meet in the demand. Whether the design overcame the composition is a question the movement's history answers less kindly than its theory does, and the profile records the charge because the campaign's later canonisation has tended to forget that it was made, at the time, by the women in whose name the demand spoke.

The exchange with Davis had a third term that neither side could inspect at leisure in the nineteen seventies and that a profile should supply, because it bears on the grading of both: the record of the states that had actually attempted the socialisation of housework. The Soviet Union of the nineteen twenties had legislated, on paper, the communal kitchens, laundries, and nurseries of the classical programme, and had then, under the pressures of scarcity and the regime's turn to the family, allowed them to wither into underfunded remnants while women's double day, waged shift and unwaged shift together, became the ordinary female condition of the socialist bloc. Federici's tradition cited the record as the socialisation answer's refutation in practice: services provided by a state that still treats reproduction as women's natural duty arrive starved, stigmatised, and staffed by women at the lowest wages, reproducing inside the public sector the hierarchy they were meant to abolish. Davis's side had its answer ready, that a programme starved by a dictatorship's priorities is not a programme refuted, and that the alternative the campaign offered had no record at all by which to be judged. The profile enters the exchange because it completes the objection's context: the dispute of 1975 was not between a fantasy and a fact but between two strategies neither of which had a working model to point to, and the half century since has supplied partial evidence to both, the persistence of the double day under every regime that has socialised production, and the equal persistence, under every regime that has not, of the wagelessness the campaign named.

## From the campaign to the witch hunts

The path from a pamphlet of 1975 to a history of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries looks eccentric until the connecting category is named. The campaign's analysis held that capital depends on a sphere of unwaged reproduction which it must control without paying for, and that the wage is the instrument that hides the dependence. Caliban and the Witch, published in 2004, asks how that arrangement was built. Marx's chapters on primitive accumulation describe the expropriation of the peasantry, the enclosures, the bloody legislation against the expropriated, as the prehistory of capital. Federici's book argues that the account is missing its second front. Alongside the separation of producers from the land ran a campaign against women: the persecution of witches across Europe and its colonies, which in her reading destroyed women's control over reproduction, broke their collective power, and devalued their labour so thoroughly that its unwaged performance could thereafter appear natural. The book's subtitle in spirit, if the phrase may be borrowed from its argument, is that primitive accumulation was also a war over the body, and that the witch hunt was one of its principal weapons.

### Why did a campaigner write a history of the witch hunts?

Because the campaign's category needed a genealogy. If capital's command over reproduction is not a timeless arrangement but a built one, then it was built somewhere and at some time, and the book locates the building in the transition from feudalism, in the same centuries and by the same violence that Marx assigned to the making of the proletariat.

The book's architecture follows from that location. It opens with the crisis of feudalism and the resistance of the European peasantry and urban poor, movements in which, on her account, women were prominent and in which communal forms of life and reproduction were defended against lords and merchants. It then reads the witch persecutions, whose great age runs from the middle of the sixteenth century into the seventeenth, as the counterstroke: a terror directed disproportionately at women, and within that disproportion at the women who embodied the older order's female powers, the healer, the midwife, the woman who knew contraception, the beggar who cursed when charity was refused. It closes by extending the analysis to the colonised world, where persecution accompanied the destruction of communal tenure and of indigenous religion in the Americas, and by drawing the line forward to the disciplining of the body in the age of mechanical philosophy. The chapters on reproduction are the load-bearing ones, and the exposition below follows the book's own order of weight: land and resistance first, then the policing of reproduction, then knowledge, then the wage relation the whole process installed.

The book's gestation explains some of its character. Its research ran through the nineteen eighties and nineties in the orbit of the Midnight Notes collective, the autonomist journal project she shared with George Caffentzis, Peter Linebaugh, and others, whose collective volume on the new enclosures had applied the category of enclosure to the structural adjustment era: the fencing of African land, the privatisation of water and seed, the destruction of subsistence economies under debt discipline. Caliban and the Witch can be read as that project's historical volume, the inquiry into how the original enclosures were accomplished in the sphere of reproduction. Its method is the one the project implies: take Marx's chapters on the so called primitive accumulation as a framework of questions rather than of answers, and search the record for the actors and the violences the framework's first telling left out. Published by Autonomedia in New York in 2004, the book travelled slowly at first through movement networks and then, across the following decade, into translation and onto syllabi in numbers that made it the most assigned statement of its tradition. That trajectory is part of its evidentiary problem, because influence arrived faster than the specialist assessment did, and the assessment, when readers go looking for it, has to be reconstructed from the general literature on the trials as much as from reviews of the book itself, a reconstruction the objections sections below perform.

The book also arrived inside a wider revival of its master category, and placing it there clarifies both its timing and its distinctness. The years around its publication saw primitive accumulation return to the centre of Marxist argument as a name for processes still underway: David Harvey's The New Imperialism, published in 2003, popularised the phrase accumulation by dispossession for the privatisations, land seizures, and debt enforcements of the neoliberal decades, and a generation of scholars took up the question of whether expropriation is capitalism's prehistory or its permanent method. Federici's book is the feminist monument of that revival, and it differs from the rest of the moment's literature in a way its title announces. Where the revival's mainstream asked how the commons are enclosed, she asked how the reproducers of the commoners were subdued, and she answered with a body of evidence, the trials, that the rest of the literature had not thought to use. The conjunction explains the book's double reception with unusual neatness: read by the theorists of accumulation as a major extension of their framework, and by the historians of its chosen evidence as an appropriation of their archive for purposes the archive does not serve. Both receptions are documented in what follows, the first in the exposition of the thesis's architecture, the second in the objections, and the profile's refusal to merge them into a single reputation is deliberate.

The book had an ancestor, and knowing it corrects the impression of a thesis sprung entire from 2004. In 1984 Federici published in Italian, with Leopoldina Fortunati, Il Grande Calibano, a study of the rebellious social body in the first phase of capital, in which the governing ideas of the later work, the body as the first machine and the first commons, the disciplining of reproduction as accumulation's hidden half, are already present in outline. Fortunati's own work of those years, on the arcane of reproduction, had argued in parallel that the sphere of reproduction is capital's disavowed foundation, and the joint book is the record of the two arguments meeting. The twenty years between the Italian study and the English one were spent, on the evidence of the finished text, in the archives and in the movements of three continents, and the later book's scale, its movement from the English village to the Peruvian mine in a single chapter's stride, is the scale the method had always promised. A reader who wants the thesis in its compact and least encumbered form could do worse than seek out the ancestor's argument as the later book's first three chapters preserve it; a reader who wants to watch a political tradition think for twenty years about one question will find no clearer exhibit in the cluster this profile belongs to.

## Caliban and the Witch: the thesis expounded

Federici's starting point is the commons. In the late medieval countryside, access to common land, to the waste, the woods, and the water, gave peasant households a margin of subsistence outside any lord's wage or market, and women, whose work centred on the household and its subsistence production, drew a particular independence from that margin. Enclosure abolished it. As common land was fenced, privatised, and turned to pasture or to commercial agriculture, the household's subsistence base shrank and its members were driven toward wage labour, while the sexual division of labour hardened: men into the wage, women into a dependence newly total because the unwaged sphere they managed no longer rested on common resources. Women appear in the book's account of the resistance that followed as central rather than incidental: in food riots, in protests against enclosure, in the defence of customary rights, the record shows women at the front, and the book reads the subsequent persecution as aimed, in significant part, at this female capacity for collective action. The witch, in the demonology of the persecuting centuries, is the photographic negative of the disciplined worker and wife: sexually insatiable yet sterile, powerful over bodies yet dependent on a pact, a woman whose fertility serves death rather than production. Federici's claim is that this figure was constructed and then hunted because the emerging order needed the real women who resembled her, the healers and the disobedient, destroyed or terrified into compliance.

The book's opening movement dwells on what the old order had contained, because its argument requires the loss to be measurable. Late medieval Europe, in her telling, was riven by a class struggle that repeatedly approached victory: the heretical movements that preached community of goods and, in some of their currents, an unprecedented freedom for women; the urban and rural revolts, the English rising of 1381 and the Florentine Ciompi among them, in which the poor demanded the abolition of lordship itself. Women figure in this world as economic actors with recognised, if subordinate, places: brewing and selling ale, trading in markets, practising crafts at the margins of the guilds, and commanding, through the commons and through customary rights of gleaning, grazing, and gathering, a subsistence not wholly mediated by a husband's wage or a lord's leave. The demographic catastrophe of the plague, by making labour scarce, briefly strengthened the hand of those who worked, and the book treats the century after the Black Death as a high water mark of popular power, the moment against which the counter offensive of the following centuries was directed. Enclosure, the price revolution that eroded wages, the criminalisation of vagabondage, and the state's new machinery of punishment belong to that offensive in her account, and the persecution of women is assigned its place inside the same movement: the disciplining of the class as a whole required, in its female half, the destruction of the powers women had exercised over reproduction, healing, and communal life. The witch hunt is thus not an episode the book adds to the transition. It is, in the book's architecture, one of the transition's principal instruments, and its timing, in the centuries when the counter offensive was at its height, is offered as the first evidence for the assignment.

Two elements of the opening movement need fuller statement, because they carry weight later and are often dropped from summaries. The first is the book's treatment of heresy. The great heretical movements of the later middle ages, Cathars, Waldensians, the currents that fed into the Hussite revolution, appear in her account as the ideological form of the communal resistance: movements that denied the church's mediation, held goods in common in some of their communities, and made room, in their congregations and their condemned conventicles, for women to preach, to prophesy, and to live outside marriage in religious community, as the Beguines of the northern towns did in their thousands. The suppression of heresy across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries is therefore, in the book's periodisation, the first act of the long counter offensive, the rehearsal in which church and state learned the techniques, inquisition, denunciation, the exemplary burning, that the witch trials would later inherit. The line the book draws from the heretic to the witch is not decorative. It supplies the persecution with a prehistory inside the class struggle and casts the witch as the heretic's successor: the same refusal of the authorised order, relocated from doctrine to the body.

The second element is the economic reversal of the post plague decades and its gendered incidence, on which the book leans heavily. Labour scarcity after the Black Death raised the price of labour and, with it, the standing of labourers, and women shared in the improvement: the records of the period show women working for wages in building, in agriculture, and in the urban crafts in numbers the following centuries would not match, and the book treats the era's relative female independence, the late marriages, the women living alone or in female households in the towns, as the social base from which the subsequent offensive dislodged them. The reversal, in her account, was engineered as much as suffered: the Statute of Labourers and its continental equivalents attempted to fix wages and compel service; the guilds progressively closed their masterships to women, so that trades women had practised as independent mistresses, brewing emblematically among them, passed into male hands as they were capitalised and scaled; and the ideological accompaniment, the new praise of marriage and domesticity addressed to women, recast the independent female worker as a disorder. Historians of women's work debate the height of the late medieval plateau and the steepness of the fall, and the objections return to the point in their own register; as exposition, the shape of the claim is what matters. The book's witch hunt falls on a population of women already being expelled from the positions that had sustained them, and is aimed, in the argument, at completing the expulsion in the sphere those positions could not reach: the body and its powers.

### What does the book claim the enclosures did to women?

It claims that enclosure stripped women of the commons based margin that had underwritten their independence, drove the household into dependence on the male wage, and provoked a female led resistance to expropriation which the authorities learned to fear. On the book's account the persecutions that followed cannot be separated from that resistance or from the devaluation of women's labour that enclosure enforced.

The reproductive chapters carry the argument from land to the body. The demographic catastrophes of the fourteenth century, plague above all, had made labour scarce and its reproduction a matter of state anxiety; by the sixteenth century, on Federici's reading, European states and churches had converged on a new determination to police fertility. The evidence she marshals is legislative and judicial. Contraception and abortion, treated in the medieval centuries as sins to be confessed, were reclassified as crimes to be punished, in some jurisdictions capitally: a French edict of 1556 required the registration of pregnancies and made concealment followed by an infant's death a capital offence, and an English statute of 1624 reversed the burden of proof for unmarried mothers whose infants died. Infanticide prosecutions multiplied. The midwife, who had presided over the female controlled space of childbirth, was licensed, supervised, and suspected, her knowledge recast as a potential instrument of abortion and of harm to infants. Within this legislative frame the witch trial appears, in the book's argument, as the extreme form of a general offensive: the woman accused of witchcraft was stereotypically accused of crimes against generation, of killing infants, causing impotence, rendering men and animals sterile, and the sabbath of the demonologists was a fertility rite inverted. The persecution, on this account, taught a population that women's control over procreation was diabolical, and transferred that control, in terror's aftermath, to the state, the church, and the husband.

Behind the reproductive legislation stands, in the book's argument, a new political arithmetic. The mercantilist common sense of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries treated population as the wealth of states: more subjects meant more soldiers, more taxpayers, and above all more labour at lower wages. A doctrine that counts people as treasure makes fertility a matter of public administration, and the book assembles the administrative record: the promotion of marriage and the penalising of celibacy, the tightening of the laws on prostitution, which moved across the sixteenth century from municipal regulation, the licensed brothel as an accepted institution, to prohibition and punishment, driving the women who had worked in the trade into clandestinity and infamy; the statutes on infanticide and concealment already noted; and the general apparatus of the bloody legislation, the whipping, branding, and confining of the masterless poor, within which the reproductive offences formed the gendered wing. The demonology then supplied the ideology that made the apparatus cohere. The witch of the treatises is defined by crimes against generation: she kills the infant in the cradle and the child in the womb, she binds the marriage bed with impotence, she dries the milk and blasts the seed. Federici's reading treats this obsession as the signature of the process, the persecutors' own confession that what was being policed was fertility: the sabbath, in the demonologists' pornography of it, is a festival of sterile sex, generation turned against itself, and the woman who presides over it is the enemy of the population policy in person. Whether the record bears that reading is the question the objections take up; as exposition, its force is that it explains why a persecution nominally about heresy was in substance about women's bodies, and why its victims were so largely the women who stood nearest to birth.

The cultural wing of the offensive, as the book presents it, worked on the figures through which popular life understood women. Carnival and the festive calendar had sustained an image of the unruly woman, the wife on top, the world turned upside down for a licensed season, which scholars of popular culture, Natalie Zemon Davis in her essay on women on top among them, have read as the safety valve and the rehearsal of a social order aware of its own reversibility. The disciplinary centuries, in the book's telling, withdrew the licence: festivities were suppressed or policed, the alehouse sociability in which women had gathered was prosecuted as disorder, and the instruments of petty correction, the scold's bridle for the woman whose speech offended, the ducking stool for the disorderly, materialised the new ideal of female silence and enclosure in iron and water. The degradation of the very word gossip, from the godparent and intimate friend of the christening to the idler whose talk is worthless, is the kind of evidence the book prizes: a whole female social world, the network of mutual aid and information that attended births, marriages, and deaths, renamed as a vice at the moment of its suppression. The exposition records these materials as the book presents them, because they are the connective tissue of its argument: the persecution of the witch cannot be understood, on her account, apart from the simultaneous demotion of every lesser form of female power, the healer's knowledge, the midwife's authority, the gossip's network, the unruly woman's licence, each reduced in its own register so that the final product, the enclosed and dependent wife, could appear as nature restored rather than as a construction achieved by violence.

### How, on the thesis, did persecution police reproduction?

By making female knowledge and female gatherings around birth into objects of suspicion. Where contraception and abortion were prosecuted as crimes and the midwife watched as a potential criminal, the witch trial stood at the end of a continuum of surveillance, associating women's autonomous management of fertility with devil worship and thereby justifying its suppression by law, pulpit, and pyre.

Knowledge is the third component, and the one that has attracted the most specific historical fire, so the book's claim needs stating with care before the objections arrive. Federici argues that the persecutions destroyed a body of female knowledge: the herbal and practical medicine of village healers, knowledge of contraceptive and abortifacient preparations, and the midwife's craft, and that this destruction was functional for an order that required women's bodies to be administered by others. The accused healer, in her reading, is not a marginal figure accidentally caught in a panic but a central target, because she embodied an authority over birth, illness, and death that answered to the community of women rather than to church or state. With the healer terrorised and the midwife licensed into subordination to male medical authority, a monopoly of legitimate knowledge over reproduction passed out of female hands. The book connects this passage to the broader disciplining of the body in the same centuries: the attack on magic and on the magical conception of the world, the rise of a mechanical philosophy that figured the body as a machine to be made regular and productive, and the reconstruction of sexuality within marriage as a duty ordered to procreation. Descartes appears in these pages not as a philosopher in the abstract but as a theorist of the self mastering a bodily machine, the intellectual form of a discipline the workplaces and the courts were imposing practically.

### What happened to the midwife, in the book's account?

She was demoted from authority to suspect. Licensing and supervision subordinated her craft to male practitioners and to the authorities, her presence at births was watched for signs of abortion or infant harm, and the knowledge she had carried was progressively reclassified, in the book's argument, from a communal female resource into a danger that legitimate medicine would replace.

The colonial chapters extend the same structure across the Atlantic. In Spanish America, persecution of indigenous religion and of the women who carried it proceeded alongside the destruction of communal landholding and the imposition of new regimes of labour; the book treats the campaigns against idolatry in the Andes and in Mexico as the colonial form of the European hunt, aimed at the reproductive and communal order of peoples being proletarianised. Slavery enters the argument as the extreme of the same logic, the reduction of the body to an instrument of production and of women's bodies to breeders under an owner's command. These chapters matter to the book's claim in a specific way: they present the persecution of women not as a European peculiarity but as a portable technology of the accumulation process, deployed wherever communal subsistence and female authority obstructed the making of a labour force. The outcome of the whole process, in Europe, the book names with the phrase the campaign had prepared: a patriarchy of the wage, an order in which the male worker's wage, however inadequate, becomes the medium of his power over the unwaged woman, her labour disappears into the wage's apparent sufficiency, and the state, having broken the older female powers, guards the arrangement. Section by section, that is the thesis. It is a large claim about four centuries, and the next movement of the profile gives the case against it the same room.

The disciplining of the body receives a philosophical chapter in the book, and its argument should be stated because it is the part of the thesis most often borrowed without acknowledgment. The same centuries that prosecuted the witch, on Federici's reading, reconstructed the human body in theory: the magical worldview, in which the body was continuous with a living cosmos and susceptible to occult forces, gave way among the educated to a mechanical philosophy in which the body figures as a machine, opaque to magic, regular in its motions, and governable by a self figured as its operator. Descartes supplies the emblem, the body as automaton and the will as its engineer, but the book's interest is not in the history of philosophy for its own sake. It is in the homology between the machine body of the philosophers and the labouring body required by the emerging economy: a body purged of unpredictable powers, its sexuality routed into marriage and procreation, its energies made calculable and its time made ownable by others. The attack on magic, in this reading, was an attack on a conception of the world in which the poor had possessed powers of their own, over weather, health, fertility, and fortune; the mechanical philosophy disarmed that world at the level of knowledge as the courts disarmed it at the level of persons. Readers of Carolyn Merchant's The Death of Nature, published in 1980, will recognise a neighbouring argument about the same transformation, and the book's originality here lies less in the parallel itself than in welding it to the history of the trials.

The colonial extension, finally, is where the book's two halves, land and reproduction, are shown operating as one process on peoples who had no commons in the European sense to lose and lost their equivalents anyway. In the Andes and in Mexico, the extirpation of idolatries, the organised campaigns in which Spanish clergy and courts hunted indigenous religion through the seventeenth century, targeted the ritual specialists, very many of them women, who carried the communal order's knowledge of healing, of ancestors, and of the agricultural calendar; the manual of Pablo Joseph de Arriaga, published in 1621, served the Peruvian campaigns as a handbook of detection and punishment. The reducciones, the forced resettlement of indigenous populations into administered towns, performed in space what enclosure performed in law, severing communities from the lands and shrines around which their reproduction had been organised. African slavery enters the account as the process at its most naked: the slave ship and the plantation treating the body as an instrument pure and simple, and enslaved women's reproduction administered as a branch of production, a regime whose descendants in the management of Black women's fertility the campaign's American context had already taught its author to see. Salem, in 1692, appears in the book's geography as the persecution's late colonial echo, falling on a community dividing over land, inheritance, and the terms of its incorporation into a commercial order. These chapters are the least densely evidenced in the book and the most programmatic, and the objections below grade them accordingly; as exposition, their claim is that a technology of accumulation, tested in Europe, travelled with colonisation because colonisation was the same process continued by other means.

The Potosí material concentrates the colonial argument into one mountain, and the book returns to it for that reason. The silver of Potosí, drawn from the cerro by the mita, the forced labour draft that consumed the indigenous communities of the Andes in rotation, was in her account a primitive accumulation in the strictest sense: the metal that lubricated Europe's commerce produced by the destruction of a communal civilisation's capacity to reproduce itself. The mita's demographic catastrophe, the villages emptied of men, the women left to sustain a subsistence the draft had made impossible, is presented as the colonial form of the book's central relation, an order that seizes labour by ruining the conditions of its reproduction and then administers the ruin as a labour regime. Against that background the extirpation campaigns read, in her argument, as the mita's ideological complement rather than as a separate religious history: the shrines destroyed and the ritual specialists punished were the institutions through which the communities had organised their collective life, and their destruction was the destruction of the alternative to the mine. The book's colonial chapters have been faulted, as the objections will show, for treating distinct processes as one; the exposition should nonetheless leave the reader with the argument's intended force, which is that the European and colonial processes explain one another, the witch and the idolater being, in the persecutors' own filing system, neighbouring species of the same enemy.

One further stratum of the exposition belongs here, because the book treats it as the persecution's institutional sibling and summaries habitually drop it: the reorganisation of poverty. The same legislatures that sharpened the statutes on reproduction built, across the sixteenth century, a machinery for the masterless poor whose logic the book reads as a single design with the trials. Marx had already catalogued the English sequence in his chapters on the bloody legislation: the statutes that whipped, branded, and in one notorious enactment of 1547 briefly enslaved the sturdy vagabond, converting the expropriated into a workforce by terrorising their refusal to be one. Federici's contribution is to insist on the gendered division inside that machinery. The workhouses and houses of correction that spread from the Amsterdam rasphuis of the fifteen nineties across the continent confined the poor under regimes of compulsory labour and moral supervision, and within them the women were disciplined as women: the unwed mother, the woman living without a man's government, the servant between places, all processed as reproductive problems before they were processed as labour problems. Poor relief itself was rebuilt on the distinction between the deserving and the undeserving, a distinction administered, in parish after parish, through the sexual conduct of the women who claimed it. The book's point is architectural rather than anecdotal. Enclosure removed the material base of the old independence; the poor law machinery patrolled the population the removal created; the reproductive statutes governed its replenishment; and the trials supplied the terror that sanctified the whole arrangement by displaying, on the scaffold and the pyre, the fate of the woman who stood outside it. Grading the architecture is the objections' business, and several of its joints, as will be seen, do not bear the load placed on them. As exposition the claim is now complete in its full extent, and it is a measure of the book's ambition that its weakest documented element, the trials, is asked to crown a structure whose other elements, the statutes, the workhouses, the enclosures, rest on records no one disputes exist.

The sabbath, in the book's reading, is where the demonologists inadvertently archived the world they were destroying, and Federici's handling of it is the exposition's most characteristic passage between evidence and construction. The trial confessions and the treatises describe, with horrified consistency, a nocturnal assembly: feasting, dancing, music, sexual licence, the communal consumption of stolen plenty, presided over by a figure who is the lord of misrule in a black cope. Read against the festive calendar of the commons, the sabbath is that calendar inverted and criminalised: the night gathering of the poor, at which the hierarchies of the day were suspended and the goods of the rich imaginatively consumed, transcribed by its enemies into a liturgy of evil. The book does not claim the sabbath happened; it claims the fantasy of it is evidence, the persecutors' dream of the communal world whose real forms, the feast, the dance, the gathering on the heath, were being policed out of existence in the same decades. The pact and the mark complete the transcription: the peasant's relation to the commons, a customary right held from the community and renewed in use, reappears in the confession as a contract signed with the devil and sealed on the body, as though the only form in which the old solidarities could be imagined, by the end, was a treason. This is the book at its most brilliant and its most exposed, since the reading is an interpretation of fantasies produced under torture, and everything the objections say about the unreliability of that record applies with special force to the passages where the interpretation is freest. The exposition flags the joint now so that the grading later can find it: the sabbath reading is the component where the distance between the document and the claim is greatest, and where the reader's verdict on the book's method will be decided.

Proto-industry supplies the exposition's last economic stratum. The putting out system, in which merchant capital distributed raw materials to cottage workers across the countryside, drew whole households, women and children centrally, into commodity production while leaving them in possession of their cottages and plots; and the book treats the long proto-industrial centuries as the contested terrain on which the sexual division of labour was renegotiated. Women's spinning, the archetypal cottage by employment, was so identified with their sex that its name became their title in the language, and the book reads the subsequent history, the mechanisation that moved spinning into the factory and redefined it as machine minding under male supervision, as a second expulsion repeating the guild closures at a larger scale: the woman who had been a producer with a recognised, if ill paid, place in a trade became, in the factory's division of labour and the cottage's ruin alike, a dependant awaiting the wage of another. The specialists of proto-industrialisation debate how far the cottage system empowered the women inside it, and the objections' caution about golden ages applies here too; the exposition's point is the argument's shape. At every stratum the book examines, land, craft, cottage, body, the same vector is found, from female production embedded in a community to female reproduction enclosed in a household, and the vector's recurrence across unlike materials is offered as the thesis's cumulative proof.

The title itself is an argument, and the exposition should decode it before closing. Caliban, in Shakespeare's Tempest, is the dispossessed native of the island, enslaved by Prospero, whose rebellion the play stages and defeats, and whose mother, the witch Sycorax, is dead before the action opens, present only as the slandered memory through which her son is ruled. Federici's conjunction of the two names proposes that the colonised rebel and the persecuted witch are the twin figures of the resistance the transition had to break: the one in the colonies, the other in the European village, each the bearer of a communal world, each demonised as the warrant for their subjection. Sycorax's absence from her own play is, in the book's reading, the perfect emblem of the process: the witch survives in the record chiefly as her enemies described her, which is the archival condition the whole work labours under and, as the objections insist, too often forgets. The reading of the play is not evidence, and the profile does not offer it as evidence; it is the book's self portrait, the clearest statement of the sympathy that organises its selections, and a reader warned of the sympathy is better placed to grade the selections than a reader who mistakes the book for a survey.

The English Revolution receives the argument's most concentrated political chapter, and its omission from most summaries is a loss, since it is where the book's two storylines, the commons and the body, are shown converging on a datable crisis. The radical sects of the sixteen forties, the Diggers who occupied St George's Hill in 1649 to farm the commons in common, the women who petitioned Parliament in their thousands and were told to return to their housewifery, appear in her account as the last rising of the world the enclosures were abolishing, and their defeat as the settlement from which the disciplined order proceeded. The regicide's republic, on this reading, enclosed as energetically as the monarchy it replaced, and the revolution's failure to become a revolution in reproduction, its restoration of the patriarchal household inside the commonwealth of saints, is the book's explanation of why the English transition could be at once the most revolutionary and the most complete. The specialists of the English Revolution will find the compression violent, and the objections' general cautions apply; the exposition notes the chapter because it displays the thesis's explanatory ambition at full stretch, a single framework required to digest a civil war, a regicide, and a commune, and because a reader who has seen the ambition will understand both the book's hold on its admirers and the resistance it meets among those whose lives are spent in one of its centuries.

The book's relation to Foucault should be stated, since he is the theorist of discipline whose shadow lies across its middle chapters and since the comparison locates its originality precisely. The great confinement of the poor and the mad, the internment that Foucault's History of Madness dated to the founding of the Hôpital Général in 1656, and the disciplinary institutions of his later work supply Federici with a ready architecture: the same centuries, the same suspicious attention to the body, the same conversion of confinement into a technology of order. Her departures from him are the argument. Where Foucault's discipline is famously indifferent to its economic function and nearly silent about women, hers is accumulation's instrument and gendered in every gear; where his confined population is the mad and the idle in general, hers is sorted by sex at the door of every institution. The specialists' verdict on the borrowing is double. The Foucauldian architecture gives the book its sweep and its readability, the sense of a single rationality assembling itself across hospitals, workhouses, courts, and pyres. It also imports the weakness for which Foucault's own confinement thesis was criticised by the historians of the asylum, a chronology too clean for the records and a rationality too unified for the institutions, so that the book's two most ambitious inheritances, Marx's accumulation and Foucault's discipline, arrive with the same defect attached: each explains the pattern by a logic the participants never stated, and each is most vulnerable exactly where it is most elegant. The profile records the kinship because an examiner or a reviewer will reach for it, and because the book is unintelligible as a period piece without it: it is the point where the Marxism of the campaign met the post structuralism of the academy and made, of the meeting, a bestseller of the movements.

The book's periodisation, finally, is not the eccentricity the mechanism is sometimes mistaken for, and fairness requires the distinction. That the middle of the seventeenth century closed an age of crisis, of revolts, wars, and state building across the continent, is a position with a long scholarly pedigree, from Eric Hobsbawm's essays on the general crisis of the European economy to the decades of debate they provoked; and that the crisis bore with particular weight on the poor, on customary rights, and on the terms of labour is common ground between the book and a historiography that shares none of its conclusions about witches. Federici's period is respectable. It is the assignment of the persecution to a function within the period that the specialists refuse, and the profile's grading has kept the two judgements separate for exactly this reason: a reader may accept the book's centuries and its crisis entire, and still conclude, with the literature, that the trials were the crisis's religious and legal weather rather than its economic engine. Distinguishing the frame from the mechanism is the last service the exposition owes the objections, since conflating them is the commonest way the book's defenders convert agreement about the period into assent to the thesis.

The book closes by refusing its period the comfort of a completed past, and the refusal is the hinge between the history and the campaign with which this profile began. The disciplining the book describes was never, on its own showing, a finished work: the women prosecuted as witches were succeeded in every century by the women prosecuted as disorderly, as unfit mothers, as welfare cheats, and the figure of the witch survived in popular memory as the emblem of a female power the order had failed to extinguish, available for reclamation by the movements that eventually reclaimed her. A history written by a campaigner ends, characteristically, in the present tense of its struggle, and a reader who finds the ending tendentious has understood the genre correctly. The exposition of the thesis is now complete in its components: land, heresy, reproduction, knowledge, speech, body, colony, and the wage. The grading follows.

## The claim and evidence table

The components above are easier to grade together than scattered through prose, and the series' device for grading them is the claim and evidence table: one row per component of the witch hunt thesis, with columns for what is claimed, the evidence offered, the specialist objection, and what survives of the claim. The objections column anticipates the detailed exposition that follows the table; it states each objection in the compressed form its authors' findings support, and the sections after the table give the findings themselves, attributed. The fourth column is the profile's own assessment and owns its judgements: a component can fail as chronology and survive as a question, and the table records the difference rather than issuing a single verdict on the book.

### What does the table's fourth column concede?

More than admirers usually allow and less than detractors usually claim. It concedes the timing, the midwife, and the scale in their strong forms, while recording that the reproductive legislation, the gendered outcome, and the wage concept survive the specialist findings. The column grades components, because the book is a set of claims and not a single one.

Two cautions belong with the table before its compression is mistaken for the argument. The objection column compresses findings that occupy whole books; compressing them into cells is a finding aid, not a substitute, and the sections that follow restore their texture and their evidence. And the fourth column's survivals are survivals of different kinds. The reproductive legislation survives as documentary fact whose interpretation is disputed. The patriarchy of the wage survives as a concept whose usefulness does not depend on the hunt having built the order it names. The gendered outcome survives as a statistic with regional exceptions the table's cells cannot hold. A reader who wants the grades defended should read on, because the defence and the attack are both in the next sections, in the historians' own terms as far as a profile can carry them.

A word on how to use the table without being used by it. A component grade is not a book grade, and the arithmetic by which a reader totals survivals into a verdict is the reader's own. The table's discipline is negative as much as positive: it prevents the commonest error in the literature around this book, which is to let the strongest component carry the weakest, so that the documented reproductive legislation lends its credibility to the untested chronology, or the failed scale discredits the surviving concept. Teachers setting the book will find the table's proper use is as a marking scheme for essays, since a student who can reproduce its distinctions has understood more history than a student who can only announce a side. With that caution entered, the objections themselves follow, at the length the exposition received, as the brief of the series requires.

## The specialists' objections, stated at full length

The objections that follow are not a rival ideology answering Federici's; they are findings from the archival study of the trials, assembled over decades by historians of the period whose own books are the standard references on what happened. That provenance is what makes them hard for the thesis to absorb. A political critic can be answered politically. A chronology cannot. The profile therefore gives the objections in the form in which the literature states them, attributed to named scholars and identified works, and at a length comparable to the exposition of the thesis itself, because anything shorter would relegate to a footnote the very evidence on which a reader's judgement of the book should rest. It is also worth saying what the objections are not. They are not a denial that the persecutions were real, that they were savage, or that women bore the overwhelming weight of them in most of Europe. Every scholar cited below built a career on documenting exactly that. The dispute is over mechanism and meaning: why it happened, where, when, at whose initiative, and in what numbers.

The machinery of the trials is common ground between the book and its critics, and describing it once, neutrally, will let the objections that follow spend themselves on interpretation rather than on facts. Witchcraft was prosecuted, in most of the jurisdictions that killed for it, as a crimen exceptum, an excepted crime, in which the ordinary rules of evidence were suspended on the ground of the offence's secrecy and enormity: anonymous denunciation could open a case, the testimony of children and of convicted witches could be admitted, and torture could be applied, in the German lands repeatedly and beyond the legal limits, to extract the confession and the names of accomplices that the procedure required for conviction. The confession, so produced, was then read back as the trial's evidentiary crown, and the chain of denunciations it generated is the mechanical explanation of the panics' growth curves, in the literature's account: one accused, tortured into naming the sabbath's company, manufactures the next dozen cases. The demonological treatises, the Malleus Maleficarum of 1486 at their head, supplied the interrogators' template, the list of questions and the theory that made the answers cohere. Federici's book and Levack's survey describe this machinery in substantially the same terms; they differ over what drove it and what it was for, which is to say over everything except the machine. The reader should hold the agreement in mind as a measure of the dispute's real location: not in the archive's contents, which both sides read, but in the philosophy of history each brings to the reading.

The chronology objection is the most mechanical and, for a thesis that ties the hunt to a transition in the mode of production, the most damaging in its simple form. Brian Levack's The Witch-Hunt in Early Modern Europe, the standard survey through successive editions, dates the great age of prosecution from roughly 1560 to the middle of the seventeenth century, with the most intense decades falling around the turn of the century, and insists that this curve rose and fell on a timetable set largely by legal and religious developments: the adoption of inquisitorial procedure and judicial torture in secular courts, the confessional conflicts of the Reformation era, and eventually the growth of judicial scepticism that choked prosecutions from within the legal system itself. Set that curve against the chronology of agrarian capitalism and the alignment the thesis requires is not there. English enclosure, the classic case, ran in waves that began well before the hunting peak and reached their statutory height long after it, yet England executed only a few hundred people for witchcraft across the whole period, roughly five hundred on the usual estimates, and did so by hanging under a legal system that restricted torture. Scotland, whose economy was no one's model of advancing agrarian capitalism, executed around two and a half thousand people, a rate per head of population among the highest in Europe. The greatest killing fields of all were the fragmented territories of the Holy Roman Empire, where the driving variables visible in the records are jurisdictional: small states, weak appellate control, local courts free to torture and to confiscate, and confessional frontiers where Catholic and Protestant authorities competed in zeal. A thesis that requires persecution to intensify where capitalist agriculture advances finds the map nearly inverted.

### Where does the chronology objection bite hardest?

At the joint between the two processes the book fuses. Enclosure and commercialisation have regional timetables of their own, spread across three centuries, while the trials cluster in a few decades and in jurisdictions whose common feature is legal fragmentation rather than agrarian transformation. The tighter the dates are drawn, the less the two curves can be made to describe one movement.

Geography deepens the same difficulty. The persecutions were at their most lethal in a band of territories, the German southwest, Lorraine, Franche-Comté, the Swiss lands, Scotland, and later Poland, whose shared features were political division and confessional exposure, while the regions of Europe where wage labour and commercial agriculture were penetrating earliest present a mixed picture: the Dutch Republic, the most commercial society of the seventeenth century, effectively stopped executing witches early in the period, and the Spanish and Italian inquisitions, for all their reputation in other matters, proved comparatively restrained in witchcraft cases, with the Spanish Inquisition's sceptical intervention after the Navarre panic of 1610 becoming a famous instance of a central authority suppressing local persecution rather than driving it. None of this refutes the observation that the hunting centuries are also centuries of economic transformation; everything in them is also something else. What it refuses is the stronger claim the book needs, that the persecution was an instrument of that transformation, applied where the transformation required it. Instruments leave a pattern of application, and the pattern in the records follows courts, confessions, and local panics rather than enclosures.

Causation, in the specialist literature, is correspondingly plural. Wolfgang Behringer's work, including Witches and Witch-Hunts: A Global History, ties the peaks of persecution to the climatic deterioration of the Little Ice Age, whose harvest failures generated the accusations of weather magic and the communal desperation that made neighbours accusable; the correlation between cold, hunger, and trial intensity in the German lands is one of the more robust findings in the field. Christina Larner's Enemies of God, the classic study of the Scottish case, bound the prosecutions to the building of the godly state, arguing that witch hunting expanded as an arm of a new machinery of moral and political discipline, and delivered the field's most quoted caution about gender: witchcraft, in her formulation, was sex related but not sex specific, an offence women were prosecuted for in overwhelming numbers without being an offence defined against women as such. Legal historians add the procedural engine: where Roman law inquisitorial methods allowed judges to proceed on denunciation and to torture for the names of accomplices, single trials snowballed into chains and panics; where accusatorial procedure and evidentiary restraint held, as in England, they did not. A reader weighing Federici's account should notice what this plurality of causes does to it. The thesis operates with one master mechanism, the requirements of accumulation, into which law, confession, and climate must be fitted as expressions. The literature's mechanisms are lower level, better evidenced, and mutually compatible in a way that leaves the master mechanism with little work to do.

The accusation objection cuts at the thesis from the opposite direction, from below rather than from the state. Robin Briggs, in Witches and Neighbours, reconstructed the social life of accusation in Lorraine from the trial records and found a world at odds with the picture of a campaign directed against women by authority. Accusations characteristically arose out of the friction of village life: quarrels over loans, over charity refused at the door, over animals sickened and children ailing after a sharp word from a neighbour. The accused were indeed disproportionately women, and disproportionately poor and old, which the thesis also says; but the accusers and witnesses were very often women too, neighbours testifying to years of accumulated suspicion, and the authorities entered late, processing conflicts the community delivered to them rather than manufacturing the conflicts. The charity refused pattern, first mapped by Keith Thomas and Alan Macfarlane for England, has the accused as the older woman turned away from a door, after whose curse the householder's misfortune is reinterpreted as malice. This does not make the persecution less gendered in its outcomes, and Briggs is explicit that the cultural materials from which suspicion was built were saturated with misogyny. It does make the mechanism unrecognisable as a war conducted by an emerging order against female power: the war, in the records, is mostly neighbours against neighbours, fought with the demonology the culture supplied.

The most interesting recent scholarship refuses the choice between the book's mechanism and Briggs's, and in doing so sharpens the problem for both. Lyndal Roper, in Witch Craze, published in 2004, and in her earlier work on the German trials, confirmed from the records what the demonology suggests: the accusations are saturated with fertility. They cluster around the lying in chamber, the weeks after childbirth when mother, infant, midwives, and female attendants were gathered in a space men did not enter; they concern dried milk, sickened infants, impotent bridegrooms, the envy of the barren for the fruitful. But the engine Roper finds in this material is not a state strategy of accumulation. It is the psychic life of the accusers and the accused: the terrors of early modern childbirth, the guilt of mothers whose infants died, the fantasies of the post partum weeks, projected onto the older woman at the bedside. Her witches are made by the anxieties of reproduction rather than by its administration. For Federici's thesis the encounter is double edged. Roper corroborates, from the deepest archival work on the German cases, the reproductive obsession the book builds on, and corroboration of the raw material from so independent a direction is not nothing. But the mechanism she documents is nearly the opposite of the book's: where the thesis needs the persecution to descend from an order's requirements, the records, on Roper's reading, keep showing it rising from the fears of the birthing room, which authorities then processed. A thesis about function must eventually become a thesis about mechanism, and it is at the mechanism that the specialist work, Briggs and Roper together, most persistently declines to follow.

The sex ratio itself, the statistic on which the book's gendered reading most securely rests, also complicates under regional examination in ways a profile must report. The European average of three quarters to four fifths women among the accused conceals jurisdictions in which the figure inverts: in Iceland, where the magical tradition prosecuted was a learned and substantially male one, around nine tenths of the accused were men; in Normandy and in parts of the Baltic and Russian north, male practitioners of magic, the cunning men and shepherds and soldiers of the records, formed large shares and sometimes majorities of those tried. The specialists' inference is not that gender is incidental; it is that the category witch was filled by local magical cultures, female in the regions where magic was women's work of healing and cursing, male where magic belonged to other trades, and that a theory of the persecution must explain the variation rather than average it away. Federici's account, pitched at the level of Europe and its transition, has little to say about why the hunt should have spared women in one province to burn men in another, and the silence is the more telling because her own framework, which ties persecution to the local organisation of reproduction, might have been expected to predict variation and instead predicts uniformity.

Beneath the particular objections runs a methodological one, which the survey literature presses with unusual unanimity and which a fair profile must state as a finding in its own right. The thesis explains the persecution by the function it served: an order needed disciplined reproducers, therefore the persecution occurred. Functional explanation of this kind, the historians object, systematically confuses consequence with cause and dissolves the agents. The judges who tortured, the theologians who wrote the treatises, the neighbours who denounced, and the magistrates who eventually stopped the trials all had reasons they could and did state, in a vocabulary of heresy, malefice, and evidence, and an explanation that treats those reasons as costumes worn by an economic requirement has not explained the episode but declined to. The point is sharpened by the enclosure evidence itself. Robert Allen's Enclosure and the Yeoman, published in 1992, the most influential revisionist study of the English process, found enclosure proceeding piecemeal across centuries, much of it by agreement, its depopulating violence concentrated in particular regions and periods rather than spread as a single expropriating wave, and the open field countryside sustaining a yeomanry far hardier than the classic narrative of dispossession allows. Allen's subject is England and his findings do not govern the continent, but the methodological moral travels: the transition the book requires, a legible campaign of expropriation running on a timetable the hunts could serve, dissolves on inspection into processes slower, more various, and less directed than any instrument theory can use. A persecution cannot be the weapon of a transition that was not itself a campaign.

The great panics supply the objection with its most vivid single exhibit, and it should be entered in evidence rather than left as atmosphere. In the prince bishoprics of Würzburg and Bamberg in the sixteen twenties, the trials escaped every boundary the campaign model predicts. Under torture driven procedure the denunciations climbed the social order: council members, clergy, students, children, and the wives and kin of the governing families themselves entered the lists, and at Bamberg the machinery consumed Johannes Junius, a mayor of the city, whose smuggled letter to his daughter, written in 1628 in expectation of execution, describes an innocent man broken by torture into confessing what was required. A terror that devours the magistrates' own households is not behaving as the instrument of anyone's accumulation strategy; it is behaving as panics behave when procedure removes the brakes, which is what the legal historians have always said. Federici's framework can absorb the panics as excess, as the terror overshooting its function, but each absorption costs the framework the precision that made it interesting: a campaign that kills its own commanders, in places and decades chosen by confessional politics, is a campaign in a sense so extended that the word has stopped discriminating. The panics also complicate the gender arithmetic from within the worst episodes, since the Bamberg and Würzburg lists include striking numbers of men and of children, a fact the book's aggregate percentages flatten and the local literature insists on.

The intellectual historians among the specialists add an objection of temperament that is easy to underrate and hard to answer. The demonologists, on the evidence of their own texts, believed what they wrote. The Malleus Maleficarum is a work of deranged sincerity; Bodin's treatise argues for severity from within a jurisprudence its author held in earnest; the judges who agonised over evidence in the records were not, in the main, executing a strategy they privately understood in other terms. A history that requires the persecution to be an instrument must either attribute to these men a cynicism their writings refute or fall back on the claim that the function operated behind their backs, through the selection of which beliefs flourished. The second claim is available to the book and is its characteristic move, but the specialists' rejoinder is that a function which operates only behind backs, leaving no trace in the intentions of any participant, and whose evidence is the outcome it is invoked to explain, has crossed the line between explanation and incantation. The rejoinder does not touch the weaker and defensible version of the argument, that the persecutions had systematic effects on the organisation of reproduction which can be studied as effects. It touches precisely the book's stronger claim, the one that gives the thesis its shape and its title's promise, that the effects were the point.

German historiography supplies the objection with its most delicate instrument, because it possesses a disciplining literature of its own. The concept of social disciplining, developed by Gerhard Oestreich and elaborated in the confessionalisation research on the German territories, describes exactly what the book describes: churches and states of both confessions, in the century after the Reformation, undertaking the systematic moral regulation of their populations, through catechism, visitation, schooling, and the policing of marriage, sexuality, and belief. Federici's readers sometimes cite this literature as corroboration. Its authors' point is nearly the opposite. The disciplining they document was confessional in motive and ecclesiastical in personnel, aimed at the making of orthodox Christians and obedient subjects of territorial churches, and it proceeded in agrarian backwaters and commercial cities with an indifference to the local state of accumulation that the book's framework cannot accommodate. Where the two accounts agree is that a great disciplining occurred. Where they divide is over whose it was, and the division is not a nuance: a disciplining ordered to confessional truth explains the persecution's map, its chronology, and its eventual relaxation as the confessional age waned, while a disciplining ordered to accumulation explains none of those three without auxiliary hypotheses. The specialists' preference is not for the less political story. It is for the story that fits the sequence of events.

### Whose accusations sent most women to trial?

On the findings assembled by Briggs and by the English studies on which he builds, the neighbours of the accused: fellow villagers, frequently women themselves, bringing years of quarrels, refused requests, and remembered curses to courts that then applied the law's machinery. Authorities and demonologists framed the offence; the supply of victims came substantially from within the community.

The midwife question is where the general objections become a single checkable case, and it has not gone well for the book's version. David Harley's article of 1990, "Historians as Demonologists: The Myth of the Midwife-witch", published in Social History of Medicine, examined the claim that midwives were prime targets of the persecutions and found it to be a historiographical construction rather than a finding. In the English and continental records he surveyed, midwives appear among the accused rarely; they appear far more often on the prosecution's side of the process, employed as searchers to examine accused women for the witch's mark and as witnesses on matters of pregnancy, birth, and infant death. The stereotype of the midwife-witch, Harley showed, descends from a handful of demonological texts, the Malleus Maleficarum notoriously among them, generalised by later writers into a sociology the records do not support. The point generalises beyond midwifery. A claim that a persecution targeted a category can be tested against the occupational profile of the accused, and where that has been done, the profile that emerges is of the poor, the elderly, the quarrelsome, and the already suspected of both the village's margins, rather than of a knowledgeable female estate being systematically liquidated. The loss of female control over childbirth across these centuries is real and documented, but the literature attributes it to licensing, to the professionalisation of medicine, and to the man-midwife's later rise, processes running on tracks parallel to the trials rather than through them.

The survival of popular magic after the trials poses the knowledge objection in its broadest form. Owen Davies, in Popular Magic: Cunning-folk in English History, published in 2003, documented the cunning folk, the village practitioners of healing, divination, and counter magic, flourishing in England not merely through the hunting centuries but into the nineteenth and twentieth, consulted by all classes, prosecuted rarely and then usually for fraud rather than for witchcraft, and embedded in their communities as a recognised trade. The finding is awkward for any account in which the trials destroyed the people's magical knowledge and practice: whatever the trials destroyed, they did not destroy that, since the practitioners were still advertising in local newspapers two centuries after the last execution. Federici's framework can distinguish, and her defenders do, between the cunning man's tolerated commerce and the autonomous female knowledge the thesis claims was broken; but the distinction has to carry the weight of the English evidence entire, in which female practitioners also persisted, and the specialists' judgement is that a destruction so selective it leaves the trade standing begins to look less like a finding than like a qualification added to save one. The episode generalises into the profile's recurring verdict on the book's strong claims: each survives, where it survives, in a form narrower than the text asserts, and the narrowing is the objections' cumulative work.

### What did David Harley show about the midwife-witch?

That she is largely a myth of the secondary literature. Searching the trial records, Harley found midwives seldom among the accused and repeatedly among the prosecution's instruments, searching the bodies of suspected women and testifying about births. The figure of the persecuted midwife descends from demonological rhetoric generalised by later writers, not from the sociology of the trials.

Numbers require their own paragraph, because this is the point at which the wider literature around the book most visibly departs from the archival scholarship, and the series' contested figures protocol applies with full force. The estimates built from the records by the specialists converge on a range: on the order of one hundred thousand prosecutions across Europe over the whole period, with Levack's widely used figures running to roughly one hundred and ten thousand trials and something under sixty thousand executions, and Behringer's global survey arriving at a comparable order of magnitude, around fifty thousand deaths. The proportion of the accused who were women stands, in the syntheses, at roughly three quarters to four fifths overall, with regional variation wide enough to forbid any single percentage: in Iceland the accused were overwhelmingly men, and male majorities or near majorities appear in Normandy, Estonia, Finland, and Russia, while parts of the Empire and Scotland prosecuted women at rates above the European average. Against this stands the figure that circulates in movement literature and across the internet, of nine million women burned. Its provenance is traceable and disreputable: it descends from an extrapolation published in the seventeen eighties by the German historian Gottfried Christian Voigt, who reasoned from a local run of records to the whole of Europe across the centuries and produced a total in the millions, which nineteenth century writers, Matilda Joslyn Gage's Woman, Church and State of 1893 prominently among them, carried into the feminist tradition, whence it passed into twentieth century retellings. The archival scholarship does not support it by two orders of magnitude. This profile states the position plainly, as the protocol requires: the higher figures in general circulation are not supported by the trial records or by any defensible extrapolation from them; the scholarly range is tens of thousands of executions; and no single number within the range should be quoted as established. It should also be said, because detractors of the book sometimes imply the opposite, that the correct range describes an atrocity. Tens of thousands of judicial killings, of mostly poor and mostly elderly women, conducted with torture, require no inflation to command horror, and the inflation in fact damages the memory it claims to honour by making the true record sound like a minimisation when it is finally stated.

A caution about the estimates themselves belongs inside the protocol, because the range's width is instructive. Trial records survive unevenly: some jurisdictions left near complete series, others left fragments, war and neglect destroyed archives wholesale, and any European total is an extrapolation across holes. The specialists handle this by stating their basis, region by region, and by letting the totals carry the uncertainty the sources impose, which is why the responsible literature speaks in orders of magnitude rather than in false precision. The movement figure's defect is therefore not merely that it is large; it is that it is exact in form, a single round enormity offered without basis, and its career illustrates the general law of contested numbers, that the figure which travels is the figure which serves. The profile's insistence on the point is not pedantry about a detail of the book's reception. The inflated number does argumentative work for the thesis, since a persecution of millions reads as a war and a persecution of tens of thousands reads as an atrocity of a different administrative kind, and allowing the first figure to stand unchallenged would smuggle into the reader's judgement, through the emotions, a conclusion the evidence sections have not earned. Tens of thousands of people tortured and killed by due process of law, most of them women, most of them poor, is the record. It is sufficient.

The records' unreliability cuts in a second direction that even handedness requires noting, because it wounds the thesis's own evidence and not only its opponents' use of the totals. The confessions on which the book's most distinctive readings rest, above all the sabbath material expounded earlier, were produced in large part under torture or its threat, shaped by interrogators' questionnaires, and conformed by the accused, over days of examination, to the script the questions supplied. Specialists therefore treat confession content as evidence about the interrogators' world first and about the accused's practices hardly at all. Federici's sabbath reading accepts the first half of that doctrine, reading the confessions as the persecutors' fantasy, and then draws from the fantasy conclusions about the communal world it inverted; the specialists' caution is that an inversion is still an interpretation, underdetermined by documents whose genre is coercion. The point does not invalidate reading the demonology symptomatically, which the whole field does. It invalidates resting a civilisational claim on the symptomatic reading alone, and it is the methodological form of the objection that the book's admirers least often engage, because it cannot be answered by citing more trials.

### Where does the nine million figure come from?

From an eighteenth century extrapolation, not from the archives. Gottfried Christian Voigt reasoned outward from a short local series of executions to centuries and a continent, and nineteenth century popularisers, Gage's history among them, carried his millions into the feminist tradition. Archival totals assembled by Levack and Behringer run to tens of thousands of executions.

A different order of objection comes from feminist scholarship itself, and for the book's likely readership it may cut deepest. Diane Purkiss, in The Witch in History, published in 1996, examined the uses modern feminism had made of the witch and found a mythology: the accused woman reimagined as a healer, a rebel, and a priestess of an older female religion, a figure flattering to her modern reclaimers and false to the record, in which the typical accused was an elderly, poor, often difficult woman destroyed by her neighbours' fears rather than by any power she wielded. Purkiss's argument is not that the persecutions were other than misogynist in their materials; it is that the feminist myth repeats, in a celebratory key, the demonologists' own error of making the witch a symbol, and erases the actual victims, whose lives were ordinary and whose destruction was squalid, in favour of an ancestress invented for present use. Federici's witch, the resister of enclosure and guardian of contraceptive knowledge, is not identical to the goddess movement figure Purkiss principally attacks, and the profile should keep that distinction. But the methodological warning transfers: where the record shows poverty and suspicion, a reading that finds organised resistance and a knowledge estate must produce evidence of them, and in the specialists' judgement the evidence produced has been thinner than the reading requires.

### What does Diane Purkiss object to in feminist retellings?

To the substitution of a usable ancestress for the women in the records. In The Witch in History she argues that reclaiming the witch as healer and rebel flatters the reclaimers, repeats the demonologists' habit of making the accused symbolic, and disappears the poor and elderly neighbours whom suspicion, not power, delivered to the courts.

What survives this battery is not nothing, and a profile that left the matter at demolition would fail its subject as surely as one that omitted the demolition. The gendered character of the persecution survives every objection: across most of Europe the accused were women in large majorities, the demonology was preoccupied with female sexuality and female generation, and no account of the trials that treats gender as incidental has carried the field. The legislative tightening around reproduction survives as documented fact, whatever share of it the trials explain. The book's insistence that the making of capitalism had a history written on bodies and on the organisation of reproduction, and not only on land titles and wage contracts, survives as a reorientation of the questions economic historians ask, visible in the work of scholars who dispute nearly every answer the book gives. And the colonial chapters' core observation, that persecution accompanied the destruction of communal orders in the Americas, survives in the weaker and defensible form of a parallel, even where the stronger claim of a single exported technology does not. What does not survive is the causal architecture in its strong form: the hunt as accumulation's instrument, timed to its needs, targeted at its obstacles, and counted in its millions. The book's defenders have sometimes replied that it should be read as a political mythopoeia rather than as archival history, but that defence concedes the specialists' case while changing the subject, and it sits badly with a text that argues from statutes, trials, and dates. The fairest summary a profile can give is that Caliban and the Witch is the most influential statement of a thesis whose individual components divide sharply under examination: its questions have been absorbed by the field that rejects most of its answers.

One further test the literature applies, and the functional form of the thesis struggles to pass, is the ending. Persecutions on the scale described do not stop by themselves; they are stopped, and the records show by whom. Across the seventeenth century the trials were choked by the legal systems that had conducted them: appellate courts, the Parlement of Paris preeminently, quashed local convictions and imposed evidentiary standards the panics could not meet; the Spanish Inquisition's central tribunal, after the Navarre affair, instructed its districts in a scepticism that effectively ended Spanish witch executions; English prosecutions withered as judges and juries grew reluctant; and the intellectual demolition of the demonological edifice proceeded from within theology and law as much as from any philosophy outside them. Levack's account of the decline gives the credit to judicial scepticism and procedural restraint, the self correction of an elite legal culture. The bearing on the thesis is uncomfortable. A persecution that functioned as an instrument of accumulation, at the height of accumulation's advance, was dismantled by the very state and legal apparatuses that the instrument reading casts as its operators, for reasons internal to their own professional culture, and at dates that precede the consolidation of the wage order it supposedly built. Instruments are discarded when their work is done; the work, on the book's own account, was nowhere near done in 1650 or 1700. The ending does not refute the claim that the trials had economic effects, everything has economic effects, but it dissolves the picture of a process steered by an order's requirements, and leaves the effects looking like the by products of a religious and legal episode whose dynamics were its own.

The defence available to the book should be stated as strongly as its proponents can state it, because the profile's grading means little if the defence is a straw figure. It runs as follows. The thesis was never, at its most careful, a claim about trials alone; its object is the whole assemblage of the transition, enclosure, bloody legislation, reproductive statutes, workhouse, and pyre together, and objections that isolate the trials' chronology from the assemblage's chronology attack a part for the whole. The mechanisms the specialists document, confessional discipline, legal procedure, climatic panic, are on this defence the local forms in which a general offensive against the old communal order was fought, not alternatives to it: the question is why those mechanisms converged, in those centuries, on the bodies of the poor and the fertility of women, and convergence is what the book's framework exists to explain. The book also has its champions inside the historical profession: Peter Linebaugh, whose own work on the commons and on the Atlantic working class shares its sources and its temper, championed it as a recovery of the history of expropriation that the academy had left to the movements, and the tradition of Marxist historiography from which it comes has generally received it as a classic of its kind. There is force in the assemblage defence, and the profile acknowledges it by having expounded the thesis at the level of the assemblage and not of the trials alone. But the defence carries a cost its users rarely count. A framework that can absorb every contrary mechanism as one of its local forms has made itself difficult to falsify, and difficulty of falsification is the precise methodological charge the specialists bring against the tradition this book represents. The assemblage reading survives the chronology objection by retreating to a level of generality at which chronology cannot reach it; whether what survives the retreat is still the book's thesis, or a mood in search of one, is the judgement the reader is now equipped to make, and the profile's own verdict is recorded in the table's fourth column and in the balance sheet that closes the article.

The range of feminist historical opinion is itself evidence here, and the profile should exhibit it rather than imply a united scholarly front against a lone dissenter. Anne Llewellyn Barstow's Witchcraze, published in 1994, is a feminist history of the persecutions that accepts the gendered frame entire: the trials, for Barstow, were a sex linked pogrom conducted through misogynist judicial process, and she says so in terms as strong as any in Federici. Yet Barstow's estimate of the dead, around one hundred thousand, is an order of magnitude below the movement figure, and her mechanism is the courtroom and the treatise rather than the requirements of accumulation; her witches are victims of a judiciary's misogyny, not targets of an economic strategy. The distance between Barstow and Federici is therefore not sympathy but explanation, and it is the distance that matters for a reader deciding what to believe: the gender of the persecution is common ground among feminist historians, while the function assigned to it, and the number assigned to it, divide precisely the scholars who share the book's politics. A profile that presented the objections as the resistance of an unsympathetic academy would be falsifying the record in the book's favour, which is a way of failing it.

The book's reception pattern completes the evidentiary picture. Its citations cluster where its framework is at home, in social theory, in feminist studies, in the literature of the movements and of the commons, while the journals and monographs of early modern history, the field whose archive it claims, engage it thinly and usually in passing. Reception is not refutation, and fields ignore books for reasons of fashion as well as of merit; but the pattern is the reverse of the one the book's standing in general culture suggests, and a journalist or student citing it as the historians' account of the trials is making an error of attribution that this profile exists to correct. The Salem literature supplies the pattern in miniature. Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum, in Salem Possessed, published in 1974, explained the most famous American outbreak through the factional geography of one village, its quarrels over land, ministry, and market orientation dividing accusers from accused along lines a map can draw. Federici's Salem, an episode in the expropriation of a commons, and the specialists' Salem, an episode in a village's civil war, are not easily reconciled, and the specialist account rests on the tax lists and the petitions. The profile's conclusion from the whole battery is neither that the book is worthless nor that the specialists are pedants. It is that the book's genre has been mis-shelved: a work of political theory with a historical argument, shelved and cited as a work of history, will be graded as a failure by the standards of the shelf it sits on and as a classic by the standards of the shelf it came from. The sections that remain grade it, as promised, by components.

The scepticism that ended the trials was not, as a decline narrative might suggest, a late arrival that dawned only when the panic had spent itself; the record preserves it operating at the persecution's height, in the person of the one inquisitor who looked. After the great Logroño trials of 1610, in which the Spanish Inquisition burned its largest group of witches, the inquisitor Alonso de Salazar Frías conducted a visitation of the Basque country, collecting the confessions the panic had produced and testing them against the places, the witnesses, and the physical claims they contained. His reports to the Suprema concluded that he had found no verified act of witchcraft at all, that the confessions dissolved on examination into dreams, suggestion, and the pressure of the proceedings, and his scepticism, institutionalised in the Inquisition's instructions of 1614, is the principal reason Spain's witch executions remained few while Germany's mounted into the thousands. The episode is the objections' perfect exhibit because it is internal to the machinery: the most powerful tribunal in Europe, at the height of its power and at the scene of its largest burning, examined the evidence by its own lights and reported that there was nothing there. A persecution administered by an order confident of its function does not produce Salazar. A persecution produced by panic, procedure, and belief, and corrigible by the first official who checked, is the literature's story in one dossier.

It remains to state, in the specialists' own composite form, the explanation of the persecution's gender that the literature offers in the thesis's place, since a profile that demolishes without reconstructing leaves the reader with a mystery the evidence does not actually contain. The composite runs as follows. The learned culture supplied a demonology in which women, constructed by theology as the weaker and more carnal sex, figured as witchcraft's natural recruits, and the treatises said so in as many words. The social order supplied the candidates: women, and especially poor and post menopausal women, lived in the dependence and friction of the village economy, begging, nursing, laying out the dead, exposed to the resentments that misfortune converts into suspicion. The reproductive life of the community supplied the occasions, the births and sickbeds and failed butter and impotent marriages around which, as Roper showed, the fantasies crystallised. And the legal machinery supplied the multiplier, converting, where torture and inquisitorial process ruled, a village's suspicion into a jurisdiction's pyres. No element of the composite requires an economic strategy, and the composite explains, as the thesis does not, the variation: intense where the machinery and the confession aligned, mild where either was missing, male in its victims where the local magical trades were male. The book's defenders are entitled to ask whether the composite explains the timing of the whole, why these elements converged in those centuries; the literature's honest answer is that convergence questions are the hardest in its discipline and that a difficulty shared is not a victory transferred. The thesis asked the right scale of question. The composite is what the evidence, so far, supports at that scale.

## The patriarchy of the wage

The concept that connects the campaign and the book, and that Federici chose as the title of her 2021 collection, deserves its own exposition, because it is the element of her work most likely to outlast the controversies around both. The patriarchy of the wage names a specific arrangement: the order in which the man's wage, paid to him individually for his labour, becomes the medium through which the whole household lives, so that the woman's unwaged labour in reproducing that household disappears behind the wage's apparent self sufficiency. The wage looks like the price of one person's work. On her analysis it is the income of a unit whose other worker is invisible, and the invisibility is not an oversight but the arrangement's operating principle: because the wage presents itself as complete, the labour that stretches it, cooks it into meals, converts it into a rested and socialised worker, need not be counted, costed, or commanded directly by capital at all. Capital obtains the reproduction of its workforce at no charge; the male worker obtains, in exchange for his dependence at work, a sphere of command at home; and the woman obtains her subsistence as a dependant, through a person rather than as of right. The phrase's bite lies in assigning the patriarchy an address inside the wage relation rather than treating male domination as a survival from before capitalism that capitalism merely inherited. On this account the wage did not dissolve an older patriarchy. It rebuilt it on new foundations, which is why the book's history spends its force on the centuries in which the wage became general.

### How does the wage conceal the work it depends on?

By presenting itself as the whole income of the worker while silently presupposing the unwaged labour that turns money into a maintained life. Meals, rest, care, and the raising of the next shift of workers are consumed as though the wage had purchased them. The concealment is structural: the wage form has no line on which the second worker's labour could appear.

The concept has limits that its critics within the tradition have marked, and the profile should mark them rather than leave the phrase as an incantation. A wage can be, and for many women has been, the instrument of escape from the arrangement the concept describes: her own wage is precisely what a woman needs in order not to live through a man's, and the feminism that fought for women's employment was not simply deceived about its interests. The concept also averages over households that differ enormously, by class and by country, in how far the single male wage ever described them; for the poorest, women's and children's waged work was never absent to be concealed. Federici's own later work complicates the phrase productively, turning toward the commons and toward forms of reproduction organised outside both wage and household, which reads in retrospect as an acknowledgment that the wage's patriarchy is a stage to be exited rather than a fate to be described. None of this dissolves the concept's core service, which is diagnostic: it explains why the demand of 1972 had to be a wage demand. If the injury is administered through the wage form, then the counter move must address the wage form, and a politics that asks only for kindness within the household, or for women's admission to waged work on the existing terms, has located the injury in the wrong place.

The concept also has a documented career inside the labour movement, which supplies the nearest thing to a historical test of it. The family wage, the demand that a man's wage suffice to maintain a dependent wife and children, was a central objective of nineteenth and early twentieth century trade unionism, and historians have fought over exactly the question Federici's concept poses: whose interest the arrangement served. Heidi Hartmann, in "Capitalism, Patriarchy, and Job Segregation by Sex", published in 1976, argued that the family wage and the exclusion of women from skilled trades were the settlement by which male workers and capital composed their conflict at women's expense, preserving men's domestic command while supplying capital with a reproduced workforce and a reserve of cheap female labour for the jobs men did not defend. Jane Humphries, writing in the Cambridge Journal of Economics in 1977, answered that the working class family's defence of the breadwinner wage and of the withdrawal of wives and children from the mills was a protection of the family's collective interest against capital's drive to employ every member at subsistence, and that the male worker's supposed patriarchal dividend has to be set against the alternative the market actually offered. The exchange is the concept's natural habitat: both sides agree that the wage form organised the subordination and the reproduction of the class together, and disagree about the distribution of benefit and blame within it. Federici's phrase survives the exchange in the way good concepts do, not by winning it but by having named the object the exchange is about: a patriarchy whose medium is not custom or law alone but the wage packet itself.

For the concept to work as more than a slogan it also needs its observable implications, and the profile should name them, since the empirical literature has quietly supplied several. If the wage conceals a second worker's labour, then households should behave as two worker units while being counted as one, and the time use record shows exactly that: the total working hours of households, waged and unwaged summed, exceed what the wage statistics report, with the excess falling overwhelmingly on women. If the concealment is structural rather than incidental, then the entry of women into waged work should not abolish the unwaged burden but displace and compress it, which is the finding the literature on the second shift consolidated. And if the arrangement is administered through the wage form rather than through direct command, then its crises should appear as crises of the wage's sufficiency, strikes over the cost of living, campaigns over family allowances and benefits, which is a recognisable description of a good deal of modern labour politics. A concept that generates checkable implications of this kind has earned its place independently of the fortunes of the witch hunt thesis, which is the position the table's fourth column records.

The phrase has had a working life beyond its author's pages, and tracing it briefly is the best evidence of the concept's independent strength. The literature on the care economy, on the family wage's persistence inside welfare states, and on the male breadwinner model's slow decomposition across the rich countries repeatedly reaches for the same thought in different vocabularies: that the wage and the benefit systems built around it presuppose and enforce a division of labour they officially deny. Scandinavian feminist political science made the cognate argument about the state, debating whether the welfare state had replaced the husband as the patriarchal provider or had emancipated women from dependence on both. Federici's version remains the most economical of the family, because it assigns the mechanism to a single form, the wage, rather than to a structure with many chambers; and economy in a concept, as in a tool, is a virtue that survives the arguments about its first application. Whether the witch hunts built that form is the historical question this profile has graded negatively in its strong version. That the form exists and conceals what the concept says it conceals is a claim about the present organisation of work that the concept's career suggests many readers, across hostile traditions, have found they could not do without.

The family wage campaigns of the organised labour movement supply the concept's most concrete historical theatre after the hunting centuries, and they deserve a place in the exposition because they show the arrangement being built by negotiation rather than by terror. Across the nineteenth century, in Britain and the United States emblematically, male trade unionists demanded not merely higher wages but a wage of a specific form: enough for a man to keep a wife at home. The demand was double faced, and the historiography has kept both faces in view. Toward capital it was a defence of the working class household against the hunger that drove wives and children into the mills at wages that undercut the men's; toward women it was a settlement that priced the husband's labour as the family's and the wife's as supplementary, building the dependence the concept names into the wage bargain itself. Federici's phrase earns its keep in this theatre because it identifies the continuity between the two moments her work covers: the order installed, on her account, amid the enclosures and the trials reappears, in the industrial centuries, as the labour movement's own demand, carried now by the organised strength of the men it positioned as earners. A patriarchy that the subordinated class's organisations themselves demand is a sturdier thing than one imposed from above, and the concept's bleakness lies in that observation. The campaign of 1972 can be read, in this light, as the first organised refusal of the settlement from inside the class that had struck it: the wives declining, at last, to be the unpriced term of the bargain.

The concept's lineage inside Marx's own text should be registered, because it is stronger than the campaign's rhetoric sometimes suggested and it disciplines the concept's use. The chapter of Capital on the transformation of the value of labour power into wages argues that the wage form extinguishes every trace of the division of the working day between the hours that replace the wage and the hours that yield the surplus, so that all the worker's labour appears as paid labour. Federici's phrase extends that analysis across the household's threshold: as the wage form conceals the unpaid portion of the waged worker's day, so it conceals, at one further remove, the unpaid labour of the worker who reproduces him, whose day has no wage form to divide and therefore no division to conceal. The extension is genuinely an extension and not a quotation, since Marx did not make it; but it proceeds by his method, the critique of a form's appearance, and it explains the concept's durability among readers who share nothing else of her politics. A form criticism travels. The patriarchy of the wage is, in the end, a form criticism of the household's accounts, and its force is the force of the original: the demonstration that what a form shows, the self sufficient wage, is the opposite of what it does, which is to organise an extraction it renders invisible.

## Federici and social reproduction theory: the value question

Readers meeting Federici through reading lists often meet her filed under social reproduction theory, and the filing is natural and wrong in an instructive way. The theories share an object, the reproduction of labour power, and a refusal to treat that object as external to capitalism. They divide on a question the domestic labour debate posed and never fully settled: whether the labour of reproduction produces value. The autonomist feminism from which Federici comes, following Dalla Costa, answered yes in the strong form: housework produces labour power, labour power is a commodity, therefore housework produces the value bearing commodity and stands inside the production of surplus value, its unwaged status being the measure of its exploitation rather than a sign of its externality. Lise Vogel, whose Marxism and the Oppression of Women of 1983 is the founding text of the position that became social reproduction theory, answered differently. On Vogel's reconstruction, domestic labour is necessary labour for the reproduction of the workforce but is not performed under the value form: it is not exchanged, not socially averaged, and so produces use values and reproduces the bearer of labour power without itself creating value, a distinction she grounded in a close reading of Capital's categories. Tithi Bhattacharya and the contributors to the 2017 collection Social Reproduction Theory developed Vogel's line into the framework now dominant in the field, within which Federici is honoured as a precursor and read, on the value question, as having taken the other side.

### On which question do Federici and Vogel divide?

On whether reproductive labour produces value. Federici, following Dalla Costa, holds that housework produces labour power itself and so belongs to value production, its lack of a wage marking the depth of its exploitation. Vogel holds that domestic labour reproduces the worker without taking the value form, necessary to capital but not itself value creating.

The division is not scholastic, because the campaign's strategy hangs on it. If housework produces value, the wage demand is the demand that a theft be priced, and the refusal horizon follows from the standpoint of the producing worker. If it does not, the politics of reproduction needs another grounding, and social reproduction theory has supplied one in the analysis of how capital free rides on life making activities it does not organise. Federici's distinctness within the broader field also runs beyond the value question to temperament and method: her work is historical and movement facing where the dominant theory is systematic, and her category of refusal, inherited from workerism, has no real equivalent in a framework built to map a structure rather than to interrupt it. The series treats social reproduction theory in its own article, and the comparison here is meant only to fix the coordinates a reader needs: same object, shared refusal of the private sphere's naturalisation, opposite answers on value, and a politics of interruption on the one side against a politics of structural mapping on the other. Filing Federici as a social reproduction theorist without the qualifier erases exactly the disagreement her own tradition was founded on.

Vogel's construction deserves a paragraph of its own, because the difference between the two positions is easily caricatured and the caricature cheats the student. Vogel began from a dissatisfaction with the dual systems theories of the nineteen seventies, in which patriarchy and capitalism figured as two structures in alliance, the position Heidi Hartmann's famous essay on the unhappy marriage of Marxism and feminism had stated most sharply. Against that dualism Vogel proposed a unitary account grounded in the reproduction of labour power: the working class must be renewed daily and generationally, that renewal requires labour performed largely by women outside the wage relation, and the subordination of women within capitalism is rooted in the organisation of that necessary labour and in men's interest in controlling it, rather than in a second system with laws of its own. Domestic labour, on her careful reconstruction of the categories, belongs to the necessary labour of society without taking the value form, since it is neither exchanged nor subject to the social averaging that constitutes value. The elegance of the position is that it explains the oppression inside one theory; its cost, from the autonomist side, is that the housewife disappears as a producing worker and reappears as a functionary of reproduction, a figure easier to analyse than to organise. Bhattacharya's development of the framework pressed its advantage outward, mapping the institutions, schools, hospitals, households, and the state, through which labour power is produced, and insisting that the map changes what counts as the working class and where its struggles occur. The field's centre of gravity now sits with that development, and Federici's position within it is that of a honoured dissenter: the precursor whose two deviations, the value claim and the politics of refusal, mark the boundary of the consensus.

Her own later divergence from both positions should also be registered, since it is where her work is still moving. The turn to the commons, visible across Re-enchanting the World and the essays on reproduction and the commoning of care, proposes that the answer to the wage's patriarchy is neither the wage for housework nor the mapping of reproduction under capital, but the construction of reproductive commons: forms of collective self provisioning, communal kitchens and gardens and care networks, that withdraw reproduction from both the market and the privatised household. Critics within her own tradition have asked whether commons built under conditions of scarcity can bear the weight the proposal loads on them, and whether the romance of commoning understates the labour, overwhelmingly female, that commons themselves require. The question is live and the profile does not settle it. What can be said is that the trajectory is consistent with the pamphlet's logic at a distance of four decades: the refusal horizon of 1975 has become, in the later work, a construction horizon, and the demand as lever has been succeeded by the commons as prefiguration, the second strategy answering the reception objection to the first by building the alternative rather than demanding it.

The convergences between the two positions deserve a paragraph of their own, lest the division be mistaken for a divorce. Both refuse the production and reproduction split as a fact of nature; both treat the boundary between the economy and the household as a political artefact maintained by force and by ideology; both have been used, by the movements of the following century, as warrants for treating care workers, teachers, nurses, and mothers as one class of labour for the purposes of struggle. The feminist strike, discussed in this profile's balance sheet, is unintelligible without the shared premise, and its organisers cite both traditions without anxiety about the value question, which suggests the question's practical weight is smaller than its theoretical weight. A profile is not required to regret that. It is required to report that the division is real where the theory is made precise, that it concerns the status of the commodity labour power itself, and that Federici's side of it is the one the campaign's strategy needed. Scholarship and strategy have different tolerances, and the same thinker can be, as here, indispensable to the second and heterodox in the first.

The divergence extends to the state, and a reader comparing the two literatures will trip over it. The developed social reproduction framework assigns the state a constitutive role in organising reproduction, through schools, hospitals, migration law, and the regulation of households, and its analyses of austerity read the state's withdrawal from provision as the transfer of reproductive costs back onto families and communities. Federici's commons turn shares the diagnosis and refuses the implied addressee: where the theory maps the state's management the better to contest it, her later writing invests in the construction of reproductive arrangements that bypass the state and the market together, in the communal kitchens, gardens, and care networks of the movements she documents from Latin America and Africa. The difference is the old one between her formation and Vogel's, restated at the level of strategy: an autonomist suspicion that institutions conceded by the state arrive with the state's terms attached, against a structural analysis that sees no outside from which to build. The profile notes the fork without grading it, since the evidence that would grade it, the career of the commons experiments themselves, is still being accumulated by the movements conducting them.

A third position on housework, developed in the same decade by the French materialist feminists, fixes Federici's coordinates by triangulating her, and a student of the cluster should have it. Christine Delphy, in The Main Enemy, published in French in 1970 and in English in 1977, argued that married women's domestic labour is exploited neither by capital nor in reproduction's antechamber but directly by husbands, within a distinct domestic mode of production in which the husband appropriates the wife's labour power entire and returns her maintenance. On Delphy's account patriarchy is not a department of capitalism but a second material system with its own relations of production, and the housewife's enemy is in the first instance the man who consumes her labour. The three positions can now be laid side by side. For Delphy, housework is exploited labour inside a separate domestic system, and its appropriator is the husband. For Dalla Costa and Federici, it is value producing labour inside the capitalist system, and its appropriator is capital, which sets the husband as its overseer. For Vogel, it is necessary labour outside the value form, and its subordination is organised through the reproduction of the workforce as a whole. The same kitchen, three incompatible maps of it: the experiment that would decide among them has never been run, because the maps differ over the theory of value itself, which is why the argument of the nineteen seventies remains the live framework of the field rather than its prehistory, and why this profile has insisted on stating each position's mechanism rather than its conclusion. Federici's map is the only one of the three that makes the wage demand a theorem's corollary, which is at once the source of the demand's intellectual force and of its exposure to Himmelweit and Mohun.

Both sides of the value division claim the same chapter of Capital, and the shared ground is worth a paragraph because it shows the dispute is exegetical before it is political. In the chapter on simple reproduction, Marx argues that the worker's individual consumption, the eating, resting, and family life by which labour power is daily restored, is itself a moment of the reproduction of capital, performed for the capitalist whether the worker enjoys it or not; the maintenance and propagation of the working class, he writes, form a necessary condition of the system, which the capitalist may safely leave to the worker's drives of self preservation and propagation. Federici's tradition reads the passage as an admission that reproduction is inside the process and then asks why the labour that performs it should stand outside the value account. Vogel's tradition reads the same passage as observing the boundary precisely: the consumption is the worker's own act, its labour uncommanded and unexchanged, a condition of the process rather than a moment of its valorisation. The text underdetermines the dispute, as founding texts do, and the field's subsequent history, in which the question migrated from the journals of the seventies into the architecture of social reproduction theory, is the history of two legitimate readings hardening into two research programmes. A student who grasps that the schism is over a chapter's implication has grasped more than a student who can recite either programme's conclusions.

## Reading Federici: the pamphlet, the book, and the later essays

The corpus is small enough to read whole and badly served by the habit of reading only its most famous item. Federici's own order of composition is also the best order of approach. The pamphlet of 1975, Wages Against Housework, survives as the first text of Revolution at Point Zero, and it gives the reader the lever argument in a few pages, at the temperature at which it was written. The campaign essays collected around it in the same volume, on counter planning from the kitchen and on reproduction and feminist struggle, show the argument being applied and adjusted across the seventies. Caliban and the Witch then presents itself as what it is: the genealogy those essays presupposed, written at the length the genealogy required. The later collections can be taken as codas. Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women returns to the hunts briefly and polemically, and is best read as the author's own abstract of the larger book rather than as a substitute for it. Patriarchy of the Wage gathers the essays in which the connecting concept is examined against Marx's texts directly. Re-enchanting the World moves the argument's centre to the commons and to the reproductive commons of the colonised and the poor, where her Nigerian years and the African work register most clearly.

### Which short text should a new reader open first?

The 1975 pamphlet, Wages Against Housework, now read most conveniently at the head of Revolution at Point Zero. It is brief, it states the lever argument without mediation, and it arms the reader to grade everything else she wrote, including the history, by the standard of the demand it explains: exposure first, refusal as the horizon.

Reading the history with the objections in hand is not a counsel of hostility; it is how the book's genuine strengths become visible. A reader who knows where the chronology objection bites can see which parts of the argument were never load bearing, and can watch the reproductive legislation chapters do their documentary work without needing the trials to have been an instrument of accumulation. A reader who knows the figures protocol can register the scale of the persecution accurately, which is to say can be properly horrified by tens of thousands rather than improperly thrilled by millions. The pamphlet, by contrast, should be read with its hostile feminist critics in hand, Davis above all, because the exchange between the lever and the reception objection is the most instructive few pages in the whole literature of the campaign, and neither side of it is dispensable.

Practical guidance on editions and pairings will save readers some wrong turns. The pamphlet should be read in Revolution at Point Zero rather than in the photocopied excerpts that circulate online, because the collection restores its context among the campaign essays and lets the reader watch the argument being applied to welfare, to the factory, and to the communes across the decade. Caliban and the Witch exists in a single English edition, and its chapters reward being read against the grain of their own order: the reproductive chapters before the narrative chapters, the table in this profile open beside them. The most instructive pairing for the history is with the eighth part of the first volume of Capital, Marx's own chapters on the expropriation of the agricultural population and the bloody legislation, read immediately before Federici's opening movement, since the book is best understood as a rewriting of those chapters with the missing half of the population restored, and the places where her evidence will not carry the rewriting become visible exactly at the joints. The most instructive pairing for the campaign is with Women, Race and Class, for the reasons the objection section gave. Translations of the history into Spanish, Italian, German, Portuguese, and other languages carried it into the feminist movements of Latin America and southern Europe with particular force, and readers in those traditions sometimes meet the book before the pamphlet, an order this profile recommends reversing wherever the texts allow.

Two starting points besides the pamphlet deserve recommendation for readers with particular errands. The theorist's Federici begins one step earlier, with Dalla Costa and James's The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community, the founding essay in which the claim about labour power is argued at its most systematic; Federici's pamphlet presupposes it, and reading the two together shows the division of labour inside the campaign, the Padua theory and the New York strategy, with unusual clarity. The historian's Federici begins with the movement literature of her formation, the Midnight Notes journals in which the new enclosures analysis was worked out collectively, because the book's method, its selection of evidence and its silences alike, is legible in that milieu as it is nowhere else. Both errands end at the same place, the recognition that this is a corpus written from inside struggles it intended to arm, and that its errors as well as its insights bear the mark of that intention.

The book's geography of reception carries one more lesson for the reader planning an approach. Its Spanish translation made it, across the following decade, a central text of the feminisms of Latin America, where the movements against femicide and for the legalisation of abortion found in its pages a genealogy for their own analysis of violence against women, and where its account of the colonial extirpations spoke to a history nearer the surface than Europe's. Verónica Gago and the Argentine movement intellectuals around the feminist strike engaged it as a living framework rather than a monument. A European reader puzzled by the book's temperature, its refusal of scholarly distance, will understand it better for knowing that much of its most serious readership reads it as a theory of their present. That dual citizenship, monograph in one hemisphere and manifesto in another, is not a confusion to be cleared up but the fact about the book that any honest profile must finally report, since the standards by which it is graded differ by the citizenship under which it is read, and this profile has graded it under both.

A concrete protocol for the solitary reader can be extracted from everything above, and the Reading section is the place to leave it. Read the pamphlet first, in an hour. Then read the book's reproductive chapters with Levack's survey open at the chapters on the peak and decline of the prosecutions, and let the two texts argue in the margins; the exercise costs an evening and teaches more about the difference between a thesis and a record than a semester of summaries. Then read the campaign's critics, Davis at their head, and return to the pamphlet to see whether it survives them; this profile's judgement is that it survives the intention charge entire and the reception charge in part, and a reader who has done the circuit will be equipped to grade that judgement rather than inherit it. The corpus is short. The circuit is the education.

Her relation to Marx himself, worked out across the essays gathered in Patriarchy of the Wage, is the last piece of the corpus a serious reader needs, because it explains the tradition's double posture of fidelity and correction. The essays take from Marx the method this profile has used throughout: the critique of forms, the refusal of natural categories, the insistence that the wage, the market, and the family are historical constructions with birthdays. They charge him, in the same breath, with having left the largest construction of all unexamined: the labour of reproduction appears in his work as a natural presupposition, performed offstage by figures whose work is not analysed, so that the critique of political economy reproduces, at its foundation, the invisibility it exists to expose. The charge is made from inside the method and is the tradition's founding gesture restated as scholarship: Dalla Costa's essay was already, in 1972, a completion of Capital by its own logic against its author. Readers deciding what to make of the whole corpus could do worse than hold that gesture in view, since it predicts both the work's strengths and its characteristic failure. A thinker whose method is to find the hidden labour in every settled account will find it, brilliantly, where it is; the risk, which the witch hunt chapters realise, is of finding it where the record will not follow, and of treating the method's fertility as the record's consent.

The pamphlet's own publication history is a small parable of the corpus's fortunes and closes the Reading section fittingly. Wages Against Housework appeared in 1975 from a Bristol feminist press in an edition measured in thousands, circulated through the movement's bookshops and conferences, was translated within the campaign's own networks, and then, as the committees dispersed, survived the way movement texts survive, in photocopies, in the bibliographies of sympathetic scholars, and in the memory of the women who had organised with it. Its republication in 2012 at the head of Revolution at Point Zero, by a movement publisher with international distribution, returned it to print in a different world, one in which its central perception had been absorbed by the scholarship and its central demand was being rediscovered by a new feminist generation as a found manuscript rather than a living claim. The thirty seven year interval between the pamphlet's editions is the material form of the profile's argument about absorption: the text was unavailable for exactly the decades in which its ideas were becoming common sense, so that the common sense arrived unattributed and the text, when it returned, could be received as a revelation. Readers who take the profile's advice and begin with it are participating in the second of its lives, and should know that the first was lived in rooms most of them will never see described.

## What the profile leaves standing

Two contributions, then, differently weathered. The demand survives as an analytical instrument: the demand as lever, a demand designed to change what an activity is perceived to be, has passed into the general equipment of movement politics and of the theory that studies it, and the profile's grading of its reception problem explains both its power and its limit. The campaign's afterlife in later movements and in the continuing argument over care is traced in the series' article on [marxism and social movements](/2022/04/01/marxism-and-social-movements/), where the demand's descendants can be compared with the original. The book survives as the most influential statement of a contested thesis: absorbed as a set of questions by the historiography that rejects most of its answers, canonical within the tradition surveyed by the [marxist feminism complete guide](/2016/09/01/marxist-feminism-complete-guide/), and permanently accompanied, for honest readers, by the objections this profile has stated. Neither standing is the one the admirers' summaries advertise, in which the campaign is a quaint policy proposal and the book a settled classic. Both standings are more interesting than that, and more durable: a strategy that still teaches strategists how a demand can be built, and a history whose errors are the kind a field learns from because the questions were large enough to be worth refuting. The series pattern this profile instantiates is the demand as lever pattern, and its moral generalises beyond its subject: judge a demand by the perception it was built to change, and judge a thesis by the components into which it can be divided, some of which will stand.

The demand's most consequential descendant illustrates the pattern's reach and its transformation. The international feminist strikes that began in 2017, in which women in dozens of countries withdrew together from waged work, from housework, and from consumption, were built by movements that named the wages for housework tradition among their sources, and their method is the lever turned into a calendar: a stoppage designed less to win a claim than to make visible, by its absence, the totality of the work women perform. Verónica Gago's account of the feminist strike, in the book published in English as Feminist International, theorises exactly this descent, treating the strike as the instrument by which the unwaged and the waged discover their continuity. The reception objection has not vanished in the descendant; the strikes are misreported annually as protests about pay gaps alone. But the lever's designers would recognise the situation, and the persistence of the pattern across half a century, in the teeth of the same misreading, is the closest thing to a controlled experiment the history of demands provides: the device works on the audiences organised to receive it, and the organising, as the campaign always insisted, is the demand's other half.

The witch herself, finally, has had the afterlife the book predicted for her, and it belongs in the balance sheet because it is evidence about the thesis's cultural truth as distinct from its archival standing. The figure reclaimed by the feminism of the late nineteen sixties, when a New York collective of 1968 took the acronym of the witch for its name and hexed the institutions of the city in theatre, was reclaimed again by the movements the book itself armed: the witch on the banners of the feminist strikes, the witch in the iconography of the campaigns against femicide in Latin America, the witch as the generic emblem of the woman who will not be governed in her reproduction. Federici did not invent this emblem and her book is not its origin, but the book gave the emblem a theory, a history, and a body count, and the movements have kept the theory and the emblem while quietly dropping the body count to the figure the banner cannot carry. A profile should notice the transaction for what it is: the culture grading the book exactly as this profile has, component by component, keeping the question and the concept, discarding the chronology and the millions. The academy and the street, for once, have marked the same components down.

The honest admirer and the honest critic of this body of work can now be given their lines, since the profile has assembled the materials for both and the difference between them is narrower than the polemics suggest. The honest admirer will say: here is a thinker who identified, earlier and more clearly than the academy, the mechanism by which half the world's labour is made invisible, who built a demand that taught two generations to see it, and whose historical imagination forced a field to defend its silences; that the demand survives as method and the book as provocation is a career most theorists would sign for. The honest critic will say: the demand's designers mistook the control they had over their text for control over its career, and the book's author mistook the coherence of her framework for evidence, so that the two works repeat one error at two scales, the confusion of a construction's elegance with its warrant. Both speeches are true, and they are true of the same property: a mind that builds instruments, levers, concepts, total histories, and trusts the building. The profile's final judgement is that the trust was better placed in the small instrument than in the large one. The lever still works wherever it is picked up, on the picket lines of the care economy and in the strike movements of the present century. The history works only where its components are disassembled and retested one by one, which is to say it works as this profile has used it, as a table and not as a tale. That asymmetry, between the instrument that travels intact and the edifice that must be dismantled to be used, is the finding the profile hands on, and it generalises as the series pattern predicts: demands should be graded by the perceptions they were built to change, and theses by the components into which they divide, because the two kinds of object fail in opposite ways when they are graded by each other's tests.

The demand also has a bureaucratic afterlife, unglamorous and real, which completes the evidence for its survival as an instrument. Marilyn Waring, a New Zealand parliamentarian turned economist, published If Women Counted in 1988, an indictment of the system of national accounts for counting the destruction of forests as income while counting the rearing of the human race as nothing; the book carried the campaign's central perception, that the official measures of the economy are built to render women's work invisible, into the one arena the campaign never reached, the statistical office. The decades since have institutionalised the perception in forms the pamphlet would scarcely recognise: time use surveys conducted by national governments, satellite accounts that price unpaid household production alongside the measured economy, and a standing literature in feminist economics for which the invisibility of care is the founding problem. None of this is the wage, and none of it aims at refusal; it is the lever's disclosure function, detached from the lever and fitted with dials. A judgement of the campaign that counts only its defeats will miss the strangeness of that outcome, in which the demand's enemies administer its insight daily. The profile counts it as the demand's third fate, alongside caricature and absorption into movement method: translation into the bookkeeping of the state it was designed to accuse.

## For students and exam candidates

Begin with the two mistakes this profile exists to prevent, because examiners on this material are alert to both. The first is describing wages for housework as a proposal to pay housewives; the second is citing Caliban and the Witch as though its thesis were the consensus of early modern historians. A script that states the lever argument, names Davis's objection, states the chronology and accusation findings with their authors, and distinguishes the components of the thesis from one another is already doing the evaluative work the bands reward. The comparison with the mainstream theoretical position belongs in the article on [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/), which should be read next, and the claim and evidence table in this profile transfers well into structured notes: the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) prepared for the series include a card built on the table's four columns, so that each component can be carried with its objection attached rather than as a slogan. On the figures question, observe the protocol this profile observes: give the range, name the scholars whose totals compose it, and state the provenance of the inflated figure before any examiner can supply it against you. An answer that volunteers the correction reads as mastery; an answer that repeats the nine million reads as a warning.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The 1975 pamphlet is the natural set text: short enough for a single seminar, sharp enough to divide a room productively, and unintelligible unless the lever argument is grasped, which gives discussion a definite object. Pair it with the chapter of Women, Race and Class in which Davis argues for socialising housework, and the seminar replays the movement's own argument at its best level. The technical substructure, the question whether domestic labour produces value, is best handled through the article on [the domestic labour debate](/2016/10/01/domestic-labour-debate/), set as preparation rather than debated cold. The Salazar visitation, recounted in the objections, makes an unmatched primary source exercise: students given the inquisitor's findings without his name reliably reproduce the scepticism he reached, and the discovery that the document is four centuries old rearranges their sense of the period more efficiently than any lecture. For the history sessions, resist excerpting the book without its critics: a pairing of Federici's reproductive chapters with Harley on the midwife-witch teaches more historical method than either text alone, and the figures paragraph of this profile can be set as a short exercise in source criticism, since the nine million figure's provenance is traceable by students themselves.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier this profile leaves is the gap between the book's questions and the archive's answers: the reproductive legislation is documented at the level of statutes, thinly at the level of enforcement, and the household and wage records that could test the patriarchy of the wage as a historical claim remain underexploited. The conceptual background for that work is the article on [primitive accumulation explained](/2011/01/01/primitive-accumulation-explained/), whose category the book extends and whose evidentiary standards the extension must meet. Journalists covering the continuing politics of care will find the contemporary form of the argument in the article on [care work and capitalism](/2016/12/01/care-work-and-capitalism/), and should take from this profile two disciplines: attribute the historians' objections to their authors rather than to a consensus in the air, and never repeat a casualty figure for the persecutions that cannot be traced to the trial based literature. The standing scholarly range, and the provenance of the inflated figure, are stated in the objections sections above and are the minimum equipment for writing about the subject in public. For the research community the profile's open bequest is the enforcement gap: the reproductive statutes are catalogued, their prosecution records are not, and the scholar who works them will be answering, with new evidence, the question this profile has had to leave at the level of the laws' existence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who is Silvia Federici?

An Italian-American feminist theorist of the autonomist Marxist current, born in Parma in 1942. She helped found the International Feminist Collective in 1972 and the New York committee of the wages for housework campaign the year after, wrote the campaign's sharpest pamphlet in 1975, and published Caliban and the Witch in 2004, the historical work on which much of her later reputation rests. She taught in Nigeria in the nineteen eighties and for many years at Hofstra University, and co-founded the Committee for Academic Freedom in Africa. Her subject throughout is the control of reproduction: how unwaged work is hidden, and how that hiding was historically built.

### Q: What was the Wages for Housework campaign?

An international feminist campaign launched in 1972 through the International Feminist Collective, with committees in Italy, the United States, Britain, Canada, and other countries. Its founding text was the 1972 essay by Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James arguing that housework produces labour power. The campaign demanded that the state pay wages for domestic work, addressing the claim to the state rather than to husbands because, on its analysis, capital as a whole was the beneficiary of unwaged reproduction. It worked through pamphlets, conferences, and argument inside the women's movement rather than through legislatures, and it was disputed by other feminists from its earliest years.

### Q: Did Federici actually want wages for housework?

As an end in itself, no. The 1975 pamphlet Wages Against Housework presents the demand as a device: unwaged domestic labour escapes notice as labour because it is classified as love, and attaching a money claim to it breaks that classification open. Establishing that the work is work puts its performer in the position of a worker, from which the work's hours, organisation, and very existence can be contested. The pay was therefore instrumental to a strategy whose horizon was refusal of the work in its existing form. Critics at the time answered that a demand is heard as its surface suggests whatever its designers intend, an objection about reception that restating the design does not remove.

### Q: What is Caliban and the Witch about?

Published in 2004, it argues that the making of capitalism in Europe and its colonies included a war against women that standard accounts of primitive accumulation omit. Enclosure, in her account, destroyed the commons based independence of peasant women; the witch persecutions of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries then broke female control over reproduction, knowledge, and collective action; and the outcome was a sexual division of labour in which women's work could be treated as a natural resource rather than as labour. The book ranges from the late medieval resistance movements to the colonisation of the Americas, and it remains her most widely read and most disputed work.

### Q: Did the witch hunts serve capitalism?

That is the book's thesis rather than a finding of the specialist literature. Federici argues that the persecutions functioned within the transition to capitalism, disciplining reproduction and devaluing women as the new order required. Historians of the trials answer that the pattern of prosecution follows other variables more closely: the intensity of persecution maps onto fragmented jurisdictions, confessional frontiers, and courts empowered to torture, and its peaks do not align with the regional advance of agrarian capitalism. England, commercially advanced, executed comparatively few; parts of the German lands, Scotland, and Poland executed many. The honest summary is that the hunts coincided with the transition, were saturated with hostility to women, and have not been shown to have been its instrument.

### Q: How many people were executed in the witch persecutions?

No exact total exists, and the protocol for contested figures is ranges with sources. Syntheses built on the trial records, Levack's and Behringer's among them, converge on the order of one hundred thousand prosecutions in Europe across the period and executions in the tens of thousands, commonly forty to sixty thousand. The far larger figures in circulation, nine million being the famous one, descend from an extrapolation published in the seventeen eighties by Gottfried Christian Voigt and popularised in the following century; the archival literature does not support them. Any single precise number quoted without its range and source should be treated as a warning sign.

### Q: What do historians say about Caliban and the Witch?

Specialists in the early modern period acknowledge the book's influence and its questions while disputing much of its evidence handling. The objections with names attached include Levack's chronology, on which the trials' peak decades track legal and confessional change rather than capitalist development; Briggs's finding that accusations welled up from neighbourhood conflict, with women prominent among accusers; and Harley's demonstration that midwives seldom appear among the accused and often served the prosecution. Feminist historians such as Purkiss add that the book's witch, a resister guarding forbidden knowledge, flatters modern readers at the expense of the poor and elderly women in the records. The book is therefore usually described as a landmark of feminist theory whose historical claims require corroboration elsewhere.

### Q: How does Federici differ from social reproduction theory?

They study the same object, the daily and generational renewal of the workforce, and part on whether the labour involved creates value. Federici's tradition, following Dalla Costa, maintains that housework produces the commodity labour power and so belongs within value production, with its wagelessness measuring how completely it is exploited. The theory associated with Lise Vogel and developed by Tithi Bhattacharya holds that domestic labour renews the worker without itself passing through the value form, necessary to capital yet not value creating. A second difference is temperamental: her framework carries the workerist politics of refusal, where the mainstream theory is built to map a structure.

### Q: What is the patriarchy of the wage?

Her name for the arrangement in which the individual male wage becomes the household's visible income and thereby acquires command over the unwaged labour that the wage silently presupposes. The money wage appears to support the family unaided; the cooking, cleaning, and care that convert money into a maintained worker and raised children appear nowhere in the account, so their performer lives as a dependant of the wage earner rather than as a worker in her own right. The concept locates male domination inside the modern wage relation rather than treating it as a leftover from an earlier order, and she later took the phrase as the title of a collection of essays.

### Q: Where should you start with Federici?

With the short pamphlet rather than the famous book. Wages Against Housework, written in 1975 and now placed at the front of the collection Revolution at Point Zero, sets out in a few pages the reasoning that everything else presupposes: that the demand was built to expose unwaged work as work and to open the way to refusing it. From there the campaign essays in the same volume show the argument at work, and Caliban and the Witch can then be read as the genealogy it attempts, ideally alongside the historians who contest it. The compact later volume Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women works as a summary once the larger book is known, not as a replacement for it.

### Q: How does the demand as lever grade a policy reading of the campaign?

Harshly, because the policy reading grades the campaign against a test its designers never set. If the demand is treated as a benefits proposal, its success would be measured by schemes enacted and incomes delivered, and the campaign scores near zero. Read as a lever, its test is different: did the claim alter what housework was publicly understood to be, and did it give the women performing it the standing of workers? Grading by the second test is not charity toward the campaign; it is applying the standard the artefact was built to meet, in the same way that a crowbar is graded on leverage rather than on whether it resembles a key.

### Q: What should a student separate between the demand's literal form and its function?

The sentence and the use. Literally, the campaign asked states to pay wages for domestic work, and a summary that stops there is accurate and misleading at once. Functionally, the request was engineered so that granting it would concede that housework is labour and refusing it would expose who benefits from its remaining unwaged. Students should hold the two apart in both directions: do not let the function launder the literal demand out of the record, since contemporaries had to respond to the literal words; and do not let the literal words settle the interpretation, since the texts explain, at length and in advance, what the words were for.

### Q: Why did a wage demand become a refusal strategy?

Because of where the wage sits in the analysis the campaign inherited from workerism. In that tradition a wage claim is not chiefly about the money; it is the act by which labour recognises itself as labour and acquires a collective standpoint against its conditions. Transposed to the household, demanding pay for housework was meant to convert an identity, the loving wife and mother, into a position, the unwaged worker, from which negotiation and refusal become thinkable. The money was the solvent applied to a naturalised identity. Hence the pamphlet's otherwise odd pairing of its demand with its title: wages against housework, the claim advanced in order to end the arrangement it priced.

### Q: How does the claim and evidence table grade the campaign's effects?

Strictly, it does not: the table in this profile grades the historical thesis, component by component, and importing its grades into the campaign's history would confuse two different audits. The campaign's effects are graded in the profile's prose, where the verdict is mixed and split by design. As a proposal in the world, the demand was received in the way its feminist critics predicted, as a scheme for paying housewives, and no legislature enacted it. As an instrument of perception it travelled far beyond the committees that built it, changing how housework could be discussed in movements and in scholarship. The two grades belong to the two tests, and the profile keeps them apart for the same reason the table keeps its columns apart.

### Q: What separates wages for housework from wages against housework?

A preposition carrying the whole strategy. Wages for housework names the demand as the campaign publicly made it, the claim that states should pay for domestic labour. Wages against housework, the title of Federici's 1975 pamphlet, names what the demand was for: the money claim was advanced as the opening move in refusing the work's existing form, not as a settlement that would leave wives in kitchens on improved terms. The campaign used the first phrase on its banners and the second on its sharpest text, and the distance between them is the distance between a policy and a lever. Readers who collapse the two phrases will misread both the campaign and its critics.
