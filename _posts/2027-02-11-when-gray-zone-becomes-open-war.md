The hardest question in the whole eastern-flank debate is not whether Russia would attack Poland. It is where the gray-zone campaign already running against Polish territory stops being coercion and starts being an armed attack, and who decides. That boundary carries enormous legal, political, and military weight, because it is what an alliance response turns on and what a national mobilization decision turns on. Yet no treaty text, no doctrine manual, and no body of practice sets it out with the precision a planner would want. The line exists in principle and dissolves in application, which is exactly the property a coercive actor exploits.

This matters because both of the obvious readings are wrong in dangerous ways. The first reading holds that a clear automatic trigger exists somewhere, that a sufficiently severe incident flips a switch, and that everyone will know the crossing when it happens. The second reading holds that no line exists at all, that the gray zone shades continuously into open conflict, and that arguing about boundaries is a lawyer's distraction from the real business of defense. A defender who believes the first will be surprised when a serious incident produces argument rather than action. A defender who believes the second will accumulate injuries without ever deciding that enough has happened, which is the slow-boiling failure that deniable campaigns are designed to produce.

![When gray-zone coercion becomes open war, a threshold and warning analysis for Poland - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

This article treats the crossing as a warning problem rather than a definitional one. Definitions matter, and the legal architecture gets its own treatment below, but the operational question for anyone responsible for Polish or allied decisions is not what the boundary is in the abstract. It is how you recognize an approach to it early enough to act deliberately rather than reactively. That reframing is what the indicators-and-warning discipline contributes here. The tradecraft developed for reading a conventional buildup, described in detail in the series treatment of [the warning signs of a Russian move on Poland](/2025/01/21/warning-signs-russian-move-on-poland/), transfers only partly to sub-threshold pressure, and the parts that do not transfer are the ones that cause failure.

The central argument advanced here can be stated as a rule. Call it the drawn-threshold rule: the gray zone is dangerous precisely because it has no bright line, so a defender must draw and signal its own boundary in advance, because a line defined only after it is crossed invites the slow approach that coercive actors exploit. This is not a legal claim about what constitutes an armed attack under the North Atlantic Treaty. It is a practical claim about defensive posture. A state that has decided in advance which categories of act it treats as a crossing, and has said so publicly enough that the message registers, converts an ambiguous accumulation into a series of decision points it controls. A state that has not done this leaves the pacing of the campaign entirely in the hands of the actor running it.

Everything that follows builds from that rule. The first sections establish what the boundary actually is and why it stays blurred, distinguishing the legal question from the political and the analytical. The middle sections supply the working framework: the difference between more of the same and something categorically new, and a structured ladder of act categories with the indicators that mark movement up it. The later sections turn to warning proper, covering how much lead time the picture realistically offers, why crossings get missed even by attentive services, and how an analyst separates a genuine boundary signal from the constant noise of a deniable campaign. The closing sections address the signaling problem, the legal and political stakes, and the honest disagreement between analysts who believe a bright line can be constructed and analysts who believe the continuum is irreducible.

A word on scope. This is a conceptual and analytical treatment throughout. It contains no operational content, no targeting logic, and no description of how any specific act would be carried out. The categories used here are the categories that appear in open policy discussion, and the reasoning stays at the level a minister, a staffer, an analyst, or a serious reader can use to think clearly about a decision. The parent treatment of the campaign as a whole is the series article on [Russia's gray-zone war on Poland](/2026/12/11/russias-gray-zone-war-on-poland/), which sets out the campaign logic this article assumes; the response side belongs to the companion treatment of [how Poland fights below the threshold](/2027/02/21/fighting-below-the-threshold/), and the canonical treatment of what the alliance obligation actually requires is the series article on [what Article 5 really obligates](/2025/11/11/what-article-5-really-obligates/), which this article defers to rather than restating.

## The Boundary Question Nobody Wants to Define Precisely

Start with the shape of the problem. In the conventional case, the distinction between peace and war is not usually hard to draw. Formations cross a border in strength, aircraft strike targets, a state announces or does not announce its intention, and the fact of armed attack is evident to anyone watching. The difficulty in the conventional case is warning, not classification: analysts argue about whether an attack is coming, not about whether one has occurred. Sub-threshold coercion inverts this. The acts are visible almost immediately, often within hours. What is contested is what they were, who did them, and whether they amount to anything more than the background friction of a hostile relationship.

### What is the gray-zone threshold?

The gray-zone threshold is the boundary between coercive acts a state absorbs as hostile pressure and acts it treats as armed attack warranting a military and alliance response. It is not fixed in treaty text. It is constructed from legal criteria, political judgment, and precedent, and it sits wherever a defender and its allies decide it sits.

That answer is deliberately uncomfortable, because the honest position is that the boundary is partly a matter of decision rather than discovery. Three separate questions get collapsed together in most public discussion, and separating them is the first analytical move worth making.

The legal question asks whether a given act meets the criteria for an armed attack as the term is understood in the law governing the use of force, which is the trigger for the right of self-defense and, through it, for the collective-defense machinery. The legal literature has developed criteria over decades, largely around scale and effects: whether an act produces destruction and casualties comparable to a kinetic strike, whether it is attributable to a state, and whether it forms part of a pattern that can be assessed cumulatively. These criteria are real and consequential, and the canonical treatment in this series belongs to the alliance-politics article rather than here.

The political question asks whether a defender and its allies will treat an act as a crossing, which is a separate matter entirely. An act can meet plausible legal criteria and still produce no collective response, because collective response requires consensus among states with differing exposure, differing threat perceptions, and differing appetites for confrontation. An act can also fall short of legal criteria and still produce a hard response, because a state that has decided it has absorbed enough may act on political grounds and construct the legal case afterward. The gap between the legal and political answers is where most of the real risk lives.

The analytical question, which is this article's territory, asks how an observer recognizes that a campaign is approaching either boundary before the crossing is complete. This is a warning problem with distinctive features. Unlike a conventional buildup, where the observable preparation and the act are separated by weeks and the indicators point in one direction, sub-threshold escalation often compresses preparation and execution into the same event and generates indicators that are individually ambiguous by design.

Holding these three apart clarifies a great deal of confused argument. When commentators dispute whether a particular incident crossed the line, they are frequently answering different questions without noticing. One party asserts that the incident met legal criteria for an armed attack, which may be defensible. Another responds that no response followed, therefore no crossing occurred, which conflates the political answer with the legal one. A third observes that nobody could prove attribution to the standard required, which is a third matter again. The incident may simultaneously satisfy a plausible legal reading, fail the political test, and remain analytically ambiguous.

## Why the Line Is Deliberately Blurred

The blurring is not an accident of drafting or a gap that better lawyering would close. It serves the interests of multiple parties, which is why it persists.

For the actor running a coercive campaign, ambiguity is the entire operating principle. The value of a deniable act lies in the response it does not provoke. An act that is severe enough to impose cost, deniable enough to complicate attribution, and ambiguous enough to divide the defender's allies delivers pressure without the risks of open conflict. Every element of that formula depends on the boundary being unclear. If a bright line existed and were universally recognized, the coercive actor would face a simple binary: stay well below it and accept limited effect, or cross it and accept war. The gray zone exists as an operating space only because the line is fuzzy, and a rational coercive actor invests in keeping it fuzzy.

The techniques for maintaining ambiguity are well documented in open policy discussion and require no operational detail to describe at the category level. Deniability is engineered through proxies, criminal intermediaries, and actors whose connection to a state is arguable rather than provable. Effects are calibrated to fall below the level that would compel a response, favoring disruption over destruction and property over persons. Acts are distributed across domains and jurisdictions so that no single national authority sees the whole pattern. Timing is spaced so that each incident is processed as a discrete event rather than as part of a campaign. Cover stories are supplied in advance, so that any given incident has an available innocent explanation: an accident, a criminal act, a technical failure, a navigation error. The series treatment of [drones over Poland and the accident-or-probe question](/2027/01/01/drones-over-poland-accident-or-probe/) works through exactly this dynamic for one category of incident.

For the defender, the ambiguity is less obviously useful, but it is not purely a liability, and pretending otherwise misreads the politics. A defender that has not committed publicly to a specific line retains freedom of action. It can absorb an incident without appearing to have backed down from a stated commitment, which preserves escalation control. It can respond to an incident more strongly than a stated rule would require, if circumstances warrant. It avoids the trap of announcing a boundary and then failing to enforce it, which damages credibility more than never having announced one. These are real considerations, and they explain why states are often reluctant to specify their lines with the precision that analysts recommend.

The alliance dimension adds a further layer. A multinational alliance has structural reasons to avoid precise boundaries. Any specific line, once articulated, becomes a commitment that binds every member, and members differ enormously in what they are prepared to be bound to. A line drawn where the most exposed frontline state wants it may be unacceptable to a member far from the frontier that fears entrapment. A line drawn where the most cautious member wants it offers the frontline state almost nothing. Constructive ambiguity in the alliance context is not intellectual laziness; it is the price of holding a coalition together across genuinely different national situations. The cohesion problem this creates is treated at length in the alliance-politics articles of this series.

There is a further reason the line resists precision, and it is the one analysts should sit with longest. Any boundary specified in advance can be probed. If a defender announces that acts producing casualties constitute a crossing, a coercive actor learns to calibrate below casualties. If a defender announces that attacks on a named category of infrastructure constitute a crossing, the actor works the categories that were not named. Specifying a line does not eliminate the gray zone; it relocates it, and the relocation is instant. This is the single strongest argument against the bright-line approach, and any serious treatment of the drawn-threshold rule has to answer it rather than dismiss it. The answer, developed further below, is that relocation is not the same as neutralization: a boundary that pushes an adversary into narrower and less effective categories of act has done real work even if it has not ended the campaign.

## More of the Same Versus Something Categorically New

The most useful analytical distinction available for this problem separates quantitative escalation from qualitative crossing. It is a simple idea with considerable working power, and it deserves careful statement because it is routinely conflated in commentary.

Quantitative escalation means more of what is already happening. A campaign that has produced a handful of infrastructure incidents produces more of them. Interference with navigation signals that affected a region intermittently affects it persistently. Influence operations that ran during one electoral cycle run harder during the next. Border pressure that involved manageable numbers involves larger ones. In every case the category of act is unchanged; only the volume, frequency, or intensity has moved. Quantitative escalation is genuinely dangerous, because accumulated pressure imposes real cost and because it can normalize a level of hostile activity that would have been unacceptable if it had appeared all at once. It is also, and this is the key point, the form of escalation least likely to be recognized as a crossing, because each increment resembles the last.

Qualitative crossing means the appearance of a category of act that was not previously in the campaign. The distinction is about kind rather than degree. An act that deliberately produces casualties where prior acts avoided them is a qualitative change even if the casualty count is low. An act attributed openly and unapologetically to a state, where prior acts were deniable, is a qualitative change even if the physical effect is identical, because abandoning deniability is a deliberate signal. An act directed at military rather than civilian targets, or at command and control rather than at commercial systems, changes category. The employment of uniformed personnel where proxies were used before changes category. Sustained, coordinated disruption of a function that a society depends on, where prior acts were episodic and recoverable, changes category.

The reason this distinction carries weight is that it maps reasonably well onto how decisions actually get made. Defenders and alliances have shown themselves capable of absorbing very large amounts of quantitative escalation, because each increment can be handled through existing channels and because no single increment forces a decision. They respond to qualitative change far more readily, because a new category of act cannot be processed as routine, forces the question of what it means, and provides the political raw material for a decision that pure accumulation does not.

This yields the first practical implication of the drawn-threshold rule. A defender monitoring only for severity is monitoring the wrong variable. Severity is continuous, and a campaign that escalates severity gradually will never present an obvious moment. Category change is discrete, which makes it observable, arguable, and usable as a decision trigger. A warning system built around category transitions catches what a severity-based system misses.

### What separates escalation from a genuine crossing?

Escalation is more of an existing category of act: greater volume, frequency, or intensity within a pattern already running. A crossing is the arrival of a category that was not there before, such as deliberate casualties, abandoned deniability, uniformed rather than proxy execution, or military rather than civilian targeting. Kind matters more than degree.

There is an important complication, and honest analysis has to carry it. Sufficient quantitative escalation can itself become qualitative. This is not a paradox; it is a recognized feature of how cumulative assessment works in both legal and political reasoning. A single incident of a given type may be beneath notice. A hundred incidents of the same type, coordinated and sustained, constitute a campaign, and a campaign is a different object than an incident. The legal doctrine that permits assessing a series of acts cumulatively rather than individually exists precisely to handle this. The practical difficulty is that no principle specifies where accumulation becomes categorical, which returns the problem to judgment. What the distinction offers is not an algorithm but a discipline: it forces an analyst to state which kind of change is being observed and to justify a cumulative reading rather than assuming it.

## The Gray-Zone Threshold Ladder

The framework below is the article's findable artifact. It sorts categories of coercive act into rungs, and for each rung it states what marks the rung, what an approach to the next rung looks like, and what a defender's signaling response would sensibly address. It is not a legal instrument and does not purport to say where the boundary lies in law. It is an analytical scaffold for tracking movement.

The ladder is built on the qualitative principle. Each rung represents a category, not a severity level, and movement between rungs is the observable event that matters. Within any rung a campaign can escalate substantially in volume without changing rung, and that is the slow-boiling risk the framework is designed to make visible.

| Rung | Category of act | What marks this rung | Indicators of approach to the next rung | Signaling response that defends the boundary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Information and influence pressure | Narrative operations, amplification, and interference in public debate; no physical effect | Shift from persuasion to operational preparation of the information environment; narratives that pre-justify a physical act | Public attribution and exposure; societal awareness programs; no military signaling |
| 2 | Non-destructive interference | Interference with signals, navigation, and civil systems; disruption without damage | Interference extended from civil to safety-critical functions; duration lengthening from episodic to persistent | Technical hardening; formal protest through international bodies; explicit statement that safety-of-life effects are treated differently |
| 3 | Property damage through deniable means | Infrastructure incidents attributed only circumstantially; damage to things, not persons | Target selection moving from commercial to essential services; recurrence rate rising; incidents clustering geographically | Attribution investment; declared policy that a demonstrated pattern is assessed cumulatively rather than incident by incident |
| 4 | Coercive pressure using persons | Instrumentalized migration, hostage-taking of nationals, pressure applied through people rather than objects | Scale increasing beyond absorptive capacity; state personnel visibly directing what was previously deniable | Border and legal measures; explicit naming of the directing state; alliance consultation short of a response demand |
| 5 | Acts producing casualties | Death or serious injury resulting from a coercive act, whether intended or accepted as likely | Prior acts trending toward casualty-producing target sets; declining care in avoiding persons | Prior public declaration that casualty-producing acts are treated as armed attack candidates; formal consultation mechanisms invoked |
| 6 | Acts against military targets or command functions | Coercion directed at armed forces, defense infrastructure, or command and control rather than civil systems | Reconnaissance patterns against defense-related sites; testing of defense-system responses | Declared treatment as an attack on the state; force-posture adjustment as a visible signal |
| 7 | Openly attributed state action | Deniability abandoned; the acting state does not contest responsibility | Rhetoric shifting from denial to justification; domestic mobilization of a legal or historical pretext | Treatment as armed attack; alliance mechanisms engaged on the collective-defense track |
| 8 | Open use of force | Conventional military action in any form, however limited | Prior rungs occurring simultaneously rather than sequentially; force posture consistent with imminent action | Defense; the boundary has been crossed and warning has ended |

Several features of the ladder are worth drawing out, because the table compresses judgments that deserve explanation.

The rungs are not a predicted sequence. A campaign does not have to climb them in order, and assuming it will is a failure mode in itself. A coercive actor can operate on rungs one through three indefinitely, jump to rung five in a single incident, or run several rungs simultaneously in different domains. What the ladder provides is a classification scheme that makes jumps visible. If an observer has been tracking activity at rungs two and three for an extended period and an incident at rung five appears, the discontinuity is the signal, and it is a signal that a severity-based system would register only as a slightly worse version of what came before.

The rungs are not equally spaced in consequence. The transitions that matter most are the ones between rung four and rung five, where persons rather than property become the object of harm, and between rung six and rung seven, where deniability is abandoned. These two transitions carry disproportionate weight in both legal and political reasoning, and a defender designing its own declared boundary would sensibly locate it at or near one of them.

The signaling column is the operative part of the framework. It answers the objection that classification is an academic exercise. For each rung, the column names what a defender can say and do in advance so that arrival at the next rung is not a surprise requiring improvised interpretation. Signaling is treated at length below, and the response side of the problem belongs to the companion article on [fighting below the threshold](/2027/02/21/fighting-below-the-threshold/), which covers what a defender does about pressure once it is recognized.

Anyone building a working version of this ladder for a specific portfolio will want to adapt the rungs to their own concerns and keep the assessment updated as a pattern develops. That is exactly the kind of structured, repeatedly revised judgment that benefits from a proper working environment: you can [track indicators and build a risk checklist on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/poland-russia-risk-monitor.html) to maintain a rung-by-rung watchlist, and [save and annotate this assessment privately in VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) if you want your own threshold notes and reasoning kept encrypted and offline on your own device.

## Reading the Ladder Against Poland's Position

An abstract framework earns its keep only when it survives contact with a specific situation. Poland's position has features that change how the rungs should be weighted, and stating them makes the framework usable rather than decorative.

The first feature is territorial exposure to more than one direction. Poland shares frontiers with Kaliningrad and with Belarus, which means coercive pressure can be applied from separate directions with separate deniability structures and can be attributed, when it is attributed at all, to different actors. This matters for the aggregation problem specifically. Activity originating from two directions is naturally handled by different parts of a security apparatus, and the pattern that would be obvious if all of it came from one place can remain invisible when it is split. A ladder-based watch has to be maintained across the whole exposure rather than per frontier, or the fragmentation the framework is designed to defeat reappears inside the framework itself.

The second feature is Poland's function as a logistics space for the wider effort supporting Ukraine. This creates a target set that is simultaneously civilian in ownership and military in significance, which is exactly the kind of category that makes boundary classification hard. An incident affecting a rail node, a port function, or a storage site can be described plausibly as an attack on commercial infrastructure or as an attack on military logistics, and the two descriptions carry very different implications for where the incident sits on the ladder. A defender that has not decided in advance how it classifies this dual-use category will find the classification argued in public during the incident, which is the worst possible time for it. This is one of the clearest cases where a prior declaration does specific, concrete work.

The third feature is the maritime dimension. The Baltic carries infrastructure whose ownership, jurisdiction, and protection responsibilities are distributed across multiple states, which multiplies the attribution and response problem. Incidents affecting seabed infrastructure are difficult to attribute, occur in spaces where presence is normal and legitimate, and involve jurisdictional questions that supply ready-made reasons for delay. The series treatment of infrastructure incidents in the Baltic covers this ground in more depth. For the ladder, the important point is that maritime incidents tend to sit at rungs two and three while carrying strategic weight that those rungs understate, which is an argument for weighting the ladder by consequence as well as by category when applying it to a specific portfolio.

The fourth feature is the information environment. Poland has a large, politically engaged public, an active media ecosystem, and a history with the acting state that supplies emotionally powerful narrative material in both directions. This cuts two ways for boundary management. It means the domestic political constraint on absorbing incidents is real and may force decisions faster than a government would choose, which limits how long a slow-boiling campaign can run before it generates its own response. It also means the information rung of the ladder is more consequential in the Polish case than a generic framework would suggest, because narratives that pre-justify a physical act have a receptive environment to work in and because contested attribution can be exploited domestically as well as internationally.

The fifth feature is alliance geography. Poland is a frontline state hosting allied forces, which means that acts affecting Polish territory can touch allied personnel and assets. This is a significant complication for boundary analysis because it changes who the affected party is. An incident that harms allied personnel on Polish soil is a different political object than one that harms Polish nationals only, and it moves the classification question directly into alliance channels rather than leaving it as a national decision. Whether this makes a response more or less likely is genuinely contested. One reading holds that allied exposure strengthens the response, because the alliance cannot easily overlook harm to its own. Another holds that it complicates the response, because it introduces additional national decision-makers with their own domestic politics. Both readings are defensible and the honest answer is that it depends on the incident and the political moment.

Taken together, these features suggest that a Polish application of the ladder would weight the transition to persons very heavily, would resolve the dual-use classification question in advance, would treat the maritime rungs as carrying more consequence than their category position implies, and would maintain the watch centrally rather than allowing it to be split by frontier or domain. None of this is exotic. It is what applying a general framework to a specific situation looks like, and it is the work that turns an analytical scaffold into something a staff can use.

## The Indicators That Mark an Approach

Warning indicators for a conventional attack are relatively well understood, and this series treats them in detail elsewhere. They rest on the fact that large-scale military action requires visible preparation that takes time and cannot be fully concealed: force concentration, logistics buildup, medical and casualty-handling preparation, communications changes, and the movement of enabling capabilities. Sub-threshold escalation does not generate the same signature. The preparation required for most coercive acts is small, cheap, and largely invisible, and the act itself is often the first observable event.

This does not mean an approach to the boundary is unobservable. It means the indicators are different in kind, and an analyst looking for conventional warning signatures in a coercive campaign will conclude, wrongly, that there is nothing to see. The indicators that actually matter fall into several families, and each requires its own reading discipline.

### Pattern indicators

The first family concerns the shape of the campaign itself rather than any individual incident. Frequency is the crudest measure and the least informative in isolation, because campaign tempo fluctuates for reasons that have nothing to do with intent, including operational opportunity, resource availability, and the adversary's own internal politics. Frequency becomes informative when it changes alongside other pattern variables.

Target-set migration is more informative. When the objects of coercive attention move from commercial and convenience systems toward essential services, and then toward safety-critical and defense-related functions, the campaign is describing its own trajectory. This is a directional signal that does not depend on any single incident being severe. A pattern that has moved steadily across categories over an extended period is more concerning than an isolated incident against a sensitive target, because the pattern indicates deliberate progression rather than opportunism.

Geographic concentration matters for a specific reason. A campaign distributed across a wide area is consistent with generalized pressure; a campaign concentrating around a particular corridor, border sector, or logistics axis is consistent with preparation for something more specific in that space. The concentration is not proof of intent, and a coercive actor aware of this indicator can deliberately distribute activity to obscure focus. But concentration is one of the few sub-threshold indicators with a plausible connection to conventional planning, which is why it earns attention.

Care indicators are subtle and valuable. Coercive campaigns that intend to remain deniable exhibit observable care: timing chosen to reduce the chance of persons being present, methods chosen to limit damage propagation, targets chosen for disruptive rather than destructive effect. A decline in that care is a directional signal about the acting state's tolerance for consequences. When incidents that would previously have been timed and placed to avoid persons stop being so timed and placed, the change in operating constraint is a genuine indicator, and it is one that precedes the transition from property to persons on the ladder above.

### Attribution-posture indicators

The second family concerns what the acting state says and how hard it works at denial. This is often more informative than the physical facts, because deniability is expensive to maintain and a state that stops paying for it has decided something.

Denial itself has grades that are worth distinguishing. Vigorous, detailed, specific denial paired with alternative explanations reflects investment in ambiguity. Perfunctory denial that does not engage the evidence reflects reduced investment. Non-denial, in which a state neither confirms nor contests, reflects a further step. Justification, in which a state explains why such an act would be understandable given the defender's own conduct, is a substantially different posture from denial and typically precedes open attribution.

Legal and historical pretext-building is the companion indicator. States preparing to abandon deniability generally construct a justificatory framework first, in domestic and international messaging, drawing on grievance, historical claim, protection of populations, or reciprocity for the defender's alleged actions. The appearance of a sustained pretext narrative that would license a category of act not yet undertaken is one of the more valuable warning signals available, and it is observable in open sources rather than requiring privileged collection. The series treatment of [Russian disinformation aimed at Poland](/2027/02/01/russian-disinformation-aimed-at-poland/) covers the mechanics of these narratives in depth.

Doctrinal and rhetorical signaling from officials rather than from state media is a third strand. Statements that recategorize the defender, that describe existing incidents as legitimate responses, or that assert the acting state's right to act against a named category of target are worth tracking with the seriousness usually reserved for physical indicators. This is not because officials announce intentions, which they rarely do, but because sustained rhetorical repositioning is costly and reflects internal decisions.

### Cross-domain synchronization

The third family may be the single most valuable, and it is the one that most distinguishes sub-threshold warning from conventional warning. Coercive campaigns are typically loosely coordinated: activity across domains proceeds on independent tempos, driven by separate organizations with separate priorities. Tight synchronization is expensive and unnecessary for routine pressure.

When activity across multiple domains begins to converge in timing and to point in a common direction, the coordination requirement has changed, and coordination requirements change when purposes change. A narrative operation, a disruption of a specific function, and pressure at a specific border sector that all intensify in the same window and all concern the same object are a different signal than the same three activities running independently. Synchronization is the sub-threshold analogue of the conventional buildup: it is the observable cost of preparing something more ambitious.

The reading discipline here matters. Apparent synchronization is easy to construct in hindsight from unrelated events, and the human tendency to see coordination where there is coincidence is well documented in the assessment literature. The test worth applying is prospective rather than retrospective: does the convergence continue, and does it involve the kind of coordination that would be expensive to fake or to produce accidentally?

### Preparation indicators that do transfer

The fourth family consists of the conventional indicators that retain value because they are hard to conceal and expensive to fake. Even a campaign designed to stay below the boundary must reckon with the possibility of a response, and preparation to absorb a response is observable. Force-posture changes, readiness adjustments, protective measures for the acting state's own assets, and precautionary steps in its own border regions all indicate that the acting state expects something. A state that intends to remain safely below the boundary does not need to prepare for the consequences of crossing it. A state that is preparing for consequences is telling an observer something about its expectations, and this indicator is comparatively resistant to deception because the preparation is real and must be real to be useful.

The methodology of reading these signals against an adversary's likely intentions is the territory of the series article on [reading Russian military intentions](/2026/08/11/reading-russian-military-intentions/), and the general tradecraft belongs there rather than being restated here.

## Ambiguous Signals and How to Weigh Them

Every indicator described above is ambiguous in isolation. This is not a defect of the indicator set; it is the defining feature of the problem, and any framework that promises unambiguous signals is selling something. The question is how to weigh ambiguity rather than how to eliminate it.

The first weighting principle is that ambiguity is asymmetric between explanations. When an incident admits both an innocent and a hostile explanation, the two explanations are rarely equally probable, and the analyst's job is to say which is better supported and why, not to record that both exist. An infrastructure failure in a region with a history of such failures, occurring during a period of no unusual activity, is probably a failure. The same event during a period of elevated activity, in a target set that has recently attracted attention, affecting a function of evident strategic relevance, is a different proposition. Context does not prove attribution, but it legitimately shifts the balance, and refusing to let it shift the balance is itself an analytical error rather than a display of rigor.

The second principle is that repetition changes the assessment in a way single incidents cannot. Any one incident may be an accident. A pattern of similar incidents, affecting a coherent target set, in a coherent period, is not well explained by accident, and the strength of the alternative explanation degrades faster than most people intuit as the pattern lengthens. This is the analytical basis for cumulative assessment, and it is the reason a defender that assesses incident by incident will systematically underestimate what is happening. Each incident, evaluated alone, will be classified as probably innocent or unattributable. The campaign, evaluated as a whole, may be clear. The methodology of incident-by-incident evaluation guarantees the wrong answer.

The third principle concerns the deliberate exploitation of ambiguity. A sophisticated coercive actor does not merely benefit from ambiguity; it manufactures ambiguity as a product. Incidents are designed to have available innocent explanations. Some incidents may be genuinely innocent and allowed to be misread as hostile, which trains the defender toward false positives and thereby discredits the defender's attribution claims. Analysts should treat the ambiguity itself as information: a pattern of incidents that each conveniently admit an innocent explanation is, at sufficient length, evidence of design rather than evidence of innocence. Perfect deniability across dozens of events is not what accident looks like.

The fourth principle is that the absence of a signal is weak evidence. Sub-threshold campaigns can pause for reasons unrelated to intent, and a quiet period does not indicate a campaign has ended or that a boundary approach has been abandoned. Conventional warning benefits from the fact that a buildup cannot be sustained indefinitely and must either be used or dispersed, which puts a clock on the observer's uncertainty. Coercive campaigns have no equivalent clock. They can run at low intensity for years, and the absence of recent activity carries far less meaning than a quiet spell in a conventional crisis.

### How does an analyst separate noise from a real signal?

By weighting context, pattern, and cost. A single ambiguous incident carries little information; a coherent pattern against a coherent target set carries much more. Signals expensive to fake, such as preparation to absorb a response or abandoned deniability, outrank cheap signals. Absence of activity proves little.

## How Much Warning the Picture Realistically Gives

This is the question a decision-maker asks first, and the honest answer has two halves that point in opposite directions. A campaign's approach to the boundary is often visible over long periods, measured in months or years. The final crossing may be visible in hours or not at all.

### How much warning does an approach actually give?

The trend is usually visible over months or years: target-set migration, declining care, hardening rhetoric, and cross-domain convergence accumulate slowly and in public. The crossing itself may offer no warning at all, because most coercive acts require little preparation and the act is the first observable event. Trend warning is long; event warning is short.

That split is the central practical fact of sub-threshold warning, and it has a consequence that is frequently missed. Because the trend warning is long, a defender is almost never genuinely surprised by the direction of a campaign. Reports accumulate, patterns become discussable, and officials are briefed. What defenders are surprised by is the arrival of a specific act at a specific moment, and they are surprised because long trend warning creates its own complacency. A trend that has been visible for two years and has not produced a crossing is easily read as a trend that will not produce one. This is the warning paradox in its sub-threshold form: the very duration of the signal degrades its capacity to prompt action.

The practical implication is that sub-threshold warning is not useful as tactical alert. It will not reliably tell a defender that something will happen on a particular night. It is useful as a decision framework: it tells a defender that the campaign has entered a phase in which certain categories of act have become materially more likely, which is the appropriate input for posture, resilience, and declaratory decisions rather than for an alert state.

There is a second reason the split matters. Because the trend is long and the event is short, the defender's opportunity to shape outcomes lies almost entirely in the trend period. Once an act occurs, the defender is responding to a fait accompli, and the range of good options has narrowed sharply. Everything a defender can do to make a crossing less likely, less useful to the adversary, or less ambiguous when it happens has to be done during the long visible approach. This is the deepest argument for the drawn-threshold rule: the declaratory work only has value if it is done before it is needed, and the long trend period is precisely the window in which it can be done.

A third consideration concerns compression. The most concerning warning picture is not steady progression but compression, where activities that had been spread across months begin occurring within weeks and then within days. Compression is observable, it is expensive to fake, and it is one of the few sub-threshold patterns with a genuine tactical warning value, because it indicates that separate lines of effort are being brought into alignment for a purpose. A defender that has been tracking a campaign at a steady tempo and observes sudden compression across multiple domains has received something close to a real alert, and the appropriate response is to treat the compression itself as the indicator rather than waiting for confirmation from any single domain.

One further consequence follows from the trend-long, event-short structure, and it concerns how a defender should treat its own past assessments. Because the approach unfolds over years, the analytical record accumulates, and that record is an asset most defenders fail to use. An assessment written two years earlier, stating what the analyst then expected and what would have changed the judgment, is the only reliable check on normalization, because it preserves a baseline that the analyst's own adjusted expectations no longer contain. Reviewing old assessments against what subsequently happened is uncomfortable and is therefore rarely done systematically. It is also the single cheapest corrective available, since it requires no new collection and no new authority, only the discipline of writing judgments down in a form that can be checked later and then actually checking them.

## Why the Crossing Gets Missed

Warning failures in this domain are not primarily collection failures. The information is generally available, often in open sources. The failures are in classification, aggregation, escalation, and decision.

### Why do observers miss the crossing?

Because each incident is processed as a discrete event by whichever agency owns that domain, no one holds the whole pattern, and normalization makes each new increment seem unremarkable. Attribution difficulty supplies a reason to defer judgment, and deferring is institutionally safer than raising an alarm that may not be vindicated.

That compressed answer contains several distinct failure modes, and each is worth unpacking because they have different remedies.

Normalization is the first and probably the most powerful. Human and institutional tolerance adjusts to what is routine. A level of hostile activity that would have prompted emergency consultations if it appeared without precedent becomes background when it arrives gradually. The adjustment is not a failure of individual attention; it is how organizations allocate scarce attention, and it operates even in services that are explicitly watching for it. Normalization is the mechanism by which a campaign boils a defender slowly, and it is the single strongest argument for pre-committing to categories rather than judging each increment against the current baseline. A category-based boundary is immune to normalization in a way a severity-based judgment is not, because the category either has or has not appeared.

Fragmentation is the second. Sub-threshold campaigns are distributed by design across domains that belong to different institutions: interference with civil systems belongs to one regulator, infrastructure incidents to another, border pressure to a third, information operations to a fourth, and criminal investigation to a fifth. Each institution sees its own slice, and each slice may look manageable. The pattern exists only at the aggregate level, and the aggregate level is precisely where no one is looking unless someone has been given the job. Fragmentation is a solvable problem, and solving it is largely a matter of assigning the aggregation task explicitly rather than assuming it happens.

Attribution deferral is the third. Because attribution is genuinely hard, and because a wrong attribution is costly and embarrassing, institutional incentives favor waiting for more evidence. Waiting is often correct on its own terms. The problem is that the aggregate effect of many individually reasonable decisions to wait is that a campaign runs indefinitely without ever being formally characterized. The remedy is to separate the assessment question from the response question. An analytical judgment that a pattern is probably state-directed can and should be made at a lower confidence threshold than a decision to respond, and collapsing the two means the assessment inherits the response's evidentiary bar.

The fourth failure mode is the one hardest to correct: the incentive against being wrong in the alarming direction. An analyst who raises a warning that is not vindicated bears a cost. An analyst who fails to raise a warning bears a cost only if a crossing actually occurs, and even then the failure is usually distributed across an institution rather than attributed to an individual. The asymmetry is well understood in the assessment literature and reliably produces under-warning. It is not corrected by exhortation. It is corrected structurally, by making it someone's explicit job to argue the alarming case, by recording assessments in a form that can be reviewed later, and by treating a well-reasoned warning that did not eventuate as a successful process rather than a failure.

A fifth mode deserves brief mention because it is specific to the boundary problem. Definitional refuge is the practice of avoiding a decision by disputing whether a threshold was met. When a serious incident occurs, the availability of a genuine definitional dispute provides everyone who prefers not to act with a respectable reason not to. The dispute is real, the arguments are legitimate, and the effect is paralysis. Pre-commitment is the only real defense: a defender that has stated in advance which categories it treats as a crossing has removed the definitional refuge from its own decision process, whatever its allies subsequently do.

## The Aggregation Problem and Who Owns It

Of all the failure modes described above, fragmentation is the most tractable, and it is worth treating separately because the remedy is institutional rather than analytical.

The problem in its simplest form: coercive campaigns are distributed across domains that belong to different organizations, and no organization's mandate covers the whole. Interference with civil systems belongs to a regulator. Infrastructure damage belongs to police and prosecutors, and is handled as crime unless and until someone establishes otherwise. Border pressure belongs to a border service. Information operations belong to whichever body has been given that portfolio, which in many states is more than one and in some is effectively none. Cyber incidents belong to a national authority with its own reporting structures. Maritime incidents belong to naval and coastguard bodies and may involve several states at once.

Each of these organizations does its job. Each sees a set of incidents that, within its own domain and against its own baseline, look like the sort of thing it deals with. The regulator sees interference that is unusual but not unprecedented. The police see property crime with no identified perpetrator. The border service sees pressure at levels it has handled before. None of them is wrong, and none of them is positioned to see that the four patterns together describe something that no one of them describes alone.

The remedy is unglamorous: assign the aggregation task explicitly to a body with the standing to demand reporting from all the domain owners and the analytical capacity to do something with it. This sounds obvious and is frequently absent, because aggregation is nobody's natural mandate and creating a mandate for it requires a decision that competes with more urgent-seeming priorities. The absence is rarely a choice; it is a default.

Several design features distinguish an aggregation function that works from one that exists on paper. It needs a standing taxonomy, so that incidents arriving from different domains are classified consistently rather than in each domain's own vocabulary. The ladder framework or something like it serves this purpose: it gives a regulator's interference report and a police force's damage report a common classification language. It needs a reporting obligation rather than a request, because voluntary reporting from busy organizations degrades quickly. It needs a longitudinal record, because the whole value of aggregation is the ability to see a trend, and a body that starts its assessment fresh each time cannot. And it needs an output that goes somewhere with authority, because an aggregate assessment that lands in a report nobody is obliged to read has changed nothing.

There is a further requirement that is more analytical than institutional. The aggregation function has to be permitted to make judgments at a lower confidence bar than the response process uses. This is the separation described earlier, and it is the point at which most aggregation efforts fail in practice. If the aggregating body is held to the evidentiary standard that would justify a response, it will produce assessments hedged into uselessness, because the standard is genuinely hard to meet for deniable acts. If it is permitted to say that a pattern is probably state-directed on the balance of available evidence, while explicitly noting that the assessment does not meet the bar for a response decision, it produces something a decision-maker can actually use. The two bars serve different purposes and collapsing them serves neither.

None of this is unique to the boundary question, and the general tradecraft of aggregation and assessment belongs to the methodology articles in this series. What is specific here is that the boundary problem is unusually dependent on aggregation, because the defining feature of sub-threshold pressure is that it is designed to look unremarkable when examined one piece at a time. A campaign that would be obvious in aggregate and is invisible in pieces is the exact case where the institutional arrangement determines the analytical outcome.

## Drawing and Signaling a Boundary

Everything above leads here. If the line cannot be discovered, it must be drawn, and if it is drawn it must be communicated, or the drawing accomplishes nothing.

### Can a defender draw its own line?

Yes, and the drawn-threshold rule holds that it must. A defender that states in advance which categories of act it will treat as armed attack converts an ambiguous accumulation into a sequence of decision points it controls. A boundary defined only after a crossing invites exactly the slow approach that deniable campaigns are built to exploit.

The design problem has several parts, and the difficulty is real. A declared boundary must be specific enough to be meaningful, general enough not to be trivially evaded, credible enough to be believed, and survivable enough that the defender can live with having stated it.

Specificity and evasion pull against each other. A boundary stated in terms of a narrow list of protected assets invites the adversary to work outside the list, and the relocation is immediate. A boundary stated in terms of categories of effect is harder to evade, because it addresses what the act does rather than what it touches. A declaration that acts producing death or serious injury will be treated as armed attack, regardless of the mechanism, the domain, or the nominal actor, is much harder to route around than a declaration protecting a named set of installations. Categories of effect, not lists of objects, are the more durable formulation.

Credibility is the harder problem and cannot be solved by declaration alone. A stated boundary that the adversary does not believe is worse than no boundary, because it invites a test and then produces a visible failure. Credibility comes from consistency between what a state says and what it has done, from the evident capacity to act on the statement, and from the statement being made at a level and in a manner that indicates it was decided rather than improvised. A boundary announced by a single official in a press interview signals less than one embedded in national security documents, repeated across administrations, and reflected in posture.

Survivability means the defender must be able to live with the declaration in the worst case. This is where the reluctance to declare comes from, and the reluctance is rational. A state that declares a boundary and then absorbs a crossing without responding has damaged itself. The design answer is to state the boundary where the state genuinely would act, not where it wishes it would. A conservative boundary that is honestly held is worth more than an ambitious one that is not, because the function of the declaration is to remove ambiguity rather than to maximize deterrent claims.

The alliance dimension complicates all of this and cannot be wished away. A national declaration binds the nation and does not bind the alliance, and the gap between them is where an adversary will operate. A frontline state can declare that a given category constitutes armed attack, and it can act on that declaration under its own authority, but whether allies concur is a separate matter that a national declaration cannot settle. What a national declaration does accomplish is to force the question into alliance channels at a time of the declaring state's choosing rather than in the aftermath of an incident. That is a real gain even where consensus is uncertain, and it is a substantially better position than arriving at an emergency consultation with no prior position at all. The obligations and mechanisms themselves belong to the canonical alliance-politics treatment rather than here.

There is one more design consideration, and it is the answer to the relocation objection raised earlier. A declared boundary does push a coercive actor into categories that were not declared, and analysts who point this out are correct. But the categories left available after a good declaration are narrower and less useful than the ones foreclosed. An adversary confined to acts that produce no casualties, that maintain full deniability, and that avoid defense-related targets is running a materially less effective campaign than one operating without those constraints. Relocation is a cost imposed on the adversary, not a defeat of the boundary. The drawn-threshold rule does not claim to end sub-threshold pressure; it claims to constrain its most dangerous forms and to protect the defender from being boiled slowly.

There is also a timing question, and it is more consequential than it looks. A declaration made during a calm period is read as policy; the same declaration made in the immediate aftermath of an incident is read as a reaction, and reactions are discounted because they are understood to reflect the pressure of the moment rather than a settled position. This creates an awkward incentive structure, because the political conditions that make a declaration feasible tend to arrive precisely when its credibility will be lowest. A government that finds it politically impossible to declare a limit in quiet conditions and then declares one under the pressure of an incident has produced the weakest available version of the commitment. The design implication is that the declaratory work belongs early in the long trend period rather than late, which is the same conclusion the warning analysis reached by a different route.

The channel matters as well. Public declaration and private communication do different work and are not substitutes. Public statement is what constrains the defender's own future decision-making, removes definitional refuge, and signals to allies and to a domestic audience. Private communication through diplomatic channels is what most reliably reaches the adversary's actual decision-makers, can carry more specificity than a public statement can safely contain, and avoids the public credibility trap by not staking prestige on the line. The two together are stronger than either alone: the public element does the internal and alliance work, the private element does the deterrent work, and the consistency between them is itself a credibility signal. A defender that says one thing publicly and something materially different privately has created a discrepancy that will eventually be discovered and that will discredit both messages.

## The Two Failure Modes

Boundary analysis exists to prevent two errors, and they pull in opposite directions. Most treatments concentrate on one and treat the other as a rhetorical afterthought, which produces frameworks that are half-useful.

The first failure mode is being boiled slowly. A defender absorbs increments, each individually manageable, until the accumulated position is materially worse than any single decision would have permitted. The mechanism has been described above: normalization moves the baseline, fragmentation prevents aggregation, attribution difficulty licenses deferral, and institutional incentives penalize alarm. The result is not a decision to accept the pressure. It is the absence of any decision at all, which is a different and worse thing, because an explicit decision to absorb pressure at least carries the possibility of being revisited.

What makes slow boiling particularly dangerous is that it degrades the defender's future options rather than just its present position. Each unanswered increment establishes that a category is available, which means the adversary can operate in that category with reduced risk thereafter. Precedent accumulates on the adversary's side. A defender that has absorbed a dozen incidents in a category and then decides to treat the thirteenth as a crossing faces the obvious question of what changed, and the honest answer, that nothing changed except the defender's patience, is a weak basis for a response that allies must join. Slow boiling therefore does not merely cost what it takes; it forecloses the response to what comes next.

The second failure mode is being tripped suddenly. A defender responds to an ambiguous incident in a way that produces conflict neither side intended. The mechanism is different and less discussed. An incident occurs with severe effects and unclear attribution during a period of elevated tension. Political conditions make non-response untenable, either because the harm is too visible to absorb or because the government has previously committed itself in ways that now bind. The response occurs before attribution is settled. The acting state, which may or may not have intended the effects that occurred, treats the response as unprovoked. Escalation follows from a sequence in which each step was defensible given what its author believed.

Sudden tripping is more likely than it sounds, for three reasons that are worth stating plainly. Coercive acts have uncertain effects, and an act calibrated to disrupt can destroy if conditions differ from expectation. Attribution under time pressure is worse than attribution at leisure, and political pressure to respond is highest exactly when attribution is weakest. And a defender that has declared a boundary has, by design, reduced its own freedom to absorb, which is the credibility trap the continuum school correctly identifies.

The two failure modes are related in an uncomfortable way: the measures that reduce one tend to increase the other. Pre-commitment reduces slow boiling and increases the risk of being tripped, because it removes the option of quiet absorption. Flexibility reduces the risk of being tripped and increases slow boiling, because it preserves the option of doing nothing indefinitely. Any honest framework has to acknowledge that it is trading between these rather than solving both.

The trade can be managed, and the ladder framework suggests how. The transition points at which pre-commitment is most valuable are also the ones at which ambiguity is lowest. Casualties are hard to mistake. Abandoned deniability is hard to mistake. Acts against military targets are hard to mistake. Committing at these transitions buys most of the anti-boiling benefit while incurring relatively little of the tripping risk, because the categories are ones a defender is unlikely to misclassify under pressure. The categories where ambiguity is highest, which are the lower rungs involving disruption, damage, and interference, are exactly where pre-commitment is most dangerous and least necessary. A defender that pre-commits high and stays flexible low is managing the trade rather than pretending it does not exist.

This is also the answer to a common objection, that declaring a boundary makes a defender hostage to its own words. It does, at the point of declaration. The design question is where to accept that hostage situation, and the answer is at the categories where the defender would act anyway and where misclassification is least likely. Declaring at those points converts a commitment the defender already holds into one the adversary can see, which is a gain at low marginal risk.

## What Historical Cases Teach About Recognizing Crossings

Historical reasoning about incremental pressure is available and useful, but it has to be handled carefully, because the cases most often invoked carry lessons that are frequently overdrawn.

The general pattern that history does support is that incremental approaches work when each increment is small enough to be absorbed and the absorbing party has not stated a limit in advance. This pattern recurs across very different periods and political systems, which is some evidence that it reflects something structural about how decisions get made under uncertainty rather than something particular to any one era. The decision problem facing a defender confronted with a small increment is genuinely hard: responding forcefully to something minor looks disproportionate, carries its own escalation risk, and may be politically impossible, while absorbing it is cheap in the moment. The rational choice at each step is absorption, and the cumulative result of a sequence of rational choices is the position the defender never would have accepted as a single proposition.

The general pattern history also supports is that stated limits, when credible, change the calculation. An adversary weighing an increment against a declared position faces a different problem than one weighing it against silence. This does not mean declared limits always hold; the record includes cases where they were tested and failed, and those cases are exactly what the continuum school points to. It means the declaration changes what the adversary is calculating, which is the mechanism deterrence runs on.

Where historical reasoning goes wrong is in the transfer of specific lessons. Three cautions are worth stating. The first is that the cases most often cited come from periods with very different information environments, alliance structures, and escalation dynamics, and the features that made incremental pressure work then may not be the features operating now. The second is that the historical record is heavily selected: cases where incremental pressure succeeded are memorable and much discussed, while cases where it was checked early and produced nothing are unmemorable and largely unstudied, which inflates the apparent success rate of the approach. The third is that historical analogies in this domain carry strong emotional and political charges that make them useful for argument and dangerous for analysis, and an analyst reaching for a precedent should ask whether it is doing analytical work or rhetorical work.

The honest summary is that history supports the structural claim, that undeclared limits invite incremental approach, and does not support confident predictions about how any particular sequence will unfold. That is a real but modest contribution, and it is the correct size of claim. The series treats historical precedent and its limits at length in its own cluster, and readers who want the full treatment of how to use history without being misled by it should go there rather than treating the compressed version here as sufficient.

## The Contested Categories Where Analysts Genuinely Differ

The ladder above places categories in an order that most analysts would broadly recognize, but several categories are genuinely disputed, and a framework that hides the disputes is less useful than one that names them. Four are worth setting out, because they are the categories in which a real incident is most likely to occur and the classification argument most likely to be had in public.

Effects delivered through networks rather than through physical means are the first and hardest. The dispute is not about whether such acts can constitute armed attack; the mainstream legal position accepts that effects comparable to a kinetic strike can qualify regardless of the means used to produce them. The dispute is about where the comparison threshold sits and how to treat effects that are severe but reversible. An act that disables a function for hours and is then restored has produced disruption, not destruction, and reasonable analysts differ on whether sufficiently severe disruption of a sufficiently essential function amounts to an attack even where nothing was permanently broken and nobody was hurt. One school holds that effects-based reasoning must follow effects wherever they occur, including reversible ones, because the harm to a society is real regardless of whether hardware survived. Another holds that reversibility is exactly the distinction that keeps the category workable, since a standard that captures reversible disruption would classify a very large volume of ordinary hostile activity as armed attack and thereby drain the term of meaning.

Coercion through economic and energy means is the second. Cutting supply, manipulating dependency, and imposing commercial cost are hostile acts that can do a state enormous damage, and the damage may exceed what a limited physical act would cause. Most analysts nonetheless place economic coercion outside the armed-attack category, on the grounds that the legal architecture distinguishes force from other forms of pressure and that collapsing the distinction would make the category unmanageably broad. The dissenting position is not that economic coercion should be treated as armed attack, but that placing it wholly outside the framework leaves a state with no adequate answer to a tool that can be more damaging than the tools inside it. That is a serious point about the framework's coverage rather than about classification, and it belongs in the discussion because it explains why energy and supply questions recur in boundary debates even though the legal answer looks settled.

Interference in political processes is the third and in some ways the most awkward. Sustained influence operations aimed at electoral outcomes are widely regarded as a serious hostile act and are almost universally placed below the armed-attack line. The awkwardness is that the potential consequence, the capture or destabilization of a state's political direction, is arguably more significant than the consequence of many acts placed higher on the ladder. Analysts differ on what to do with this mismatch. One position accepts it as a limitation of a framework built around force and looks elsewhere for the answer. Another argues for treating sustained, large-scale, state-directed political interference as a category requiring its own response architecture rather than trying to fit it into the armed-attack question at all. The second position is gaining ground in policy discussion and is probably the more useful, since it stops the framework being asked to do work it was not designed for.

Distributed infrastructure in shared or contested spaces is the fourth. Seabed cables and pipelines, satellite services, and other systems whose ownership crosses jurisdictions present an attribution problem, a jurisdictional problem, and a responsibility problem simultaneously. The analytical dispute concerns whether damage to such systems should be classified by effect, which would place a severe incident high on the ladder, or by the difficulty of the response, which pushes it lower in practice because no single state is clearly the injured party with clear standing to respond. The gap between the effect-based and the practice-based answer is unusually wide in this category, and it is a gap a coercive actor can be expected to notice.

The common thread across all four is that each is a place where the category the framework assigns and the consequence the act delivers do not line up. That mismatch is where a coercive actor will concentrate, because it is where the defender's response machinery works least well. A defender designing a declaration should therefore give disproportionate attention to precisely these four rather than to the categories where the answer is clear, since the clear categories are the ones an adversary will avoid.

## What the Adversary Sees

Boundary analysis conducted only from the defender's side is incomplete, and the omission causes specific errors. It is worth asking what a coercive actor observes and how it reasons, while being careful not to overclaim knowledge of another state's internal decision-making.

The first observation is that an adversary reads responses, not statements. What a defender has done when tested carries far more information than what it has said it would do, and a sequence of absorbed incidents teaches more than any declaration. This is the mechanism behind the precedent effect described earlier, and it is the reason a declaration made after a period of visible absorption starts from a credibility deficit. An adversary that has watched a defender absorb a category is entitled to conclude that the category is available, and no announcement fully undoes that conclusion.

The second observation is that an adversary reads alliance divergence with particular attention. The gap between a frontline state's position and a distant ally's position is a specific, exploitable feature, and it can be probed cheaply. Incidents can be selected to fall exactly where the frontline state would want a response and the distant ally would not, which produces the most useful possible outcome from the coercive actor's perspective: pressure delivered, response withheld, and alliance division demonstrated. Analysts should expect this to be a deliberate objective rather than a side effect, because it is the highest-value return available from a low-cost act.

The third observation concerns the adversary's own uncertainty, which is systematically underestimated. A coercive actor operating below a boundary it cannot see precisely is also taking risk, and it does not know where the defender's actual limit lies any better than the defender's own analysts do. Its calibration is based on inference from past defender behavior, which is an imperfect guide, particularly where a defender's tolerance has domestic political limits that the adversary cannot observe. This is a genuine source of accidental crossing: an act intended to sit safely below the line can land above it because the actor misjudged either the effects or the defender's political position. The implication is that sub-threshold campaigns carry escalation risk for the actor running them, not just for the defender absorbing them, and that a defender's declaratory clarity reduces risk for both parties rather than only constraining the adversary.

The fourth observation is about mirror-imaging, which is the standing hazard in this kind of reasoning. It is tempting to assume that a coercive actor is executing a coherent, centrally directed campaign with a clear theory of the boundary and a plan for approaching it. Some of it may be. Some of it is likely to be opportunistic, driven by organizational incentives, resource availability, and local initiative rather than by a strategy that would be recognizable if written down. Attributing more coherence to an adversary than the evidence supports produces the specific error of reading pattern into activity that has none, and then adjusting posture in response to a pattern that does not exist. The corrective is to hold campaign coherence as an assessment to be tested rather than an assumption to be reasoned from, and to notice that the indicators most resistant to this error are the expensive ones: coordination that would be costly to produce accidentally is evidence of direction in a way that a coincidence of tempo is not.

## The Legal and Political Stakes of the Line

The boundary is consequential because a great deal is attached to it, and the attachments run in both directions.

On the legal side, the classification of an act as armed attack is what activates the right of self-defense, which is in turn what makes a collective-defense response lawful. This is why the classification question is not academic and why states argue about it seriously. The criteria developed in the law governing the use of force turn substantially on scale and effects, on attribution to a state, and on whether a series of acts may be assessed cumulatively. Applying these criteria to acts that produce disruption rather than destruction, that are executed through intermediaries, and that occur in domains the framework was not designed around is genuinely contested among serious lawyers, and this article does not attempt to resolve it. The canonical treatment of what the alliance obligation requires, including the important distinction between what triggers consultation and what triggers a defense commitment, belongs to the series article on Article 5 obligations, and readers who want that ground should go there.

On the political side, the stakes are different and in practice often larger. A crossing that is acknowledged commits a government to a response it may not want, at a moment it did not choose. A crossing that is not acknowledged spares the government that commitment at the cost of demonstrating that the category in question can be entered without consequence. Both outcomes have costs, and governments weigh them under pressure with incomplete information.

The asymmetry between a frontline state and a distant ally shapes this weighing profoundly. For the state that absorbs the coercion, each unacknowledged crossing is a direct injury and a demonstration that its territory is available for pressure. For an ally at a distance, the same incident is a foreign event whose principal salience is the risk of being drawn into a confrontation. These are not bad-faith positions; they are honest reflections of different exposure. Any realistic account of how a crossing would be handled has to begin from the fact that alliance members will assess the same facts differently because they face different consequences from the same decision.

There is also a domestic dimension that is frequently underweighted. Public tolerance for absorbing coercive acts is not unlimited, and the political cost of visible non-response accumulates. A government that has absorbed a sequence of incidents may find that its own population, rather than its adversary, forces the decision. This is a genuine constraint on the slow-boiling strategy from the coercive actor's side, and it means the accumulation dynamic is not entirely one-directional. A campaign that pushes too far too visibly may generate the political conditions for exactly the response it was designed to avoid, which is one of the more important limits on the utility of sub-threshold pressure.

## The Bright-Line School and the Continuum School

Serious analysts disagree about whether a workable boundary can be constructed at all, and the disagreement is worth presenting properly rather than resolved by assertion.

The bright-line school holds that a defender can and should construct a clear, declared boundary and enforce it. The argument runs as follows. Deterrence requires the adversary to know what will trigger a response, and an undeclared boundary provides no deterrent because it communicates nothing. Ambiguity in this context is not sophistication; it is abdication, and it hands the pacing of the campaign entirely to the actor running it. Every historical case in which a state absorbed accumulating pressure without setting a limit ended with the pressure continuing until something forced a decision under worse conditions. The relocation objection is acknowledged and answered as above: pushing an adversary into narrower categories is a gain, and the alternative is no constraint at all. On this reading, the practical difficulties of declaration are real but manageable, and the failure to declare reflects political reluctance dressed up as analytical caution.

The continuum school holds that the search for a boundary misunderstands the phenomenon. Coercion and armed conflict are points on a single spectrum of hostile interaction rather than distinct states separated by a line, and the attempt to impose a binary on a continuum produces worse decisions rather than better ones. Any declared boundary is either so specific that it is trivially evaded or so general that it commits the state to responses it will not make. Declaration converts a flexible situation into a rigid one and creates a credibility trap: the adversary will test the line precisely because it has been drawn, and the defender will then face a choice between a response it does not want and a visible climbdown. On this reading, the better approach is continuous, proportionate, cumulative response that imposes cost across the whole spectrum without ever staking everything on a single classification decision.

Both schools are arguing from real observations, and each is strongest exactly where the other is weakest. The bright-line school is right that undeclared boundaries deter nothing and that accumulation without a stated limit reliably produces the slow-boiling failure. The continuum school is right that declared boundaries get probed, that specificity invites evasion, and that credibility traps are real and costly.

The position this article takes, offered as an assessment rather than as settled fact, is that the disagreement is narrower than it appears because the two schools are optimizing for different failure modes. The continuum approach is well suited to the lower rungs of the ladder, where continuous proportionate cost-imposition is the sensible tool and where any declaration would be both unenforceable and unnecessary. The bright-line approach is well suited to the upper rungs, where the transitions carry categorical weight, where the defender genuinely would act, and where the absence of a stated position is most exploitable. A defender does not have to choose one philosophy for the entire spectrum. It can run continuous cost-imposition below a declared boundary and a stated categorical commitment at and above it, which is close to what the ladder framework implies and close to what capable defenders appear to do in practice.

What would change this judgment? Principally, evidence about how adversaries actually respond to declared boundaries. If the observable record showed that declaration reliably triggered probing that defenders then failed to answer, the continuum critique would be substantially strengthened. If it showed that declared categories were respected while undeclared ones were exploited, the bright-line case would be close to settled. The honest position is that the open record is thin on this point, that both schools read the available cases in their own favor, and that a reader should treat confident claims in either direction with caution.

## A Working Method for the Reader

The framework above is useful only if it can be operated. What follows is a method that a staffer, analyst, or serious reader can apply without privileged access, stated as a sequence of judgments rather than as a checklist.

Begin by fixing the categories. Write down the rungs that matter for the portfolio in question, using the ladder above as a starting point and adapting it to the specific exposure. The point of doing this first, before looking at any incident, is that categories fixed in advance are immune to the normalization that categories improvised during an incident are not. A category list written during a calm period will look different from one written after a serious event, and the calm-period version is the one worth trusting.

Then establish the baseline. Record what is currently happening at each rung, in what volume, and with what characteristics. The baseline is not a judgment about whether the current level is acceptable; it is a factual record against which movement can be measured. Most defenders do not have this, which is why they cannot tell whether a given month is unusual.

Track movement rather than level. The question at each review is not whether the current activity is concerning, which invites the normalization trap, but whether the activity has changed category, changed target set, changed care, changed attribution posture, or converged across domains. These are the five movement variables that carry information. A review that concludes activity is at a similar level to last quarter has said nothing useful; a review that concludes target selection has migrated toward essential services for the third consecutive quarter has said something important.

Weight signals by cost. When several indicators point in different directions, prefer the ones that would be expensive for an adversary to fake. Rhetoric is cheap. Physical preparation to absorb a response is expensive. Abandoning deniability that has been maintained at cost is expensive. Analysts routinely overweight rhetoric because it is abundant and easy to collect, and underweight preparation because it is sparse and hard to interpret.

Decide the response categories in advance, separately from the assessment. For each rung, write down what the defender would do, distinguishing what it would do unilaterally from what would require allied concurrence. The purpose is not to bind anyone. It is to discover, in advance and in calm conditions, where the defender's actual commitments lie, which is almost always different from where its rhetoric suggests they lie. The discovery is the valuable part, because it identifies the gap between stated and real positions before an adversary identifies it.

Finally, review on a fixed schedule rather than on incidents. Incident-driven review guarantees that assessment happens under the conditions least favorable to good judgment, and it guarantees that quiet periods produce no assessment at all, which is exactly when a trend becomes visible. A fixed review cycle, applied whether or not anything happened, is the practical defense against both normalization and the absence-of-signal error.

A method of this kind is worth maintaining as a living document rather than a one-time exercise, since its whole value lies in the longitudinal record. Building the rung watchlist and running the periodic review is straightforward to do properly: [track indicators and build a risk checklist on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/poland-russia-risk-monitor.html) for the recurring assessment cycle, and keep the reasoning, the baseline record, and the response-category decisions [saved and annotated privately in VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the material stays encrypted on your own device and can be revisited and revised as the picture develops.

## What the Warning Discipline Yields

The verdict of this analysis is not a location for the boundary. Anyone who offers a precise location is overreaching, because the boundary is partly constructed rather than discovered and its construction depends on decisions that have not been made. The verdict is about method.

First, the crossing question should be treated as a warning problem rather than a definitional one. The definitional debate is legitimate and unresolvable in the timeframe that matters. The warning question is tractable. A defender that tracks category transitions, attribution posture, care indicators, and cross-domain convergence knows a great deal about where a campaign is heading, even if it cannot say where the line sits.

Second, category beats severity as the monitoring variable. Severity is continuous and normalizes; category is discrete and does not. A monitoring system organized around the rungs of a ladder catches the discontinuities that a severity-based system smooths over. This is the single most transferable recommendation in this analysis and the one most easily implemented, because it requires a change in how existing information is classified rather than new collection.

Third, the warning picture is long on trend and short on event, which means the defender's leverage lies in the trend period. Declaratory work, resilience investment, aggregation arrangements, and alliance groundwork all have to be done during the visible approach, because none of them can be improvised in the hours after an incident. A defender that treats the long approach as a period of monitoring rather than a period of preparation has wasted the only real advantage the sub-threshold warning picture provides.

Fourth, the drawn-threshold rule holds. A boundary defined only after it is crossed invites the slow approach, removes the defender's control over pacing, and supplies definitional refuge to everyone who would prefer not to decide. Drawing a boundary in advance does not eliminate ambiguity, does not prevent probing, and does not guarantee allied concurrence. It does convert an open-ended accumulation into a set of decision points the defender has thought about in advance, which is the difference between being surprised and being ready.

Fifth, and finally, the two failure modes are equally real and pull in opposite directions. A defender can be boiled slowly, absorbing increments until the accumulated position is far worse than any single decision would have permitted. A defender can also be tripped suddenly, responding to an ambiguous incident in a way that produces a conflict neither side intended. The warning discipline described here is designed to reduce both, and any framework that reduces one while ignoring the other is only half a framework. The comparison between the pressure campaign and the open-invasion risk, and the question of where planning weight should actually sit, is taken up in the series article on [hybrid war versus open invasion risk](/2027/03/01/hybrid-war-vs-open-invasion-risk/); the resilience side of the answer belongs to the treatment of [building resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), which covers the measures that reduce what any crossing can achieve.

A reader who takes one thing from this analysis should take the reframing. The question is not where the line is. The question is whether you will notice the approach, and whether you decided in advance what you would do about it. The first is a matter of tradecraft. The second is a matter of political will, and it has to be settled before the incident that tests it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When does gray-zone pressure become open war for Poland?

There is no automatic conversion point. Coercive pressure becomes open war when a defender and its allies decide that an act or an accumulated pattern constitutes armed attack and respond militarily, which is a decision rather than a discovery. In practice the transition is most likely to be recognized when a categorically new type of act appears: deliberate casualties, abandoned deniability, uniformed rather than proxy execution, or action against military targets. Sheer volume of familiar acts is far less likely to produce recognition, because each increment resembles the last and gets processed as routine. This is why the practical answer depends heavily on whether a defender has decided in advance which categories it treats as a crossing, rather than on any property of the acts themselves.

### Q: Where does the gray-zone threshold actually lie?

Nowhere fixed. The boundary is constructed from three separate things: legal criteria concerning scale, effects, and attribution; political judgment about whether a defender and its allies will act; and precedent built by what states have actually treated as a crossing. These three can give different answers about the same incident, and the gaps between them are where coercive campaigns operate. An act can plausibly meet legal criteria and still produce no response, or fall short of them and still produce one. The most useful working position is that the boundary sits wherever a defender has credibly said it sits and can be seen to mean it, which makes the location partly a matter of the defender's own choices rather than an external fact to be found.

### Q: Why is the gray-zone line deliberately blurred?

Because ambiguity serves several parties at once. For the coercive actor it is the entire operating principle: acts that are severe enough to impose cost, deniable enough to complicate attribution, and ambiguous enough to divide the defender's allies deliver pressure without the risks of open conflict, and every element of that formula depends on the boundary being unclear. For the defender, an undeclared line preserves freedom of action and avoids the credibility damage of announcing a limit and then failing to enforce it. For a multinational alliance, precision is costly because any specific line binds every member, and members differ sharply in what they will accept being bound to. The blurring is therefore stable, because too many parties benefit from it.

### Q: What separates quantitative escalation from a qualitative gray-zone crossing?

Quantitative escalation is more of the same: greater volume, frequency, or intensity within a category of act already running. Qualitative crossing is the appearance of a category that was not previously present. The distinction matters because defenders absorb enormous amounts of the first and respond much more readily to the second. An act producing deliberate casualties where prior acts avoided them is a qualitative change even at low casualty counts. Abandoned deniability is a qualitative change even where the physical effect is identical. The complication worth carrying is that sufficient accumulation can itself become qualitative, since a sustained coordinated campaign is a different object than an isolated incident, but no principle specifies where accumulation tips over, which returns the judgment to the analyst.

### Q: What indicators mark an approach to the gray-zone threshold?

Four families. Pattern indicators cover target-set migration from commercial toward essential and then defense-related functions, geographic concentration, and declining care about avoiding persons. Attribution-posture indicators cover the grade of denial, from detailed rebuttal through perfunctory denial to justification, and the construction of legal or historical pretexts for acts not yet undertaken. Cross-domain synchronization covers convergence in timing and direction across activities that normally run on independent tempos, which is the sub-threshold analogue of a conventional buildup. Preparation indicators cover the acting state's own protective and readiness measures, which are informative because a state confident of staying below the boundary has no reason to prepare for the consequences of crossing it.

### Q: Why must Poland draw and signal its own gray-zone threshold?

Because a boundary defined only after a crossing hands the pacing of the campaign entirely to the actor running it. Pre-commitment does three things an undeclared position cannot. It converts an open-ended accumulation into a sequence of decision points the defender has thought about in advance. It removes definitional refuge from the defender's own decision process, so that a serious incident cannot be neutralized by an internal argument about whether it counted. It communicates something to the adversary, where silence communicates nothing at all. The declaration will not bind allies and will be probed, but a defender arriving at an emergency consultation with a stated prior position is in a substantially stronger place than one arriving with none.

### Q: Is there a clear automatic line between gray-zone and war?

No, and believing there is constitutes one of the two standard errors. No treaty text specifies a trigger, no doctrine supplies a switch, and no body of practice has settled the matter for acts that produce disruption rather than destruction or that are executed through intermediaries. What exists instead is a set of contested legal criteria, a political process that may or may not reach consensus, and precedent that is thin in exactly the areas that matter most. The opposite error, holding that no boundary exists at all, is equally damaging, because it licenses indefinite absorption. The workable position is that a boundary can be constructed and declared by a defender even though none is given.

### Q: Can accumulated gray-zone pressure boil Poland slowly?

Yes, and this is the failure mode the whole framework is designed to prevent. The mechanism is normalization: institutional and public tolerance adjusts to what has become routine, so a level of hostile activity that would have prompted emergency consultation if it appeared without precedent becomes background when it arrives gradually. Each increment is judged against the current baseline rather than the original one, so no increment ever looks like a crossing. Fragmentation compounds it, because activity distributed across domains belongs to different institutions and the pattern exists only at an aggregate level where nobody is necessarily looking. A category-based boundary resists this, because a category either has or has not appeared regardless of what has become normal.

### Q: What marks a genuine crossing from gray-zone to attack?

Category change rather than severity increase. The transitions carrying the most weight are the move from property to persons, where acts begin producing death or serious injury rather than damage and disruption, and the move from deniable to acknowledged, where the acting state stops contesting responsibility. Action against military targets and command functions rather than civil systems is a third. These matter because they cannot be processed as routine, because they force the question of what the act means, and because they supply the political raw material for a decision that pure accumulation does not. A crossing is more usefully understood as an act a defender cannot classify as more of the same, whatever its measured severity.

### Q: Why does a gray-zone line defined only after crossing invite exploitation?

Because retrospective definition is always available to be argued away. When an incident occurs and no prior position exists, the definitional dispute becomes the vehicle for whoever prefers not to act, and the dispute is genuine enough to be respectable. The adversary can therefore approach any point knowing that arrival will produce argument rather than response, which is precisely the condition under which the slow approach works. Retrospective definition also lets the defender's own baseline drift, since each judgment is made against what has recently become normal rather than against a fixed standard. Prior definition removes both effects: the argument has been had, and the standard does not move.

### Q: How much warning does an approach to the gray-zone threshold give?

The trend is long and the event is short. Target-set migration, hardening rhetoric, declining operational care, and cross-domain convergence accumulate over months or years and are largely visible in open sources, so a defender is rarely surprised by direction. The specific act may offer no warning at all, because most coercive acts require little preparation and execution is the first observable event. This split means sub-threshold warning is not useful as tactical alert but is valuable as a decision framework, telling a defender that certain categories have become materially more likely. The exception worth watching is compression, where activity that had been spread across months occurs within days, which carries genuine short-notice value.

### Q: Why do observers miss a gray-zone campaign crossing the line?

Rarely for want of information. The standard failures are normalization, where tolerance adjusts to the routine; fragmentation, where each domain belongs to a different institution and nobody holds the aggregate; attribution deferral, where the genuine difficulty of attribution supplies an institutionally safe reason to postpone judgment indefinitely; and the incentive asymmetry that penalizes an unvindicated warning far more reliably than a missed one. A fifth is definitional refuge, where an available argument about whether a boundary was met substitutes for a decision. Each has a structural remedy: assign aggregation explicitly, separate the assessment bar from the response bar, and pre-commit to categories so that normalization cannot move the standard.

### Q: Can one gray-zone incident by itself push the situation into open war?

It can, and this is the second failure mode, the mirror of slow boiling. A single incident that produces significant casualties, that is attributed rapidly and publicly, and that arrives in a period of already elevated tension can generate political conditions in which non-response is untenable. The path to conflict then runs through a defender's forced choice rather than through an adversary's decision, which means it can occur even where neither side sought it. This is why escalation control belongs in the same framework as boundary-setting. A defender that has thought in advance about which categories it treats as a crossing has also thought about which it does not, and that second half is what preserves the option not to escalate.

### Q: How does an analyst separate gray-zone noise from a threshold signal?

By weighting context, pattern, and cost rather than judging incidents in isolation. Context legitimately shifts the balance between innocent and hostile explanations, and refusing to let it shift is an error rather than rigor. Pattern degrades innocent explanations faster than intuition suggests, since perfect deniability across dozens of events is not what accident looks like. Cost is the sharpest filter: signals expensive to fake, such as an acting state preparing to absorb a response or abandoning deniability it had been paying to maintain, outrank cheap signals such as rhetoric alone. Absence of activity carries little information, because coercive campaigns can pause indefinitely without any change of intent.

### Q: Do casualties move a gray-zone act across the threshold?

Casualties are the single strongest category marker available, but they are not automatic. They matter because they engage legal criteria concerning scale and effects far more directly than disruption does, because they are politically impossible to normalize, and because deliberately accepting them signals a change in the acting state's tolerance for consequences. What prevents them from being automatic is attribution, which remains contested regardless of the harm caused, and alliance politics, where members facing different exposure may reach different conclusions about the same deaths. The practical implication is that a defender that intends casualties to function as its boundary should say so in advance, because the category's weight comes from prior commitment as much as from the harm itself.

### Q: What makes a declared gray-zone threshold credible to the adversary?

Consistency, capacity, and level. Consistency means the declaration matches what the state has actually done when tested, since a stated limit contradicted by past absorption is discounted immediately. Capacity means the evident ability to act on the statement, because a commitment a state cannot execute deters nobody. Level means the declaration is embedded in national security documents, repeated across administrations, and reflected in posture rather than voiced once by a single official. The design rule that follows is to state the boundary where the state genuinely would act rather than where it wishes it would, because a conservative limit honestly held does more work than an ambitious one that will not survive its first test.
