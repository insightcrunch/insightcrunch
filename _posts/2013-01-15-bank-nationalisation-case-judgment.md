The Bank Nationalisation Case is filed in the memory of the legal profession as a case about property, and it is usually taught as the high-water mark of judicial resistance to economic legislation. That filing is wrong, and it conceals what the eleven judges who decided Rustom Cavasjee Cooper v Union of India on 10 February 1970 actually did. The nationalisation they struck down was re-enacted within weeks and never seriously disturbed again. The compensation doctrine they applied was removed from the Constitution by amendment within two years and the property right itself within eight. What survived, and what grew, was a holding about method: a holding about how a court is to test a law against the fundamental rights chapter, and about whether those rights stand in separate compartments or answer to one another. The interpretive question the case posed was never really about banks. It was about whether a statute that expropriates property is to be judged only by the article that governs expropriation, or by every right its operation in fact invades.

![The Bank Nationalisation Case, showing the Cooper judgment, the eleven-judge bench and the effect test - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That question mattered because the answer given twenty years earlier, in A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras, had organised the whole of Indian fundamental rights law around separation. Under Gopalan, each right in Part III occupied its own compartment, a law was classified by its object and its pith and substance, and once classified it was tested against the one article that governed its subject and against no other. A preventive detention law was tested against Article 22 and not against the freedoms in Article 19. A property law was tested against Article 31 and not against the equality guarantee read with the freedoms of trade and of holding property. The Cooper bench, by ten votes to one, discarded that architecture. In its place the Court laid down that the rights are not mutually exclusive, that a law is to be judged by its direct operation and effect on the rights of the person before the court, and that a petitioner is entitled to have the statute measured against every guarantee its working actually touches. Every later expansion of rights in India, from the widening of the right to life in Maneka Gandhi to the recognition of privacy in Puttaswamy, runs on rails that Cooper laid. The banking case changed the constitution of the rights chapter while appearing to decide a dispute about compensation for fourteen banks.

This article takes the judgment apart in the order a lawyer needs it: the statute as it stood and the two grounds on which it fell, the standing holding that let a shareholder through the door, the Gopalan doctrine and what replaced it, the dissent, the legislative and constitutional sequel, and the lineage that makes the case the doctrinal bridge between the silo reading of the nineteen fifties and the integrated reading on which the privacy and civil liberties decisions rest.

## The statute the Court was asked to judge

The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969 was Act 22 of 1969, and it arrived by a two-step route that mattered to the litigation. On 19 July 1969, with Parliament about to meet, the government took over the fourteen largest Indian commercial banks by ordinance, the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969, promulgated by the acting President, V.V. Giri. The ordinance was replaced within weeks by the Act, which Parliament passed in August 1969 and which was deemed to operate from 19 July 1969, the date of the ordinance. The full passage record of that statute and of its 1970 successor, including the debates and the committee stages, belongs to [the account of the bank nationalisation Acts and their legislative history](/2013/01/01/bank-nationalisation-acts-india/), and is summarised here only to the extent the judgment requires.

The Act named fourteen banking companies and transferred the whole of each undertaking to a corresponding new bank, a corporation owned by the Central Government, with effect from the date of the ordinance. The fourteen were selected by a deposit threshold. Every Indian banking company whose demand and time liabilities were not less than fifty crore rupees fell inside the net, and every one below it, together with every foreign bank operating in India however large its Indian business, fell outside. The names are a roll of the Indian banking of the period: the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda, the United Commercial Bank, Canara Bank, the United Bank of India, Dena Bank, Syndicate Bank, the Union Bank of India, Allahabad Bank, the Indian Bank, the Bank of Maharashtra and the Indian Overseas Bank.

Three operative features of the statute carried the constitutional defects. The first was the transfer itself: Sections 4, 5 and 6, read with the Second Schedule, vested each undertaking in the new bank and prescribed how the price of that vesting was to be computed. The second was the prohibition that followed the transfer. The named banking companies were forbidden to carry on banking business at all, and were confined to a narrow residue of non-banking activity connected with winding down what had been taken. The third was the compensation scheme. The existing banks were not promised a price fixed in the Act. They were promised an amount computed in accordance with principles set out in the statute, and payable not in cash at the transfer but in government securities. Each of those three features generated one limb of the petitioner's case, and the scheme as a whole generated the discrimination argument: the same banking business that the fourteen were forbidden to conduct remained open to every smaller Indian bank and to every foreign bank in the country.

### Which banks did the 1969 Act take over?

The Act took over fourteen named Indian banking companies, being every Indian bank whose demand and time liabilities stood at fifty crore rupees or more: among them the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India, the Punjab National Bank, the Bank of Baroda and Canara Bank. Foreign banks and smaller Indian banks were left untouched.

The petitioner attacked the Act on the equality article, Article 14, on the freedoms guaranteed by Article 19(1)(f), the right to acquire, hold and dispose of property, and Article 19(1)(g), the right to practise a profession or carry on a trade or business, and on Article 31, in particular clause (2), which then provided that no property could be compulsorily acquired save for a public purpose and under a law that fixed the amount of compensation or specified the principles on which compensation was to be determined and given. There was also an argument founded on Article 301, the freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, and a challenge to the ordinance route itself. The Union defended the Act as a measure of social control of banking within the legislative entries, defended the compensation principles as a legislative judgment into which the Court could not enter after the Fourth Amendment, and defended the selection of the fourteen as a reasonable classification founded on size. Attorney-General Niren De appeared for the Union. For the petitioner, N.A. Palkhivala made the argument that has since become the standard account of the case, and his submissions on the interrelation of the rights supplied much of the structure the majority eventually adopted.

## What a transfer of undertaking meant in practice

The phrase the statute used, transfer of the undertaking, deserves unpacking, because the compensation argument cannot be understood without knowing exactly what changed hands. The undertaking of a banking company is not its share capital and it is not its name. It is the whole going business: the deposits it held and the obligations they represented, the loan book and the securities portfolio, the branch network and the premises, the staff and their accumulated knowledge of borrowers, the licences, the agency arrangements, the standing instructions, the customer relationships of decades. On the appointed day the statute moved all of it, by operation of law and without individual conveyances, from each named company to a corresponding new bank, a body corporate created by the statute itself and owned by the Central Government. The depositor who banked with the Central Bank of India on 18 July 1969 banked with the Central Bank of India on 20 July 1969, at the same branch, served by the same staff, under the same nameplate; only the ownership behind the counter had changed, and the statute's draftsmen had taken care that the customer should notice nothing.

What was left behind with the old company was the legal husk: the corporate person, its shareholders, and those assets and liabilities the statute did not transfer, together with a restricted permission to carry on business other than banking for the purposes of realising what remained. It is that residue which explains the shape of the discrimination complaint. The old companies were not dissolved; dissolution would at least have been even-handed in its finality. They were kept alive and simultaneously disqualified from the only business they existed to conduct, while the market they vacated was left open to every competitor the statute had not named. The shareholder's grievance had the same structure. His shares remained shares, in a company that still existed, but the enterprise that gave them value had been lifted out of the company by statute, and the question the Court had to answer was whether that operation touched his rights or only the company's. The transfer mechanics also explain the compensation scheme's design. Because the undertaking moved as a whole and at a stroke, the state needed a method of pricing fourteen whole businesses inside a single statute, and the method it chose, computation by prescribed principles rather than a negotiated or scheduled price, became the second ground of the challenge.

## The competence question the Court answered on the way through

One limb of the petition has dropped out of most accounts because it failed completely, but it completes the picture of the case and it mattered to the drafting of every takeover statute after 1970. The petitioners challenged Parliament's legislative competence: the Union List gives Parliament banking, and it gives Parliament the acquisition and requisitioning of property, but the argument ran that an Act which seized the entire undertaking of a company, including assets and activities that were not banking at all, could not shelter under the banking entry, and that its true character had to be found elsewhere, with consequences for its validity. The Court rejected the challenge. The pith and substance of the Act was the acquisition of the undertakings of banking companies for the purpose of a public banking system, a subject within Parliament's fields, and the circumstance that an acquired undertaking carries with it everything the business owns and does, banking and non-banking alike, does not convert an acquisition law into legislation on some other subject. The distinction the answer illustrates is the one the judgment as a whole depends on: pith and substance remains the correct method for locating a law within the Seventh Schedule, because competence is a question about the legislature's field; the effect test is the correct method for measuring the law against Part III, because rights are a question about the citizen. Confusing the two methods was the analytical heart of the Union's defence, which asked the Court to carry the classification that settled competence across into the rights inquiry and to let the entry decide the articles. The Court's refusal to allow that carry-over, while itself using pith and substance for the purpose it fits, is the judgment's most transferable piece of technique, and it is routinely the first thing a competent modern challenge separates.

## The shareholder at the door: standing

Before the merits, the Court had to decide whether Rustom Cavasjee Cooper could be heard at all, and the holding on that threshold question is a significant company law decision in its own right, routinely cited far outside the banking context. Cooper was a shareholder in several of the named banks and a director of the Central Bank of India. He held shares; he did not hold the undertakings. The assets expropriated belonged in law to the banking companies, and a company is a legal person separate from its shareholders. The Union's objection followed a well-marked path. A company incorporated in India is not a citizen, and the freedoms in Article 19 are confined to citizens, so the companies could not invoke them. A shareholder, the argument ran, has no fundamental right in the assets of the company; his right is to a share in profits and in the residue on winding up. On the authorities as they stood, the objection had real strength. In Chiranjit Lal Chowdhuri v Union of India, decided in 1951, a shareholder of the Sholapur Spinning and Weaving Company had failed in a similar challenge to a takeover statute, the Court holding that the rights affected were the company's and that the shareholder's own rights were not directly infringed. The State Trading Corporation case of 1963 had confirmed that a corporation cannot claim the Article 19 freedoms even where every shareholder is a citizen.

The majority in Cooper did not overrule that line; it refined the question. The test, the Court held, is not whose property the statute names but whether the impugned state action infringes the petitioner's own fundamental rights, directly and inescapably, whatever person the action formally addresses. Cooper's shares were his property. The value of those shares was not an abstraction: it depended entirely on the undertaking behind them, and the state action that stripped the companies of the undertaking and forbade them the banking business destroyed the substance of what his shares represented, including his right, as a shareholder, to the dividends and to the effective enjoyment of the investment. Where a law operates in that way on the shareholder himself, the shareholder may maintain a petition under Article 32 complaining of the infringement of his own rights, and in measuring that infringement the Court may look at what the law does to the company whose shares he holds.

The distinction the case therefore draws has organised shareholder litigation since. A shareholder cannot borrow the company's rights, and cannot complain merely because the company's assets have been dealt with in a way that affects him reflectively and at a distance. He can complain where the law's operation on the company is, in substance and in direct effect, an operation on rights the Constitution guarantees to him: his property in the shares, his freedom to carry on the business the statute forbids him, his right to equal treatment. That is the first appearance in the case of the effect test, and it appears at the stage of standing before it appears at the stage of doctrine. The form of the nationalisation was a transfer of undertakings from companies to corporations. Its effect was on men and women who held the shares, and the Court resolved to judge it by the effect. Palkhivala's petitioner was, on that footing, entitled to be heard, and eleven judges proceeded to the merits.

## The shadow of Golaknath: why the bench had eleven judges

The size of the bench was itself an announcement. Benches of eleven are not assembled for ordinary error correction; they are assembled when the Court proposes to reconsider the foundations of a field, and every informed observer in 1969 understood that the Gopalan settlement of 1950 was on the table. Gopalan had been decided by six judges, and the convention of bench discipline meant that its authority could be displaced with confidence only by a substantially larger formation. The political moment reinforced the institutional one. Three years earlier, in Golak Nath v State of Punjab, another bench of eleven had held by six to five that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights at all, a holding that had frozen the amendment route for land reform and property legislation and had set the Court and Parliament on the collision course that the Twenty-fourth and Twenty-fifth Amendments were soon to map. The bank case arrived in the middle of that confrontation, and it arrived carrying the property question in its most politically charged form: not a zamindari abolition or a ceiling law, but the takeover of the commanding institutions of private credit by a government that had made the takeover the emblem of its economic programme.

The domestic politics of July 1969 belong in the record because they explain both the ordinance route and the ferocity of the argument. The Prime Minister, Indira Gandhi, had taken the finance portfolio from Morarji Desai days before the takeover, in a rupture inside the ruling party that would harden into a formal split within months. Bank nationalisation was the policy on which the contest within the Congress was publicly fought: the party's established leadership favoured social control of banks through regulation, and the Prime Minister's camp carried the argument for ownership. The ordinance of 19 July 1969, promulgated by the acting President while the elected President's office stood vacant after the death of Zakir Husain, took the banks on the eve of the parliamentary session, presenting the legislature with a completed act of state. A challenge was certain, and it was equally certain that the challenge would be argued, on both sides, as a case about the Constitution and not about banking administration. The Union defended the statute with the Attorney-General at the head of its team. The petitioners retained N.A. Palkhivala, whose reputation had been made in tax and company matters and who used the case to advance the integrated theory of the rights he had argued in print and in court for years. When eleven judges sat, they knew they were being asked to do three things at once: to price the boundary between legislative valuation and judicial review, to decide whether the equality article tolerates the disabling of named companies, and to say whether the architecture of Part III erected in 1950 still stood. The judgment answered all three, and it is the third answer that proved indestructible.

## The doctrine Cooper inherited: Gopalan and the compartments

To see what Cooper changed, the starting point is the case it displaced. A.K. Gopalan v State of Madras was decided in 1950, in the first year of the Constitution, on a challenge to the Preventive Detention Act of that year, the statute whose own history and reading are treated in [the account of the Preventive Detention Act, 1950](/2010/06/15/preventive-detention-act-1950-explained/), which is where that cluster owns the compartment doctrine in its original setting. Gopalan, a Communist leader detained under the Act, argued that preventive detention invaded his freedom of movement under Article 19(1)(d) and could survive only if it also satisfied Article 19's tests of reasonable restriction. The Court refused the argument. The majority held that the articles of Part III deal with distinct subject matters: Article 19 protects specified freedoms against specified kinds of restriction, Article 21 protects life and personal liberty against executive action unsupported by law, and Article 22 supplies the code for preventive detention. A law of preventive detention was therefore to be tested against Articles 21 and 22, and the freedoms in Article 19 simply had no application to it. Each article was, in the metaphor that attached itself to the case, a separate compartment, watertight against the others.

The consequences of the compartment doctrine were large and systematic. It meant that the classification of a statute determined its constitutional exposure almost completely. Draft a law as a property acquisition law and it answered to Article 31 alone; the acquiring state did not have to show that the restrictions it imposed on the owner's freedoms of trade and property were reasonable under Article 19, because Article 19 was a different compartment. Draft a law as a detention law and it answered to Articles 21 and 22 alone. The doctrine also carried a method: to classify the law, the court looked to its object and its subject matter, to the legislative entry it belonged under, to what the statute purported to do. That method, the object test, and the compartment doctrine, were two faces of one position. If each right governs only laws aimed at its subject, then the way to find which right governs is to identify the law's object.

By 1970 the position had already been softened at its edges without being abandoned. A line of cases had held, against the broadest reading of Gopalan, that a law might have to answer to more than one article: the passport case, Satwant Singh Sawhney v D. Ramarathnam in 1967, read Article 21 with generosity and reached a result the compartments would have made difficult, and in Kharak Singh in 1963 Subba Rao J., in dissent, had argued the integrated position in full, that the rights overlap and that personal liberty is the residue of the freedoms. The majority doctrine nevertheless remained the authority a petitioner had to displace, and it was the Union's simplest answer in Cooper: the Act was a law for compulsory acquisition of property under Article 31(2); Article 31 was a complete code on acquisition; the petitioner had to live within it; and within it, after the Fourth Amendment, his compensation complaint was confined to the narrow ground that the principles stated in the law were irrelevant to the value of what was taken. If the compartments held, most of the petition failed at the threshold of classification.

## The road between Gopalan and Cooper: how the compartments loosened

The Cooper bench did not write on a blank page, and the two decades between the two cases explain why ten judges were ready to move. The loosening came from three directions, and a reader who traces them can see the judgment arriving before it was delivered. The first direction was the speech jurisprudence. In cases such as Express Newspapers v Union of India in 1958 and Sakal Papers v Union of India in 1962, the Court, faced with fiscal and regulatory measures aimed at the newspaper industry, asked what the measures did to the freedom of the press rather than accepting their fiscal or industrial labels, and struck or read them by their operation on the right. A tax or a wage regulation that in substance controlled circulation was treated as a speech measure because that was its effect. The reasoning was confined to its facts each time, but its logic was the effect test in embryo, waiting for a case large enough to generalise it.

The second direction was the shareholder litigation itself. The Sholapur mill takeover produced two decisions in the republic's first years: Chiranjit Lal Chowdhuri in 1951, which turned the shareholder away, and Dwarkadas Shrinivas v Sholapur Spinning and Weaving Company in 1954, where a bench presided over by Das C.J. examined the same takeover on its merits and analysed in depth what the state had taken and whose interests the taking engaged. The two cases together left the law in an unsatisfying state that any careful advocate could exploit: the company's property could be taken under an ordinance whose validity the courts would examine when the state or the company raised it, while the shareholder, whose investment was the human substance of the enterprise, was told his injury was not his own to plead. The third direction was internal to the Article 21 line itself. In Kharak Singh v State of Uttar Pradesh in 1963, the majority struck down domiciliary visits while refusing the broader claim, but Subba Rao J.'s dissent set out the integrated theory entire: that the rights are not a catalogue of isolated guarantees but together secure the conditions of human freedom, and that a measure must be tested against whichever of them its operation touches. Satwant Singh Sawhney's passport decision in 1967 then showed a majority reaching a liberty-protective result by reading Article 21 generously rather than by the compartment method. By 1969, in short, the compartments survived as the stated doctrine while the Court's practice had been leaking through them for a decade. Cooper's achievement was to say so, to retire the stated doctrine, and to give the practice a canonical formulation. Judgments that ratify a drift already under way tend to be the durable ones, because the profession has been rehearsing their reasoning in advance; Cooper is the type specimen.

## The compensation guarantee the petitioner inherited

The second thread the bench had to handle was older than the compartments: what Article 31(2) promised a person whose property the state took. As enacted in 1950, the clause required a public purpose and a law that fixed the amount of compensation or specified the principles for determining it. In State of West Bengal v Bela Banerjee, decided in 1954, the Court read the word compensation in its ordinary sense: a just equivalent of what the owner had been deprived of. Parliament answered with the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, which amended Article 31(2) to provide that no acquisition law could be called in question in any court on the ground that the compensation provided by it was not adequate. The contest then moved to the margin the amendment had left. In P. Vajravelu Mudaliar v Special Deputy Collector, decided in 1965, the Court held that the Fourth Amendment did not make the legislature the unreviewable judge of its own payment. The principles specified in the law still had to be relevant to the determination of the value of the property taken, and a law whose principles produced an amount that was illusory, or that rested on considerations having nothing to do with the property at the date of acquisition, remained open to challenge, not for inadequacy in the court's estimation but for failure to be compensation legislation at all.

That was the narrow bridge on which the Cooper petitioners had to cross, and everyone in the case understood its width. The Court could not re-open adequacy; the Fourth Amendment forbade it. It could ask whether the 1969 Act's principles were principles for determining the value of the undertakings taken, or whether they were a set of accounting directions that, applied honestly, would produce a figure bearing no relation to that value. The majority crossed on exactly that bridge, and the care with which it did so explains both the power of the judgment and its vulnerability. A holding that a redistributive legislature must pay a just equivalent was always going to be answered by amendment. A holding about method, about how rights interlock and how effect displaces object, could not be amended away without amending the rights chapter itself, and no government attempted that. The sequel, traced below, bears the distinction out: the compensation holding was reversed by constitutional amendment inside two years; the method holding was never reversed at all.

## How the case was argued

The architecture of the petitioner's argument is worth reconstructing, because the judgment follows it closely and because it became the template for rights litigation after 1970. It began with the man, not the statute: Cooper's own rights as a shareholder and director, in his shares, in the dividends they carried, and in the business the Act forbade. From that footing Palkhivala submitted that the Act had to answer to the whole of Part III. Article 31 was not a self-contained code, because the rights in Articles 14, 19 and 31 protect different aspects of a citizen's position and a single law may trench upon all of them together; Gopalan's mutual exclusivity, it was argued, rested on reading each article as though the others did not exist, and it ought to be reconsidered by the larger bench. On compensation, the submission was precisely framed to fit the Vajravelu margin: not that the Court should value the banks, but that the principles in the statute were not principles of compensation at all, because they directed attention to balance-sheet entries rather than to the value of the undertaking as a going concern, excluded elements of real worth, and then discharged the resulting sum in securities over time, so that the owner was denied the equivalent the Constitution promised even on the most deferential reading of the Fourth Amendment. On equality, the submission was the shortest in the case: Parliament may classify for acquisition, but it may not, having acquired, disable the fourteen from an occupation it leaves open to every other bank in the country, foreign banks included.

The Union's argument, led by Attorney-General Niren De, was equally systematic and deserves to be recorded as more than a foil. On competence, the Act marched under the banking entry and the acquisition entry, and its pith and substance was acquisition of property for a public purpose, which carried it into Article 31(2) and into no other article's territory. On the interrelation point, the Union stood on Gopalan and on the structure of Part III: Article 19(1)(f) protects the citizen's right to hold property against restrictions, Article 31 governs the distinct and greater power of taking property away altogether, and a taking cannot be recharacterised as a restriction in order to import the reasonableness test of Article 19(5) through a side door. On compensation, the Union submitted that the Fourth Amendment had closed the question the petitioner sought to reopen: the legislature had specified principles, the adequacy of the product was non-justiciable, and a court that examined whether the principles were generous enough was examining adequacy under another name. On equality, size was a differentia with an obvious nexus to the control of credit, and the prohibition on the named companies was no more than the necessary consequence of the transfer, since a company whose undertaking has been acquired cannot sensibly continue as a bank. On the threshold objection, the Union relied on Chiranjit Lal Chowdhuri and the State Trading Corporation case: the grievance was the company's, the company was not a citizen, and the petitioner's reflective loss as a shareholder could not manufacture a fundamental rights claim. Each of those submissions had authority behind it, and on the law as it stood in 1969 several of them looked stronger than the submissions that defeated them. The judgment is a measure of how completely the Court was persuaded that the 1950 settlement had failed to describe the document it governed.

## Eleven judges, one dissent

The bench was constituted of eleven judges, among the largest the Court had assembled, because the case put in issue the continuing authority of Gopalan and the construction of Article 31 after a line of earlier decisions. The judges were S.M. Sikri, J.C. Shah, J.M. Shelat, Vishishtha Bhargava, G.K. Mitter, C.A. Vaidialingam, K.S. Hegde, A.N. Grover, A.N. Ray, P. Jaganmohan Reddy and I.D. Dua. The arguments ran over the shape of the rights chapter as much as over banking, and the judgment, delivered on 10 February 1970, ran to a conclusion the political world felt immediately: by ten votes to one, the Act was declared unconstitutional and void. Shah J. wrote the judgment of the majority, for himself and nine colleagues. Ray J. dissented alone.

The structure of Shah J.'s opinion is worth attention, because it is the structure of the effect test applied as method rather than announced as slogan. The Court first disposed of standing, by the route described above. It then took the challenges in turn: the equality challenge, the freedoms challenges, and the compensation challenge. On each, the question asked was the same question put three ways: not what the Act called itself or which entry it marched under, but what the Act did, and to whom, and against which guarantee that doing had to be measured. On the equality ground, the Act failed because the fourteen were singled out for a prohibition imposed on no competitor. On the freedoms, the petitioner's rights under Article 19(1)(f) and 19(1)(g) were held to be directly invaded by an Act that dispossessed the companies and forbade the business. On compensation, the Act failed because the principles it specified were not relevant to the value of the undertakings and the amount they yielded was not compensation in the constitutional sense. Sections 4, 5 and 6 read with the Second Schedule were the operative provisions, and they were not severable from the whole; the Act fell in its entirety. The Court declined to rest anything on the ordinance challenge, the Act having replaced the ordinance, and the Article 301 argument carried the petitioner no further once the Act had fallen on the other grounds.

One feature of the majority opinion deserves emphasis against the folklore that later grew around the case. The Court did not hold that banking could not be nationalised. It did not question the public purpose of the acquisition, it did not question Parliament's competence to legislate for the takeover of banking companies, and it did not hold that the state must pay a market price as a condition of social legislation in general. It held that this Act, as drafted, discriminated in the manner described and paid by principles that were not compensation principles. Both defects were within the drafting gift of the government to cure, and the government cured them, as the sequel shows, in a matter of weeks. The judgment was therefore, on its property side, an instruction in constitutional drafting delivered at the point of a struck-down statute. On its method side, it was something much larger, and the method side is where the analysis must go next.

## One voice for ten judges

A feature of the decision that shaped its authority deserves a paragraph of its own. Ten judges spoke through a single opinion. Shah J. wrote for himself and for Sikri, Shelat, Bhargava, Mitter, Vaidialingam, Hegde, Grover, Jaganmohan Reddy and Dua JJ., and none of the ten added a concurrence. The institutional choice mattered. A doctrine announced in one voice by ten judges of an eleven-judge bench enters the reports with a weight that fragmented majorities cannot match, and the propositions of Cooper could be quoted for fifty years without the preliminary inquiry, necessary after Kesavananda Bharati three years later, into which of eleven opinions commanded the majority on which point. Kesavananda, decided by thirteen judges in eleven opinions with a summary of the majority view signed after the event, left generations of lawyers to reconstruct its holdings; Cooper left nothing to reconstruct. Part of the explanation is temperamental and part is structural. The Cooper majority were agreed on the destination and content to let one colleague chart the route, and the route charted was a method rather than a list of conclusions, which is the kind of holding a single opinion states best. The solitary dissent completed the clarity: a reader of the case knows exactly what was decided, exactly what was rejected, and exactly who stood where, and that legibility is itself a component of the judgment's power in the classroom and in the courtroom. Few benches of comparable size have managed it, and the contrast with the case that dominates the following section of this article's story is the sharpest in the Indian reports.

## The first ground: hostile discrimination

The equality holding is the least remembered part of Cooper and the most mechanically instructive, because it shows the effect test working on a ground where no doctrine had to change. Article 14 forbids the state to deny any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws, and by 1970 its settled construction permitted classification for a legitimate purpose provided the classification rested on an intelligible differentia bearing a rational relation to the purpose. The Union's defence of the fourteen was a classification defence: size was the differentia, and size bore an obvious relation to the purpose of social control of the banking system, because the fourteen between them held the commanding share of the deposits and the credit of the country. If the Act had done no more than acquire the fourteen, that defence might well have held throughout.

The Act, however, did two things, and the second was fatal. Having acquired the undertakings, it prohibited the named banking companies from carrying on banking business altogether, while imposing no such prohibition on anyone else. A foreign bank with a large Indian business could continue to accept deposits and lend. A smaller Indian bank could do the same, and could grow into the space the fourteen had vacated. The named companies alone were excluded from the occupation of banking as such, and were left with a statutory shell and a limited licence to carry on business other than banking in the course of realising what remained to them. In the phrase the judgment made standard, this was hostile discrimination: the vice was not that the state had selected the largest banks for acquisition, but that, having taken their undertakings, it disabled the transferors from a trade it left open to every competitor, Indian and foreign, without any justification connected to the purpose of the Act. The majority treated the prohibition as inseparable from the scheme. The Act did not survive its equality defect by severance, because the transfer and the prohibition were parts of one design, and the design was to retire the fourteen companies from banking permanently while public ownership replaced them.

The point repays a lawyer's attention because of what it reveals about the judgment's temper. The discrimination was a drafting choice, not a necessity of nationalisation. A statute that acquired the undertakings and compensated the companies, leaving them legally capable of such business as companies in their position might lawfully pursue, would not have carried the same Article 14 exposure; and in the event, the defect was among those the re-enacted statute of 1970 addressed. The Court was not striking at the socialisation of banking. It was striking at a prohibition that punished fourteen named companies by exclusion from an occupation, a technique the equality article does not permit whatever the merits of the acquisition itself.

## Singling out by name: the classification doctrine behind the equality holding

The hostile discrimination holding applied a body of Article 14 doctrine that had been built in the republic's first decade, and a reader who knows that doctrine can see both why the holding was available and why it was not inevitable. The settled test, stated in Ram Krishna Dalmia v Justice S.R. Tendolkar in 1958, permits the legislature to classify persons and things for the purposes of a law, presumes the classification to be constitutional, and asks only whether it rests on an intelligible differentia and whether that differentia bears a rational relation to the object of the statute. Legislation may even be directed at a single person or a single company, provided the circumstances make the singling out reasonable rather than arbitrary. The earlier decision in State of West Bengal v Anwar Ali Sarkar in 1952 had shown the other side of the doctrine, striking down a special courts law whose selection of cases for a harsher procedure rested on no guiding principle the Court could accept. Between those poles lay the Cooper question: not whether the fourteen could be selected, for the size threshold and its relation to control of credit made that selection defensible under Dalmia, but whether the disabling prohibition could ride on the same classification.

The majority's answer separates the two operations of the Act and applies the doctrine to each. Selection for acquisition is one legislative act, justified by the object of acquiring the commanding heights of the banking system. Prohibition from the occupation is a second and different act, and for it the size of the bank supplies no justification at all, because the interests said to require public ownership of the large banks do not require that their former owners be barred from a trade open to banks of every other size and nationality. A classification that is rational for acquisition does not become rational for exclusion merely because the same statute performs both. The phrase hostile discrimination captures exactly that gap between the classification and the burden: a burden imposed on the class that the class's defining characteristic cannot explain, and that falls on no one outside it. It remains the cleanest illustration in the reports of the proposition that Article 14 reviews not only who is chosen but what is done to them after they are chosen.

## From hostile discrimination to manifest arbitrariness: the Article 14 line

The equality holding also has a progeny, quieter than the effect test's but traceable in the reports. Cooper's discrimination analysis proceeded by the classical classification route and found the vice in the mismatch between the class chosen and the burden imposed. Four years later, in E.P. Royappa v State of Tamil Nadu, Bhagwati J. restated the equality guarantee at a higher level of generality: equality is antithetic to arbitrariness, and an action that is arbitrary is, by that quality alone, unequal. Maneka Gandhi carried that proposition into the centre of Article 14 doctrine, and the modern cases have completed the movement by applying the arbitrariness inquiry to legislation itself, most prominently in Shayara Bano v Union of India in 2017, where a plurality struck down a statutory practice as manifestly arbitrary. The line from Cooper to that result is not a straight citation chain, and it should not be drawn as one. It is a line of intellectual descent: the Cooper majority had shown a court examining what a statute actually does to the class it names, and refusing to let the legislature's own characterisation of its classification close the inquiry. Once that refusal is generalised from classifications to legislation as such, manifest arbitrariness is where it arrives. The discrimination holding of 1970 is therefore best understood as the classical form of a review the Court later learned to conduct in a more abstract vocabulary, and a pleader attacking a modern singling-out statute will still find the Cooper formulation, a burden the class's defining trait cannot explain, the most concrete version of the argument available.

## The second ground: compensation that was not compensation

The compensation holding turns on the particular scheme the 1969 Act adopted, and it is misstated in most summaries, which say simply that the Court found the amount too low. The Court had no power to find the amount too low; the Fourth Amendment had taken adequacy out of its hands. What it examined was the principles. The Act did not fix the sum for each bank in its text. It directed that the amount be ascertained in accordance with principles set out in the statute and its schedule, and those principles directed a computation built from the balance sheet: values assigned to categories of assets and liabilities by prescribed methods, in a framework that treated the undertaking as a collection of items to be added and subtracted. The petitioner's case, which the majority accepted, was that this mode of computation was not a method of valuing what had been taken at all. What had been taken was not a heap of assets but fourteen going concerns: banking businesses with branch networks, deposit franchises, customer connections, staff organisations and goodwill built over generations, whose worth as undertakings exceeded any sum extracted from their balance sheets by rules that ignored the earning capacity and the business as a whole.

Two features of the scheme compounded the irrelevance. The principles prescribed methods of valuation for individual assets which, in important applications, denied value to elements that formed part of the undertaking's real worth, so that the arithmetic could be performed faithfully and still arrive at a figure unrelated to the value of the property acquired. And the amount, once computed, was not to be paid over in money at the taking: it was to be satisfied in government securities, so that the owner received, in exchange for a business taken on a single day, paper whose real value depended on terms extending into the future. Put together, the majority held, principles of that character were not principles for determining compensation within Article 31(2), because they were not relevant to the value of the property at the date of acquisition; the amount they produced was illusory, a payment bearing the name of compensation without its substance; and a law that acquired property on such terms impaired the guarantee of Article 31(2) as that clause had been construed since Bela Banerjee and Vajravelu. On that ground, as on the equality ground, the operative provisions were void, and being inseverable, they carried the whole Act down.

The discipline of the holding should be marked. The Court did not substitute its own valuation. It did not hold that the securities form of payment is unconstitutional in every acquisition, or that deferred payment can never feature in a lawful scheme. It held that this scheme, in its combination of irrelevant principles and deferred satisfaction, did not answer the description of compensation which the Constitution, in the text as it stood, required. The government could pay, and pay by a scheme of its own design, provided the principles it chose were relevant to value and the result was a real and not a nominal equivalent. That invitation, too, was accepted in the re-enactment, which abandoned the computation scheme and wrote fixed amounts for each bank into its Second Schedule.

## Anatomy of the computation the Court condemned

The compensation holding is often reported as though the Court had simply preferred a higher number. It had no number of its own, and its reasoning was structural in a way that repays attention, because the same structure recurs wherever courts review a statutory valuation. The Act's principles directed the valuer to proceed asset class by asset class and liability by liability, applying prescribed rules to each entry: what the undertaking's investments should count for, how its premises and other assets should be brought into account, what deductions should be made. Three vices, in the majority's analysis, ran through the scheme. The first was omission of the subject. A bank's undertaking is worth more than the sum of its ledger entries because it is a functioning system for taking deposits and making loans, with a franchise, a reputation and a network whose earning power is the real asset; a method that prices the entries and never the system is not a method of valuing the property taken. The second vice was distortion within the entries themselves: prescribed rules for particular assets bore no necessary relation to what those assets would fetch or what they contributed to the business, so that fidelity to the rules could produce, and in the Court's estimation did produce, figures disconnected from value at the date of taking. The third vice was the medium of payment. Value, even if correctly computed at the date of acquisition, is not delivered if the owner is handed securities maturing across decades; the time structure of the payment is part of the amount, and a scheme that ignores it pays less than it computes.

From those vices the legal conclusion followed by the route Vajravelu had marked out. Article 31(2) did not require the Court to approve the principles as wise; the Fourth Amendment forbade the adequacy inquiry. It required the principles to be principles for determining compensation, that is, relevant to the value of the property being acquired. Principles that omit the subject's defining value, distort the components and discount the payment through deferral are not rendered relevant by being enacted; they fail the threshold test of what the clause demands, and a law built on them impairs the guarantee. That is why the holding could coexist with the Court's disavowal of any intention to fix the price of banks, and why the re-enactment's answer was so simple: a legislature that prices the property itself, in figures stated in the statute, leaves a court nothing to characterise as irrelevant except the figures, and the figures were the one thing the Constitution, after 1955, would not let the court weigh.

## The compensation test at work: what relevance review demanded

The holding's practical importance lies in the discipline it imposed on drafters of acquisition statutes, stated here as three working rules. The first is that every principle a statute prescribes must be capable of bearing on the property's value at the date of taking; a principle directing the valuer to a consideration unconnected with that value does not become valid by being enacted alongside sound ones, because the court examines the scheme as a whole, asking whether its arithmetic, honestly performed, answers the constitutional description. The second is that the subject of the valuation is the property actually taken: where the state acquires a going concern, principles confined to the concern's separable assets omit the element, its earning organisation and connection, in which much of the value resides, and the omission is a failure of relevance, not an exercise of legislative choice. The third is that the product is judged as delivered and not as computed: an amount payable over time in securities is worth what the securities are worth to the owner receiving them, and a court applying the test treats deferral and the medium of payment as part of the amount, not as machinery beneath its notice.

Those working rules explain the pattern of the sequel. A legislature that fixes its amounts in the statute has performed the valuation itself and left the relevance inquiry almost nothing to attach to, which is why the re-enactment of 1970 took that form and why the form recommended itself generally. And when the Twenty-fifth Amendment removed compensation from the text, the Kesavananda bench preserved the test's residue, relevance and non-illusoriness, because review pitched at a scheme's threshold character could survive where review of its generosity could not.

### Did the Supreme Court hold that bank nationalisation itself was unconstitutional?

It did not. The Court expressly left Parliament's competence and the public purpose untouched, and struck the Act down on two drafting-dependent grounds: the discriminatory bar on the named banks carrying on banking business, and compensation principles that produced an illusory amount. Both defects were curable, and were cured on re-enactment.

## The holding that outlived the case: the effect test

Everything so far could have been decided within the old architecture, and the compensation ground in fact was. The transformation lies in the reasoning by which the majority connected the articles, and it must be stated precisely. The Union's structural argument was the Gopalan argument: that Articles 19 and 31 occupy different fields, that a law for compulsory acquisition must be tested under Article 31 alone, and that the petitioner's attempt to add Article 19(1)(f) and 19(1)(g) to the inquiry was an attempt to test one law against two codes. Shah J.'s answer rejected the premise. The Constitution's fundamental rights, the majority held, are not a set of sealed compartments each governing a separate subject. A person's rights under Article 19 and his rights under Article 31 may both be invaded by one and the same law, and the validity of the law must then be tested against each. The measure of a statute is not its object, its form, or the entry under which it was enacted; the measure is the direct operation and effect of the statute on the rights of the individual before the court. Where the direct and inevitable effect of a law is to abridge a protected freedom or to take property, the law must satisfy the conditions of every article it engages, and it cannot shelter behind the article it was drafted to fit.

Three consequences of that formulation explain its reach. First, it changed pleading. A petitioner was no longer required to elect a compartment and accept its limits; he was entitled to map the statute's actual operation and to complain of every right that operation infringed, which is how Cooper himself converted a corporate acquisition into a shareholder's case under Articles 14, 19 and 31 at once. Second, it changed the state's burden. Classification by pith and substance, the doctrine by which a law's true character is located for the purpose of legislative competence, was disentangled from rights review: a law may be in pith and substance an acquisition law for the Seventh Schedule and yet be an unreasonable restriction on a freedom for Part III, because the two inquiries ask different questions. Third, it supplied the conceptual foundation on which the next generation of doctrine was built. Once rights interlock and effect governs, a law depriving a person of liberty can be tested for fairness and reasonableness in substance, a law touching the home or the body can be tested against privacy, and the freedoms can be read as a connected code of the conditions of a free citizen. That is precisely the use Maneka Gandhi made of the approach eight years later, and the use the nine judges in Puttaswamy made of Maneka Gandhi's line a generation after that.

| Question | The position under Gopalan | The position after Cooper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How is a law characterised for rights review? | By its object and subject matter, and the legislative entry it belongs under | By its direct operation and effect on the rights of the person affected |
| Against which rights is a law tested? | Against the single article governing its compartment | Against every article of Part III its operation engages |
| What governs, object or effect? | The object of the law determines which guarantee applies | The effect of the law determines which guarantees must be satisfied |
| What must a petitioner plead? | That the law falls within the article he invokes and offends it | That the law, in its actual working, infringes his own rights under any article |
| What did the position make possible or impossible? | Detention and acquisition laws insulated from Article 19 review | Maneka Gandhi's widening of Article 21, and in time the privacy holding in Puttaswamy |

The table compresses a shift that the profession absorbed over the following decade rather than on the morning of the judgment. Gopalan was not ceremonially buried in Cooper; its specific holding on preventive detention had to wait longer for full reconsideration. What Cooper killed beyond recall was the mutual-exclusivity doctrine, the proposition that the articles are mutually exclusive fields into which a statute must be assigned once and for all. After Cooper, no court could again refuse a rights argument on the ground that the petitioner had knocked at the wrong article's door. The unit of constitutional analysis became the effect on the person, and it has remained the unit since.

## The freedoms limb: what Articles 19(1)(f) and 19(1)(g) added to the case

The majority's treatment of the two freedoms is the part of the judgment where the effect test can be watched doing mechanical work, and it is worth slowing down for it. Under the Gopalan settlement, the petitioner's Article 19 claims were vulnerable to a short answer: the Act was a law of acquisition, acquisition is the subject of Article 31, and Article 19 has nothing to say to a taking, because the right to hold property under Article 19(1)(f) is a right against restrictions short of deprivation and the right to carry on a business under Article 19(1)(g) is a right against regulation, not against the state's acquisition of the business itself. Shah J.'s judgment refused that short answer by changing the unit of analysis from the law to the claimant. The question was not whether an acquisition law, in the abstract, is examinable under Article 19. The question was whether this Act, in its operation on this petitioner, invaded rights Article 19 guarantees to him. His shares were property he held within Article 19(1)(f), and the Act rendered the holding worthless in substance while leaving the paper in his hands. The business of banking, in which he was engaged as a director and through which his investment operated, was closed to the companies by statutory prohibition, and that prohibition answered to Article 19(1)(g) unless the Constitution saved it.

The Union had a textual answer to the trade limb that deserves record, because it shows the judgment was not won against a straw man. The second proviso to Article 19(6) permits the state to make a law relating to the carrying on by the state of any trade or business, whether to the exclusion, complete or partial, of citizens or otherwise; a state monopoly, in other words, is not an unreasonable restriction merely because it excludes citizens. Nationalisation is the textbook case the proviso was written for. The majority's route round it is instructive. The proviso protects a monopoly law against the charge that exclusion from the trade is itself unreasonable. It does not protect the law against the other articles: a monopoly created by expropriation must still pay what Article 31(2), as it stood, required, and a monopoly created by disabling named citizens while their foreign and domestic competitors trade on must still answer to Article 14. The freedoms limb therefore mattered less as an independent striking ground than as the proof of the method. Once the Act had to be measured against the shareholder's rights under Articles 14, 19 and 31 together, the compartments were finished, because the state could no longer choose the single article under which its law would be judged by choosing the form of the law. That delinking of Article 19(1)(f) from Article 31(2), and of the rights chapter from the classification of statutes, is the holding every subsequent multi-right challenge in the Indian reports presupposes.

## Severability: why the whole Act fell, and not a clause of it

A reader who accepts both holdings may still ask why the consequence was the death of the statute rather than the excision of its offending parts, and the answer lies in the law of severability as the Court had stated it in R.M.D. Chamarbaugwalla v Union of India in 1957. The inquiry there prescribed is whether the valid and invalid parts of a statute are so inextricably mixed that the legislature would not have enacted the valid without the invalid, and whether what remains after excision is an independent and workable law or a torso the legislature never designed. The 1969 Act failed that inquiry at its centre. Its scheme was a single transaction: take the undertaking, forbid the business, pay by the prescribed computation. Strike the compensation provisions, and the acquisition stands as a taking without the price the Constitution requires; a court cannot write a price into a statute, and reading one in would be legislation, not construction. Strike the prohibition, and the transfer provisions are arguably workable, but the statute that remains is not the statute Parliament passed, because the permanent retirement of the fourteen companies from banking was not an incidental feature of the design but one of its objects. The Court therefore declared Sections 4, 5 and 6 read with the Second Schedule void as the operative core, and the whole Act void with them.

The consequences of total invalidity were more theatrical in statement than in fact. The undertakings had been administered by the new banks for seven months, deposits had been accepted and loans made in the ordinary course, and none of that activity was unravelled: the judgment established that the acquiring statute was void, and the practical and legislative response treated continuity as the overriding necessity, with the re-enacted Act deemed to operate from 19 July 1969 so that the interval was covered retrospectively. The episode is a standing lesson in the difference between the constitutional and the administrative planes. A declaration of invalidity speaks to the legal authority under which power was exercised; it does not rewind the banking system. Legislatures that re-enact promptly and retrospectively can confine even a total defeat to the plane of authority, which is what the Parliament of 1970 did, and the profession's memory of Cooper as a thunderbolt owes more to the size of the bench and the doctrine than to any disruption in the banking system, which experienced none.

## The dissent of Ray J.

Ray J. would have dismissed the petitions and upheld the Act in full, and his dissent is the necessary counterweight to any account that treats the majority's conclusions as self-evident. On the doctrinal question he stood with Gopalan. The rights in Part III, in his view, still addressed distinct subject matters, and the interrelation the majority asserted between Article 19 and Article 31 was not one the text compelled. On compensation, his position followed from a stricter reading of the Fourth Amendment: the fixation of compensation, and the choice of principles by which it is computed, is a legislative function; the amendment had withdrawn adequacy from judicial review precisely to end litigation of this character; and a compensation scheme enacted by Parliament could be impeached only on the narrowest grounds, as where the power is exercised in fraud of the Constitution or for an extraneous purpose, not because the court considers the principles inapt. On the ordinance and the legislative process, he treated the satisfaction of the President and the advice of ministers as matters the courts could not go behind, and he found no violation of Articles 14, 19(1)(f) or 31 in the Act at all.

The dissent matters for three reasons quite apart from its author's later prominence. It identifies the genuine constitutional difficulty in the majority's position: the compensation holding required the Court to say that a scheme was not compensation at all, a judgment that sits close to the adequacy judgment the Fourth Amendment forbade, and the line between reviewing relevance and reviewing adequacy is one judges must draw case by case without a rule to draw it with. It represents, secondly, the strongest form of the democratic objection to the whole enterprise: a Parliament elected on a programme of social control of credit had its central measure annulled on grounds that turned on judicial assessment of valuation method, and a constitution that permits that result must defend it in terms of rights, not merely of technique. And it shows, thirdly, that the effect test was a choice, not a discovery. Ten judges preferred the integrated reading and one judge, reasoning from the same text and the same precedents, preferred the compartments. Constitutional doctrine at this level is made by such choices, and the candour of the dissent about the alternative is part of why the majority's choice has worn well.

## The government's case, taken seriously

The stance this article must take toward the judgment is fixed by the record: the Court was right about the statute before it, and the statute before it was a flawed instrument. But the government's losing arguments deserve their full strength, because the usual telling casts the Union as a careless draftsman when it was in fact making a serious claim about the allocation of constitutional authority. The claim was this. The Directive Principles commit the state to securing that the ownership and control of the material resources of the community are so distributed as best to subserve the common good, and to preventing the concentration of wealth. Banking was the commanding height of credit allocation in an economy where a small circle of industrial houses had historically been connected, through interlocking directorships, with the largest banks and the credit decisions they made. Social control of banking had been official policy through the nineteen sixties, first by regulation and then, when regulation was judged insufficient, by ownership. An elected Parliament that takes the fourteen largest banks into public ownership for those purposes is not engaged in a frolic; it is executing the economic programme on which it sought and held office. Against that programme, the Constitution set one relevant condition: pay compensation in accordance with Article 31(2) as it stood. The government's case was that it had satisfied that condition by enacting principles, that the Fourth Amendment had settled that the product of those principles was not examinable for adequacy, and that the Court's contrary conclusion substituted judicial valuation policy for legislative valuation policy while disclaiming any such substitution.

That case failed, and it failed on facts the government itself had drafted. The discrimination point did not require any theory of compensation at all: the Act had singled out fourteen named companies for exclusion from an occupation it left open to their foreign and domestic competitors, and no purpose of social control required that exclusion. The compensation point, though closer, turned on the same feature: the government had chosen not to fix amounts but to prescribe a computation, and the computation it prescribed did not attempt to value the undertakings as undertakings. A legislature that wants judicial deference for its valuation scheme is in the strongest position when the scheme visibly aims at the value of what is taken. The 1969 scheme, in the majority's analysis, aimed at book entries. The judgment is therefore best read not as a court obstructing socialism but as a court enforcing the two conditions the constitutional text actually imposed, equality of treatment and compensation that is compensation, while expressly disclaiming any view on the policy those conditions constrained. Whether a constitution should impose even those conditions on redistributive legislation is a genuine question, and Parliament gave its answer to it in the years that followed, by changing the text.

## The sequel: re-enactment in weeks, amendment in two years

The political consequence arrived first and fastest. The nationalised banks had been in public hands since July 1969 and the administration of the banking system could not be unwound on the strength of a judgment about the acquiring statute's validity. Parliament therefore re-enacted the acquisition in corrected form: the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970, Act 5 of 1970, which re-transferred the undertakings of the same fourteen banks to the corresponding new banks, deemed to operate from 19 July 1969 so that the continuity of public ownership was unbroken. The defects identified by the Court were addressed in the drafting. The prohibition structure that had carried the discrimination was recast, and the compensation scheme was abandoned in favour of certainty: Section 6 of the new Act gave each existing bank the compensation specified against its name in the Second Schedule, payable at the bank's option in cash by three equal annual instalments with interest, or in government securities of prescribed maturities and rates, or in a mixture. The amounts were higher than the 1969 computation would have produced, though the profession has never treated them as generous. The re-enacted Act was not struck down, and the nationalisation it effected stood. The interval during which the largest banks in India were, in strict law, held under a void statute is one of the quiet marvels of the period, and it passed without a run on a single bank, which says something about the difference between constitutional validity and administrative fact.

### What happened to the nationalised banks after the judgment?

They stayed nationalised. Parliament re-enacted the acquisition through the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970, deemed to operate from 19 July 1969, correcting the defects Cooper had identified and fixing compensation amounts in the statute. Public ownership of the fourteen banks was never interrupted in practice.

The constitutional consequence took longer and cut deeper, and it is here that the amendment sequel belongs, with the doctrine that eventually contained it treated in [the account of the basic structure doctrine](/2010/02/01/basic-structure-doctrine-explained/). Parliament's considered response to the compensation holding was the Constitution (Twenty-fifth Amendment) Act, 1971. It did two things. It amended Article 31(2) by substituting the word amount for the word compensation, and by providing that no law fixing an amount or specifying principles could be called in question on the ground that the amount was not adequate or that the whole or part of it was to be given otherwise than in cash. It also inserted Article 31C, which provided that a law giving effect to the policy of the state towards securing the principles in Article 39(b) and 39(c), the distribution of material resources and the prevention of concentration of wealth, could not be deemed void for inconsistency with Articles 14, 19 or 31, and it attempted to protect such laws further by providing that a declaration in the law that it was for giving effect to that policy could not be called in question in any court. The design is unmistakable: the judgment's compensation route was to be closed as a route, for the whole class of redistributive legislation and not merely for banking.

The amendment came before the Court in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973, and its treatment there must be stated with precision, because summaries routinely overstate it in both directions. The thirteen judges upheld the substitution of amount for compensation: Parliament could amend Article 31(2) so that adequacy ceased to be the measure, though the amount fixed still could not be illusory or arbitrary in the sense of bearing no relation to the property taken, and the principles specified for fixing it still had to be relevant to that end. The Court struck down, as beyond the amending power, the latter part of Article 31C: the clause that would have barred the courts from examining whether a law declared to implement Article 39(b) and (c) did in fact do so. A legislature could protect its redistributive laws from the rights articles within the amendment's terms, but it could not make its own declaration conclusive and thereby transfer to itself the judicial function of characterising its laws. Cooper's compensation holding, as a constraint on the amount, thus survived Kesavananda only in the attenuated form of a relevance and non-illusoriness review. The effect test, by contrast, was not challenged by the Twenty-fifth Amendment, was not in issue in Kesavananda, and passed into the succeeding decade intact. Parliament had answered the part of Cooper it could answer by amendment, the part about money. The part about method remained, because amending it away would have meant rewriting the rights chapter, and the basic structure judgment delivered the same week had placed limits even on that.

## The re-enacted Act in practice

The terms on which the 1970 Act settled the compensation question repay a closer look, because they show how a legislature answers a relevance judgment without conceding a valuation. The re-enactment abandoned principles altogether and fixed the price itself: the Second Schedule named a sum in lakhs of rupees against each existing bank, the largest sums attaching to the Central Bank of India, the Bank of India and the Punjab National Bank. Section 6 then gave each bank its choice of the medium: cash by cheque drawn on the Reserve Bank, in three equal annual instalments carrying interest at four per cent; or government promissory notes or stock certificates repayable at par, maturing at ten years with interest at four and a half per cent or at thirty years with interest at five and a half per cent; or a mixture of cash and securities. The structure answered the judgment point by point. A sum fixed in the statute cannot be attacked as the product of irrelevant principles, because the legislature has itself performed the valuation the Constitution entrusts to it, and after the Fourth Amendment the product was not examinable for adequacy. The option of cash instalments met the complaint that the owner was confined to distant paper.

The former companies lived on in their reduced form, holding their untaken assets and pursuing such non-banking business as the statute allowed, and the system the re-enactment secured became the durable structure of Indian banking: fourteen nationalised banks alongside the State Bank group, dominating deposits and directed credit for a generation. A second nationalisation followed in 1980, when six further banks were taken over by the corresponding Act of that year, on the legal foundation the 1970 settlement had built, and without any repetition of the Cooper litigation's success. Whatever is to be said about the economics of that settlement, and this article takes no position on the economics, its constitutional durability is a fact of the record: the acquisition the Supreme Court annulled in February 1970 was, in substance, the acquisition under which Indian banking operated for the following half century. The judgment's own analysis predicts that outcome. A court that strikes a statute for defects of drafting while affirming the competence and the purpose behind it should expect the statute back, corrected; and a doctrine of rights that survives the correction is worth more, as doctrine, than a veto on policy would have been.

## The supersession of April 1973

The institutional consequence of the period belongs in this article because the Cooper bench stood at its centre, and it must be stated with the restraint the subject requires: the facts are documented, and the reader is entitled to draw conclusions from them without the article drawing them first. On 24 April 1973, the thirteen judges in Kesavananda Bharati delivered their judgment, holding by seven to six that Parliament's amending power did not extend to damaging the basic structure of the Constitution. The judges of the Cooper majority were heavily represented on both benches, and the three judges next in seniority for the office of Chief Justice of India, J.M. Shelat, K.S. Hegde and A.N. Grover, had each been in the majority in Cooper and in Kesavananda Bharati. Chief Justice Sikri retired on 25 April 1973. By the convention observed since the Court's inception, the senior-most judge, Shelat J., would have succeeded him. On 26 April 1973, A.N. Ray, who had dissented alone in Cooper, was appointed Chief Justice of India in supersession of Shelat, Hegde and Grover. The three superseded judges resigned their offices. The government defended the departure in Parliament through the Law Minister, H.R. Gokhale, on the ground that seniority alone was not the governing criterion for the appointment, pointing to the Law Commission's earlier recommendation that the Chief Justice should be chosen for experience, ability and administrative competence and not by seniority only. The Bar protested formally, observing a day of abstention, and the appointment was challenged and upheld in litigation the following year.

Nothing in that sequence requires embellishment, and the article adds none. Two facts only need fixing in the reader's mind. The first is that the convention of seniority, though nowhere written in the Constitution, had been treated by the Court and the executive alike as settled practice, and its breach was understood at the time by participants on every side as a departure of the first order rather than a routine appointment. The second is that the episode recurred in altered form in 1977, when H.R. Khanna, the dissenting judge in the habeas corpus case of the Emergency, was superseded in favour of M.H. Beg, after which the seniority convention was restored and has since been treated as part of the constitutional morality surrounding the office. The supersession of 1973 is the point at which the doctrinal story of Cooper and the institutional story of the Indian judiciary intersect: the judgment that a dissenting judge had opposed became, through his elevation, part of the prehistory of the struggle over who appoints the judges, a struggle the Emergency cluster of this series meets again in the transfer and confirmation controversies of the following years.

## The criticism of the judgment, and the Court's answer

The reaction to the judgment in February 1970 supplies a documented footnote that belongs in any full account of the case, because it fixed an early boundary of permissible criticism of the Court. Within days of the decision, a public meeting in Delhi organised by a political forum heard ministers and members of Parliament criticise the judgment in strong terms, including the suggestion that such decisions would encourage those who had rejected constitutional methods of achieving socialism. The criticism came before the Court in contempt proceedings, and Chief Justice Hidayatullah, delivering the Court's judgment in that matter, drew the line that has governed since: fair and temperate criticism of a court, even if strong, is not contempt, but attributing improper motives to judges, or vilifying the institution so as to obstruct its functioning, is a serious contempt of which notice will be taken. The episode matters to this article for a structural reason. It shows the judgment's opponents fighting the decision on the plane of public persuasion even while the government prepared its legislative answer, and it shows the Court distinguishing, at the very moment of its greatest political exposure, between criticism of its reasoning, which it expressly protected, and attacks on its motives, which it did not. The distinction is recognisably the same discipline the majority opinion had practised: engage the operation and the effect, not the imputed purpose.

## The lineage: from Cooper to Maneka Gandhi to Puttaswamy

The doctrinal line that descends from Cooper is the reason the case is taught, and it should be drawn with the links in their order. The first link is Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided in 1978. Maneka Gandhi's passport had been impounded under the Passports Act without a hearing, and the case required the Court to construe Articles 14, 19 and 21 together in a way Gopalan's compartments had been designed to prevent. The seven judges who decided it proceeded on Cooper's foundation as a matter of course: the rights are not mutually exclusive, a law depriving a person of personal liberty must also stand the test of the freedoms and of equality, and the procedure Article 21 requires must be right, just and fair, not arbitrary or oppressive. The transformation of Article 21 into the source of a substantive fairness review, from which the rights to livelihood, to a clean environment, to education and in time to privacy were derived, is Maneka Gandhi's work; but the analytical permission for it, the refusal to let classification by subject decide the reach of review, is Cooper's. Between the two cases stands the demolition of the property right itself, completed by [the Forty-fourth Amendment and the deletion of the fundamental right to property](/2010/03/01/44th-amendment-act-1978-explained/), which in 1978 removed Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 from Part III and left property to the protection of Article 300A, a constitutional but no longer fundamental right. It is one of the ironies the series keeps meeting that the article of the Constitution on which Cooper's facts turned ceased to exist as a fundamental right within eight years of the judgment, while the method the judgment created went on to its largest applications in fields the property debate never touched.

The second link is the privacy line. When a nine-judge bench in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India held in 2017 that privacy is protected as an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty and as part of the freedoms guaranteed by Part III, the Court's reasoning ran through the integrated structure: the plurality traced the development of the rights jurisprudence from the compartment reading through its abandonment, and [the privacy and Aadhaar judgment's place in that doctrinal lineage](/2019/05/15/puttaswamy-privacy-aadhaar-judgment/) is as heir to Cooper's method as it is to the specific Article 21 cases. Decisions of the same generation on decriminalisation, on adultery and on the rights of sexual minorities plead and reason in the same grammar: identify the direct effect of the law on the person, test it against every guarantee engaged, and refuse the answer that the statute belongs to a compartment where the claimed right does not live. The point is not that Cooper decided those cases; it decided none of them. The point is that each of them would have had to begin by displacing Gopalan if Cooper had not already displaced it, and constitutional litigation in India would look materially different, narrower at every joint, if the banking case had gone the other way.

A live academic argument should be recorded rather than resolved by assertion. Constitutional scholars, among them Gautam Bhatia in his work on the transformative Constitution, have argued that the effect test has been applied unevenly: that courts invoke the integrated reading eagerly in some classes of case and relapse into classification and deference in others, notably where economic legislation and national security are concerned, and that the promise of Cooper is therefore honoured as rhetoric more consistently than as method. The counter-argument, made by defenders of the Court's economic jurisprudence, is that the effect test was never a promise of equal scrutiny for every statute: it governs which guarantees a law must answer to, not the intensity with which each guarantee is enforced, and deference on the merits of economic policy is compatible with rigour about the rights engaged. Both arguments are made in good faith by people who accept the holding, and the dispute is about its application rather than its authority. What is not in dispute, in the literature or in the reports, is the holding's place in the line of authority. When an Indian court is asked to confine a rights claim to a single article, the answer it gives is the answer Shah J. gave in 1970.

## Bennett Coleman: the first working of the machine

Doctrines are best tested by their next case, and Cooper's method was put to work within two years in a field far from banking. In Bennett Coleman and Co. v Union of India, decided in 1972, the newsprint control policy of the government was challenged by a newspaper company together with its shareholders and editors. The state raised the familiar threshold objection: the company is not a citizen, the policy addresses the company, and the individuals before the court complain of injuries that are the company's to complain of. The Court, applying the reasoning Cooper had established, looked through the form to the operation of the measure: the newsprint restrictions bore directly on the shareholders' and editors' own freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a), and the petition was maintainable and the policy examinable against that freedom. The parallel is exact and deliberate. In Cooper, a law addressed to companies was tested against the shareholder's rights because its effect fell on them; in Bennett Coleman, a policy addressed to a company was tested against the citizen's speech because its effect fell there. The standing holding and the effect holding, born in a property case, migrated at once into the heartland of civil liberty, and the migration demonstrates the article's central claim about the judgment better than any commentary. A doctrine framed for the expropriation of banks turned out to be a general theory of how state action meets the rights chapter, and it travelled because Shah J. had stated it at the level of method rather than at the level of the banking facts.

## The questions Cooper left open

A judgment of this size settles its questions and manufactures the next generation's, and candour about the residue is part of reading it correctly. Three questions were left structurally open, and the reports have been answering them piecemeal since. The first is intensity: the effect test decides which guarantees a law must answer to, but it does not itself say how searchingly each guarantee is to be enforced, and the space between gateway and intensity is where the deference doctrine of R.K. Garg later settled, without any formal reconciliation of the two cases ever being attempted by a bench of comparable size. The second is the corporate boundary of the standing holding: the citizen shareholder may complain where his own rights are directly hit, but the company itself remains a non-citizen outside Article 19, and the line between the shareholder's direct injury and his merely reflective loss continues to be litigated in company and tax cases where the Cooper route is invoked by petitioners whose real complaint is the corporation's. The third is the compensation residue under Article 300A: Kesavananda Bharati preserved a relevance and non-illusoriness review of the amount in the amended Article 31(2), and the Article 300A cases have since reproduced that review in the new text, but the outer limits of the doctrine, how far a court may go in examining a legislature's chosen principles under a provision that mentions no compensation at all, remain a matter of case-by-case development rather than settled rule. None of these open questions diminishes the holding. They are the ordinary condition of a foundational case, whose ratio is fixed and whose penumbra is the profession's continuing work.

## Property after the property right: Article 300A and the afterlife of the compensation idea

### Is the right to property still a fundamental right after Cooper?

No. The Forty-fourth Amendment of 1978 deleted Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 from Part III and inserted Article 300A, which provides only that no person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law. Property remains a constitutional right, but it is no longer a fundamental right, and Article 32 does not lie for its enforcement.

The afterlife of the compensation idea under Article 300A is the least expected part of the Cooper story, because the idea refused to stay buried. Article 300A says nothing about compensation at all; it requires only authority of law. In Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar v State of Gujarat, decided in 1995, the Court confirmed that the right under Article 300A is a constitutional right but not a fundamental right, and not part of the basic structure in the manner of the deleted articles. Yet when the Karnataka land acquisition legislation reached the Court in K.T. Plantation v State of Karnataka in 2011, the bench read the bare text of Article 300A with the discipline the older cases had built: a law depriving a person of property must serve a public purpose, the deprivation must be just, fair and reasonable in the Maneka Gandhi sense, and where compensation is provided its principles cannot be irrelevant or the amount illusory without the law failing the article. The vocabulary is the vocabulary of Vajravelu and Cooper, transplanted into a provision whose drafters in 1978 may be supposed to have intended a thinner protection. Courts have since repeated the pattern in acquisition cases under the modern land statutes: the compensation inquiry, expelled from the fundamental rights chapter, re-entered through the legality requirement of the surviving article, in the relevance and non-illusoriness form that Cooper had refined. The amendment battle of 1970 and 1971 was therefore won by Parliament on the constitutional text and lost, in part, on the judicial method, because the method had learned to locate the same discipline in whatever text remained available. That is a general lesson about doctrinal change that the banking case teaches better than any other in the series: holdings about how to read provisions migrate across provisions, and a legislature that amends the text without displacing the method will meet the method again in the next text.

## The counter-tradition: deference to economic legislation

An honest account of Cooper's legacy must also record the doctrine that grew up alongside the effect test and limits its practical operation in the economic field, because the two coexist in the reports and a pleader who knows only one of them misjudges every case. In R.K. Garg v Union of India, decided in 1981 on a challenge to the Bearer Bonds legislation, Bhagwati J. stated the counter-position in its canonical form: laws relating to economic activities should be viewed with greater latitude than laws touching civil rights, the legislature must be allowed freedom of experiment in complex economic matters, and a court will not invalidate an economic measure because it considers the measure theoretically unsound or imperfectly designed. The BALCO disinvestment case of 2001 applied the same restraint to the opposite policy direction, holding that the merits of a decision to disinvest a public undertaking are for the executive and not for judicial second-guessing. Between Cooper and R.K. Garg the working law of economic legislation is defined by their conjunction. Cooper governs the gateway: which rights a measure must answer to, whose rights they are, and whether a discriminating or expropriating feature must justify itself. R.K. Garg governs the intensity of review once the gateway is passed: within the rights engaged, economic policy choices receive latitude that civil liberties choices do not. The 1969 Act failed at the gateway, on defects that no doctrine of deference could have saved, because discrimination against named companies and compensation by irrelevant principles are not policy choices of the kind latitude protects. A measure that survives the gateway, as the 1970 Act did, is largely proof against the merits review that unsuccessful petitioners keep inviting courts to undertake.

## Three misreadings of Cooper that the record does not support

The case attracts confident error, and three errors recur often enough in commentary and in argument to be worth correcting in one place. The first is that the Court held bank nationalisation unconstitutional. It held a particular Act unconstitutional for stated defects, affirmed the competence to nationalise, and saw its judgment complied with by re-enactment within weeks; a holding that cannot survive a competent redraft is not a veto on the policy. The second is that Cooper restored the full compensation jurisprudence of Bela Banerjee, under which courts assessed whether the owner received a just equivalent. It did not: the Fourth Amendment's bar on adequacy review stood throughout, the majority worked deliberately within the Vajravelu margin of relevance, and the Twenty-fifth Amendment then removed even that margin as a fundamental rights matter, leaving the attenuated review that Kesavananda Bharati described. An argument that cites Cooper for adequacy review misstates both the case and the century. The third is that Cooper overruled Gopalan root and branch. It displaced the mutual-exclusivity doctrine and the object test for rights review, and those were the parts that mattered for the future. But Gopalan was a preventive detention case, and the strand of its reasoning that concerned Articles 21 and 22 was eroded by stages, through Maneka Gandhi's reconstruction of Article 21 and later decisions, rather than in a single overruling in 1970. Precision on this point matters for more than pedantry: the series' civil liberties cluster shows the detention jurisprudence evolving across decades, and a reader who believes it was all settled in the banking case will misread everything that follows, including why the Emergency habeas corpus case could be argued as it was only six years later.

The commentators divided along lines the judgment invites. H.M. Seervai, in his Constitutional Law of India, defended the majority's reasoning and treated the Twenty-fifth Amendment's attempt to place redistributive legislation beyond the rights articles as the greater constitutional danger, a position consistent with his broader defence of judicial review in the period. Granville Austin's narrative history of the working of the Constitution placed Cooper in the sequence of confrontations between a Parliament committed to its social programme and a Court committed to the compensation text, and treated the episode as one of the defining institutional collisions of the republic's second decade. Those readings frame the case as a victory and a collision respectively, and both are defensible. What neither reading does, and what this article has argued the record forbids, is to treat the case as chiefly a victory about money. The money was settled by amendment and re-enactment. The method was settled by the judgment, and it is the method that every subsequent generation of Indian rights litigation has inherited.

## Not the privy purses case

Cooper is persistently confused in public discussion with the privy purses decision, and the two should be separated cleanly because the conflation scrambles the doctrines of both. The privy purses case, Madhav Rao Scindia v Union of India, decided in December 1970, concerned the President's orders derecognising the former rulers of the princely states and thereby terminating their privy purses, after a constitutional amendment bill to abolish the purses had failed in the Rajya Sabha. The Court, by a large majority, struck the orders down, holding that the power to recognise a ruler under Article 366(22) did not contain a power to derecognise en masse as a device for achieving what the failed amendment had attempted. The case is about the limits of executive power under a definition clause and about the politics of the princely settlement. Cooper is about the fundamental rights of shareholders, the equality article, and the structure of Part III. They share a season, a government on the losing side, and a place in the sequence of confrontations that led to the Twenty-fourth and Twenty-fifth Amendments and then to Kesavananda Bharati. They share nothing else, and an argument about the effect test that cites the privy purses case, or an argument about derecognition that cites Cooper, has mistaken its authority.

## The practical consequence for anyone pleading rights

For the advocate, Cooper is not history but equipment. Its holdings supply the opening moves of a modern fundamental rights challenge: plead the client, not the abstraction, and locate the client's own right that the law touches; map the law's direct operation across the whole of Part III rather than accepting the compartment the state proposes; where property or an economic interest is taken, plead equality alongside the taking, because the discrimination limb may be the limb the state cannot answer; and where a scheme of payment is attacked, plead the relevance of its principles to value rather than the adequacy of its product, a discipline that survives the Twenty-fifth Amendment in attenuated form and still decides cases under Article 300A. Teachers who set the case for a class will find that its structure survives being laid out visually, and a reader assembling the Gopalan and Cooper positions side by side for revision can [open the slide deck in the ReportMedic PPT viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) to work through the comparison table at lecture pace. The general account in this article is information about a decided case and its progeny, and not advice on any live matter, for which the record of the particular statute and the law reports must be consulted directly.

## The craftsmanship of the opinion, and a worked application

Shah J.'s judgment has had an unusual fate for an opinion of its length: it is cited less for its sentences than for its moves. The reports and the commentaries have distilled it into a set of propositions that function in argument as a checklist. The fundamental rights are not mutually exclusive. The validity of a law is to be judged by its direct operation and effect on the rights of the individual, and not by its object or form. A shareholder may maintain a petition where the impugned action infringes his own rights, though he may not agitate the company's. A law that disables a named class from an occupation open to its competitors is hostile discrimination. Principles for determining compensation must be relevant to the value of the property acquired, and an amount that is illusory is not compensation. Each of those propositions can be lifted out of the banking facts without loss, which is why the case is cited in service law, in tax, in education and in speech cases whose facts share nothing with 1969. Opinions acquire that portability when their reasoning is pitched at the level of method, and the contrast with the compensation holding is the proof: the holding that was pitched at the level of the 1969 Schedule died with the Schedule, and the holdings pitched at the level of method are alive in every volume of the reports since.

A worked application shows the method in harness. Suppose a modern statute vests the undertaking of a class of named financial companies in a public corporation, fixes the price by a formula in a schedule, and bars the named companies from the business permanently while leaving the business open to others. A challenge built on Cooper is assembled in the order the judgment itself was assembled. The petitioner is chosen for his own rights, a citizen shareholder whose holding the statute empties, and the petition pleads his rights rather than the company's. The grounds are then mapped by effect, not by the statute's self-description: the equality ground attacks the permanent bar as hostile discrimination, because the class's defining trait cannot explain exclusion from an open occupation; the property ground attacks the formula by the relevance test, asking whether the formula's inputs are capable of valuing the undertaking as a going concern or merely of pricing its parts; the freedoms ground pleads the prohibition against the citizens whose occupation it closes. The state will answer with competence, with deference to economic legislation, with the modern compensation text, and each of those answers has its Cooper-era counter already in the reports. That a hypothetical of this kind can be pleaded at all, in that order and with those moves, fifty years after the property article was deleted, is the practical measure of what the eleven judges built.

## Verdict

The verdict this article defends is the brief's own claim, because the record sustains it: the most important banking case in Indian law is not about banking. Cooper's property holdings were answered by Parliament within two years and their constitutional foundation was removed within eight; its discrimination holding was a drafting instruction that the re-enactment obeyed within weeks. Its standing holding and its effect test, by contrast, have never been overruled, qualified at the root, or seriously threatened, and they form part of the working method of every Indian court that decides a rights case. A reader who knows Cooper only as the case that struck down bank nationalisation for illusory compensation knows the least durable third of it. The durable part is the sentence with which this article began: a law is to be judged by what it does to the rights of the person before the court, and not by the subject it purports to deal with. Fourteen banks supplied the occasion. The sentence supplied the legacy. In the architecture of this series, the case is the hinge between the property Constitution of the first two decades and the liberty Constitution of the decades that followed, and no account of either side of that hinge is accurate without it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in the Cooper case?

The Court declared the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969 unconstitutional and void, by ten votes to one, on 10 February 1970. It held that the Act discriminated against the fourteen named banks under Article 14 by forbidding them banking business while leaving competitors free, and that its compensation principles offended Article 31(2) because they were not relevant to the value of the undertakings taken. It also discarded the doctrine that fundamental rights occupy separate compartments and held that a law must be tested against every right its operation infringes.

### Q: Why was the compensation held to be illusory in Cooper?

The 1969 Act did not fix a price for the banks. It prescribed principles for computing an amount, and those principles proceeded by balance-sheet arithmetic on categories of assets and liabilities, ignoring the value of each undertaking as a going concern: its branches, deposit franchise, customer connections and goodwill. The sum so computed was then to be satisfied in government securities rather than paid at the taking. The majority held that principles of that character are not relevant to the value of the property acquired, so the amount produced was illusory, compensation in name without the substance Article 31(2) required.

### Q: How did Cooper overrule the Gopalan doctrine?

Gopalan had held that the fundamental rights occupy separate compartments, so that a law was tested only against the article governing its subject matter. Cooper rejected that mutual-exclusivity doctrine. Shah J. held that the rights in Part III are not mutually exclusive, that one law may invade several rights at once, and that its validity must be tested against each right it touches. The Court asked what the nationalisation law did to the shareholder's rights under Articles 14, 19 and 31 together, rather than confining the inquiry to Article 31 because the statute was an acquisition law. Gopalan's specific detention context survived longer; its compartment method did not.

### Q: What is the effect test in constitutional law?

The effect test is the method Cooper substituted for the object test. Under the object test, a court classified a law by its stated object and subject matter, and tested it only against the constitutional provision governing that subject. Under the effect test, the court examines the direct operation and effect of the law on the rights of the person before it, and tests the law against every fundamental right that operation infringes, whatever the law calls itself and whatever its object. A statute drafted as an acquisition law may therefore have to satisfy Articles 14 and 19 as well as the property article.

### Q: Can a shareholder challenge a nationalisation law affecting the company?

Yes, on the Cooper holding, where the law's operation infringes the shareholder's own fundamental rights and not merely the company's. A shareholder cannot borrow the company's rights, because a company is not a citizen and cannot claim the Article 19 freedoms. But the shares are the shareholder's property, and where a takeover law destroys the substance of what the shares represent, the shareholder may petition under Article 32 complaining of the infringement of his own rights under Articles 14, 19 and 31, and the court may measure that infringement by what the law does to the company.

### Q: What did Justice Ray say in his Cooper dissent?

Ray J., dissenting alone, would have upheld the Act in full. He maintained the Gopalan position that the fundamental rights address distinct subject matters, rejected the majority's interrelation of Articles 19 and 31, and found no violation of Articles 14, 19(1)(f) or 31. On compensation he held that the fixation of compensation and the choice of principles are legislative functions, that the Fourth Amendment had withdrawn adequacy from judicial review, and that a compensation scheme could be impeached only on the narrowest grounds, such as a fraudulent exercise of power. He also treated the President's satisfaction in promulgating the ordinance as a matter the courts could not go behind.

### Q: How did Parliament respond to the Cooper judgment?

Parliament responded on two levels. Within weeks it re-enacted the acquisition through the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1970, deemed to operate from 19 July 1969, correcting the discrimination defect and fixing compensation amounts for each bank in the Second Schedule. Within two years it amended the Constitution itself: the Twenty-fifth Amendment Act, 1971 substituted the word amount for compensation in Article 31(2), barred challenges to adequacy, and inserted Article 31C to protect laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) from Articles 14, 19 and 31. The compensation holding was thereby reversed by amendment; the effect test was untouched.

### Q: Why does Cooper still matter beyond banking?

Because its method survived everything that happened to its subject matter. The property right it applied was deleted from Part III by the Forty-fourth Amendment in 1978, and its compensation doctrine had already been reversed by the Twenty-fifth Amendment. What remains in daily use is the holding that fundamental rights interlock and that a law is judged by its effect on the person, which underlies Maneka Gandhi's transformation of Article 21 in 1978 and the integrated reasoning of the nine judges who recognised privacy in Puttaswamy in 2017. Cooper also remains the leading authority on when a shareholder may challenge state action against his company.

### Q: Which Act was struck down in the Cooper case?

The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969, Act 22 of 1969, which had nationalised fourteen major commercial banks. The Act had replaced an ordinance of 19 July 1969 and was deemed to operate from that date. The Court held its operative provisions, Sections 4, 5 and 6 read with the Second Schedule, void for impairing Article 31(2), held the Act hostile in its discrimination against the named banks under Article 14, and found the defective provisions inseverable, so the whole Act fell. Parliament replaced it with a re-enacted Act of 1970.

### Q: Who was Rustom Cavasjee Cooper and why did he bring the petition?

Rustom Cavasjee Cooper was a shareholder in several of the fourteen nationalised banks and a director of the Central Bank of India. When the 1969 Act transferred the banks' undertakings to new government corporations and forbade the old companies to carry on banking, the value and substance of his shareholding were directly affected. He petitioned the Supreme Court under Article 32 in his own name, contending that the Act infringed his rights under Articles 14, 19(1)(f), 19(1)(g) and 31. The Union objected that the rights, if any, were the companies' and not his; the Court's rejection of that objection is itself a leading holding on shareholder standing.

### Q: How many judges decided the Cooper case and by what majority?

Eleven judges heard the case, among the largest benches the Supreme Court had constituted, because the continuing authority of Gopalan and the construction of Article 31 were in issue. The Act was struck down by ten votes to one. Shah J. delivered the judgment of the majority for himself and nine colleagues: Sikri, Shelat, Bhargava, Mitter, Vaidialingam, Hegde, Grover, Jaganmohan Reddy and Dua JJ. Ray J. dissented alone and would have upheld the Act. The judgment was delivered on 10 February 1970 and is reported at (1970) 1 SCC 248.

### Q: What is the difference between the object test and the effect test?

The object test asks what a law is for and what subject it belongs to, and then tests it only against the constitutional provision for that subject. It is the natural companion of the compartment doctrine: an acquisition law answers to the property article alone, whatever else it does. The effect test asks what the law does in its direct operation to the rights of the person challenging it, and tests it against every guarantee that operation engages. The difference decides cases, because a statute that passes the provision it was drafted under may fail another provision its effects invade, as the 1969 Act did on equality.

### Q: What did Cooper decide about the ordinance that first nationalised the banks?

Very little, deliberately. The takeover had begun under the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969, promulgated on 19 July 1969 by the acting President, and the petitioner challenged that route as well as the Act. Because Parliament had replaced the ordinance with the Act, which was deemed to operate from the ordinance's date, the Court treated the challenge to the ordinance as raising no separate live question and decided the case on the Act. Ray J., in dissent, addressed the point more fully and held that the President's satisfaction under Article 123, exercised on ministerial advice, was not a matter courts could go behind.

### Q: What did the Cooper Court mean by hostile discrimination?

The phrase describes discrimination that singles out a class for a disability rather than merely selecting it for a burden rationally connected to the law's purpose. The selection of the fourteen largest banks for acquisition could be defended as classification by size. What could not be defended was the Act's further step of prohibiting the named companies from carrying on banking business at all, while every smaller Indian bank and every foreign bank in India remained free to conduct exactly that business. That exclusion of the named companies from an occupation open to all their competitors was the hostile discrimination that offended Article 14.

### Q: How did the Twenty-fifth Amendment answer the Cooper ruling on compensation?

The Constitution (Twenty-fifth Amendment) Act, 1971 replaced the word compensation in Article 31(2) with the word amount, and provided that no acquisition law could be called in question on the ground that the amount fixed was inadequate or was payable otherwise than in cash. It also inserted Article 31C protecting laws that give effect to Article 39(b) and (c) from challenge under Articles 14, 19 and 31. In Kesavananda Bharati in 1973 the Court upheld the substitution of amount, while striking down the part of Article 31C that would have made a legislative declaration of purpose unexaminable by the courts.

### Q: What happened to the senior judges of the Cooper bench in April 1973?

On 24 April 1973 the Kesavananda Bharati judgment held that Parliament may not amend the basic structure of the Constitution. Chief Justice Sikri retired on 25 April. By the settled seniority convention, Shelat J. should have succeeded him, followed in time by Hegde and Grover JJ., all three of whom had been in the majority in Cooper and in Kesavananda. Instead, on 26 April 1973, Ray J., the lone Cooper dissenter, was appointed Chief Justice in their supersession. The three superseded judges resigned. The government defended the appointment in Parliament on the ground that seniority alone should not govern the choice.

### Q: How did Maneka Gandhi build on Cooper to widen Article 21?

Maneka Gandhi v Union of India, decided in 1978, took from Cooper the premise that the fundamental rights are not mutually exclusive and that a law must be tested against every right its operation engages. On that premise the Court held that a law depriving a person of personal liberty under Article 21 must also satisfy Articles 14 and 19, and that the procedure it prescribes must be right, just and fair rather than arbitrary. Gopalan had insulated Article 21 from that cross-testing; Cooper removed the insulation for property and freedoms, and Maneka Gandhi carried the integrated method into life and liberty, from which the modern Article 21 jurisprudence descends.

### Q: Is the Cooper case the same as the privy purses case?

No. They are distinct decisions of the same period, frequently conflated. Cooper, decided on 10 February 1970, struck down the bank nationalisation Act of 1969 on equality and compensation grounds and laid down the effect test. The privy purses case, Madhav Rao Scindia v Union of India, decided in December 1970, struck down presidential orders derecognising the former princely rulers and terminating their privy purses, holding that the recognition power in Article 366(22) contained no power of mass derecognition. The cases share a government on the losing side and a place in the road to the Twenty-fifth Amendment; their doctrines do not overlap.

### Q: What had the courts held about compensation before Cooper, in Bela Banerjee and Vajravelu?

In State of West Bengal v Bela Banerjee in 1954, the Court held that compensation in Article 31(2) meant a just equivalent of the property taken. Parliament replied with the Fourth Amendment in 1955, barring any challenge on the ground that compensation was inadequate. In P. Vajravelu Mudaliar v Special Deputy Collector in 1965, the Court held that the amendment did not end review: the principles a law specified still had to be relevant to the value of the property, and an amount produced by irrelevant principles remained open to challenge as illusory. Cooper applied that Vajravelu margin; it did not restore full adequacy review.

### Q: Why is the Cooper reasoning cited in the Puttaswamy privacy decision?

The nine judges in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v Union of India, who recognised privacy as a fundamental right in 2017, built their reasoning on the integrated structure of Part III that Cooper established. Privacy was located across the rights, as an intrinsic part of Article 21 and of the freedoms, rather than inside a single compartment, and the plurality traced the doctrinal development from the compartment reading of the early cases through its abandonment. That mode of reasoning, testing a state measure by its effect on the whole constellation of a person's rights, is the method Shah J. laid down in the banking case in 1970.
