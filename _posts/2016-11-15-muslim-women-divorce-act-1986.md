Few statutes in India are described as confidently, and as inaccurately, as the Muslim Women Divorce Act 1986, formally the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986. The standard description, repeated in textbooks, editorials and examination notes, is that the Act confined a divorced Muslim woman's claim against her former husband to the iddat period and took away the maintenance remedy that the general law gives every other woman in the country. That description captures what the statute was widely understood to do in the political controversy of 1986. It does not capture what the statute says, and it has not captured the law since the Supreme Court construed the Act in 2001 and confirmed, in 2010 and again in 2024, that the general maintenance provision remains open to a divorced Muslim woman alongside it. This article is a pillar account of the Act itself: its seven sections read closely, the distinction at its centre between a provision and maintenance, the machinery of relatives and the Wakf Board that it sets behind the former husband, the option and transitional provisions that complete its scheme, and the three Supreme Court decisions that settled what the text means. The reader who finishes it should be able to state what a divorced Muslim woman is entitled to, against whom, in which forum, and under which of two coexisting statutes she may choose to proceed.

## What existed before the Muslim Women Divorce Act 1986

The general maintenance provision against which the 1986 Act was written sits in Chapter IX of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, in Sections 125 to 128. Section 125 empowers a Magistrate of the first class, on proof that a person with sufficient means has neglected or refused to maintain his wife who is unable to maintain herself, to order a monthly allowance at such rate as the Magistrate thinks fit. An Explanation to the section provides that "wife" includes a woman who has been divorced by, or has obtained a divorce from, her husband and has not remarried. The Explanation that brings a divorced woman within the word "wife" was itself a deliberate reform of comparatively recent vintage when the 1980s litigation began. The predecessor provision, Section 488 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898, had obliged a man to maintain his wife and his legitimate or illegitimate children, and its text had not extended the wife's claim beyond the subsistence of the marriage; the Law Commission's work that preceded the Code of 1973 recommended, and the new Code enacted, the express inclusion of a divorced woman who has not remarried within the definition, on the ground that the obligation to prevent destitution should not evaporate at the moment the marriage that created the dependence is dissolved. Section 125 therefore arrived in 1973 already carrying, in its Explanation, the proposition that the 1986 controversy would be fought over: that a former husband's duty under the general law survives his divorce. The provision is deliberately indifferent to the personal law of the parties. It is a summary, welfare-oriented remedy aimed at preventing destitution, and it had been applied to divorced Muslim women on the same terms as to everyone else, subject to one qualification in Section 127(3)(b): where a divorced woman had received the whole of the sum payable to her on divorce under the customary or personal law applicable to the parties, the Magistrate was to cancel the maintenance order. That qualification made the legal effect of sums paid at divorce, above all the mahr or dower, a recurring battleground, and it was litigated through the 1970s in decisions that went both ways on whether a payment of mahr extinguished a former husband's liability to pay a monthly allowance to a woman who could not support herself.

The qualification in Section 127(3)(b) had already produced, before 1985, two decisions of the Supreme Court that fixed the poles of the argument and that any account of the 1986 Act's background must carry, because the Act's mahr clause cannot be understood without them. In Bai Tahira v. Ali Hussain Fidaalli Chothia, (1979) 2 SCC 316, the Court refused to treat a payment of mahr and iddat maintenance as discharging a former husband's obligation under Section 125 to a woman unable to maintain herself: the sums payable on divorce under the personal law, it held, fall within Section 127(3)(b) only where they are a reasonable substitute for maintenance, a consideration for the divorce that actually discharges the welfare obligation the Code imposes, and an illusory or nominal amount paid at the end of a long marriage does not answer that description. In Fuzlunbi v. K. Khader Vali, (1980) 4 SCC 125, the Court carried the same approach further, holding that a divorced woman's right under Section 125 survived the payment of her mahr where the amount was not, in reality, a sum sufficient to keep her from destitution, and reading Section 127(3)(b) as aimed at genuine settlements rather than at the mechanical fact of a dower payment. Both decisions came from Benches presided over by V.R. Krishna Iyer J., and both were attacked in the years that followed on the same ground later pressed against the 1985 Constitution Bench: that they had subordinated the personal law to a welfare code by interpretation. Their importance for the present statute is structural. They established, before the 1986 Act was drafted, that Indian maintenance law had already rejected the proposition that the mahr is a buy-out of a divorced woman's support, and the Act, for all the controversy about its purpose, did not in terms restore that proposition either: it placed the mahr in Section 3(1)(c) as one clause of a four-part settlement, alongside a provision and maintenance clause whose whole point, on any reading, is that the mahr alone was not the measure of the husband's obligation. The continuity is easy to miss in the political literature, which treats 1986 as a clean rupture, and it matters for the construction question: a court asked in 2001 whether Parliament had intended the mahr and three months of support to exhaust the husband's liability was being asked to attribute to Parliament an intention that the pre-existing case law had spent a decade refusing to find in the Code.

In 1985 a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court held that Section 125 applied to a divorced Muslim woman regardless of her personal law, a decision whose facts, reasoning and political aftermath are the subject of [the Shah Bano judgment and the legislative response](/2016/12/01/shah-bano-case-legislative-response/) and are stated here in that single sentence because this article owns the statute that followed, not the judgment that preceded it. Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act in 1986, in the Thirty-seventh year of the Republic. It is Act 25 of 1986, assented to on 19 May 1986, and it carries no deferred commencement clause, so it operated from that date. Supporters and opponents in 1986 agreed on one thing: both described the new statute as displacing the 1985 decision for divorced Muslim women and substituting a self-contained scheme drawn from the personal law. Whether the enacted text did that is a different question, and the distance between what the Act was understood to do and what it has been held to do is the central fact about it. A reader who keeps that distinction in view will follow everything that follows in this article; a reader who collapses it will repeat the commonest error in the field.

The passage itself was swift. The Bill was brought forward by the government in the first months of 1986, debated through the spring session against the background of the public controversy the 1985 decision had generated, and passed by both Houses in time to receive the President's assent on 19 May 1986. Its statement of objects recorded that the decision had produced controversy about the true legal position and that it was proposed to specify the rights of a divorced Muslim woman at divorce. The detailed story of that passage, the speeches, the resignations and the calculations belong to the article that owns the judgment and the legislative response together, and they are not retold here. What this article needs from the passage record is only two durable facts. The first is that the legislature moved within a year of the judgment, which marks the Act as a responsive statute drafted against a specific decision rather than as a considered codification of divorce economics; responsive drafting explains the text's compression, its reliance on the personal law's own institutions, and its silence at the points where the courts later had to work. The second is that the enacted text is markedly more measured than the controversy around it: whatever was said in the campaign for and against the Bill, the seven sections Parliament actually passed contain no clause stating that maintenance ends at iddat and no clause closing the Code to the women the Act covers, and it is the sections, not the campaign, that the courts were later bound to construe.

The statute also has to be placed within the small cluster of enactments that govern Muslim personal law in India, a field in which, as [the complete guide to personal law in India](/2016/09/01/personal-laws-india-complete-guide/) maps it, a large uncodified core is surrounded by a handful of statutes passed at moments of acute controversy. The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act of 1937 directs that questions of marriage, dissolution of marriage, maintenance and dower between Muslims be decided by the Shariat rather than by custom. The Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act of 1939 gives a Muslim woman statutory grounds on which she may obtain a decree of dissolution from a court. The Act of 1986 is the third member of that cluster for present purposes: it does not codify the law of divorce, it does not touch the grounds or forms of divorce, and it does not even govern maintenance during marriage. Its entire field is the economic consequence of a divorce that has already happened under Muslim law, and within that field it speaks only to the position of the divorced woman. Everything in it flows from the divorce outward: what must be settled at the divorce itself, and what happens afterwards if the settlement fails to keep her from destitution.

## The shape of the Act: seven sections and three definitions

The Act's own long title states its purpose without ornament: it is an Act to protect the rights of Muslim women who have been divorced by, or have obtained divorce from, their husbands, and to provide for matters connected with or incidental to that purpose. As enacted, it extended to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir, an exception that stood in the text for as long as the constitutional arrangements it reflected survived; the official text of the Act has since omitted the exception, and the Act extends to the whole of India. Its architecture is unusually spare. Section 1 carries the short title and extent. Section 2 defines the terms on which everything else turns. Section 3, headed "Mahr or other properties of Muslim woman to be given to her at the time of divorce", carries the entitlements against the former husband and the machinery for enforcing them. Section 4, "Order for payment of maintenance", carries the fallback liability of relatives and of the State Wakf Board. Section 5 is the option to be governed by Sections 125 to 128 of the Code. Section 6 empowers the Central Government to make rules, under which the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Rules, 1986 were made. Section 7 is the transitional provision for applications pending at commencement. Seven sections in all, of which two do nearly all the work.

### How many sections does the 1986 Act have, and where does its weight sit?

The Act has seven sections, and its weight sits in two of them. Section 3 states what a divorced woman is entitled to from her former husband and how a Magistrate enforces it. Section 4 states who must maintain her afterwards if she cannot maintain herself. The remaining sections carry the definitions, the option, the transition and the rule-making power.

That distribution matters because most commentary on the Act is commentary on a statute its authors appear not to have read at this level of closeness. Section 3 is written as a single integrated settlement to be completed at the divorce: a provision for the woman, maintenance for her, provision and maintenance for the children she keeps, the mahr, and the return of her properties. Section 4 is written as a second, separate proceeding that arises only later, only if she has not remarried, and only if she is unable to maintain herself once the iddat period has run. The two sections answer different questions at different moments, and conflating them is the first step toward the standard inaccurate summary, in which the whole Act is described as though Section 4's post-iddat fallback were the measure of the former husband's obligation under Section 3.

Section 2's definitions reward the same closeness. A "divorced woman" is a Muslim woman who was married according to Muslim law, and has been divorced by, or has obtained divorce from, her husband in accordance with Muslim law. Both limbs of the divorce are covered: the woman divorced by her husband and the woman who obtained the divorce herself, whether by khula, by mubaraat, or by a decree under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act of 1939, stands inside the definition. Nothing in the definition requires the divorce to have taken any particular form, and nothing makes the Act's benefits depend on who initiated the dissolution. The breadth of the definition is worth a further moment, because it disposes of a recurring assumption that the Act is a statute about talaq alone. The forms of dissolution under Muslim law are several. A husband may divorce by talaq. A wife may obtain release by khula, a dissolution at her instance, ordinarily on terms the parties settle; or by mubaraat, a dissolution by mutual consent in which the release is bilateral. And a wife may obtain a decree of dissolution from a court under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, whose Section 2 sets out the grounds, among them that the husband has neglected or failed to provide for her maintenance for a period of two years, that he has been sentenced to imprisonment for seven years or more, that he has failed to perform his marital obligations for three years, and that he has treated her with cruelty. Every one of those routes ends in the same statutory person: a Muslim woman who was married according to Muslim law and has been divorced by, or has obtained divorce from, her husband in accordance with Muslim law. The definition's indifference to the route matters in both directions. A woman who herself sued for dissolution on the ground of her husband's failure to maintain her is not disqualified from the Act's settlement by having been the moving party; and a husband cannot resist a Section 3 application by showing that the divorce was of a kind his school regards differently, because the Act asks only whether there was a marriage under Muslim law and a divorce under Muslim law. The economic consequences Parliament attached to divorce attach to the fact of it.

The definition of "iddat period" is more elaborate, and it is examined in its own right below, because the whole of Section 3 is timed by it. "Magistrate" means a Magistrate of the first class exercising jurisdiction under the Code of Criminal Procedure in the area where the divorced woman resides, a definition that fixes the forum by her residence rather than by the place of marriage or of divorce, and that ties the Act's summary machinery to the same class of court that administers Section 125. "Prescribed" simply means prescribed by the rules made under the Act.

The iddat definition has three branches. Where the divorced woman is subject to menstruation, the iddat period is three menstrual courses after the date of divorce. Where she is not subject to menstruation, it is three lunar months after her divorce. Where she is enceinte at the time of her divorce, it is the period between the divorce and the delivery of her child or the termination of her pregnancy, whichever is earlier. Iddat is, in the personal law, a waiting period that follows the dissolution of a marriage, serving among other purposes the ascertainment of pregnancy, and the Act borrows it as its clock. But the Act uses the period in a way that must be stated precisely, because the standard summary gets it backwards. Under Section 3, the iddat period is the time within which the former husband's obligations must be discharged: the provision and maintenance are "to be made and paid to her within the iddat period". On the face of the text, the period governs when the husband must pay, and what he must pay is described by the separate phrase "a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance". Whether the period also governs how much he must pay, confining the substance of the entitlement to the needs of those few months, is the question the text leaves open and the courts had to answer. The Act itself does not say, in terms, that maintenance ends with the iddat period. It never uses words to that effect, and the whole subsequent history of the statute is contained in that silence.

Two features of the iddat definition deserve emphasis before the entitlements are reached, because both recur in practice. The first is that the Act borrows iddat purely as a measure of time and strips it of the other functions it performs in the personal law, where the period also regulates the revocability of certain divorces and bars a fresh marriage until it expires; within the four corners of this statute, iddat does nothing except fix the window inside which the Section 3 settlement must be completed and the point after which Section 4's different machinery may begin. The second is the pregnancy branch. Where the woman is enceinte at the divorce, her iddat runs until the delivery of the child or the termination of the pregnancy, whichever is earlier, which may extend the window well beyond three months, and the extension interacts with the children's clause of Section 3: a child born after the divorce enters the world already inside the settlement, with provision and maintenance owed for the child for two years from birth. The definition thus quietly handles the hardest case, the woman divorced while pregnant, by giving the settlement a longer runway and the unborn child a place in it, a piece of drafting care that the Act's reputation rarely credits.

## Section 3: the settlement the Act requires at the divorce

Section 3(1) opens with a non-obstante clause: notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force, a divorced woman shall be entitled to the four things that follow. The first is a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance to be made and paid to her within the iddat period by her former husband. The second, where she herself maintains the children born to her before or after her divorce, is a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance to be made and paid by her former husband for a period of two years from the respective dates of birth of such children. The third is an amount equal to the sum of mahr or dower agreed to be paid to her at the time of her marriage or at any time thereafter according to Muslim law. The fourth is all the properties given to her before or at the time of marriage or after her marriage by her relatives or friends or the husband or any relatives of the husband or his friends. The four clauses are not four versions of one payment. They are four different kinds of entitlement, with different juridical characters, and the section's design is a settlement in which each plays a distinct part.

The non-obstante clause that introduces the four entitlements also needs its effect stated exactly, because it is routinely over-read. "Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law" gives the entitlements priority where another law is inconsistent with them: a rule of personal law or a term of the divorce settlement that purported to give the woman less than the section gives her would yield to the section. What the clause does not do, and what no reported construction has made it do, is extinguish other remedies. It creates and protects rights; it is not worded as a bar, an ouster or a repeal, and it names no other statute. The contrast with the way Parliament writes an ouster when it intends one is instructive: Section 4 carries its own non-obstante clause for the different purpose of overriding obstacles to the relatives' liability, and Section 7, the one provision that does displace pending Code proceedings, does so in express words directed at identified applications. The architecture therefore distinguishes carefully between priority, which Sections 3 and 4 assert, and exclusivity, which only Section 7 asserts, and only for the transitional moment. Much of the later judicial history is the courts' refusal to let the priority clauses be inflated into the exclusivity clause that Parliament wrote separately and narrowly when it wanted one.

Begin with the distinction on which everything else turns, the distinction inside the first clause between a provision and maintenance. Maintenance is periodical support: a sum paid across time to meet recurring needs, the kind of obligation Section 125 of the Code expresses as a monthly allowance. A provision is something made for the future: an arrangement, ordinarily capital in character, settled at a point in time so that the person provided for can live from it afterwards. The clause requires both, and it requires both to be "made and paid" within the iddat period. Two readings of that requirement were possible, and the history of the Act is the contest between them. On the first reading, the iddat period measures the entitlement itself: the husband must maintain his former wife for the iddat period and make such provision as those months require, and at the end of the period his obligation is spent. On the second reading, the iddat period measures only the time for performance: the husband must, within that window, make and pay a provision that answers his former wife's needs, including her future needs beyond the window, together with maintenance; the deadline is a deadline for settlement, not a limit on what is settled. The text, read without preconception, supports the second reading at least as readily as the first, because it attaches the period to the verbs, to the making and paying, and not to the needs being provided for. A provision is by its nature forward-looking: a "provision for the iddat period" that is exhausted by the period's own maintenance is not a provision at all but maintenance under another name, and Parliament's use of two words has to be given work to do. This is the textual hook on which the later construction turned, and it is why a pillar account of this statute has to linger on two ordinary nouns.

The children's clause, the second entitlement, is narrower than readers often assume and broader than its critics allowed. It applies where the divorced woman herself maintains the children born to her before or after the divorce, and it entitles her to a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance from the former husband for those children for a period of two years from the respective dates of their birth. The two-year measure runs from birth, not from divorce, so for a child born well before the divorce the clause may already be spent, and for a child born at or after the divorce it covers the first two years of life. The clause does not make the 1986 Act a children's maintenance code, and it was never the only word on the subject: children have their own independent claim under Section 125 of the Code, and the Supreme Court held in Noor Saba Khatoon v. Mohd. Quasim in 1997 that a Muslim father's obligation to maintain his minor children under that section is not cut down by the two-year clause in the Act of 1986, so that the children's general-law entitlement continues until majority on its own terms. The clause is best understood as part of the divorce settlement, a contribution to the children's earliest years quantified within the same proceeding, sitting alongside rather than displacing the children's own remedy. Its two conditions and its arithmetic repay precision. The first condition is custody in fact: the clause is engaged "where she herself maintains the children", so it is the mother who is actually keeping the children who receives the contribution, and a father who retains the children after the divorce neither pays under this clause nor receives under it, the Act containing no mirror provision for a father maintaining the children of the marriage. The second is the measure: two years from the respective dates of birth, computed child by child, so that in a family with children of different ages the clause may be fully alive for the youngest, partly spent for the next, and exhausted for the eldest, all within one application. The choice of the first two years of life as the measure tracks the personal law's own allocation of infant custody, under which the young child's care rests ordinarily with the mother, and it confines the father's Section 3 exposure for children to the period of that custody's most intensive demands. Beyond the second birthday the 1986 Act falls silent on the children, and it is at exactly that point that Noor Saba Khatoon's holding does its work: the children's claim under Section 125 is their own, it is not derived from the mother's settlement, it does not depend on the mother's remarriage or means, and it runs until the child attains majority on the Code's own terms. A Magistrate winding up a divorce under Section 3 is therefore dealing with the children's earliest maintenance only, and an adviser who treats the two-year clause as the children's whole entitlement repeats, in miniature, the standard error about the Act as a whole.

The third clause, the mahr, imports into the statute an institution of the personal law that predates it by centuries. Mahr, or dower, is the sum of money or property that the husband is bound to pay or deliver to the wife in consideration of the marriage, fixed by the marriage contract or by law where the contract is silent. It is classified in the personal law as prompt, payable on demand during the marriage, or deferred, payable on the dissolution of the marriage by divorce or death. It is the wife's exclusive property, it is a debt owed to her rather than a gift, and on a divorce any unpaid portion becomes immediately recoverable. What the 1986 Act does with mahr is specific and limited: it makes the amount of the mahr agreed at the marriage or thereafter an entitlement enforceable in the same summary proceeding as the provision and maintenance, so that a woman need not sue separately on the dower debt to recover it at the divorce. It does not create the mahr, fix its amount, or treat its payment as buying out the other entitlements. That last point matters historically, because the pre-1986 litigation under Section 127(3)(b) of the Code had repeatedly raised exactly that argument, that a husband who had paid the mahr had discharged everything. Neither the Code as construed in 1985 nor the Act of 1986 as construed in 2001 accepts it: the mahr is one clause of the settlement, not a substitute for the rest.

The mahr clause also performs an evidentiary function that practitioners understand and commentators understate. In a Section 3 proceeding the Magistrate must quantify an unliquidated provision on contested evidence of needs, standard of life and means, but the mahr is a liquidated figure: it is written in the nikahnama or proved from the marriage's record, it is classified by the personal law itself as prompt or deferred, and the deferred portion falls due precisely on the event, divorce, that the proceeding is winding up. The clause therefore gives the summary court one hard number around which the softer assessments arrange themselves, and it gives the woman a debt she can prove from a document rather than from an argument. Two qualifications complete the picture. Where the marriage contract fixes no mahr, the personal law implies a proper dower assessed on the woman's position and her family's circumstances, so the clause is not defeated by the contract's silence; and where the mahr was fixed generations of inflation ago at a figure that has become nominal, the clause yields a nominal sum, which is exactly why the construction of the first clause matters so much: the provision, unlike the mahr, is measured by the Magistrate at the date of the divorce against the woman's needs and the standard of the life she actually lived, and it is the provision, not the dower, that carries the economic weight of a long marriage's dissolution.

The fourth clause, the properties, is the settlement's restorative element. A woman is entitled to all the properties given to her before or at the time of marriage or after her marriage by her relatives or friends, or by the husband or any of his relatives or friends. The clause collects the gifts that accumulate around a marriage, jewellery, money, household goods and other property given to her from both sides of the family, and makes their delivery to her a statutory entitlement at the divorce rather than a matter to be fought over as a question of title in a civil suit. Read together, the four clauses describe a complete winding-up of the marriage's economics at the moment of divorce: capital provision and maintenance for the woman, a defined contribution for the young children in her care, the dower debt paid, and her own property restored to her. The design's coherence is real, whatever view is taken of its adequacy, and it explains the argument its supporters pressed in 1986 with complete sincerity: that the Act gave a divorced woman a settled capital sum, her dower and her property in hand at the divorce, in place of a small monthly allowance that had to be claimed month by month and enforced against a reluctant payer. The adequacy of that exchange, measured against women with no earning capacity after long marriages and small recorded mahrs, is the counter-argument, and it is taken up in the section below that states the two positions in their own terms.

## The Magistrate's order under Section 3, and what happens if it is not obeyed

Entitlements on paper become real through Section 3(2) and what follows it. Where a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance, or the amount of mahr or dower due, has not been made or paid, or the properties referred to in the fourth clause have not been delivered to a divorced woman on her divorce, she may apply to a Magistrate for an order; and the application may be made not only by the woman herself but by anyone duly authorised by her on her behalf, a facility that matters for a claimant who cannot readily attend a court. The forum is fixed by the definition in Section 2: a Magistrate of the first class exercising jurisdiction under the Code in the area where the divorced woman resides. The application is therefore brought where she lives, not where the marriage was celebrated, where the husband lives, or where the divorce was pronounced, and it is heard by the same class of summary court that administers the general maintenance provision. The residence rule is a considered access decision, and it contrasts with the general provision's wider venue. Section 126 of the Code lets maintenance proceedings be taken in any district where the respondent is, where he or his wife resides, or where he last resided with his wife, a spread of forums that reflects the reality that a neglected wife may be living with her parents far from the matrimonial home. The Act of 1986 chooses one forum only, the Magistrate of the area where the divorced woman resides, and in doing so it both simplifies and narrows: the woman sues at her own doorstep, which is the forum she can actually reach, but the husband's convenience and the place of the marriage drop out of the calculation entirely. For a statute whose claimant is by hypothesis a woman without support in the aftermath of a divorce, the choice of her residence as the single venue is one of its most practically humane features, and it passes unnoticed in commentary that treats the Act as nothing but a restriction.

The design assumption underneath this machinery is worth surfacing, because it explains the section's otherwise puzzling timetable. The Act expects the settlement to happen at the divorce itself, as part of the divorce's own transaction: the personal law's practice had always treated the divorce as the moment at which the mahr fell due and the wife's property was restored, and Section 3 generalises that moment into a complete winding-up, to be performed within the iddat period that follows the pronouncement. The Magistrate's jurisdiction under Section 3(2) is therefore framed as a default jurisdiction, engaged "where" the provision has not been made or paid or the properties not delivered; the litigation is the exception path for a settlement that failed, not the ordinary mode of obtaining one. That is why the order is directed within a month and why no interim machinery is supplied: the statute imagines a single, prompt accounting at the end of a marriage, conducted if necessary by a Magistrate, rather than a continuing supervisory relationship between the court and the former spouses. Every distinctive feature of the Act, its capital form, its deadline, its silence on variation, follows from that single imagining, and every practical criticism of the Act is, at bottom, a criticism of the imagining: marriages of long duration, ended unilaterally, between parties of unequal information and power, rarely produce a voluntary winding-up of the completeness the section describes, and the exception path becomes, in the generality of cases, the road itself.

Section 3(3) then states the conditions and the standard for the order. The Magistrate may act if satisfied that the former husband, having sufficient means, has failed or neglected to make or pay within the iddat period a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance for the woman and the children, or that the mahr or dower has not been paid or the properties have not been delivered. The order is to be made within one month of the date of the filing of the application, a direction of unusual briskness in Indian procedural legislation, qualified by a proviso under which, if the Magistrate finds it impracticable to dispose of the application within that period, reasons must be recorded and the application may be disposed of afterwards. The quantum the Magistrate fixes is governed by three stated considerations: the needs of the divorced woman, the standard of life enjoyed by her during her marriage, and the means of her former husband. Those three factors, needs, marital standard and means, are the same triad that governs maintenance adjudication under the general law, and they are the route by which the capital provision under this section is quantified: the Magistrate projects the woman's needs, measured against the life she had in the marriage, capitalises them into a provision, and tests the result against what the husband can pay. No ceiling is stated anywhere in the section, and no formula is prescribed; the figure is a judicial assessment on those three factors, which is why outcomes under Section 3 vary as widely as the marriages they close. The one-month direction deserves a comparative note, because it is stricter on its face than anything the general provision attempts. The Code, even after its 2001 modernisation, sets its sixty-day target only for the interim application, the emergency bridge, and leaves the final order to the ordinary pace of a summary trial; the Act of 1986 directs the final order itself within a month of filing. Both targets are honoured unevenly in the district courts, and both are drafted with an escape: the Code's is the phrase "as far as possible", the Act's is the proviso requiring reasons to be recorded where disposal within the month is impracticable. The comparison is a small lesson in legislative technique. Parliament in 1986, legislating for a claimant class it knew to be at its most vulnerable in the weeks after a divorce, chose speed as a substantive protection and wrote the target into the section that creates the order; a generation of Magistrates have treated the proviso as the operative clause and the month as an aspiration, which is less a criticism of the courts than a demonstration that a disposal target without a consequence for missing it functions, in every statute that contains one, as an exhortation.

### What interim relief can a Magistrate give while a Section 3 application is pending?

The Act contains no interim relief provision of its own, no equivalent of the interim maintenance the general provision allows while a claim is being decided. Its answer to delay is structural: the entitlement is a settlement quantified in one order, and Section 3 directs that order within one month of filing, with reasons recorded if the target is missed.

The absence is worth pausing on, because it is one of the genuine structural differences between the two routes this article compares. Under Section 125 of the Code, a claimant who cannot support herself during the litigation is not left to wait for the final order: the 2001 amendment to the Code wrote an express interim maintenance power into the section, with litigation expenses, and directed that an application for interim relief be disposed of, as far as possible, within sixty days of service of notice on the respondent. Under the 1986 Act there is no such bridge. The woman's protection against a slow proceeding is the one-month direction and nothing else, and in a contested case the proviso's escape route, reasons recorded and disposal afterwards, is available on the face of the section. An advocate comparing the routes for a client without resources will weigh that difference heavily, and it is one reason the general provision, where it is available, is so often the first filing even where a Section 3 application is also contemplated.

Enforcement is where Section 3 bares its teeth. Section 3(4) provides that if any person against whom an order has been made fails without sufficient cause to comply with it, the Magistrate may issue a warrant for levying the amount of maintenance or mahr or dower due in the manner provided for levying fines under the Code of Criminal Procedure, and may sentence that person, for the whole or part of any amount remaining unpaid after the execution of the warrant, to imprisonment for a term which may extend to one year or until payment if sooner made. Two protections for the defaulter are written into the same clause: he must be heard in defence, and the sentence must be imposed according to the provisions of the Code. The architecture is deliberately coercive rather than compensatory, and in one respect it is more coercive than the general provision it is usually compared unfavourably with: Section 125(3) of the Code backs its monthly allowance with imprisonment of up to one month for each month of default, while Section 3(4) of the Act permits imprisonment extending to a full year against the single quantified sum. The comparison cuts against the lazy version of the standard summary, in which the Act is presented as toothless beside the Code. Its teeth are real. What the Act does not provide is an appeal of its own: it contains no appellate clause, and a party aggrieved by a Magistrate's order must take the matter up through the ordinary channels the Code provides against magisterial orders, a silence consistent with the Act's method throughout, which is to borrow the Code's machinery wherever it needs machinery of its own.

## Section 4: the liability that begins where the former husband's ends

Section 4 opens with its own non-obstante clause and addresses a situation Section 3 does not: the iddat period has run, the divorce settlement has been made or has failed, and the divorced woman is still unable to maintain herself. Where a Magistrate is satisfied that a divorced woman has not remarried and is not able to maintain herself after the iddat period, the Magistrate may make an order directing such of her relatives as would be entitled to inherit her property on her death according to Muslim law to pay her such reasonable and fair maintenance as the Magistrate determines fit and proper. The standard is the same triad as in Section 3, her needs, the standard of life enjoyed by her during her marriage, and the means of the relatives; and the maintenance ordered is payable by the relatives in the proportions in which they would inherit her property, at such periods as the Magistrate specifies in the order. The section thus distributes the obligation across the class of her presumptive heirs in the shares in which they would take her estate, an internal logic drawn directly from the personal law's own scheme of succession: those who would benefit from her property on her death carry, in the same proportions, the cost of keeping her while she lives.

The provisos then impose an order of priority inside that class. Where the divorced woman has children, the Magistrate shall order only the children to pay maintenance to her; only if the children are unable to pay does the liability pass to her parents. A further proviso deals with inability within the liable group: if a parent ordered to pay a share cannot pay it for want of means, the Magistrate may, on proof of that inability, order that the share be paid by such of the other relatives as appear to have the means, in such proportions as the Magistrate thinks fit. The section is therefore not a flat liability of all relatives but a sequenced one, children first, then parents, then the wider class of heirs, with shares reallocated where a particular relative cannot carry the share first assigned. One machinery gap in Section 4 should be recorded alongside its design. The section directs that maintenance be payable at such periods as the Magistrate specifies, but it contains no clause for the alteration of the order on a change of circumstances: no equivalent of the Code's Section 127 power to raise, reduce or cancel an allowance when means and needs move. A relative ordered to pay on the strength of present means who later loses them, or a woman whose needs deepen with age or illness, will search the Act in vain for the variation application that the general law makes routine. The omission is consistent with the Act's conception of Section 4 as a residual safety net rather than a living maintenance relationship, but it is a further reason the section has functioned as a statement of responsibility rather than as a working remedy, since an order that cannot be adjusted to the payer's changing fortunes invites default, and default under Section 4 has no dedicated enforcement clause of the kind Section 3(4) gives the settlement against the husband.

Only when that sequence is exhausted does the final fallback appear. Under Section 4(2), where the divorced woman has no relatives of the kind described, or such relatives or any of them have not enough means to pay the maintenance ordered, the Magistrate may direct the State Wakf Board, established under Section 9 of the Wakf Act, 1954 or under any other law in force in a State and functioning in the area in which the woman resides, to pay the maintenance determined under the section, or the unpaid shares of it. The Wakf Board is the payer of last resort in the Act's design, the community's endowed institution standing behind the family's failure. A wakf is a permanent dedication of property for purposes recognised by Muslim law as religious, pious or charitable, and the State Wakf Boards are the statutory bodies, constituted under the Wakf legislation, that supervise the administration of wakfs within each State, maintain their records and collect the contribution the Wakf Act levies on wakf income for the Board's fund. Parliament's choice of this institution as the terminal obligor followed the personal law's own welfare logic, in which the community's endowments carry a charge for its destitute members, and it gave the Act a closing guarantee that no merely secular drafting would have supplied from within the community's institutions. The difficulty, visible from the start and confirmed by the section's working, is one of institutional fit. A Wakf Board is a regulator and custodian of endowed property, financed by a levy on the endowments it supervises, with chronic demands on its resources from the mosques, graveyards and charities within its charge. It was not constituted as a maintenance service, it maintains no machinery for assessing or paying individual monthly support, and its liability under Section 4(2) is to pay such maintenance as the Magistrate determines, a direction easier to record in an order than to convert into a monthly payment across years. The section thus ends the Act's chain of obligation at its institutionally weakest link, a fact that both sides of the 1986 argument noticed, the supporters treating the Board's presence as a guarantee of last resort that would rarely be reached, the critics treating its weakness as the measure of the guarantee's worth.

The composition of the liable class carries a consequence that the section's critics identified early and that a close reading confirms: the burden Section 4 distributes falls on the woman's natal family, and it falls on its women as well as its men. The relatives who would inherit her property include her mother and her daughters, each an heir in the personal law's table, and the section draws no distinction of sex among the obligors it conscripts; a daughter of full age with means may be ordered to maintain her divorced mother in the share she would one day inherit, and a mother may be ordered to maintain her divorced daughter. The design is internally faithful to its premise, that the obligation follows the inheritance, but the premise produces a distribution in which the costs of a failed marriage are socialised across the women of the claimant's own family, the very persons whose economic position in that generation was often as exposed as hers. That feature of the section, more than the Wakf Board's remoteness, explains the reluctance of claimants and courts alike to work it: an application under Section 4 asks a Magistrate to mulct the claimant's mother and children, and the cases in which family relations survive such an application are not the majority of the cases in which it is needed.

The mechanics of the inheritance proportion repay a moment's attention, because they show the section thinking in the personal law's own categories rather than in the Code's. If a divorced woman dies, her property passes under Muslim law to her heirs in the shares that law assigns, children and parents taking as primary heirs and the wider kindred taking in the orders the law prescribes. Section 4 conscripts that table prospectively: the relatives who would take those shares are the relatives who must pay, and they pay in the same proportions, so that the son who would inherit the larger share carries the larger share of his mother's maintenance and the parent who would take a fixed portion carries that portion of the burden. The Magistrate's order therefore requires two findings that no other maintenance statute in India demands: a finding about a hypothetical future succession, identifying who her heirs would be if she died, and a translation of their expectancies into maintenance shares. That is a genuinely distinctive piece of statutory engineering, and it explains both the section's intellectual elegance, which its defenders praised, and its practical fragility, which its critics predicted: everything depends on the existence of identifiable relatives, with ascertainable means, within the reach of a Magistrate's process, and on a claimant with the resources to run a second proceeding after the first has already failed to keep her.

Three analytical observations belong to a pillar account of this section, because each qualifies the way the Act is usually described. The first is that Section 4 liability is not the former husband's liability continued by other means. It arises only after the iddat period, only on proof of inability to maintain herself, only if she has not remarried, and it is measured by the relatives' means and inheritance shares rather than by the marriage. The former husband is simply not in the section: once the Section 3 settlement is complete, his statutory exposure under the Act is complete with it, subject always to the construction of Section 3 examined below. The second observation is that the section is the part of the Act that its 1986 critics fastened on, and their objection was structural, not textual quibbling: a woman whose relatives were as poor as she was, or who had no relatives within the class, was remitted to a Wakf Board whose obligation depended on the Board's own resources and on her ability to set another proceeding in motion, so that the guarantee at the end of the sequence was weaker than the obligation at its start. The third observation is empirical and should be stated with the restraint the subject requires: Section 4 has generated very little reported litigation in the decades since 1986, and the Wakf Board direction in particular is rarely met in the reported decisions, a pattern visible in the case law itself rather than in any counted statistic. The section operates, in practice, less as a living remedy than as the design's statement of where responsibility was intended to rest once the marriage's own obligations were wound up.

## Section 5 and Section 7: the option and the transition

Section 5 is headed "Option to be governed by the provisions of sections 125 to 128 of Act 2 of 1974", the referenced Act being the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. It provides that if, on the date of the first hearing of an application under Section 3(2), a divorced woman and her former husband declare, by affidavit or any other declaration in writing in the prescribed form, either jointly or separately, that they would prefer to be governed by the provisions of Sections 125 to 128 of the Code, and file the declaration in the court of the Magistrate, the Magistrate shall dispose of the application accordingly. Every element of that sentence does work. The option is exercised inside a pending Section 3 proceeding, not by originating a claim under the Code. It is exercised at a fixed moment, the date of the first hearing of the application. And it is exercised by declaration, the Rules of 1986 prescribing the form, Form B under Rule 8, in which the affidavit or declaration is to be filed. Most importantly for accuracy, the declaration may be made either jointly or separately: the section does not condition the option on the two parties filing together, and accounts of the Act that describe it as requiring a joint declaration misstate the text. Each party declares a preference to be governed by the Code's provisions, and the Magistrate then disposes of the application under those provisions.

The timing condition is the provision's least examined feature and, on analysis, its most revealing. The option exists only at the first hearing of an application under Section 3(2), which means it exists only for a woman who has already commenced the Act's own proceeding. A woman who wished from the outset to proceed under the Code, and who filed there directly, never passed through the moment at which the option could be exercised at all; and a woman inside a Section 3 proceeding could find her route to the Code dependent on declarations filed at a hearing whose date she did not control, in a proceeding she might have filed precisely because she believed the Act was her only remedy. If the section were in fact the exclusive door to the Code, as the restrictive High Court reading later supposed, then the door would open only from inside the Act, and only at one early instant; the consequence, that a divorced Muslim woman's access to the general welfare provision turned on a procedural formality inside a statute enacted to displace that provision, was the reductio that the Supreme Court's eventual route around the section quietly acknowledged. The Court never needed to strike Section 5 down or read it down. It simply declined, in 2010 and again in 2024, to treat it as the source of the woman's right to proceed under the Code, locating that right in the Code itself and in the Act's character as an addition rather than a derogation.

The practical career of Section 5 has been stranger than its drafting suggests. For the first years of the Act, husbands resisting claims under the Code relied on the section's logic by inversion: they argued that a divorced Muslim woman could reach Sections 125 to 128 only through the Section 5 door, so that a free-standing application under the Code, filed without any declaration, was not maintainable at all. The Andhra Pradesh High Court's Full Bench accepted reasoning of that kind in Usman Khan Bahamani v. Fathimunnisa Begum in 1990. The argument had a certain textual neatness, and it made Section 5 the single most consequential short provision in the Act, because on that reading the option was not an option at all but a gate, and a gate that a former husband who refused to declare could keep shut. The later Supreme Court decisions examined below dismantled that reading from the other end: they did not rest the availability of the Code on Section 5 at all, holding instead that the Code's provision was never displaced for a divorced Muslim woman who chose to invoke it directly. Section 5 survives as a mechanism for converting a Section 3 proceeding into a Code proceeding at its first hearing, a facility of real but narrow use, and its history is a warning against treating any single provision of this Act as the key to the whole.

Section 7, the transitional provision, is shorter and was spent quickly, but it mattered greatly to the women whose claims were in court in May 1986. It provided that every application by a divorced woman under Section 125 or under Section 127 of the Code, pending before a Magistrate on the commencement of the Act, shall, notwithstanding anything contained in the Code and subject to the provisions of Section 5, be disposed of by the Magistrate in accordance with the provisions of the Act. A pending general-law claim was thus carried into the new scheme by force of the section, unless the parties exercised the Section 5 option and kept it under the Code. The provision confirms, if confirmation were needed, that Parliament in 1986 understood itself to be moving divorced Muslim women out of the general provision and into the new Act: transition is where a legislature's assumption about the relationship between two statutes is least guarded, and Section 7's assumption is unmistakable. That the courts later held the two regimes to coexist, so that a provision designed as a one-way valve became a historical footnote for pending cases only, is part of the same story of an enacted text being settled by construction into a shape its transitional clause did not predict. Section 6, the rule-making power, and Section 1 have been noticed already; with Section 7 the survey of the Act's seven sections is complete, and the article turns to the disagreement that has surrounded the text since the week it was passed.

## The two positions on the Act, stated in the terms their proponents use

Because this statute sits at the meeting point of personal law, constitutional equality and legislative supremacy, the brief for this article requires the strictest form of evenhandedness: what the statute provides and what the courts have held are to be set out, the competing positions are to be presented in the terms their proponents use, and the article is to reach no view of its own about which position should have prevailed. The positions below are those taken in and around the enactment of 1986 and maintained in the argument since; they are reported, not adopted.

Those who supported the statute argued, first, that the general maintenance provision applied a single uniform rule to a matter that the Constitution left to be governed, for Muslims, by Muslim personal law, and that a community was entitled to have its own law applied to its own members in a matter of marriage and its dissolution. On that view the defect of the 1985 decision was not its compassion but its method: it had reached a result for a divorced Muslim woman through a religion-neutral code in disregard of the personal law that the parties had married under, and it was for Parliament, not for a court, to reset the position. They argued, second, that the statute was not a subtraction but a substitution, and on their account a generous one. The general provision offered a divorced woman a modest monthly allowance, fixed in an era when the Code had capped such allowances at five hundred rupees a month, recoverable month by month against a former husband who might default, abscond or plead changed means, and terminable in the ways the Code itself provided. The Act, by contrast, secured her at the divorce itself a capital provision quantified by her needs and her marital standard of life, her mahr, and the whole of her property, and placed behind that settlement the further guarantees of her relatives and of the Wakf Board. A lump sum in hand, they said, was worth more to a divorced woman than a paper right to small monthly sums. They argued, third, that the structure of Section 4 was not an abandonment but an expression of the personal law's own distribution of responsibility, under which the maintenance of a woman who cannot maintain herself rests on her family in the order of her inheritance, supported by the community's endowed institutions, and that a legislature faithful to the personal law would reproduce that distribution rather than fasten every consequence of a divorce on one man for the rest of his life.

Those who opposed the statute argued, first, that it withdrew from one defined category of women, Muslim women divorced under Muslim law, a remedy that remained available to every other woman in India, and that a classification by religion producing a lesser entitlement for the women of one community was discrimination in substance whatever its form. The general provision was a welfare measure against destitution, they said, deliberately framed without regard to religion by the same Parliament that had enacted the personal laws, and to fence one group of women out of it was to make religion the measure of a citizen's protection against poverty. They argued, second, that the substitution was illusory in the cases where protection mattered most. A capital provision is only as good as the Magistrate's quantification of it and the husband's ability to pay it; the mahr in most marriages was a nominal sum recorded in the nikahnama, not a fund; the relatives on whom Section 4 fastened liability were drawn from the same stratum of poverty as the woman herself in the cases of real hardship; and the Wakf Board at the end of the sequence was an institution with its own straitened resources and no tradition of paying personal maintenance. On their account the Act replaced a continuing, adjustable obligation owed by the person whose act had ended the marriage with a one-time settlement and a chain of weaker obligors. They argued, third, in the language of constitutional politics, that the episode set a precedent about how a judicial decision protecting a minority woman's individual right could be undone when the organised voice of her community's leadership demanded it, and that the precedent, rather than any single section, was the lasting injury.

Between those two cases, which have not moved in forty years because they rest on different premises about the claims of community autonomy and individual equality, sits the enacted text and what the courts have done with it. The text, as the earlier sections of this article show, does less than the opposition feared and less than the support claimed in one crucial respect: it nowhere says that maintenance ends with the iddat period, and it nowhere says that the general provision is closed to a divorced Muslim woman. The courts' working out of that silence is the subject of the next section. What can be said here, neutrally, is that the controversy of 1986 was conducted almost entirely at the level of what the Act was for, and barely at all at the level of what its seven sections say; and that the subsequent legal history belongs to the sections.

## How the courts settled the Act: the High Court split and Danial Latifi

The question the text left open, whether the iddat period limits the substance of the Section 3 entitlement or only the time for its performance, went first to the High Courts, and the High Courts divided on it in terms that framed the issue for the Supreme Court. A Full Bench of the Gujarat High Court in Arab Ahemadhia Abdulla v. Arab Bail Mohmuna Saiyadbhai in 1988 took the expansive reading: the reasonable and fair provision that Section 3 requires the former husband to make within the iddat period is a provision for the divorced wife's future, extending beyond the iddat period itself, so that what must happen inside the period is the making of an arrangement adequate for the life she faces after it. A Full Bench of the Andhra Pradesh High Court in Usman Khan Bahamani v. Fathimunnisa Begum, decided in 1990, took the restrictive reading: it held that the husband's liability does not extend beyond the iddat period, that if the divorced woman is unable to maintain herself after that period the liability devolves on the relatives and the Wakf Board under Section 4, and that where no Section 5 declaration has been filed the provisions of Sections 125 to 128 of the Code have no application to her claim. Between those two Full Benches the whole field of dispute was marked out: the same five words, "to be made and paid within the iddat period", carried opposite conclusions about whether the Act was a bridge to a capital settlement for a woman's future or a terminus for her claims against the man who divorced her.

The constitutional challenge reached a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court in Danial Latifi v. Union of India, (2001) 7 SCC 740, decided on 28 September 2001 by a Bench of G.B. Pattanaik, S. Rajendra Babu, D.P. Mohapatra, Doraiswamy Raju and Shivaraj V. Patil JJ., with the judgment delivered by Rajendra Babu J. Danial Latifi had been counsel for Shah Bano in the 1985 case, and the writ petitions he and others brought attacked the Act under the equality and life guarantees of the Constitution, Articles 14, 15 and 21: the submission, in substance, was the opposition case stated above, that the Act singled out divorced Muslim women for a lesser regime and left them, after a few months, to the charity of relatives and a Wakf Board. The Court's response is the most instructive part of the judgment for any student of construction. It recorded that, read on their plain terms and at first impression, the Act's provisions appeared open to the constitutional challenge pressed against them; a statute that confined a divorced woman's support to the iddat period and then cast her on her relatives would have presented exactly the difficulties the petitioners described. But the Court applied the settled approach that where an enactment is open to two constructions, the one that sustains its validity is to be preferred to the one that destroys it, and it found that the saving construction was available on the text. The petitions were therefore dismissed and the Act upheld, but upheld as construed, which in this instance meant construed in a sense its 1986 opponents had said it could not bear and its 1986 supporters had not claimed for it.

The scale and age of the challenge deserve notice, because they shaped the decision's authority. The lead petition was Writ Petition (Civil) No. 868 of 1986, filed in the year of the Act itself, and by the time the Bench assembled, the cause list carried a large accumulated batch of writ petitions, transferred cases, criminal appeals and special leave petitions spanning the whole period from 1986 to the hearing, as the judgment's own header records. A challenge to the constitutional validity of a central statute of this prominence is heard by a Constitution Bench of five judges, as Article 145(3) of the Constitution requires for a case involving a substantial question of constitutional interpretation, and the fifteen years the batch spent awaiting that hearing were years in which the High Courts' conflicting readings, the Gujarat and Andhra Pradesh Full Benches among them, governed the Act's daily application in the districts. The 2001 judgment therefore arrived not as an early word on a new statute but as the long-deferred settlement of a mature conflict, and its reading-down technique must be judged with that in mind: the Court was choosing between two constructions each of which had been administered for years by High Courts of coordinate authority, and the construction it chose was the one under which the larger number of settled expectations, and the statute itself, could survive.

The route to that conclusion ran through the right to life as well as through equality. The petitioners' case, reflected in the authorities the Bench considered, which included the livelihood reasoning of Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation and the expanded Article 21 of Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, was that a statute which withdrew continuing support from a defined class of divorced women and remitted them to relatives and a Wakf Board attacked the conditions of a life with dignity, and not merely the formal equality of a classification. The Court did not need to strike the Act down on that ground, because the construction it adopted removed the premise: a Section 3 that obliges the former husband to fund his divorced wife's future, quantified within the iddat period but measured by her needs beyond it, does not abandon her to destitution at the period's end, and a Section 4 that then stands behind the settlement as a residual family and community guarantee reads as supplementation rather than substitution. The analytical point for the student of this statute is that the Article 21 argument and the textual argument were the same argument at different altitudes: both turned on whether "provision" in Section 3(1)(a) meant something real for the years after iddat, and the Court's answer to the small question carried the large one with it.

The construction itself follows the textual analysis set out in this article's Section 3 discussion, and the Court's conclusions, stated in its own summary, resolve into four propositions. First, a Muslim husband is liable to make a reasonable and fair provision for the future of the divorced wife, which obviously includes her maintenance as well, and such a provision extending beyond the iddat period must be made by him within the iddat period in terms of Section 3(1)(a). Second, the liability of a Muslim husband to his divorced wife under that clause to pay maintenance is not confined to the iddat period. Third, a divorced Muslim woman who has not remarried and is not able to maintain herself after the iddat period can proceed under Section 4 against the relatives liable to maintain her in proportion to the property they would inherit from her on her death according to Muslim law, including her children and parents, and if the relatives cannot pay, the Magistrate may direct the State Wakf Board to pay. Fourth, the provisions of the Act do not offend Articles 14, 15 and 21 of the Constitution. Read together, the first and second propositions convert Section 3 from a three-month maintenance clause into a capital settlement obligation measured by the woman's future needs, while the third preserves Section 4 as a genuine but residual tier. The Act that emerged from the construction is thus neither the Act of the 1986 opposition's account, which ended a woman's rights at the iddat boundary, nor quite the Act of its supporters' account, since the capital provision it guarantees her is quantified by the standard of the general maintenance law's own factors and extends across the future her supporters' rhetoric rarely priced.

The judgment has been described, in commentary from both directions, as an act of judicial reconciliation, and the description is defensible on either view of it. For its defenders, the Court did exactly what the doctrine of reading down exists to do: it took a statute whose literal sense invited invalidation, found in its words a sense that was both linguistically available and constitutionally sound, and preserved the legislature's enactment while securing the substance of the divorced woman's protection. For its critics, among them the petitioners who had sought the Act's destruction and commentators who shared their premises, the Court saved the statute by rewriting it: the Parliament of 1986, on the record of its own transitional provision and its own debates, had intended to end the general law's application to divorced Muslim women, and a construction that produced a generous capital obligation and left the Act's validity intact achieved by interpretation what the political branches had declined to achieve by amendment. Both descriptions agree on the operative facts, which are the facts that matter for practice: the Act stands, Section 3 means what the Constitution Bench said it means, and any account of the statute that stops at the iddat period is, since 2001, simply wrong.

## The construction at work after 2001

Between the Constitution Bench's decision and the decisions of 2010 and 2024 lies a decade in which the construction had to be operated by Magistrates and High Courts on real claims, and its working in that decade explains why the later Supreme Court decisions took the shape they did. Three features of the construction's early life stand out. The first is quantification. A Magistrate applying Section 3 after 2001 had to convert the standard of a woman's married life into a capital figure, and the reported orders show a recognisable method settling in: a monthly sum is first assessed as the maintenance the woman's needs and marital standard would support, that sum is then projected across the future the provision must cover, and the resulting capital amount is ordered as the provision, with the mahr and the properties dealt with as separate heads rather than as deductions from it. The method preserved the discipline of the monthly figure familiar from the general provision while honouring the Act's one-time form, and it produced orders of a wholly different magnitude from the three months of support the restrictive reading had contemplated. The second feature is the treatment of the mahr within the settlement. Consistently with the clause structure of Section 3(1) and with the approach of Bai Tahira a generation earlier, the mahr was treated as an independent debt falling due at the divorce, recoverable in the same proceeding, and not as a credit to be set against the provision: a husband could not answer a claim for a reasonable and fair provision by pointing to a dower fixed decades earlier at a figure the marriage had long outgrown. The third feature is the boundary the construction drew around Section 4. Because the Constitution Bench had located the former husband's real obligation inside Section 3 and measured it by the wife's future, Section 4 resumed the residual character its place in the Act suggested: a provision for the woman whom the settlement had genuinely failed, against relatives and the Board, and not the ordinary destination of an ordinary claim. Claims that in the restrictive years had been routed to relatives were, after 2001, routed back to the settlement where they belonged. The construction therefore did not merely raise the quantum of awards under the Act. It re-sorted the Act's own internal traffic, and by the time the Supreme Court returned to the subject in Shabana Bano, the proposition that the general provision had also survived could be presented, as the 2010 Bench presented it, as the natural completion of a structure the Constitution Bench had already built.

## The second question: the road back to Section 125

Danial Latifi settled the meaning of the 1986 Act. It did not, in terms, settle the separate question whether a divorced Muslim woman could simply proceed under Section 125 of the Code as though the Act were not her only map, because the petitions before the Constitution Bench attacked the Act's validity rather than presenting an individual claim under the Code. That question was answered in two further decisions. In Iqbal Bano v. State of U.P., (2007) 6 SCC 785, the Supreme Court held that proceedings under Section 125 are in the nature of civil proceedings, and that a Muslim woman's application under that section, filed while the marriage subsisted, does not become infructuous merely because the husband divorces her during its pendency; the beneficial character of the provision required its availability to be read generously rather than defeated by the timing of the divorce. In Shabana Bano v. Imran Khan, (2010) 1 SCC 666, a Bench of B. Sudershan Reddy and Deepak Verma JJ., in a judgment delivered by Deepak Verma J. on 4 December 2009, converted that bridge into a holding. Shabana Bano had applied under Section 125 before the Family Court at Gwalior; the husband pleaded the divorce and the 1986 Act; the courts below treated her claim through the Act and denied her the Code's continuing maintenance. The Supreme Court set those orders aside and held, in terms that admit no ambiguity, that even a divorced Muslim woman is entitled to claim maintenance from her former husband under Section 125 after the expiry of the iddat period also, as long as she does not remarry, and that the amount cannot be restricted to the iddat period only. The Court rested the conclusion on a cumulative reading of Danial Latifi and Iqbal Bano: if the Constitution Bench's construction meant that the husband's obligation under the personal statute itself reached beyond iddat, no coherent ground remained for holding the general welfare provision, enacted for all women, to stop at a boundary the special statute had been read as crossing.

The position was restated, comprehensively and at the level of a mapped taxonomy, in Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana on 10 July 2024, in separate concurring judgments of B.V. Nagarathna and Augustine George Masih JJ. The case was, in its facts, an ordinary one, which is part of its significance. The wife had obtained interim maintenance under Section 125 from the family court; the husband, who had pronounced talaq, contended before the Telangana High Court and then the Supreme Court that a divorced Muslim woman's remedy lay exclusively under the 1986 Act, and indeed that even a resort to the Code had to begin with an application of the kind contemplated by Section 5 of that Act; the High Court rejected the contention but reduced the interim figure, and the husband's appeal brought the whole architecture before the Court. The appeal was dismissed. The Court held that Section 125 applies to all married women, Muslim women included; that for a divorced Muslim woman the provisions of the 1986 Act are applicable alongside Section 125, the option lying with her to seek remedy under either law or under both, because the 1986 Act is not in derogation of Section 125 but in addition to it; that where Section 125 is also resorted to, any order passed under the 1986 Act is to be taken into consideration under Section 127(3)(b) of the Code, the accounting provision through which sums already received on divorce are credited; and that a woman divorced by a pronouncement that the 2019 statute makes illegal may take relief under that statute's subsistence provision or, at her option, under Section 125, including where the divorce arrives during the pendency of her Section 125 petition. Nagarathna J.'s judgment added the human register the subject had lacked in appellate prose for some years, recording that maintenance is not charity but a right, and that the homemaker's dependence within a marriage is a fact the law of maintenance exists to answer. Section 125 of the Code has since been re-enacted in substance as Section 144 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and the 2024 holding, delivered on the Code's text days after the new Sanhita commenced, states the position for the corresponding provision: the general route is open.

The two concurring judgments in Mohd. Abdul Samad reached the common conclusions by distinguishable roads, and the difference is instructive about how the settlement of this field was completed. Masih J.'s reasoning worked from the texts outward: the 1986 Act, on its own terms, is a statute for a divorced woman as it defines her, its entitlements under Section 3 are wider than maintenance alone, embracing the provision, the mahr and the properties, and nothing in it professes to repeal or override the Code's maintenance chapter for the woman who invokes it; the Act is therefore an addition to the Code's scheme, and the appropriate response to a husband's objection that his former wife had chosen the wrong statute was that the choice the law gives her is real. Nagarathna J.'s reasoning worked from the constitutional register inward: Section 125 is a social welfare provision rooted in the State's obligation to prevent destitution, indifferent to religion because destitution is indifferent to religion, and a construction of the 1986 Act that fenced Muslim women out of it would reproduce, under a new label, the inequality the Constitution Bench in Danial Latifi had been at pains to construe the Act away from. Both roads converge on the proposition that organises the field, and the convergence of a textual and a constitutional route on the same result is what gives the 2024 restatement its settling force: a future Bench asked to reopen the question would have to disturb not one line of reasoning but two independent ones, each sufficient for the conclusion.

One further decision belongs to the settlement's completion, because it organised the practical coexistence the 2024 case then mapped. In Rajnesh v. Neha, (2021) 2 SCC 324, the Supreme Court, confronted with the proliferation of simultaneous maintenance claims by the same parties under the general provision, the domestic violence statute and the personal laws, laid down guidelines for the whole field: parties to maintenance proceedings must disclose their assets and liabilities on affidavit, a claimant must disclose any previous maintenance proceedings and orders, and amounts paid under one statute are to be adjusted against amounts awarded under another, so that the overlap of remedies produces coordination rather than double recovery. The guidelines did not concern the Act of 1986 directly, but they supplied the administrative grammar for the cumulative world that the decisions on the Act had created: once a divorced Muslim woman may proceed under either statute or both, some machinery must exist to prevent the same need being paid for twice and to let each forum know what the other has done, and Rajnesh v. Neha is that machinery. Read with Section 127(3)(b) of the Code and the accounting direction in Mohd. Abdul Samad, it completes the picture of a field that moved, over four decades, from an assumed election between two hostile statutes to a coordinated stack of remedies under judicial management.

### Does the 1986 Act apply to a woman married under the Special Marriage Act?

No. The Act reaches a woman married according to Muslim law and divorced in accordance with Muslim law. A marriage under the Special Marriage Act, 1954 is a civil marriage under a secular statute, and a woman divorced from it takes her remedies under that Act and under the general maintenance provision, which the 2024 decision confirms applies to her.

That taxonomy is the practical yield of the 2024 decision, and it is worth stating as the decision states it, because it organises every later question in this field. Section 125 applies to all married women, including Muslim married women. It applies to all non-Muslim divorced women. For divorced Muslim women, the answer forks on the law of the marriage: women married and divorced under the Special Marriage Act take Section 125 in addition to that Act's remedies; women married and divorced under Muslim law take Section 125 and the 1986 Act together, at their choice, either or both. The fork vindicates, from an unexpected direction, the care taken in Section 2 of the 1986 Act with the definition of a divorced woman: the statute's boundary is the boundary of the personal law itself, and the general provision flows around it on both sides. A comparison of [the personal and secular routes through marriage law](/2017/01/15/personal-vs-secular-marriage-law-india/) takes that fork as its starting point, and the reader who wants the full map of what changes when a couple marries under the civil statute rather than the personal law should follow it there.

## A Section 3 application worked through, and the Code's route beside it

The provisions acquire their practical meaning when they are run, in sequence, on a representative set of facts. Take, purely as an illustration of the machinery, a marriage of long duration ended by the husband's pronouncement of divorce: the wife has no independent earnings, the nikahnama records a mahr that was fixed at a modest figure many years earlier and never paid, gifts of jewellery and household property from both families remain in the matrimonial home, and two young children are with her. On the divorce, Section 3(1) speaks immediately, without any application being filed: the former husband is to make and pay, within the iddat period, a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance for her, provision and maintenance for the children she maintains for two years from their respective births, the mahr, and delivery of the properties. In the ordinary case nothing of the kind happens voluntarily in full, and the woman, or a person she duly authorises, applies under Section 3(2) to the Magistrate of the first class for the area where she resides. At the first hearing, Section 5 has its moment: either party, by the prescribed declaration, may state the preference for the Code's provisions, and if the declaration is filed the Magistrate disposes of the application accordingly; if it is not, the application proceeds under the Act. The Magistrate then examines the three matters Section 3(3) specifies: the husband's means, his failure to make the provision, and the quantum, which is fixed by her needs, the standard of life she enjoyed during the marriage, and his means. The order is to come within a month of filing, with recorded reasons if it cannot. If the husband does not pay, Section 3(4) supplies the warrant and, after he is heard, imprisonment of up to a year against the unpaid sum.

The illustration shows why the post-2001 construction is not an abstraction. On the restrictive reading, the figure the Magistrate would fix in such a case is bounded by the needs of roughly three months, and the mahr and the returned jewellery would be the substance of the woman's settlement after a long marriage. On the construction Danial Latifi adopted, the same three factors produce a capital sum: the Magistrate estimates the monthly support the woman's needs and marital standard imply, projects it across the future the provision must cover, and orders that capitalised provision, together with the children's clause, the mahr and the properties, as one settlement payable within the period. The reported decisions after 2001 show Magistrates and High Courts quantifying the provision in exactly that way, reasoning from a monthly figure to a lump sum, sometimes with the arithmetic exposed in the order. The method has its own discipline problems, since a capital award cannot be revisited if it proves inadequate, where a monthly allowance can be altered on a change of circumstances; that asymmetry is a real feature of the Act's route, and it belongs in any honest comparison of the two.

The Code's route on the same facts runs differently at every joint. The woman applies under Section 125, in the district where the husband is, where she resides, or where he last resided with her, under Section 126; where a family court is established for the area, it is the family court that in practice entertains the application, as it did in Shabana Bano itself. She must show that the respondent, having sufficient means, neglects or refuses to maintain her and that she is unable to maintain herself; the Explanation to Section 125 makes her, as a divorced woman who has not remarried, a "wife" for the section's purposes. While the case proceeds she may be awarded interim maintenance and the expenses of the proceeding, with the interim application directed to be disposed of, as far as possible, within sixty days of service of notice. The final order is a monthly allowance at the rate the Magistrate thinks fit, the original statutory ceiling of five hundred rupees having been removed by the Code of Criminal Procedure (Amendment) Act, 2001, together with the power in Section 127 to alter the allowance on proof of changed circumstances, and the cancellation rule in Section 127(3)(b) under which, after the 2024 decision, what she has received under the 1986 Act is brought into account. Default is backed by Section 125(3): a warrant to levy the amount as a fine, and imprisonment of up to one month for each month of unpaid allowance. The accounting between the two routes, which the 2024 decision regularised, deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is the point at which the cumulative system could have collapsed into double payment. Section 127(3)(b) of the Code directs a Magistrate to cancel a maintenance order where the divorced woman has received the whole of the sum payable to her on divorce under the personal or customary law of the parties; in the pre-1986 litigation that clause was the husband's standard answer, and Bai Tahira and Fuzlunbi had confined it to sums that were genuinely a substitute for maintenance. After 2001 a new question arose in the opposite direction: if the woman had recovered a capital provision under the 1986 Act as construed in Danial Latifi, did that receipt cancel or reduce what the Code would otherwise give her? Mohd. Abdul Samad's answer is measured and is stated in its conclusions: where Section 125 is also resorted to by a divorced Muslim woman, any order passed under the 1986 Act shall be taken into consideration under Section 127(3)(b). The direction converts the old cancellation clause into an accounting rule. What she has actually received under the Act, the provision, the mahr and the value of the properties, is credited in the Code proceeding, so that the two statutes together answer her need once, through whichever combination of capital and monthly payments the two forums produce. The contrast with Section 127(1), under which a Code allowance may be altered at any time on proof of a change in circumstances, completes the asymmetry noted earlier: the Code's stream can be re-measured as life changes, the Act's settlement cannot, and the accounting rule is the joint through which a later, alterable order absorbs an earlier, final one.

Neither route requires her to elect between them at the threshold after 2024; the illustration simply shows that they are different instruments, one producing a capitalised settlement through a borrowed summary court, the other a continuing, adjustable monthly order through the same court wearing its Code jurisdiction.

A maintenance claim under either route is, in the end, a documentary exercise, and the point is practical enough to record in a pillar that practitioners will read. The woman's case rests on papers: the nikahnama that records the marriage and the mahr, the proof of the divorce and its date, from which the iddat window and every deadline in the Act is counted, the evidence of the husband's means, which decides quantum under both statutes, and the record of what was paid or delivered at the divorce, which Section 127(3)(b) of the Code brings into account after 2024. Those papers accumulate across years and across hands, in scanned orders, bank statements, income spreadsheets and correspondence, and keeping them ordered is half the litigation. A reader assembling such a file, or an adviser reviewing one assembled by a client, will commonly need to move between documents, spreadsheets and presentations in a single sitting, and can [open the office files of a maintenance case in one browser window with the ReportMedic office file viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) rather than chase separate applications for each format. The discipline the file imposes is the discipline of the statutes themselves: everything in this field turns on dates, documents and the order in which they were made.

One procedural silence deserves recording before the comparison is drawn up in the artifact table. The 1986 Act borrows the Magistrate from the Code but does not say, in terms, what happens to its applications where the State has established a family court for the area, and the reported decisions have had to work out whether the establishment of a family court diverts a Section 3 application from the Magistrate whom Section 2 names. The general maintenance provision, by contrast, is expressly within the family court's statutory list through Section 7 of the Family Courts Act, 1984, which covers proceedings under Chapter IX of the Code. A claimant choosing between the routes in a district with a family court is therefore choosing between a forum whose title to hear her is textually plain and one whose title has had to be litigated, a small point of practice that never appears in the political literature about this statute and that usefully illustrates how much of its real life is procedural rather than ideological.

## The later statute on the same shelf, and the remedy that belongs to every woman

The Act of 1986 acquired, in 2019, a companion whose similar name causes persistent confusion between the two, and a pillar account of the earlier statute should separate them cleanly. The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019, Act 20 of 2019, assented to on 31 July 2019 and deemed to have come into force on 19 September 2018, followed the Constitution Bench decision in Shayara Bano v. Union of India, (2017) 9 SCC 1, in which a majority of three to two set aside the practice of talaq-e-biddat, instant triple talaq. Where the 1986 Act assumes a completed divorce and regulates its economic aftermath, [the Triple Talaq Act 2019](/2016/12/15/triple-talaq-act-2019-explained/) attacks one mode of pronouncing divorce itself: its Section 3 declares a pronouncement of talaq by a Muslim husband, in any form, to be void and illegal, and its Section 4 makes the pronouncement an offence punishable with imprisonment extending to three years and a fine. For the woman on whom such a pronouncement is made, the 2019 Act provides in Section 5 an entitlement to a subsistence allowance for herself and her dependent children, in such amount as the Magistrate determines, and in Section 6 a right to the custody of her minor children as the Magistrate determines. The 2024 decision in Mohd. Abdul Samad knitted the two statutes together at exactly this joint: a woman divorced by a pronouncement the 2019 Act makes illegal may take the subsistence allowance under that Act or proceed under the general maintenance provision at her option, and if her Section 125 petition is pending when the divorce is pronounced she may continue it or take recourse under the 2019 Act. The two Muslim Women Acts are therefore not successive versions of one law. The Act of 1986 winds up a divorce that Muslim law recognises; the Act of 2019 denies effect to one form of divorce and supports the woman whose marriage it treats as subsisting. Confusing them produces errors in both directions, attributing the criminal provisions of the later Act to the earlier, and the capital settlement of the earlier to the later.

### How is the 1986 Act different from the Triple Talaq Act 2019?

The two Acts answer different questions. The 1986 Act assumes a divorce effected under Muslim law and regulates consequences: provision, maintenance, mahr and property from the former husband. The 2019 Act declares instant triple talaq void and illegal, makes pronouncing it an offence, and gives the woman a subsistence allowance and custody orders while the marriage is treated as subsisting.

Alongside both statutes stands a remedy that belongs to no personal law at all. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005, Act 43 of 2005, in force from 26 October 2006, protects a woman in a domestic relationship, which includes a relationship in the nature of marriage and a marital relationship in a shared household, and it gives her rights that neither maintenance statute contains: a right to reside in the shared household under Section 17, protection orders under Section 18, residence orders under Section 19, and monetary reliefs under Section 20, which extend to loss of earnings, medical expenses, loss from the destruction of property, and maintenance for the aggrieved person and her children, including an order under or in addition to an order of maintenance under Section 125 of the Code. Section 26 allows those reliefs to be sought within other pending legal proceedings, and Section 36 declares that the Act is in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law. For a divorced Muslim woman the practical consequence is that [the further remedy available to any woman in a domestic relationship](/2015/12/01/domestic-violence-act-2005-explained/) may answer the need that the 1986 Act does not address at all, above all housing: the Act of 1986 contains no residence right and no protection jurisdiction, and a woman whose urgent problem is that she has nowhere to live is asking a question the maintenance statutes were never built to answer. The remedies accumulate rather than elect between one another, because each statute says so in its own way: the 2005 Act by its express non-derogation clause, the 1986 Act by the construction that placed it in addition to the general provision, and the general provision by the 2024 holding that the choice among them belongs to the woman.

## What the Act does not do

A pillar account owes the reader the statute's boundaries as plainly as its contents, because in this field the expensive errors are usually jurisdictional: a claimant asks the Act for something it never promised, or a commentator condemns it for failing at a task it never undertook. The Act does not regulate divorce. It prescribes no grounds, no procedure, no waiting period and no reconciliation machinery; the divorce it presupposes is effected entirely under Muslim law, and a woman who wants the divorce itself, rather than its consequences, goes to the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act of 1939 and the courts that administer it. The Act does not govern maintenance during marriage: a wife neglected while the marriage subsists claims under the personal law and under Section 125, and the 1986 Act is simply not addressed to her until there is a divorce to wind up. It contains no custody jurisdiction beyond the children's maintenance clause in Section 3(1)(b), and it awards no residence, no protection order and no compensation for violence; those absences are filled, where they are filled, by the domestic violence statute and the general law of custody. It fixes no amounts: beyond the mahr, which it borrows from the marriage contract, every figure in it is the product of a Magistrate's assessment on stated factors. And it does not extinguish anything else the woman may have: her title to her own property, her remedies for the marriage period, and after the decisions of 2010 and 2024 her access to the general maintenance provision all survive it. Three further boundaries complete the map. The Act is confined to divorce: it says nothing to the widow, whose position after her husband's death is governed by her inheritance, by the dower debt she may retain his property against until it is paid, and by her claims on her own children and parents under the general law, a wholly different configuration that the Act's machinery does not touch. It does not abolish the ordinary civil remedies that run alongside its summary ones: the mahr is a debt, and it was recoverable by a civil suit before 1986 and remains so, the Act's proceeding being an addition of a faster forum rather than the substitution of the only one, a pattern the Supreme Court's treatment of the general provision later generalised across the whole field. And it is not temporally fenced in its own terms: apart from the transitional Section 7, which dealt with the applications pending at its commencement, the Act contains no clause confining its entitlements to divorces pronounced after 19 May 1986, and its definitions speak of a divorced woman without qualification of date. The statute is a winding-up code for the economics of a Muslim divorce, seven sections long, and it rewards being read as exactly that and nothing more.

## The 1986 Act among the personal law settlements

Before the comparison with the general provision is drawn, a shorter comparison inside the personal laws fixes the Act's distinctiveness, because every other personal statute in India answers the same question, what a husband owes when a marriage ends, in the opposite form. The Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956 gives a Hindu wife a right to be maintained by her husband during her lifetime and, in defined circumstances, to live separately without forfeiting that right, and it extends a maintenance claim to the widowed daughter-in-law against her father-in-law's estate; the obligation is periodical, supervised by the civil courts, and measured by the parties' position and needs. The Indian Divorce Act, 1869 gives the Christian wife alimony pending the suit and permanent alimony on its conclusion, again periodical, again continuing, again revisable in the court's hands. The Parsi Marriage and Divorce Act, 1936 follows the same pattern of interim and permanent alimony within its own matrimonial courts. In each of those systems the post-divorce obligation is a stream, not a settlement: the former husband remains the obligor, the payments recur, and the court's file stays open to variation as means and needs change. The Act of 1986 is the sole member of the family that converts the obligation at the divorce into a capital event, completes it inside a waiting period borrowed from the personal law, and then reassigns the future, first to the woman's heirs presumptive and finally to a religious endowment board. That is why arguments about the Act so often pass each other by. Its defenders were comparing it, favourably, with a five-hundred-rupee monthly allowance in an unreformed Code; its critics were comparing it, unfavourably, with the continuing alimony streams of the neighbouring personal laws. Both comparisons are available in the record, and the Act looks like a different statute depending on which shelf it is placed on. The 2001 construction, which made the capital settlement answerable to the woman's future needs, can be understood as the judiciary's way of placing it on the second shelf while keeping it in the form Parliament chose.

## Two routes to maintenance

The comparison the brief for this article requires is ready to be drawn, and it is drawn in the table that follows, the findable artifact of this pillar. Two cautions frame it. The first is that the table compares instruments, not outcomes: a route that looks weaker line by line may produce the larger recovery on particular facts, because the 1986 Act's capital provision has no analogue in the general provision and the general provision's monthly, alterable allowance has no analogue in the Act. The second is that the general position described here is a map of the statutes and the settled decisions, and does not substitute for advice on an individual case, where limitation, forum and the state of the record are unforgiving. With those cautions recorded, the routes stand as follows.

| Question | The 1986 Act | Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 |
|---|---|---|
| Who may apply | A divorced woman, or anyone duly authorised by her on her behalf | A wife unable to maintain herself; "wife" includes a divorced woman who has not remarried |
| Against whom | The former husband under Section 3; after iddat, her relatives in inheritance proportions, then the State Wakf Board, under Section 4 | The husband or former husband with sufficient means who neglects or refuses to maintain her |
| Period covered | Provision and maintenance made and paid within the iddat period, the provision extending to her future needs beyond it as construed in 2001; Section 4 maintenance after iddat while she cannot maintain herself and has not remarried | Monthly maintenance from the order until remarriage, cancellation or alteration under Sections 125 and 127 |
| Form of the order | A single settlement: capital provision and maintenance, children's provision for two years from birth, the mahr, and delivery of her properties | A monthly allowance, with interim maintenance and litigation expenses available during the proceeding |
| Forum | A Magistrate of the first class for the area where the divorced woman resides | A Magistrate of the first class under Section 126 of the Code; in practice the family court where one is established |
| Time it takes | The statute directs an order within one month of filing, with reasons recorded if it takes longer | No outer limit for the final order; the interim application is to be disposed of, as far as possible, within sixty days of service of notice |
| Enforcement | Warrant to levy the amount as a fine, and imprisonment extending to one year or until payment if sooner made | Warrant to levy the amount as a fine, and imprisonment extending to one month for each month of default |

Read as a decision rather than as a diagram, the table yields different first moves for different claimants, and the brief's reader payloads can be answered directly. A woman whose former husband has realisable assets and whose need is to close the marriage's accounts and be independent of monthly enforcement will often be better served by the Act's capital settlement, mahr and property in one proceeding, accepting that the sum cannot later be revised upward. A woman whose need is continuing support, whose former husband's means may improve, or whose case needs money during the litigation itself will usually begin under the general provision, where interim relief exists and the allowance can be altered on changed circumstances. An advocate will notice that the two filings are not alternatives to be weighed once and abandoned: after 2024 they may be pursued together, with the 1986 Act's recovery credited in the Code's accounting, and the sequencing of the two is a tactical question about forums, speed and the husband's asset position rather than a jurisdictional gamble. A student or a journalist needs the table's last row before all the others, because it is the row the standard summary omits. One cost of litigation deserves a line of its own, since it decides real cases at the margin. Neither route gives the claimant a civil costs regime, and the 1986 Act says nothing about the expenses of the proceeding at all; the general provision alone, since its 2001 amendment, lets the Magistrate order the expenses of the proceeding as part of the interim relief, a small but practical advantage for a claimant litigating, by definition, from a position of need.

## The honest assessment, and the verdict

### Has Parliament amended or repealed the 1986 Act since it was passed?

No. The Act stands as enacted in 1986, seven sections long, unamended and unrepealed. Everything that has changed about this law has changed by construction rather than legislation: the Constitution Bench's reading of Section 3 in 2001, the restoration of the general provision in 2010, and the mapping of the two cumulative routes in 2024.

That stillness is itself the first item of the assessment. This series has repeatedly found that a statute's operative meaning was settled by construction rather than by the text Parliament enacted, and it has found constructions running in both directions: provisions read down to save them, provisions read up to fulfil them, and, in the land acquisition lapse provision examined elsewhere in this series, a construction that widened a statute's effect well beyond the case its drafters had principally in view. The Act of 1986 is the clearest instance in the series of construction running against the grain of the enactment's apparent purpose. The legislature of 1986 legislated, on the evidence of its own transitional provision, to move a class of claimants out of a general code and into a special one; the courts, over twenty-three years and three decisions, produced a position in which the special statute survives, is upheld, means something generous, and displaces nothing. Whether that sequence is celebrated as the constitution working through the courts or regretted as the legislature's will being negotiated away depends on the premises the observer brought to 1986, and this article has undertaken to adopt neither set. What is not open to dispute is the mechanism, and the mechanism is a standing lesson in how Indian statutes actually acquire their meaning: the text proposes, in unusually compressed language; the first construction arrives within two years in a High Court; the conflict of constructions forces the constitutional question; the constitutional court saves the text by settling its sense; and the settled sense, not the parliamentary intention reconstructed from the debates, is what the next generation of Magistrates applies.

On the statute's own working, the assessment divides by section. Section 3, as construed, is a functional settlement provision whose capital logic suits the cases where the husband has means and the marriage's accounts can genuinely be closed; its weaknesses are the absence of interim relief, the irreversibility of a capital quantification, and the dependence of everything on a summary court's assessment of means in a one-month frame the proviso immediately softens. Section 4 is the design's weakest member in practice, not because its sequencing is unintelligent but because its obligors are, in the run of cases, drawn from the same economy as the claimant, and because its final guarantor, the Wakf Board, is directed to pay sums it was never funded to carry as a maintenance service; the section's near-absence from the reported case law is the record of that weakness. Section 5 is a mechanism overtaken by the decisions: designed as a bridge, argued for a generation as a gate, it survives as a conversion facility inside pending proceedings that the direct availability of the general provision has made largely unnecessary. Section 7 is spent. The Act as a whole therefore does its real work in one section of seven, and does it, since 2001, in a sense that neither its movers nor its opponents described in 1986. A reader who wants the political story of how that improbable outcome was produced should go to the article that owns the sequence of judgment and response; a reader who wants the field's remaining open controversy, the demand for a uniform civil code under Article 44, should go to the debate article in this cluster; a reader who wants the neighbouring statute of 2019 or the personal and secular comparison has been pointed to both above. A final assessment concerns the reporting and teaching of the Act, because the brief for this article identifies the state of the secondary literature as part of the problem the pillar exists to correct. The three errors that recur across textbooks, coaching material and news commentary are the three this article has addressed in sequence: that maintenance under the Act ends with the iddat period, which has been false as a statement of the law since 2001; that the Act ousted the general maintenance provision for divorced Muslim women, which has been false since 2010 and comprehensively mapped as false since 2024; and that the Act stands alone in the field, which omits the children's independent claim, the domestic violence statute's additional remedies and the accounting rules that coordinate overlapping awards. Each error is a fossil of the 1986 controversy, preserved by the habit of writing about the statute from its politics rather than from its text and its constructions. An examination candidate who reproduces them loses the marks the question was designed to give for the construction; a journalist who reproduces them misinforms precisely the readers, divorced women and their families, for whom the true position is worth money. The corrective is unglamorous and complete: seven sections, three decisions, two cumulative routes.

The verdict of this pillar is the namable claim with which it began, stated at the outset and tested through the sections since: the statute says less than everyone assumes. It never confined maintenance to the iddat period in terms; it never closed the general provision in terms; and the law of divorced Muslim women's maintenance in India is the law of two cumulative statutes and three Supreme Court decisions, of which the Act of 1986, accurately read, is only the first element.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Muslim Women Divorce Act 1986 provide?

The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986, Act 25 of 1986, is a seven-section statute that regulates the economic consequences of a divorce under Muslim law. Section 3 entitles a divorced woman, from her former husband and within the iddat period, to a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance, to provision and maintenance for the children she maintains for two years from their births, to her mahr or dower, and to the delivery of all properties given to her at or around the marriage. Section 4 provides for her maintenance after the iddat period by her relatives, and failing them by the State Wakf Board, if she has not remarried and cannot maintain herself. Section 5 lets the parties opt for the general maintenance provision, and Section 7 dealt with applications pending at commencement.

### Q: What is the iddat period and why does it matter?

Iddat is the waiting period that follows the dissolution of a Muslim marriage, and Section 2 of the Act defines it in three branches: three menstrual courses after the divorce where the woman is subject to menstruation, three lunar months where she is not, and, where she is enceinte at the divorce, the period until the delivery of the child or the termination of the pregnancy, whichever is earlier. It matters for two reasons. Section 3 times the former husband's obligations by it: the provision, maintenance, mahr and properties must be made, paid or delivered within the iddat period. And Section 4 is keyed to its expiry, because the liability of relatives and of the Wakf Board arises only where the woman cannot maintain herself after the iddat period has run.

### Q: Who is liable if a divorced woman cannot maintain herself?

The Act answers in sequence. The first liability is the former husband's, under Section 3: he must make and pay a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance within the iddat period, quantified by her needs, the standard of life she enjoyed during the marriage and his means, and construed since 2001 as a provision for her future beyond the iddat period. If, after the iddat period, she has not remarried and is still unable to maintain herself, Section 4 makes her relatives liable: those who would inherit her property on her death under Muslim law, in the proportions in which they would inherit, with her children liable first and her parents next if the children cannot pay. If she has no such relatives, or they lack the means, the Magistrate may direct the State Wakf Board for the area where she resides to pay the maintenance.

### Q: Did the 1986 Act take away the right to maintenance?

That was the political understanding of 1986, on both sides of the debate, but it is not what the courts have held the Act to do. The Act nowhere states that maintenance ends with the iddat period. In Danial Latifi in 2001 the Supreme Court upheld the Act by construing the Section 3 obligation to make a reasonable and fair provision as extending to the divorced wife's future beyond the iddat period, the provision being made and paid within it. In Shabana Bano in 2010 the Court held that a divorced Muslim woman may claim maintenance under the general provision of the criminal procedure code after the iddat period, as long as she does not remarry. In Mohd. Abdul Samad in 2024 the Court confirmed that the 1986 Act operates in addition to the general provision, not in derogation of it, and that the choice of remedy belongs to the woman.

### Q: What did Danial Latifi decide about the 1986 Act?

Danial Latifi v. Union of India, (2001) 7 SCC 740, decided on 28 September 2001 by a Constitution Bench of five judges, upheld the constitutional validity of the Act while construing it. The Court recorded that a literal reading confined to the iddat period would have exposed the Act to the challenge under Articles 14, 15 and 21, and adopted the construction that sustained validity. Its conclusions were that a husband is liable to make a reasonable and fair provision for his divorced wife's future, including her maintenance, extending beyond the iddat period though made within it; that his liability to pay maintenance is not confined to the iddat period; that a woman unable to maintain herself after iddat may proceed under Section 4 against her relatives and, failing them, the Wakf Board; and that the Act offends no provision of the Constitution.

### Q: Can a divorced Muslim woman claim under the general maintenance provision?

Yes. The general provision, Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, whose corresponding provision in the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 is Section 144, applies to a divorced Muslim woman in addition to her remedies under the 1986 Act. The Supreme Court held in Shabana Bano v. Imran Khan, (2010) 1 SCC 666, that her petition under Section 125 is maintainable after the iddat period for as long as she does not remarry, and confirmed in Mohd. Abdul Samad v. State of Telangana in 2024 that the 1986 Act is not in derogation of Section 125 but in addition to it, so that she may proceed under either statute or both. Where both are used, what she has received under the 1986 Act is brought into account under Section 127(3)(b) of the Code.

### Q: What is mahr and how does it count?

Mahr, or dower, is the sum of money or property that a husband is bound to pay or deliver to his wife in consideration of the marriage, fixed by the marriage contract or implied by law where the contract is silent. It is classified as prompt, payable on demand during the marriage, or deferred, payable when the marriage ends by divorce or death. It is the wife's exclusive property and a debt owed to her. Under Section 3(1)(c) of the 1986 Act she is entitled, at the divorce, to an amount equal to the mahr agreed at the marriage or thereafter, recoverable in the same summary proceeding as her provision and maintenance. It counts as one head of the settlement, not as a substitute for the rest: the Supreme Court held in Bai Tahira in 1979, and the structure of Section 3 confirms, that paying the mahr does not by itself discharge the husband's maintenance obligations.

### Q: Can parties opt out of the Muslim Women Divorce Act?

Section 5 of the Act provides the option. If, on the date of the first hearing of an application under Section 3(2), the divorced woman and her former husband declare, by affidavit or any other declaration in writing in the prescribed form, which the Rules of 1986 supply as Form B, either jointly or separately, that they would prefer to be governed by Sections 125 to 128 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, the Magistrate shall dispose of the application accordingly, under the Code's provisions rather than the Act's. The option is thus exercised inside a pending Section 3 proceeding, at its first hearing, and each party may declare separately; a joint filing is not required. Since the decisions of 2010 and 2024, the option is no longer the only door to the general provision, because a divorced Muslim woman may apply under Section 125 directly.

### Q: Where must a divorced Muslim woman file her application under the 1986 Act?

She applies to a Magistrate of the first class exercising jurisdiction under the Code of Criminal Procedure in the area where she resides. Section 2 of the Act defines "Magistrate" in those terms, so the forum follows her residence rather than the place of the marriage, the place of the divorce or the husband's residence. The application is made under Section 3(2), where the provision and maintenance, the mahr or dower, or the properties due to her have not been made, paid or delivered on her divorce. She need not file it personally: the same sub-section allows anyone duly authorised by her to make the application on her behalf, a facility designed for a claimant who cannot readily attend the court herself.

### Q: How quickly must the Magistrate decide a Section 3 application?

Section 3(3) directs the Magistrate to make the order within one month of the date on which the application is filed. That is a direction for the final order, not merely for interim relief, and it is stricter on its face than the timelines of the general maintenance provision. The direction carries its own qualification: a proviso allows the Magistrate who finds it impracticable to dispose of the application within the month to dispose of it after that period, provided the reasons are recorded. In practice the proviso is frequently used and contested applications take longer, but the one-month rule remains the statutory benchmark against which delay in a Section 3 proceeding is measured, and a claimant is entitled to press it on the court.

### Q: What can the Magistrate do if the former husband does not pay what Section 3 orders?

Section 3(4) supplies the enforcement machinery. If a person against whom an order has been made fails without sufficient cause to comply with it, the Magistrate may issue a warrant for levying the amount of maintenance, mahr or dower due in the manner provided for levying fines under the Code of Criminal Procedure. If any amount remains unpaid after the warrant is executed, the Magistrate may sentence him to imprisonment for the whole or the unpaid part, for a term that may extend to one year or until payment is sooner made. Two safeguards accompany the power: the defaulter must be heard in his defence, and the sentence must be imposed according to the provisions of the Code. The one-year ceiling makes the Act's enforcement, on this point, more coercive than the general provision's one month of imprisonment for each month of default.

### Q: Does the 1986 Act provide anything for the children of the marriage?

Yes, within a defined limit. Section 3(1)(b) entitles a divorced woman who herself maintains the children born to her before or after the divorce to a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance for them from her former husband, for a period of two years from the respective dates of the children's births. The measure runs from birth, child by child, so it covers the children's earliest years and may already be spent for an older child. The clause is not the children's whole entitlement. Children have their own independent claim under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and the Supreme Court held in Noor Saba Khatoon v. Mohd. Quasim in 1997 that a father's obligation to maintain his minor children under that section is not cut down by the Act's two-year clause, so the children's general-law claim continues until majority.

### Q: What happened to maintenance applications that were already pending when the 1986 Act commenced?

Section 7, the transitional provision, answered that question at the time. Every application by a divorced woman under Section 125 or Section 127 of the Code of Criminal Procedure that was pending before a Magistrate on the commencement of the Act was to be disposed of by that Magistrate in accordance with the provisions of the 1986 Act, notwithstanding anything in the Code itself. The one qualification was Section 5: if the parties filed the prescribed declaration at the first hearing preferring to be governed by Sections 125 to 128 of the Code, the Magistrate was to dispose of the application under the Code instead. The provision shows what Parliament in 1986 assumed it was doing, moving an entire class of pending claims from the general provision into the new statute, and it is spent history for any claim filed long after the Act commenced.

### Q: What is the difference between a provision and maintenance in Section 3?

The two words do different work, and the whole construction of the Act turns on the difference. Maintenance is periodical support: sums paid across time to meet recurring needs, of the kind the general provision awards as a monthly allowance. A provision is something made for the future: an arrangement, ordinarily a capital sum, settled at one point in time so that the person provided for can live from it afterwards. Section 3(1)(a) requires both a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance, and requires both to be made and paid within the iddat period. If the clause meant only three months of support, the word "provision" would add nothing to "maintenance". In Danial Latifi the Supreme Court gave the word its forward-looking sense, holding that the provision must answer the divorced wife's needs beyond the iddat period, though it is made and paid within it.

### Q: Can a divorced Muslim woman also claim relief under the Domestic Violence Act 2005?

Yes. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 protects a woman in a domestic relationship, including a marital relationship in a shared household, and Section 36 of that Act declares that its provisions are in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law. Its monetary reliefs under Section 20 include maintenance for the woman and her children, expressly including an order under or in addition to an order under Section 125 of the Code, and Section 26 allows its reliefs to be sought within other pending proceedings. It also supplies what the 1986 Act does not contain at all: protection orders and residence orders in the shared household. The remedies therefore accumulate with her claims under the 1986 Act and the general maintenance provision rather than competing with them, and after the 2024 decision the choice and combination of routes rest with her.

### Q: Does remarriage change what a divorced woman can claim under the 1986 Act?

It changes some claims and not others, and the line follows the structure of the Act. The Section 3 settlement crystallises at the divorce: the provision, the mahr and the properties are entitlements that fall due within the iddat period, and a provision once made and paid is her capital, not a payment conditional on her remaining unmarried. Section 4 is different in terms: its liability arises only where the Magistrate is satisfied that the divorced woman has not remarried, so a woman who remarries cannot set that machinery in motion afterwards. The general provision takes the same line as Section 4: a divorced woman counts as a "wife" under Section 125 only if she has not remarried, the Supreme Court's formulation in Shabana Bano is that the claim continues as long as she does not remarry, and the Code requires an existing order to be cancelled from the date of her remarriage.

### Q: What property must be handed back to a divorced woman under Section 3?

Section 3(1)(d) entitles her to all the properties given to her before or at the time of marriage or after her marriage by her relatives or friends, or by the husband, or by any relatives of the husband or his friends. The clause collects the gifts that accumulate around a marriage from both sides of the family, jewellery, money, clothing, household goods and other property given to her, and makes their delivery to her an entitlement of the divorce settlement rather than a matter for a separate civil suit about title. Where the properties have not been delivered on the divorce, she may apply under Section 3(2), and the Magistrate's order under Section 3(3) may direct their delivery, backed by the enforcement machinery of Section 3(4). The property clause is distinct from the mahr: the mahr is the dower debt under the marriage contract, while the properties are the gifts that were hers already.

### Q: Can a woman who obtained the divorce herself claim under the 1986 Act?

Yes. Section 2 of the Act defines a divorced woman as a Muslim woman who was married according to Muslim law and has been divorced by, or has obtained divorce from, her husband in accordance with Muslim law. Both limbs are covered expressly. A woman who obtained the dissolution herself, whether by khula, by mubaraat, or by a decree of the court under the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939, on grounds such as her husband's failure to maintain her for two years or his cruelty, stands inside the definition exactly as a woman divorced by her husband's pronouncement does. Her Section 3 entitlements to a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance, the children's clause, the mahr as the contract and the personal law determine it, and the return of her properties, are therefore available to her on the same terms, and the identity of the party who initiated the divorce is not a ground for refusing them.

### Q: Does the 1986 Act give a divorced woman a right to stay in the matrimonial home?

No. The Act contains no residence right and no provision about the matrimonial home. Its four Section 3 entitlements are a provision and maintenance, the children's clause, the mahr, and the delivery of the properties that were given to her; none of them is a right to occupy premises, and the property clause is about handing back her own goods, not about where she is to live. A woman whose need is housing must look to a different statute. The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 gives a woman in a domestic relationship a right to reside in the shared household under Section 17 and empowers the Magistrate to pass residence orders under Section 19, including orders restraining her dispossession. The general maintenance provision, for its part, awards money only. Housing is therefore the clearest example of a need that the maintenance statutes, the 1986 Act included, were never built to answer.

### Q: How does the Magistrate decide how much is a reasonable and fair provision?

Section 3(3) names the three considerations: the needs of the divorced woman, the standard of life enjoyed by her during her marriage, and the means of her former husband. No ceiling and no formula are prescribed. In practice, since the Constitution Bench's construction in Danial Latifi made the provision answerable to the woman's future, the assessment proceeds in two movements: a monthly figure is first fixed as the maintenance her needs and marital standard would support, and that figure is then projected across the future the provision must cover and capitalised into the single sum the section requires to be paid within the iddat period. The husband's means discipline the result at both stages, and the mahr and the children's clause are dealt with as separate heads rather than deducted from the provision. Because the award is a capital settlement, it is not variable afterwards in the way a monthly allowance under the general provision can be altered on changed circumstances, which is a real consideration in choosing between the two routes.
