Romeo and Juliet is the most rented property in advertising, and the rent is never paid in money. No estate licenses the lovers, no agent negotiates their image, and yet the pair have fronted campaigns for telephone networks, denim, perfume, wine, cigars, jewellery and ice cream across a hundred and forty years of documented trading. This article reads that trading as a price list. Its verdict is simple: advertising is the honest broker of the play's afterlife, because advertising alone states, in selections any buyer can audit, what the culture actually values in the story. The balcony is the most valuable thirty seconds in English drama. The tomb, in a hundred and forty years of documented campaigns, has never attracted a single bid. Every other element of the story, the feud, the names, the window, the kiss, the courtyard wall in Verona, has been priced, packaged and sold by somebody. Between those two prices lies the whole public meaning of the play, and the campaigns surveyed here, from a one-shilling pack of paper scraps to a hundred pieces of Paris high jewellery, are the ledger in which that meaning is kept.

![The balcony tableau of Romeo and Juliet restaged as advertising imagery - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The play arrives with its own advertisement attached. The Chorus opens the action by giving the ending away: "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 6), and promises only "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue, 12) in exchange for attention. The Prologue is a poster that spoils the plot on purpose, because the play's first sellers understood that foreknowledge is a feature: an audience told the cost in advance watches the spending. The advertisers who followed are less candid than the Chorus. They keep the promise and strike the disclosure, selling the meeting while declining to mention the price the meeting exacts. That single editorial habit, repeated across media and centuries, is the subject of everything that follows.

A note on evidence governs the survey, because advertising history is a field littered with affectionate invention. Every campaign treated here is carried at the precision of a dated trade report, a brand's own record or a named scholarly study, and where the record divides, as it does on the year of a French telephone spot and the acquisition date of a cigar marque, the division is stated rather than smoothed. Retellings that circulate in listicles without a date, an agency or a source have been left out, however famous. The result is a smaller corpus than the play's reputation might suggest and a harder one: fourteen cases across a hundred and forty years, each able to bear the weight the argument puts on it. The argument needs that hardness, because its claim is quantitative at heart. It says the market has priced the play's parts, and a price is only meaningful if the transactions are real.

## Why does advertising reach for Romeo and Juliet first?

The scholar Douglas Lanier, whose work on Shakespeare in popular culture maps the whole field, records the trade's preference in one sentence: "Almost all global Shakespearean advertising dwells on one of three topoi: Shakespeare himself, Hamlet, and Romeo and Juliet, the last of which offers by far the most fruitful territory for marketers" (Lanier, 2012, p. 514). Cristina Paravano of the University of Milan, in the fullest study of the play in a single product sector, adds the proportion: around thirty percent of all Shakespeare-themed advertisements on television and the web allude to this one play (Paravano, 2021). A Hamlet can sell melancholy, and the playwright's face can sell authority, but only the lovers sell a complete situation that a passer-by decodes in a glance: two houses, one prohibition, one window, one climb.

The asset has even been priced in the abstract. A brand-valuation study of 2012, reported in the trade press, estimated that a Shakespeare brand, if one existed to be owned, would be worth six hundred million dollars, double the combined values the same exercise assigned to Elvis Presley, Marilyn Monroe and George Foreman (Paravano, 2021). The figure is a publicity artifact in its own right, and this article treats it as such, but its direction is sound. The names carry a charge that no campaign has to build, only to borrow, and the charge renews itself through channels the advertiser does not fund: schoolrooms, wedding readings, [the phrases of the play lodged in everyday English](/2029/11/02/phrases-in-everyday-english/), where lines about star-crossed lovers and parting's sweet sorrow circulate as common property. The advertiser's outlay is therefore close to zero at the point of recognition, which is the most expensive point in any other campaign. The playtext is centuries beyond copyright. What remains is pure margin: meaning manufactured by others, harvested at the cost of a costume and a window frame.

Lanier supplies the technical term for the asset's flexibility. Writing on the Shakespearean name as a cultural logo, he credits the material with an "extraordinary linguistic semantic pliability" (Lanier, 2002, p. 262): the names bend to whatever the borrower needs them to certify without snapping into parody or offence. The survey's later sections are a catalogue of the bending. The same two names can certify a cigar's romance, a perfume's rebellion, a cheese's partnership and a telephone tariff's mercy, and in each case the borrowing reads as apt rather than absurd, because the names long ago stopped denoting two specific teenagers in Verona and came to denote a relation: two things drawn together across a resistance. A brand that buys a relation rather than a character can cast the relation in anything, which is why the casting in this survey descends from actors to a seal to two foodstuffs without the reference ever failing. Pliability of that order is not a property of most famous stories. A skull cannot be recast as a heroine. A balcony, and the names attached to it, can be recast as anything that faces anything else across a gap.

The street level of the same economy is the merchandise trade, which Paravano surveys alongside the campaigns: phone cases and covers printed with the playwright's face, with props from the plays, with stills from the films and with paintings of the famous moments, Ford Madox Brown's 1870 balcony scene among them. Susan Bennett's formulation for the face applies to the lovers as well: an image become "the signifier beyond all others in an international marketing economy" (Bennett, 1996). Sujata Iyengar names the objects "Shakescraft", items that carry the brand between high and low culture and sell affiliation as much as protection for a telephone. The merchandise matters to a survey of advertising because it shows the asset working without any advertiser's brief at all. Sellers with nothing to say about a product beyond its surface reach for the same two figures the telephone companies reach for, and for the same reason: the lovers are the one literary property whose meaning a buyer can be relied on to bring to the transaction. A case printed with a skull needs its play named. A case printed with a balcony does not.

The asset also renews itself on a schedule no media plan has to fund. Each year's school cohorts meet the play as a set text, each year's weddings borrow its sonnets, each year's Valentine's trade reprints its names, and each renewal restocks the recognition the advertisers draw on. Lanier's point about reproduction, adjudicated in full later in this article, begins here: the official culture teaches the play as tragedy, at length, with the ending attached, and the commercial culture skims the recognition that teaching produces. The skimming is not theft from a fixed stock. It is a dividend on an endowment the culture keeps replenishing for reasons of its own, which is why the asset has appreciated across a century that destroyed most of the brands borrowing it. Nextel is gone. The balcony it rented is still let.

### Why must the lovers be recognizable to viewers who have never read the play?

Because bought media allows no footnotes. A thirty-second spot or a poster glimpsed from a train must complete its meaning inside the attention it can seize, so the reference has to work for the viewer who knows the story only as a shape: two young figures, a barrier between them, a meeting conducted across it.

Recognition at that depth is not shallowness in the audience. It is the precise depth at which the play exists in public, and advertising both exploits that depth and, as the verdict section argues, maintains it.

The shape survives translation because it is architectural rather than verbal. Costumes can be updated, the window can become a phone screen, the houses can become rival families, rival networks or rival tariffs, and the diagram still reads. Paravano notes that the play's own subject suits it for the sector that has used it most: it "dramatises lack of communication on different levels", a tragedy about messages that arrive late, letters that go astray and speeches delivered to the wrong listener (Paravano, 2021). A story about failed connection is the natural patron of every product that promises connection, and the advertisers of telephones merely took the hint the plot had been offering for four centuries. The sections that follow test the verdict against the record: first the printed century that fixed the image, then the scene the image comes from, then the campaigns themselves.

## Romeo and Juliet in the age of the trade card

The lovers entered advertising through the cheapest color printing of the nineteenth century. Chromolithography, developed from the 1830s, made the trade card the first widely distributed form of color advertisement: small chromolithographed cards, handed out with purchases, gathered by children, pasted into scrapbooks, and produced in numbered series so that collecting them became part of the purchase. Harvard's Baker Library, whose exhibition of the genre remains a standard account, describes literary cards drawing on Shakespeare among the staple subjects of the form, alongside beautiful women, children, flowers and patriotic scenes. The production system behind the cards matters as much as their subjects. Lithographers printed the images as stock, in advance of any customer, and a business bought the design it liked and had its own name and address overprinted, so that the same lovers could front a grocer in one town and a druggist in the next, the picture a shared asset and only the lettering proprietary. The trade card is therefore the survey's first perfect specimen of the economics the recognition section describes: an image whose meaning was manufactured elsewhere, rented for the price of an overprint, and renewed by the collecting habits of children, whose albums preserved the advertisement long after the goods were consumed. A card pasted into an album in 1890 was still advertising in 1910, at no further cost to the seller whose name it carried. The economics deserve emphasis. A trade card is an advertisement the customer asks to keep. The image had to earn its place in the album, and a scene from a famous play earned it the way a famous face earns a magazine cover: recognition, pre-installed.

The record preserves the lovers in exactly this economy. The Victoria and Albert Museum catalogues a set of chromolithographed relief scraps issued by Siegmund Hildesheimer and Company under the title Characters from Shakespeare, a series of twelve sold in packs at one shilling. The Romeo and Juliet scrap, dated by the museum to about 1890, shows William Terriss as Romeo and Mary Anderson as Juliet, the stars of the production that opened at London's Lyceum Theatre on 8 March 1882. In the image the lovers embrace on the balcony, a rope ladder hangs from the balustrade behind them, and lines of the dialogue are printed beside Romeo's head. The museum's description adds a detail worth pausing over: the sheet labels the moment as Act 3, Scene 5, the dawn parting, although the picture shows the balcony courtship. Even in 1890, in other words, the saleable unit was not a scene but the tableau, and the caption could point at whichever scene the image vaguely resembled. The tableau had already detached from the text it illustrated.

Two further features of the scrap deserve record. The first is that it sells actors as much as characters: Terriss and Anderson are named in the museum's description because their faces, famous from the Lyceum stage, were part of the merchandise, an endorsement economy running decades before the word celebrity acquired its commercial sense. The scrap buyer was pasting into an album not merely a scene from a play but the likenesses of the two performers London most associated with the roles, so that theatrical fame, print commerce and the lovers' image reinforced one another in a single pennyworth of paper. The second feature is the series form itself. Twelve characters, one shilling, a set to complete: the scraps taught buyers to collect Shakespeare as a gallery of detachable figures, each purchasable without the others and each intelligible alone. The lovers were sold from the start as one panel in a portable pantheon, and the panel was the balcony. When the twentieth century's advertisers inherited the image, they inherited it pre-formatted by the scrap trade: framed, captioned loosely, and certified by the faces of the famous.

The same card economy carried Shakespeare into the sweet trade. The Chromobase project on French trade cards describes the Shakespeare chromos issued by the Chocolaterie d'Aiguebelle, a chocolate factory whose cards paired a stage scene with a medallion portrait of the playwright and a short laudatory biography on the reverse, above the praise of the chocolate itself; the image and text were then reused by many other companies in France and Europe. The card the project describes in detail is a Hamlet, and this article claims no Aiguebelle balcony, but the mechanism is the documented point: chocolate makers bought Shakespeare wholesale, portrait, biography and scenes together, because the playwright's respectability sweetened the product before the sugar did. The Aiguebelle card's construction deserves a closer look, because it is the whole nineteenth-century method on one piece of card. The front carries the culture, a graveyard scene after a Salon painting, all gravity and recognition. The reverse carries the commerce, but carries it in culture's clothing: a biography of the playwright, adapted from Larousse's great dictionary, occupies the first half, and only the bottom of the card arrives at the excellence of the chocolate. The advertisement spends most of its space pretending to be education, and the pretense is the strategy: a card that teaches is a card that is kept, and a card that is kept advertises for years. The plates' afterlife completes the lesson. The same picture and text passed from the chocolate factory to many other companies, an early trade in stock imagery, so that a Shakespeare scene became a reusable commercial asset detached from any single seller, which is precisely the status the balcony holds in the survey's later centuries. A second nineteenth-century mode ran alongside the image trade. Douglas Lanier instances an 1885 advertisement for Ridge's Baby Food that vested its authority in three Shakespeare quotations, lines from King John and The Taming of the Shrew pressed into service as certificates of wholesomeness. Between the Hildesheimer scrap and the baby-food advertisement, the whole grammar of the next century is already present: the play as a picture everyone can read, and the play as an authority everyone is expected to trust.

## The balcony: the most portable scene in advertising

What the campaigns rent, almost without exception, is one configuration. A young woman stands at a lit window above; a young man stands in shadow below; between them runs a vertical distance that desire has to cross. The configuration is a complete narrative in a single frame. It contains wanting, forbidding and the promise of a crossing, and it resolves at any size, in silence, in any costume, in any language, because none of its information is verbal. The playtext behind it, in the Folger Shakespeare, gives the scene its furniture in a handful of lines: "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3); "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2.33); "The orchard walls are high and hard to climb" (2.2.63). The scene's own words for the arrangement are a window, walls and a question about a name. The balcony itself, as the series' textual articles establish, is the performance tradition's contribution, a piece of staging the image-makers of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries built and the advertisers inherited ready-made. Advertising did not distort a scene so much as adopt an illustration.

### What does the balcony give an advertiser that no other scene can?

A product-shaped vacancy at the center of a famous picture. Between the window and the ground there is a gap that the story leaves open for a crossing device: a ladder in the Hildesheimer scrap, a thrown stone in a telephone spot, a photograph of a wall on a wine label.

The advertiser's product is cast as the thing that crosses, and the casting requires no explanation, because every viewer already knows what the gap is for.

Consider what the configuration excludes by design. It excludes the families, whose feud exists only as the reason for the altitude. It excludes the Friar, the Nurse, the Prince, the whole machinery of Verona. It excludes, above all, sequence: the balcony is a moment that refuses to say what happens next, which is why campaigns can park inside it indefinitely. A duel cannot be frozen without implying a corpse; a wedding cannot be frozen without implying a household; the balcony implies only approach. The thirty seconds of theatre that advertising rents are therefore not the play's most beautiful or most famous lines but its most suspendable instant, the one scene whose meaning does not depend on an ending. The advertisers did not find the play's center of gravity. They found its center of suspension, and the distinction is the article in miniature.

The configuration is also cheap to build, and the cheapness compounds the portability. Two performers, one raised platform, one light: the most valuable image in the play requires the smallest company and the least scenery of any famous moment in Shakespeare, a fact the production tradition discovered long before the advertisers did. It is equally the most reproducible. A poster can hold it, a label can hold it, a six-second bumper can hold it, because its information is carried by relative position rather than by detail: high and lit against low and dark, with the eye travelling upward along the line of desire. The text even scripts the travel. Romeo, asked how he entered the orchard, answers in the scene's own physics: "With love's light wings did I o'erperch these walls; / For stony limits cannot hold love out" (2.2.66-67). The crossing in the play is imagined as flight over a barrier, which is why the Orange campaign's lovers, leaving their windows to meet in mid air, are not decorating the scene but literalizing its central metaphor. Advertising keeps returning to the balcony because the balcony is the one place in the play where the story's problem and the product's promise have the same shape: a distance, and something that crosses it.

The play also supplies the crossing with its own prop, and the prop's career in the imagery is a small history of the form. Romeo's practical answer to the orchard walls is a rope ladder, the cords he sends by the Nurse, and the production tradition fixed on the object: the Hildesheimer scrap hangs it from the balustrade behind the embracing lovers, where it reads as both evidence and promise, the means of the next ascent folded into the picture of the first. A rope ladder is, in the taxonomy of this survey, the original product placement: a purchasable device, introduced by the story itself, whose function is to defeat the story's obstacle. Every later campaign merely substitutes its own device into the ladder's position in the frame: a push-to-talk handset, an unlimited plan, a chosen number, a camera that makes the climb unnecessary by collapsing the distance into a screen. The balcony's vacancy, described in the answer above, has had a tenant since the text was written, and the advertisers' originality consists of nothing more than re-letting it.

The balcony has a twin image, and the twin's absence from the record is as instructive as the balcony's presence. The dawn parting, in which the lovers separate at the window as the lark sings, is the play's other great window scene, painted and staged almost as often as the courtship, and close enough to it in composition that the Hildesheimer sheet could caption the courtship with the parting's scene number and pass. Advertisers take the courtship and leave the parting, for a reason the survey's logic predicts. The parting faces backward along the story: it is the meeting already expiring, the lovers in the act of losing each other, and its emotional direction is toward the plot's machinery, the exile, the potion, the tomb. A campaign can freeze the approach because the approach implies nothing but itself; the parting implies everything after, and everything after is what the form exists to exclude. The two window scenes thus divide the play's commercial value between them with unusual neatness: one is the most valuable image in English drama, and the other, its mirror in the same frame, has no recorded price at all.

## Romeo and Juliet in the mobile-phone campaigns, 2003 to 2016

The richest documented corpus in the survey is the work of the telephone companies, and it has a scholar. Cristina Paravano's study "My Kingdom for an iPhone", published in the journal Lingue e Linguaggi in 2021, examines five campaigns in which mobile providers restage the balcony scene, and her summary finding governs this whole section: "In all the examples analysed, the marketing specialists downplayed the play's tragic and erotic qualities in a bid to attract potential buyers with a more amusing or romantic rendition of the story" (Paravano, 2021). The campaigns span thirteen years, four countries and every strategy available to the form, and they agree on one thing: the play is a machine for dramatizing a blocked message, and a telephone company is a machine for unblocking it. The product writes itself into the plot as the friar's letter, delivered on time.

The plot point deserves its textual weight, because it is the hinge of the tragedy and the whole reason the sector owns this play. The catastrophe of Romeo and Juliet is, mechanically, a delivery failure. Friar Laurence's letter, explaining that Juliet's death is a feint, never reaches Mantua: Friar John, its carrier, reports that he could not send it, nor get a messenger to bring it, the searchers of the town having sealed the house against infection (5.2). Romeo acts on false news because true news was quarantined in transit. A telecommunications company contemplating that plot sees not a tragedy but a service outage with a body count, and its advertisement writes the correction: coverage where the letter failed, a signal where the messenger was stopped. Wellman and Rainie's counterfactual, quoted below, merely states in sociology's register what every creative team in the corpus saw in the story's engineering. The play is the only canonical tragedy whose disaster a phone demonstrably prevents, and the sector's thirteen-year tenancy of the balcony is the industry's recognition of a structural fact about the text, not a coincidence of taste.

The corpus opens with compression. In 2003 Nextel Communications, then the fifth largest wireless company in the United States, built one of nine spots for its push-to-talk phones around a stage performance of the play, in which the entire tragedy is exchanged in a single volley of dialogue: "Romeo, Juliet, I love you, ditto, die, marry him, never, no, better now, no, kids" (quoted in Paravano, 2021). The spot closes on the tagline "Nextel. Done". Marjorie Garber read the volley as a modern version of classical stichomythia, the line-for-line fencing of Greek and Shakespearean dialogue (Garber, 2008), and Barbara Hodgdon placed the spot as evidence of "what the play has become post-Baz Luhrmann" (Hodgdon, 2009). Paravano reports sales figures showing profits up by 23.5 percent across the campaign period of 2003 and 2004. One word in the volley matters more than the rest: "die" survives the edit. Nextel keeps the deaths, but keeps them as a beat in a joke about speed, a syllable between "ditto" and "marry him". The tragedy's ending is present, and priced at nothing.

Paravano frames her cases with the standard anatomy of persuasive communication set out by Michael Mulvey and Carmen Medina: the actors of the advertisement, the setting in time and place, the system of visual and sound cues, and the slogan or tagline. Run the five campaigns through that anatomy and a pattern in the casting column appears. Nextel casts a stage company, T-Mobile casts a motorcyclist and a girl with a phone, Orange casts two anonymous tenants, Vodafone casts a seal and a dubbed film star's voice, Apple casts schoolchildren and a parent. The casting descends a ladder of theatricality across the thirteen years, from professional performance to amateur ritual, while the setting ascends a ladder of abstraction, from a theatre to a garden to facing windows to a school hall that is also a cinema. The corpus is not a set of variations on a theme so much as a single long negotiation about how much of the play's machinery the advertisement needs to show before the audience will complete the rest. The answer declines steadily, which is the poster's answer, arrived at early: almost none.

The sociologists Barry Wellman and Lee Rainie gave the sector its founding counterfactual in a much-cited piece titled "If Romeo and Juliet had mobile phones", concluding that the lovers' course "would have been more connected, and perhaps would have run more smoothly", and that with phones "they might have lived happily ever after" (Wellman and Rainie, 2013). The campaigns that follow Nextel behave as if the industry had adopted the counterfactual as a brief. In T-Mobile's thirty-second American spot of 2008, documented in the trade archive at Adland and analyzed by Paravano, Juliet in Renaissance dress waits with her phone while Romeo, in present-day clothes, rides a motorbike. The feud has been restated as a tariff problem. Her father objects, in the spot's mixed register, "thou betrayest thy family's cell plan", and then resolves the four-century hatred in a single explanation: he never really hated Romeo's family; Romeo "was just too expensive to call". The unlimited plan is the peace treaty. Where the play's Verona cannot repeal its hatred in five acts, the carrier repeals it in one billing adjustment, and the joke's precision is that the audience accepts the substitution instantly, because the feud had long since become, in public understanding, an obstacle to a conversation rather than a system of honour and violence.

The substitution works because the play itself leaves the feud empty. The Chorus calls it an "ancient grudge" (Prologue, 3) and never explains it; no act, scene or speech in the text assigns the Montague-Capulet hatred a cause, an origin or even a subject of dispute. The feud is pure obstruction, a wall with nothing behind it, and four centuries of audiences have accepted the vacancy because the plot needs only the wall. Advertising inherits the vacancy and fills it with whatever obstacle the product removes: a tariff in T-Mobile's spot, family disapproval in Cosmote's, species difference played for comedy in Vodafone's. A hatred with a stated cause could not be repriced so freely, since the cause would argue back. The advertisers' freedom with the feud is thus a textual fact before it is a commercial one. Shakespeare built the obstacle as a blank, and the blank is the second most valuable property in the play, after the window it surrounds.

France removes the words altogether. The Orange spot analyzed by Paravano, which her essay dates to 2009 in its abstract and to 2004 in its analysis, a division this article carries rather than resolves, shows a young man and a young woman in facing buildings who leave their windows and meet in mid air, to the campaign line "Orange intense: communiquons sans limites", let us communicate without limits. There is no dialogue. Graham Holderness reads the spot as a case of Shakespeare "quoted as a familiar source for images of beauty, love, transgression of barriers and a transcendent emotional liberty" (Holderness, 2018). The identification of the floating pair as the lovers is carried by the score, Nino Rota's love theme from Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film, a borrowing whose licensing economy is the separate subject of [the series' study of the love theme in advertising and trailers](/2029/03/16/love-theme-in-advertising/). The detail that matters here is the division of labour: the picture supplies two bodies and a gap, the music supplies the names, and the viewer supplies the story. Nothing in the execution requires a single line of the play.

The corpus's comic extreme is Italian. Vodafone Italy's campaign of 2013, which Paravano notes had received no critical attention before her study, casts Juliet as a seal, voiced by the comedian Luciana Littizzetto, waiting in a garden at night in a setting that recalls Zeffirelli's film. Romeo, played by the American actor and model Kyle James, throws a stone at the balcony to attract her attention and hits her instead. Romeo's voice is itself a citation: James was dubbed by Francesco Pezzulli, the Italian voice of Leonardo DiCaprio, so that the campaign's Romeo sounds, to its first audience, like the Romeo of Luhrmann's film. Juliet complains, in Paravano's translation, that she was "starting to get really paranoid"; Romeo protests that the pair cannot talk, belonging to two different families; and the campaign answers with its tagline, "you choose", an offer of unlimited calls to one chosen number. Paravano's reading of the ending is exact: the spot "suggests the possibility of a happy ending only for Juliet, excluding both her death and a long lasting relationship with Romeo". The same strategy appears in Romanian dress in Ogilvy and Mather's spot for Cosmote, titled simply "Romeo si Julieta" and discussed in scholarship of 2016, which keeps two young lovers and an opposing father while dropping every textual reference; Gabriela Iuliana Colipca-Ciobanu concludes that "the Shakespearean hypotext is at least in the back of the advertisers' mind" (Colipca-Ciobanu, 2016). The name alone, at that point, is doing all the work the balcony used to do.

The Romanian case earns its place in the corpus for what it proves about the template's portability. Paravano's five campaigns come from the United States, France and Italy, countries where the play is a school text and a standing cultural fixture. Cosmote's spot shows the same machinery, lovers, obstructing father, forbidden contact, running in a market reached through an international agency network, with no line of the text and no famous score to identify it, the title alone carrying the reference. Colipca-Ciobanu's cautious phrasing, the hypotext in the back of the advertisers' mind, understates what the execution demonstrates: by the second decade of the century the template had become part of the international agency repertoire, a stock solution to the brief that asks for young desire obstructed, deployable in Bucharest as readily as in Los Angeles. The asset's liquidity, priced in the recognition section by a valuation study, is visible here in its working form.

### What changes when Juliet, not Romeo, gets the punchline?

The campaigns quietly reverse the play's distribution of initiative. In the text, Juliet proposes and Romeo falters, but in the public shape of the story she is the figure at the window, waiting to be reached, the still point of the picture.

The telephone spots hand her the device, the complaint and the decision: T-Mobile's Juliet holds the phone that sets the plot's price, Vodafone's Juliet insults Romeo, mocks his lateness and is offered the choosing as the campaign's whole proposition. The advertisers are not conducting feminist criticism. They are courting the buyer most likely to choose a plan, and they have discovered that the story sells better when its heroine is the customer rather than the prize.

The corpus closes with the gaze itself for sale. Apple's spot titled "Romeo and Juliet", released on 5 December 2016 and made by TBWA Media Arts Lab as part of the "Practically Magic" campaign for the iPhone 7, opens as cinema: a castle, a night garden, child actors playing the balcony scene with full production value. The camera then withdraws to show a school hall and a father filming the performance on his phone, his eyes wet, and the spot closes on the line "Your movies look like movies on iPhone 7". Paravano's objection is the sharpest in her study: nothing in the execution points to the brand, and the advertisers could have used any play, opera or musical, since "there is no connection between the play and the product advertised". The trade press heard it differently. Angela Natividad's notice in Adweek called the spot "neither noble nor true to life" but "pretty", "a Shakespearean rendition of how we'd actually like these moments to look and feel". Both readings are correct, and their collision is the corpus in one spot: the play contributes nothing but the feeling, and the feeling is the product being demonstrated. The father is not buying a telephone. He is buying the guarantee that his daughter's school play will look, in his archive, the way it felt from his seat, and the lovers are the calibration standard by which that guarantee is measured.

Paravano adds the observation that explains why this particular play, and not another, can calibrate a parent's archive. The spot's realism is institutional: the school production is where the culture stores its Shakespeare, and the father's pride, as she notes, attaches not only to his daughter but to the idea that she is playing a Shakespearean character. The advertisement thus draws twice on the commons. It borrows the balcony's image, and it borrows the schoolroom's ceremony, the one occasion on which a large part of the audience has personally stood inside the play's machinery, as performers, parents or conscripts. A carrier selling call time needs the feud; a handset maker selling a camera needs the ritual. The corpus's last case is accordingly the least about the lovers and the most about the audience, which is where a survey of advertising should expect to end: with the discovery that the public's own participation in the play's reproduction is the deepest asset in the account, and that Apple, alone among the five, itemized it.

Stepped back from, the corpus describes a sector learning the asset's terms over thirteen years. The early campaigns rent the whole play and apologize for its length, compressing the tragedy into a volley or a tariff joke; the middle campaigns discover that the names can travel without the text, carried by a score or a casting decision; the final campaign discovers that even the names can be left implicit when the ritual itself, a school stage and a watching parent, does the identifying. Each stage spends less of the play and gets the same recognition, which is the poster's lesson learned from inside the moving image. The sector also marks the boundary of the borrowing's usefulness. Telecommunications needed the lovers while connection itself was the product being sold and its failures were the story's subject; once the handset became the product, the play's function narrowed to certifying the handset's memory of the ritual. The balcony outlasted the business problem that made it indispensable, as it had outlasted the trade card and would outlast the poster, passing to the next medium with its geometry intact and its ending still unclaimed.

## H&M and the Romeo and Juliet film that kept the deaths

Against the whole telecom corpus stands a single large exception, and the trade press recorded its reception. In 2005 the photographer and director David LaChapelle made a five-minute Romeo and Juliet film for H&M, a cinema advertisement for the retailer's jeans, screened ahead of feature films. LaChapelle declined the industry's edit. The film stages the story at length, in his saturated manner, and keeps its violence and its deaths: the lovers die on screen, in a work whose entire purpose is to sell denim. Campaign magazine's diary column reported the result from an Odeon cinema in Kingston, where a staffer watched the audience giggle a few minutes into the film, then begin to boo, then applaud wildly when it ended. The applause, on the diary's telling, was for the ending of the advertisement rather than the ending of the story.

The exception prices the rule with unusual clarity. Five minutes is an eternity in bought media, and the tragedy at full length inverted the commercial contract: the audience had paid for a feature and found itself conscripted as the congregation at a double funeral staged to move jeans. The market's verdict, delivered by booing in the dark, was not a verdict against the play. It was a verdict against the play's duration and its ending inside a selling situation. Every other case in this survey can be read as the industry's correction of LaChapelle's experiment, and the correction is always the same pair of cuts: shorten the story to the meeting, and stop before the tomb. Nextel, which also keeps a death, keeps it for exactly one syllable and wraps it in a joke about speed. H&M kept it for minutes and wrapped it in denim, and the difference in reception is the most instructive data point in the whole record: the tragedy is not unsaleable, it is unsaleable at length, in earnest, between the audience and something it wants more.

The choice of product sharpens the lesson. Denim is the uniform of the young, and the lovers are retail's eternal teenagers, the pair every youth brand borrows when it wants desire with a pedigree. LaChapelle, whose commercial work had long treated celebrity and eros as a single saturated tableau, was the director most likely to refuse the form's euphemisms and stage the story whole, and H&M, a retailer selling youth its own image, was the client most likely to let him. The experiment's failure therefore cannot be dismissed as a mismatch of director and brand. It was the best-case test of the full tragedy in a commercial setting: the right product, the right director, the largest canvas the medium offers, a captive audience in the dark. The audience's answer stands in the trade record, and the industry's subsequent practice, in which no comparable attempt recurs in the documented survey, is the answer's echo. What the booing priced was not the play's quality. It was the play's ending, weighed in the only currency a cinema audience holds, its willingness to sit still.

The cinema setting sharpens the measurement, because cinema advertising reverses the ordinary direction of payment. A television viewer pays for a commercial with attention inside a free stream and can leave the room; a poster taxes a glance. A cinema audience has paid money for a seat and surrenders five minutes of a purchased evening to the advertiser's film, and its patience is therefore the most expensive attention in the survey, bought at the highest rate and forfeited in public when the contract is breached. LaChapelle's film spent that patience on a double suicide staged in earnest, and the booing was an invoice presented in real time. No other case in the record offers so clean a price signal, because no other case asked so much. The telephone spots ask thirty seconds and repay them with a joke; the poster asks two seconds and repays them with a kiss. The H&M film asked three hundred seconds and repaid them with a funeral, and the market's response, recorded by the trade press in the room where it happened, stands as the survey's single experimental measurement of the ending's commercial value. It is negative.

## A poster with one word: advertising after the text runs out

The limiting case of the survey is a poster. Douglas Lanier describes a poster campaign run in Swiss cities in 2004 and 2005 for Espace 2, the cultural channel of Radio Suisse Romande: two teenagers kiss in a subway train filled with inattentive passengers, accompanied by a single word of caption, "Shakespeare." No balcony, no costume, no quotation, no names. Lanier's question is the right one: in what sense is this Shakespeare? His answer shapes the argument of this article. The poster works because the kiss plus the caption resolves, in the passer-by's mind, to exactly one story, and the story is not resolved by knowledge of the text but by the public shape the campaigns of a century had already built. The adult passengers looking away are the feud redrawn as indifference; the teenagers are the lovers redrawn as commuters; the channel buys, with one word, the aura of youthful desire conducted under the nose of authority.

### How can a poster say Shakespeare with two teenagers and one word?

Because the audience completes the image without charge. The caption names the author, the kiss names the play, and the memory of everyone who passes supplies the window, the feud and the risk that the poster declines to draw unaided.

The unpaid labour of recognition is the whole medium of Shakespearean advertising: the brand contributes a frame and a word, and the culture, which has been maintaining the lovers' image for its own reasons, delivers the meaning free.

The poster also exposes the direction of travel in the survey's evidence. The Hildesheimer scrap of about 1890 printed the actors' faces, the balcony and a passage of dialogue; the Ridge's advertisement of 1885 needed three quotations to borrow authority. By 2004 the requirement had fallen to one word, and the word was not even a line of the play but the author's surname. Text drains out of the form across the century, image concentrates, and the play's contribution narrows to a situation and a pair of names. What the telecom corpus dramatizes and the poster states is that the names alone carry the full charge, which is why the survey's next section leaves images behind and follows the names onto the labels where they live permanently.

Lanier's larger claim in the essay that preserves the poster is about proportion. Under the pressure of mass media, he argues, the ratio of image to word in the culture's Shakespeare has inverted: where a nineteenth-century advertisement vested its authority in columns of quotation, the contemporary artifact vests it in a picture and a caption, and asks whether the picture still counts as the author it names. The Swiss poster is his exhibit because it tests the boundary with the least possible material, and it is worth noting which play the boundary case required. A skull and a caption might gesture at Hamlet, but a skull is also a poison warning and a pirate flag; the image would not hold its reference. Two teenagers kissing holds its reference with no support at all, because the lovers are the one Shakespearean property whose public form is a situation rather than an object. The poster could not have been made from any other play in the canon. That is the survey's finding about the asset in its purest experimental form: of all the stories in the culture's stock, this is the one that can be advertised with a single word because it is the one the audience is already carrying.

The poster also collapses Lanier's three topoi into a single sheet. Its caption invokes the first topos, Shakespeare himself, by surname alone; its image invokes the third, the lovers, without naming them; and the second topos, Hamlet, is present by pointed absence, since the same layout with a brooding teenager would resolve to nothing at all. The economy is worth stating as a ratio. One word of copy purchases the author, the play, the feud, the window and the risk, because each element is supplied by a different layer of the passer-by's schooling: the surname from the curriculum, the situation from the culture's wallpaper of images, the stakes from the phrases lodged in ordinary speech. No advertising for any other literary property on record achieves that ratio, and the ratio, more than any campaign budget, is the measure of the position this play holds in the stock of shared references. The Espace 2 poster is the survey's limiting case because it is the point at which the advertiser's contribution approaches zero and the asset's yield does not fall.

## Romeo and Juliet as brand names: cigars, perfume, and a flavor

A campaign ends; a name stays on the shelf. The deepest borrowings in the survey are not scenes but the lovers' names fixed to products for decades, and they show what the borrowing costs when it cannot be switched off at the quarter's end. A brand named for the lovers must live inside the story it has rented, and each of the three great name-borrowings survives by arresting that story at a different point.

The three survivals also differ in legal form, and the differences map the asset's range. A cigar marque holds the names as a trademark, defensible property renewed by use. A perfume house holds one name, amended, as a brand, a coinage that points at the heroine while standing at a lawyer's distance from her. A national cuisine holds the pair as a generic term, property of nobody, which no maker can own and every maker can use. The asset performs in all three registers without modification, appreciating in the first, appreciating in the second and simply persisting in the third, where persistence without an owner is the strongest evidence of value the survey contains: a name valuable enough to be kept in daily use by people who gain nothing exclusive from keeping it.

The oldest is a cigar. Romeo y Julieta was founded in Havana in 1875, some accounts say 1873, by Inocencio Alvarez and Manin Garcia, and took its name from the play, a naming whose career in the language belongs to the survey of the word "Romeo" elsewhere in this series. The marque's early decades already ran on borrowed glory: trade histories record a run of tasting awards between 1885 and 1900, and the gold medals from those exhibitions remain displayed on the brand's logo, so that the packaging itself is a small archive of borrowed prestige, literary on one side and competitive on the other. The advertising history proper begins under Jose Rodriguez Fernandez, known as Don Pepin, who acquired the brand in 1903 by most trade accounts, though one retailer history gives 1908. Rodriguez promoted the marque as theatre. He opened a shop in Verona at the Hotel Capulet, named a racehorse Julieta, and then attempted the boldest transaction in this entire survey: he offered to buy the Palazzo Capuleti in Verona and operate it as a cigar showroom, with, in the trade-retold account preserved by Holt's Cigar Company, a young woman beautiful as Juliet appearing on the balcony for the customers below, so that a smoker might sit "in the very rooms where the young lovers met". The city refused. Rodriguez returned to Havana and built a replica of the palazzo there, by report with a copy of the balcony included, and the marque's promotion continued at the level of twenty thousand personalized cigar bands produced for individual wealthy customers. The analytic point is not the eccentricity. It is that the first great Romeo and Juliet advertiser tried to buy the set itself, and Verona, which has sold the balcony to the whole world in pieces ever since, recorded its single refusal when one buyer asked for the original. The cigar's lovers, meanwhile, are frozen before the story starts: they preside over a product of adult leisure as eternal hosts, and the tragedy is not edited out so much as never allowed to begin.

The marque's later history supplies the names section with its hardest evidence. After the Cuban revolution the brand split in two, a Cuban Romeo y Julieta produced under Habanos and a separate line made in the Dominican Republic for markets closed to Cuban goods, and both lines continue, each trading on the identical pair of names. Trade writers treat the duplication as a curiosity of trademark law, but for a survey of advertising value it is an experiment with a control group: strip away the factory, the leaf, the distribution and the customer list, divide them between two companies on two islands, and observe what each successor refuses to surrender. Both surrendered everything except the names. The marque also absorbed the endorsement economy's grandest denomination when the Churchill size took its name from the marque's most famous smoker following his 1946 visit to Havana, so that the catalogue came to hold a cigar named for a statesman inside a brand named for two fictional lovers, prestige stacked on prestige, every layer rented from somebody else's fame. The first layer's rent, alone among them, has never been invoiced.

The perfume house Juliette Has a Gun performs the opposite arrest: it keeps the heroine and discharges her from the plot. Romano Ricci, great-grandson of Nina Ricci, created the house in 2005 by some profiles and launched it in December 2006 by the trade accounts, a division the record carries openly. The founder arrived with a dynastic brief: his great-grandmother Nina founded the house that bears her name, his grandfather Robert Ricci created L'Air du Temps, among the century's canonical perfumes, and profiles record a four-year apprenticeship in the trade before he persuaded Francis Kurkdjian to compose the opening fragrances. The genealogy matters to the borrowing's meaning. A scion of a house built on a romantic classic chose, for his own founding act, the heroine of the culture's founding romance, and then armed her: the gesture is legible only because every reader of the label knows what the play's Juliet lacked. The first fragrances, among them Lady Vengeance and Miss Charming, were composed with the perfumer Francis Kurkdjian. In the house's own formula, relayed by The Perfume Society, "the innocent Juliet of Shakespeare is transposed to the 21st century with a gun", the gun standing as "metaphor for the perfume", a weapon of seduction and, in the house's phrase, a symbol of liberation, "sometimes with an aftertaste of revenge". The brand's commercial proof arrived with Not a Perfume in 2010, a fragrance built on a single synthetic molecule, whose success led Ricci to expand the line around it. Everything about the borrowing is candid. The name sells a corrected Juliet, a heroine reissued with the means to refuse her ending, and the correction is the product: what the perfume promises its buyer is not romance but the heroine's escape from romance's consequences. Of all the cases in the survey, this is the only one that advertises against the play while trading on it, and its success suggests the market had been waiting for a Juliet permitted to survive.

Brazil arrests the story at the table. Romeu e Julieta is the national name for a pairing of queijo minas, the mild white cheese of Minas Gerais, with goiabada, a firm guava paste, documented as a dish and a naming convention in Atlas Obscura's food record. The pairing comes from Minas Gerais and is served, in the accounts of Brazilian food writers, after lunch, at parties and as an everyday snack: a slice of the paste laid on a slice of the cheese. The casting is explicit in Brazilian usage: the cheese is Romeu, the guava paste is Julieta, salty against sweet, white against red, each mild alone and inseparable in combination. The name has spread across the country's food industry as a flavor in its own right, stamped on ice creams, cheesecakes, brigadeiros and pizzas by makers far beyond any single brand, with the cheese rendered as an ice cream and the guava as its sauce in Minas Gerais itself. No campaign devised this; a cuisine did, which makes the case the survey's control experiment. Given the lovers' names and no advertiser at all, a culture still files the pair under union rather than catastrophe, and still stops the story at the meeting of two complementary things. The feud, in the Brazilian reading, was never the point. Complementarity was, and the tomb has no flavor.

The case deserves one more turn, because it inverts the direction of every other borrowing in the survey. A campaign takes the lovers out of the story and sets them to work on a product; the Brazilian table takes two products and discovers the lovers already inside them, waiting for a name. The cheese and the paste existed before the casting, as the balcony existed before the telephone companies, and the naming merely certifies a resemblance the culture had already tasted: two unlike things, each improved past recognition by the other, served as one item or not at all. That the certification chose these lovers, rather than any other famous pair, is the recognition asset working at the level of appetite. And the Brazilian case quietly answers the question the telecom corpus poses. Set the story loose in a culture without a client to please, and it still ends at the union. The edit, it turns out, is not an advertiser's invention. It is the culture's own preference, which the advertisers merely invoice.

The automobile trade supplies the survey's honest footnote. Alfa Romeo's marque name has an unrelated origin in a surname, as this series' study of the word establishes, but the company also sold a Giulietta, a model name launched with the Type 750 in 1954 and revived for the hatchback of 2010. When the revived Giulietta reached the British market in 2010, its launch campaign, created by Leo Burnett and fronted by the actress Uma Thurman, quoted not the lovers but The Tempest, "Such stuff as dreams are made on", and the motoring press recorded its uncertainty about the name itself: the connection between the company and the choice of Giulietta, Car Enthusiast reported in August 2010, "is not clear, with some suggesting homage to Shakespeare's Juliet". The division is worth preserving at exactly that grade. The most famous automotive pairing of the names turns out, on inspection, to be one surname the play did not give and one model name the record will not confirm it gave, advertised with a quotation from another play entirely. The names attract attributions the way the balcony attracts productions, and a survey of the advertising that declines to correct the magnetism would be repeating it.

## Romeo and Juliet at the luxury end: a Verona wine and a Paris jewel case

At the top of the market the borrowing changes character. Luxury does not rent thirty seconds; it buys the whole setting, and it can afford more of the story than a phone plan can. Two cases, one from the lovers' own city and one from Paris, mark the pole.

Famiglia Pasqua, a winery founded in Verona in 1925, builds its PassioneSentimento line explicitly as what its own winemaker notes call the house's Romeo and Juliet project: the red wine of the line is described by the winery as the "founding wine and starting point of the Romeo&Juliet project". The red is made by the appassimento method of the Valpolicella tradition, its Corvina and Croatina grapes, with Merlot, dried in wooden crates until they lose around thirty percent of their water, and the winery's own copy sells the process in the story's vocabulary, calling the result "a red wine like love and passion". The bottles, a red, a white, a Prosecco and, from December 2020, a Prosecco Rose, carry a label that startles by what it omits. There are no lovers on it, no balcony, no Verona skyline. The label is a photograph by Gio Martorana of the graffiti-covered wall at the entrance to Juliet's courtyard in the Via Cappello, the twenty-foot stretch of stone on which visitors leave their messages of love. The winery's own notes frame the transaction without embarrassment: the house describes the line as a wine created in its city of origin to tell "the love story that made Verona famous all over the world", a sentence in which the city, the winery and the story certify one another in a closed circle of borrowed fame. The United States launch release for the Rose edition describes the label in those terms and prices the line at sixteen dollars. The courtyard economy that manufactures that wall, admission by admission and message by message, is the subject of [the series' profile of Verona's economy built on two fictional lovers](/2029/07/27/verona-economy-fictional-lovers/), and the Pasqua label is that economy's most elegant export: Verona selling, in a bottle, the handwriting of its own visitors.

### Why is a wall of other people's handwriting the right label for a Verona wine?

Because the wall is the one part of the myth the customers manufacture themselves. The balcony was built by a restorer, the tomb is a sarcophagus with a disputed past, but the graffiti wall is written daily by the lovers' public, and a photograph of it certifies the bottle.

The certification works by association with something the buyer already trusts, the way a portrait of the playwright once certified baby food. Pasqua does not sell the story. It sells proof of attendance at the story's shrine, and the proof fits on a label because the shrine's visitors had already reduced it to handwriting.

The Paris case is the survey's summit. In July 2019, during Paris Haute Couture Week, Van Cleef and Arpels unveiled its Romeo and Juliet high jewellery collection, one hundred unique pieces built from the play. Nicolas Bos, the maison's president, explained the choice in terms that no telephone executive would use: "For us, the choice of Romeo and Juliet was a natural one", he said, since literature is one of the maison's major sources of inspiration, and the house had saluted Shakespeare before, with an A Midsummer Night's Dream collection in 2003. The collection was conceived in dialogue with the choreographer Benjamin Millepied, whose contemporary production of the ballet the maison supported, and with drawings by the Italian artist Lorenzo Mattotti as an iconographic source. Its pieces map the play at a density no other advertiser attempts. A Balcone brooch renders the balcony dripping with emeralds and garnets. Paired clips figure the two lovers. A Verona necklace takes the city's architecture as its pattern. Red and blue stones run through the collection as the colours of the two houses. The Fiore bracelet, built around two pear-shaped aquamarines of some fifty carats each and reported by Elite Traveler to have taken 1,645 hours of work, sets the stones, in the magazine's phrase, "connected, but never touching", with hidden diamond palmettes beneath them that the maison glosses as the lovers' clandestine marriage vows.

The collection proves that the standing edit is a function of price rather than of taste. A jeweller selling unique pieces to collectors can afford the secret marriage, the feud rendered as heraldry, even separation itself, aestheticized as two great stones held apart. What even a hundred unique pieces omit is the vault. There is no tomb brooch, no apothecary's vial in diamonds, no piece for the Friar's failed letter. At the luxury pole the edit does not disappear; it refines itself from deletion into discretion. The death is not denied. It is simply not jewelled, and a survey that prices the play's parts has to record that the most generous buyer in the entire record still stopped at the same wall as the cheapest phone plan.

The launch itself obeyed the collection's logic. Artnet's report of the unveiling records the maison staging its Verona under the glass dome of the Hotel d'Evreux in Paris, the courtyard dressed as an Italian garden with olive trees and bougainvillea, and Prokofiev's score sounding in the room, a launch environment built from the same three materials as the jewellery: place, desire and discretion. The maison's dance lineage runs back to 1967, when a meeting between Claude Arpels and the choreographer George Balanchine inspired the ballet Jewels, and the Romeo and Juliet collection returns the compliment half a century later by building a ballet's source story into gems in dialogue with Millepied's production. Several of the pieces are transformable, engineered to be worn in more than one configuration, so that even the mechanics of the jewellery rehearse the collection's theme of union and separation. Nothing in the launch or the work reaches past the lovers' separation to their end. The most literate advertiser in the survey, the one whose president speaks of literature as a founding inspiration and whose craftsmen spent 1,645 hours on a single bracelet, read the whole play and made the same cut as a thirty-second phone spot. The cut is not a failure of reading. It is the reading the market pays for, executed at the highest level of craft the market commands.

## Advertising's standing edit: the balcony bought, the tomb left

Lay the cases side by side and a single editorial line appears. Nextel ends on the word "die", spoken as one beat in a joke about speed. H&M ends on the deaths themselves, and is booed for it. Every other documented case in the survey stops at or before the meeting's consummation of attention: the embrace on a scrap of paper, the kiss on a poster, the lovers meeting in mid air, the feud dissolved by a tariff, the heroine offered a choice, the balcony preserved as a father's home movie, the union sealed in two stones that never touch. Paravano states the pattern as her corpus's law: the tragic and erotic qualities are downplayed in favour of a more amusing or romantic rendition (Paravano, 2021). The February trade performs the same selection at the level of the calendar, keeping the meeting and the window for the feast of lovers, as [the series' account of the play as a Valentine icon](/2029/09/14/romeo-and-juliet-and-valentines-day/) documents in its own territory. The wedding trade performs it at the level of the ceremony, borrowing the balcony sonnet and the shared lines for readings while the plot that surrounds them goes unmentioned at the altar. The edit, in other words, is not a habit of agencies alone. It is the standard public handling of the play wherever the play is used rather than studied, and the advertisers are simply its most systematic practitioners, the ones whose selections are preserved in dated, attributable records that a survey can audit.

The contrast that convicts the edit of being a decision rather than an ignorance is the play's own publicity. The Chorus sells the two hours' traffic by announcing the cost in the sixth line: "A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life" (Prologue, 6). The Prince closes the account the advertisers refuse to open: "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). The original sellers believed the deaths were part of the attraction, the foreknown price that makes the spending of the balcony scene legible. The play's advertisers hold the opposite belief, and hold it with a unanimity no conspiracy could enforce: the woe has no buyer. The schools, as this series' record of challenges shows, fight over the deaths in the open, through boards, statutes and hearings. Commerce performs the same removal without a hearing, a vote or a single complaint on file, which makes the commercial edit the more complete censorship of the two: the one that never has to announce itself.

Why is the line drawn exactly there? The balcony can be pointed at a future. It promises a state the buyer might enter: connected, chosen, desired, airborne between two buildings. The tomb forecloses every future it could be asked to sell, and publicity, as the next section's critics establish, is a trade in futures. Even the exceptions obey the logic. Nextel sells speed, and for speed the death is admissible as a unit of haste, the whole tragedy consumed at push-to-talk pace. LaChapelle sold denim with the deaths at full length and in earnest, and the audience's booing is the sound of the logic being violated: a funeral is not a future, and five minutes of it cannot be amortized by a pair of jeans. The edit is therefore not prudery and not ignorance of the text. It is the market pricing the play's scenes by their orientation in time, and finding exactly one of them facing forward.

Paravano's finding has a second half that the campaigns confirm just as uniformly: the erotic is edited with the tragic. The play's balcony scene is a negotiation conducted at the edge of a bedchamber, and its charge in the theatre is inseparable from that proximity, yet the commercial balcony is kept chaste with a discipline no censor imposed. Apple's lovers are children, performing for a parent. The Swiss poster's kiss is the corpus's maximum of contact, and it is a kiss in a public carriage, witnessed and ignored. Vodafone replaces the heroine's body with a seal's, converting desire into banter. Orange removes the bodies' meeting from the earth altogether and floats it, decorporealized, between two facades. The pattern is exact enough to state as a rule of the form: the campaigns may show wanting, because wanting is the engine of purchase, but they may not show the wanting satisfied or punished, because satisfaction ends the wanting and punishment indicts it. The balcony is the play's one great image of desire suspended at full charge, neither consummated nor destroyed, which is the precise emotional state a sales message needs its audience to remain in. The advertisers did not choose the scene despite its suspension. The suspension is the scene's commercial essence, and the play's other famous moments, the marriage, the duel, the tomb, all fail the same test for the same reason: each of them resolves.

## Do advertisements mirror Romeo and Juliet, rob it, or reproduce it?

The critics divide, and the survey's evidence can judge them. The advertising historian Stephen Fox built The Mirror Makers (1984), the standard history of the American industry, on the claim embedded in its title: advertising mirrors the society that produces it, registering its values more faithfully than its sermons. On Fox's account, a century of balconies simply registers the play's centrality, and the campaigns are evidence about the audience rather than about the trade. The cultural critic Judith Williamson, in Decoding Advertisements (1978), described the opposite flow: advertisements construct meaning by juxtaposition, transferring the charge of a known referent system onto an unknown product, so that the lovers function as a battery the product plugs into. John Berger, in the publicity chapter of Ways of Seeing (1972), located the energy source. Publicity, he wrote, "is not merely an assembly of competing messages: it is a language in itself", a language that manufactures glamour, glamour being the state of being envied, and that feeds deliberately on the spectator's schooling: "What he has learnt at school of history, mythology, poetry can be used in the manufacturing of glamour. Cigars can be sold in the name of a King, underwear in connection with the Sphinx, a new car by reference to the status of a country house." Berger's list, written decades after Rodriguez built his replica balcony in Havana, reads as a summary of this survey composed in advance, cigars included.

Berger's mechanism deserves a paragraph of its own before the dissenters are heard, because the corpus tests it in an unusual direction. In Berger's account, publicity shows the spectator a transformed version of the spectator's own future: the figure in the image is who the buyer might become, and glamour is the envy that figure attracts. The lovers' advertisements conspicuously decline that address. No campaign in the survey invites its audience to become Romeo or Juliet, an invitation the story's ending would poison, and the glamour is instead displaced onto a third position: the buyer is cast as the one who removes the obstacle, the supplier of the ladder, the plan, the chosen number, the camera that preserves the scene. The future being sold is still the buyer's, as Berger's grammar requires, but the buyer enters the story as its rescuer rather than as its victim, arriving with the product in the role the play reserves for the Friar, and succeeding where the Friar's arrangements failed. The refinement costs Berger's model nothing and buys the survey an explanation for the corpus's strangest regularity, that the audience is never asked to identify with the lovers at all. Identification is the one transaction the play's ending makes unsafe, and the industry, without ever stating the rule, routes around it in every documented case.

Against both the mirror and the battery stands Graham Holderness, whose The Shakespeare Myth of 1988 founded the study of Shakespeare in advertising and who dissents from the robbery model in terms: "Capitalism can now produce Shakespearean materials that display a textual richness and diversity that do justice to the dramatic works from which the material originally derives" (Holderness and Loughrey, 2016). His reading of the Orange spot, quoted earlier, credits a mobile-phone commercial with quoting Shakespeare for transcendent emotional liberty (Holderness, 2018). Douglas Lanier answers with the cooler finding that advertising "typically is not a source of new ideas about Shakespeare" (Lanier, 2012, p. 499), while performing a service no academy can match: it is "an important force for reproducing perceptions of Shakespeare from generation to generation and for disseminating them throughout a society, in forms at least as powerful as the tomes and performances of the 'official' guardians of 'proper' Shakespeare" (Lanier, 2012, p. 514). The practitioner's account, finally, is the honest minimum: Max Sutherland's work on advertising and the consumer's mind, cited by Paravano, treats the famous tune and the famous image as attention devices and memory joggers, and his research on humour in advertising supplies the mechanism behind the corpus's comic register, since, as he puts it in Paravano's citation, "there is less counter-arguing with humorous ads because viewers process them as entertainment rather than engage in a true/false evaluation of the product" (Sutherland, 2008). The balcony, on Sutherland's account, is a device for lowering the audience's guard with borrowed feeling, and the account has the virtue of explaining the executions one by one without claiming they mean anything.

The verdict on the critics follows the cases. Fox's mirror is the weakest instrument in the box, because the campaigns demonstrably do not reflect the play their audiences read: they reflect the poster the audiences recognize, and the mirror shows a culture admiring its own abridgement. Williamson's transfer is exact within any single execution, since value flows one way, from the lovers to the tariff, the denim and the scent, and nothing flows back; the Pasqua label is the exception that maps the rule, because there the borrowed value is the customers' own handwriting, returned to them at sixteen dollars a bottle. Holderness's justice claim fails on the survey's strongest test, since the one execution that attempted the whole story at length was received as an interruption, and the corpus's intelligence is everywhere spent on casting, compression and voice rather than on the text. Lanier's reproduction model carries the adjudication. What advertising does for the play, across a hundred and forty years, is maintenance: it keeps the balcony oiled as the play's public meaning, renewing the image in each generation's dominant medium, paper scrap, cinema film, television spot, poster, label, jewel, at a reach the official guardians cannot rival. Berger supplies the reason the maintenance always stops at the same frame. A language that sells futures cannot spend its grammar on a tomb, and so the reproduction is guaranteed, by the economics of the form itself, to be a reproduction with the ending removed.

## The verdict on Romeo and Juliet in advertising

Read as accounts, the campaigns price the play's assets with a consistency no committee of critics has matched. The balcony is dear and universally liquid: it has sold thread-adjacent respectability in the age of the trade card, call time in four countries, denim, a telephone's camera and a radio channel's tone. The names are an annuity: a cigar has drawn on them since 1875, a perfume since 2006, a national cuisine for as long as its records run. The feud is worth a joke and nothing more, repriced by T-Mobile as a billing error and by Vodafone as a misunderstanding a seal can shout down. The kiss is worth a poster with a single word of copy. The tomb, in the whole documented record, has received no bid at all, and the one attempt to include the deaths at their true weight was booed in a Kingston cinema. The industry is not the tragedy's enemy, and it is not its vandal. It is its most accurate valuer, and the valuation is the criticism: what a culture will pay to keep in circulation is what that culture, in public, means by the play.

The survey's two bookends state the asset's trajectory in a single comparison. In 1885 an American baby-food maker needed three quotations to borrow the playwright's authority, because the image alone could not yet be trusted to arrive. In 2004 a Swiss radio channel needed one word, because the image could no longer fail to arrive. Between those poles the words drained away exactly as fast as the recognition compounded, and the compounding is the point the mirror theorists miss: recognition of this order is not found in nature. It is manufactured, by schools and stages in part, but in measurable part by the advertising itself, each campaign paying a small royalty of attention into the fund the next campaign draws on. The trade is thus in the position of a tenant who improves the property he rents, and the improvement is real even though the rent is never paid. Lanier's reproduction thesis, adjudicated in the previous section, is simply this bookkeeping stated as criticism.

The survey's geography corroborates the mechanism. Its rows are set in Britain, the United States, France, Italy, Switzerland, Romania, Cuba and Brazil, markets with no common language, no common medium and no common advertising industry, sharing one condition: in each of them the play circulates as common cultural property, taught, staged, filmed and quoted until its central situation belongs to people who have never opened it. Where that condition holds, the asset is liquid and the borrowing appears, in forms as different as a Swiss poster, a Romanian telephone spot and a Brazilian lunch. No row in the table comes from a market where the play is a specialist taste, and the absence is itself a finding: the advertising follows the schooling, the staging and the speech, never the other way round. An advertiser cannot manufacture recognition of this depth at any budget, which is why the trade's whole strategy across a hundred and forty years has been tenancy rather than construction. The campaigns are best understood as a series of leases on a property the culture maintains for its own purposes, and the leases, read together, are the most complete record anyone keeps of the property's condition: which rooms are lit, which are let, and which one, at the back of the house, has stood empty and unoffered since the first tenant arrived.

The chronology carries one more pattern worth the record. Each new advertising medium, as it arrives, reaches for the same thirty seconds first, as if the balcony were the shakedown test by which a young form proves it can carry shared meaning. Print proved it with the scrap and the trade card, fixing the tableau before photography was cheap. Cinema proved it twice, in Zeffirelli's imagery as the carriers' borrowed memory and in LaChapelle's film as the form's one refusal. Television proved it five times in thirteen years, at compression ratios the print age could not have imagined. The poster proved it by subtraction, the label by substitution, the jewel case by accumulation. The sequence is not a history of taste. It is a history of media auditioning before the same examiner, and the examiner's question never changes: can the new form make a stranger feel the approach of two people across a forbidden distance, quickly, silently and without the ending? Every medium in the survey answered yes, and charged accordingly.

The cliche keeps the play in culture, as the series thesis holds, but the campaigns refine the claim: the cliche that circulates is a specific thirty seconds of the play, maintained at commercial expense because it faces forward, and the tragedy's remainder circulates on other budgets, in classrooms and theatres, subsidized by institutions rather than by advertisers. Romeo and Juliet in advertising is therefore not a dilution of the play's afterlife. It is the afterlife's audited account, and the audit's finding is that the public play is a balcony with a product climbing toward it, while the tomb waits, as it has waited since 1890, for a buyer the market has never produced.

A survey is defined as much by its refusals as by its rows, and the refusals here follow the evidence standard stated at the outset. Campaigns retold in roundup articles without a date, an agency or a surviving record have been left out, however often they are repeated, because a price list built on folklore would price nothing. Details the record divides, the year of a French telephone spot, the acquisition date of a cigar marque, a perfume house's founding against its launch, a car model's homage, are carried as divisions rather than resolved into the smoother story. What remains after those refusals is smaller than the play's fame and stronger than its legend: fourteen transactions across a hundred and forty years, each documented at the precision its sources allow, and together sufficient to support the article's single claim. The claim is not that advertisers love the play, or that they have been kind to it. It is that their ledgers, kept for money and therefore kept honestly, record which parts of the story the culture values, and at what price, and in which order. Read in that spirit, the advertising of Romeo and Juliet is the most candid criticism the play has ever received.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why do advertisers choose Romeo and Juliet more often than any other Shakespeare play?

Because no other play offers a complete situation that a stranger can decode in a glance. Douglas Lanier's survey of global Shakespearean advertising finds the field divided among three magnets, the playwright himself, Hamlet, and this play, with the lovers "by far the most fruitful territory for marketers" (Lanier, 2012). Cristina Paravano puts the play's share at around thirty percent of all Shakespeare-themed television and web advertising. The reason is compression: two houses, one prohibition, one window and one climb summarize the whole property, and the summary works for viewers who have never read a line. Recognition is the most expensive thing an advertisement buys, and this play supplies it free.

### Q: Which scene from the play appears most often in advertising?

The balcony scene, by an overwhelming margin. Across the documented record, from the Hildesheimer paper scraps of about 1890 to Apple's school-play spot of 2016, the campaigns restage the same configuration: a woman at a lit window above, a man in shadow below, and a gap between them that the product is invited to cross. The scene's dominance is practical before it is romantic. It reads in silence, at poster size, in any costume and any language, and it suspends the story at the point of approach, before consequences begin. Other scenes require plot; the balcony requires only geometry, which is why it is the single most rented image in the play's commercial afterlife.

### Q: What happens to the whole play inside Nextel's thirty-second commercial of 2003?

It is compressed into a single volley of dialogue. One of nine spots for Nextel's push-to-talk phones in 2003, the commercial stages a costumed performance in which the lovers exchange the entire tragedy in a breathless sequence: "Romeo, Juliet, I love you, ditto, die, marry him, never, no, better now, no, kids". Marjorie Garber read the volley as a modern stichomythia, the line-for-line fencing of classical dialogue. The spot closes on the tagline "Nextel. Done", and the campaign's reported profits rose by 23.5 percent across 2003 and 2004. Notably, the deaths survive the compression, but only as the single word "die", a beat inside a joke about speed.

### Q: How does T-Mobile's 2008 spot turn the feud into a phone-plan problem?

By repricing it. In the thirty-second American spot documented in the Adland trade archive, Juliet waits in Renaissance dress with her phone while Romeo arrives by motorbike in modern clothes. Her father objects in mock-archaic language, "thou betrayest thy family's cell plan", and then explains that he never really hated Romeo's family at all: Romeo "was just too expensive to call". The remedy is the product, an unlimited plan, which dissolves four centuries of staged hatred by removing its tariff. The joke works because the audience has already reduced the feud, in public understanding, to an obstacle in the way of a conversation, and the carrier simply bills the obstacle and then waives the charge.

### Q: Why does the French Orange commercial give the lovers no dialogue?

Because the picture and the music divide the labour between them. The Orange spot, which Cristina Paravano's study dates to 2009 in its abstract and to 2004 in its analysis, shows a young man and woman leaving facing windows and meeting in mid air, wordlessly, under the line "Orange intense: communiquons sans limites". Graham Holderness reads the execution as Shakespeare quoted "as a familiar source for images of beauty, love, transgression of barriers and a transcendent emotional liberty" (Holderness, 2018). Identification is carried by the score, Nino Rota's love theme from Zeffirelli's 1968 film, which names the floating pair as the lovers more efficiently than any line of dialogue could, while the product, limitless communication, is enacted by two bodies crossing the gap without a word.

### Q: Why is Juliet a seal in Vodafone Italy's 2013 campaign?

Because the campaign is built on the heroine's refusal to play her assigned part. Vodafone Italy's 2013 commercials cast Juliet as an impertinent seal, voiced by the comedian Luciana Littizzetto, waiting in a night garden where Romeo, played by Kyle James, throws a stone at her balcony and hits her instead. James was dubbed by Francesco Pezzulli, the Italian voice of Leonardo DiCaprio, so the campaign's Romeo arrives pre-voiced by the film Romeo a generation of Italians already knew. The seal Juliet complains, mocks Romeo's lateness and is offered the campaign's proposition, unlimited calls to one chosen number, under the tagline "you choose". Paravano reads the ending as a happy one for Juliet alone, her death excluded and the relationship made optional.

### Q: How does Apple's 2016 spot turn a school play into a feature film?

By revealing the camera. Released on 5 December 2016 and made by TBWA Media Arts Lab for the iPhone 7 "Practically Magic" campaign, the spot opens as full cinema: a castle garden at night, child actors playing the balcony scene with professional production value. The shot then withdraws to disclose a school hall and a father filming the performance on his phone, and the closing line assigns the miracle to the device: "Your movies look like movies on iPhone 7". The play supplies the emotional standard against which the phone's video is measured, since a parent's recording of a child's Juliet is precisely the home movie every buyer wants to look like a film. The lovers are not selling the telephone; they are calibrating it.

### Q: What did H&M's five-minute Romeo and Juliet film do that other campaigns refuse to do?

It kept the ending. David LaChapelle's five-minute cinema film for H&M in 2005 staged the story at length to sell jeans and declined the industry's standing edit, carrying the lovers through to their deaths on screen. Campaign magazine's diary reported the consequence from an Odeon cinema in Kingston, where the audience giggled, then booed, then applauded wildly when the film ended, applause the diary understood as relief that the advertisement was over. The case is the survey's control experiment: the tragedy at full weight, inside a selling situation, was received as an interruption. Every other documented campaign can be read as the industry's correction of LaChapelle's experiment, shortening the story to the meeting and stopping before the tomb.

### Q: What did Van Cleef and Arpels build from the play in 2019?

A hundred pieces of high jewellery. Unveiled during Paris Haute Couture Week in July 2019, the maison's Romeo and Juliet collection comprised one hundred unique pieces, conceived, as its president Nicolas Bos explained, in dialogue with Benjamin Millepied's contemporary production of the ballet and with drawings by Lorenzo Mattotti. A Balcone brooch renders the balcony in emeralds and garnets; paired clips figure the lovers; red and blue stones carry the colours of the two houses. The Fiore bracelet sets two aquamarines of some fifty carats each, in the phrase of Elite Traveler's report, "connected, but never touching", over hidden diamond palmettes the maison glosses as the clandestine marriage vows. Even at this density of narrative, the collection includes no tomb.

### Q: How can a Swiss poster advertise Shakespeare with no line from any play?

By trusting the audience to finish the picture. The posters Douglas Lanier describes, run in Swiss cities in 2004 and 2005 for Espace 2, the cultural channel of Radio Suisse Romande, show two teenagers kissing in a subway train full of inattentive passengers, captioned with a single word: "Shakespeare." No balcony, costume or quotation appears. The kiss names the play, the caption names the author, and the passers-by supply the feud, the risk and the window from a public image of the lovers maintained by a century of exactly such borrowings. The poster is the limiting case of Shakespearean advertising: text has drained out of the form entirely, and recognition, contributed free by the culture, does all the work the copy once did.

### Q: What appears on the labels of Pasqua's Romeo and Juliet wines?

A photograph of a wall. The Verona winery Famiglia Pasqua, founded in 1925, builds its PassioneSentimento line as its self-described Romeo and Juliet project, and the labels of its red, white, Prosecco and Prosecco Rose carry Gio Martorana's photograph of the graffiti-covered wall at the entrance to Juliet's courtyard in the Via Cappello, the stretch of stone where visitors leave messages of love. No lovers, no balcony and no skyline appear. The winery, whose United States release priced the line at sixteen dollars, sells the one element of the myth its own customers manufacture: the handwriting of the shrine's visitors, bottled. The label certifies attendance at the story rather than retelling it, which is why it needs no picture of the play at all.

### Q: Why does the perfume house Juliette Has a Gun arm its Juliet?

Because the brand sells her escape from the plot. Romano Ricci, great-grandson of Nina Ricci, created the house in 2005 by some profiles and launched it in December 2006 by the trade accounts; its first fragrances were composed with Francis Kurkdjian. In the house's own formula, relayed by The Perfume Society, "the innocent Juliet of Shakespeare is transposed to the 21st century with a gun", the gun serving as metaphor for the perfume itself, a weapon of seduction and a symbol of liberation. The commercial proof came with Not a Perfume in 2010, built on a single synthetic molecule, whose success expanded the line. The name promises the buyer not the romance but the revision: a Juliet equipped, this time, to survive her own story.

### Q: In Brazil's Romeu e Julieta, which food plays Romeo and which food plays Juliet?

The cheese plays Romeo and the guava paste plays Juliet. Romeu e Julieta is Brazil's documented name, recorded in Atlas Obscura's food pages, for the pairing of queijo minas, the mild white cheese of Minas Gerais, with goiabada, a firm red guava paste: salty against sweet, white against red, each incomplete alone. The casting has spread through the country's food industry as a flavor in its own right, appearing in ice creams, cheesecakes, brigadeiros and pizzas from countless makers, with the cheese rendered as an ice cream and the guava as its sauce in Minas Gerais. No advertiser devised the pairing. A cuisine assigned the lovers to two ingredients that must be eaten together, and in doing so produced the survey's control case: the names, left to a culture without a client, still file the couple under union rather than catastrophe.

### Q: What did the cigar promoter Jose Rodriguez try to buy in Verona?

Juliet's house, to smoke in. Jose Rodriguez Fernandez, known as Don Pepin, acquired the Romeo y Julieta cigar marque in 1903 by most trade accounts, one retailer history says 1908, and promoted it as theatre: a shop in Verona at the Hotel Capulet, a racehorse named Julieta, and an offer to purchase the Palazzo Capuleti and run it as a cigar showroom, with a young woman beautiful as Juliet appearing on the balcony so that smokers might sit, in the trade-retold phrase preserved by Holt's Cigar Company, "in the very rooms where the young lovers met". Verona refused. Rodriguez built a replica palazzo in Havana instead, by report with a copy of the balcony, and the marque's promotion ran to twenty thousand personalized cigar bands for wealthy customers. The episode is the survey's boldest transaction: the first great Romeo and Juliet advertiser tried to buy the set itself.

### Q: Where did Alfa Romeo's Giulietta model name come from?

The record does not settle the question, and the honest answer is that the homage is suggested rather than proven. Alfa Romeo's marque name descends from the surname of Nicola Romeo, a separate matter, but the company launched a Giulietta model with the Type 750 in 1954 and revived the name for the hatchback of 2010. When the revived car reached Britain, its launch campaign by Leo Burnett, fronted by Uma Thurman, quoted The Tempest, "Such stuff as dreams are made on", rather than the lovers. The motoring press of August 2010 recorded the uncertainty plainly: the connection between the company and the choice of the name Giulietta "is not clear, with some suggesting homage to Shakespeare's Juliet". The pairing attracts the attribution; the archive declines to sign it.

### Q: What did a 2012 valuation claim a Shakespeare brand would be worth?

Six hundred million dollars, hypothetically. A brand-valuation study of 2012, reported in the trade press and cited in Cristina Paravano's study of Shakespeare and mobile phones, estimated that a Shakespeare brand, if one existed to be owned, would be worth six hundred million dollars, double the combined values the same exercise assigned to Elvis Presley, Marilyn Monroe and George Foreman. The figure is best read as a publicity artifact rather than an appraisal, since the asset it prices cannot be bought, sold or licensed, but its direction matches the documented evidence of this survey: the name carries a charge that campaigns borrow at no charge, renewed by schools, weddings and everyday speech at no cost to the borrowers. The valuation measures the size of the commons from which every case in the survey draws.

### Q: How do the phone commercials Cristina Paravano studied change the ending?

They repeal it. Paravano's study of five mobile-phone campaigns, published in Lingue e Linguaggi in 2021, finds that in every case the specialists "downplayed the play's tragic and erotic qualities in a bid to attract potential buyers with a more amusing or romantic rendition of the story". Nextel's spot of 2003 keeps the deaths only as the word "die" inside a joke about speed; the campaigns that follow, as Paravano notes, move toward happy endings, since a carrier's promise is that connection saves. T-Mobile dissolves the feud into a pricing dispute, Vodafone Italy offers its seal Juliet a choice and excludes her death, and Apple's spot preserves only the balcony, reframed as a father's home movie. The pattern matches the sociologists' counterfactual: with phones, the lovers might have lived happily ever after, and the advertisers sell the counterfactual.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch advertising survey, and what does each row record?

The InsightCrunch advertising survey is the table in this article's verdict section, built as the quick reference for the play's commercial afterlife. Each row records one documented case of Romeo and Juliet in advertising: the case and its maker, the year, the medium, what was borrowed from the play, and the point at which the borrower stopped the story. The rows run from the Hildesheimer paper scraps of about 1890 through the telephone campaigns of 2003 to 2016 to the Van Cleef and Arpels collection of 2019, and the survey admits no case it cannot date or attribute. Read down the final column, the rows compose the article's evidence: embrace, meeting, kiss, choice, wall, union, and, in only two cases, the deaths, once as a syllable and once to booing.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch advertising survey to argue that the market buys the meeting and declines the ending?

Start from the survey's final column, which prices the play scene by scene. Set the balcony rows, Nextel's compression, T-Mobile's tariff, Vodafone's choosing Juliet, Apple's home movie, against the ending's two appearances in the entire record: the word "die" retained as a beat in a 2003 joke about speed, and H&M's full-length deaths of 2005, which Campaign magazine reported being booed in a Kingston cinema. The pattern licenses the thesis that the commercial afterlife is an audited selection rather than a dilution: the market maintains, at its own expense, exactly the thirty seconds of the play that face forward, and leaves the tomb to institutions with other budgets. Close by noting the exception's function: the booed film is not a refutation of the pattern but its price tag, the one measurement of what the ending costs in a selling situation.

### Q: Do the advertisements change what the play means, or only repeat what audiences already believe it means?

On the documented evidence, they repeat, and the repetition is itself a kind of change. Douglas Lanier's adjudicated position is that advertising "typically is not a source of new ideas about Shakespeare" (Lanier, 2012), and the survey bears him out: no campaign in the record interprets the play, argues with it or discovers anything in it. Yet Lanier also calls advertising a force "at least as powerful as the tomes and performances of the 'official' guardians" in reproducing perceptions of Shakespeare across generations. A meaning repeated at that scale, in every generation's dominant medium, with the ending removed each time, hardens into the public meaning, whatever the text says. The advertisements do not revise the play. They decide which play the public is given to remember, and they have been deciding, in the same direction, since about 1890.
